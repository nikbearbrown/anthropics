Chapter 7. Product. Building and Shipping. If you're reading this book, there's a reasonable chance you're building something. Maybe it's a sales product. Maybe it's a course or a membership site. Maybe you're a vibe coder who built an app with cursor or bolt and now needs to figure out what to build next. How to describe what you're building and how to communicate changes to people who use it. The product plugin brings product management discipline to people who don't have a product team. And discipline here doesn't mean bureaucracy. It means the practice of clearly defining what you're building, for whom and why, so that you build the right thing instead of the interesting thing. For solo builders, this plugin serves as the structured thinking partner you don't have. It pushes you to answer the questions a good product manager would ask, who is this for? What problem does it solve? How will you know it's working? What are you not building and why? What IT does? Feature specification. PRDs, product requirements, documents, and feature specs that define what you're building clearly enough that you or anyone helping you build can execute against them. This isn't paperwork for its own sake. It's the document that prevents you from getting halfway through building something and realizing you never decided how it should handle edge cases. User story generation. I want users to be able to book calls, becomes a set of discrete testable requirements. A user can see available time slots, select one, provide their information, receive a confirmation, and reschedule, if needed. Each story is small enough to build in a focused session. Research synthesis. Consolidate user feedback, survey results, support tickets, and competitive intelligence into actionable insights about what to build next. The product plugin doesn't just summarize feedback, it identifies patterns and helps you prioritize based on frequency, severity, and strategic alignment. Roadmap thinking. What should you build next? What can wait? The product plugin helps you think through prioritization, not with a fancy framework, but by forcing you to articulate why one feature matters more than another. For solo builders who tend to chase whatever is most exciting, this structured prioritization is genuinely valuable. Release communication. Release notes, change logs, and update announcements that tell users what changed and why they should care. Connecting from the user's perspective, not the builders. Getting started. Install from the plugin's sidebar. The product plugin works immediately for documentation and planning tasks. For richer integration, connect it to your project management tool. Gira, linear, notion, GitHub issues, or whatever you use to track work. If you use analytics, amplitude, mix panel, post-hog, connecting those gives clawed access to actual usage data when analyzing features. If you don't use any of these tools, if your project management is a text file, and your analytics is checking stripe every morning, the product plugin still works. It just works from what you tell it rather than pulling from connected systems. Visual workflows. From idea to spec, when you know what you want to build but haven't defined it clearly, I want to add a booking feature to my website. Users should be able to see my available time slots. Pick one, provide their contact info, and get a confirmation email. Write a feature spec that I can build from. Cloud produces a structured specification. What the feature does, who it's for, the user flow, edge cases to handle. What happens if two people try to book the same slot, and acceptance criteria, how do you know it's done, whether you're building this yourself or handing it to a developer. The spec ensures you've thought through the details before writing the first line of code. User story breakdown. When a feature is too big to build in one go. Break down the booking feature into user stories I can build one at a time. Each story should be something I can complete in a focused work session. Cloud decomposes the feature into discrete, buildable pieces. Each with a clear description of what the user can do when it's complete. You build story by story. Testing is you go, rather than trying to ship everything at once. The what should I build next? Conversation. This is where the product plug-in earns its keep for solo builders. When you have ten ideas and limited time. Here are the features I'm considering for the next quarter. List them. Have about 40 hours of building time per month. Given what I know about my users, they're mostly freelance designers who use the tool for client proposals. Help me prioritize. What should I build first? What can wait? And what should I probably not build at all? Cloud applies prioritization thinking. What has the highest impact for the most users? What's technically feasible within your constraints? What aligns with your product direction? And what's a distraction dressed up as a good idea? Having someone push back on your feature ideas, even an AI someone, prevents the common solo builder trap of building what's fun instead of what matters. User feedback synthesis. When you've accumulated feedback from customers. I have a folder of customer feedback emails and survey responses from the past three months. What are the top three things people are asking for? What are the biggest pain points? And is there anything surprising? Patterns I might not have expected. Cloud reads through the feedback, identifies patterns, and presents them ranked by frequency and severity. This is the kind of analysis that small product teams skip because it's time consuming. And then they build the wrong thing because they optimized for the loudest voice instead of the most common need. Release notes. When you've shipped updates and need to communicate them. I just launched three updates. A new dashboard view, faster load times on mobile, and a fix for the calendar sync bug that six people complained about. Right release notes that are clear, concise, and focused on what users will notice. Cloud drafts notes that emphasize user benefit rather than technical implementation. Your dashboard now loads in under a second on mobile matters more to users than optimized react rendering pipeline. You review, adjust tone, and publish. Decision documentation. When you're choosing between approaches. I'm deciding between building my own authentication system and using clerk. Document the trade-offs. Cost, complexity, flexibility, maintenance burden. So I can make a decision and remember why I made it later. Cloud structures the comparison and you make the call. Six months from now, when you wonder why you chose one approach over the other, the document is there. This habit, documenting decisions when you make them, is one of the highest leverage practices a solo builder can adopt. Tips. Spec before you build. Even for small features, a five minute spec prevents an hour of rework. The product plugin makes speccing fast enough that there's no excuse to skip it. The discipline of writing down what you're building before you build it catches ambiguities, hedge cases, and scope creep before they cost you time. Use it for decision documentation. We chose approach A over approach B because of X and Y is easy to write in the moment and invaluable six months later. Every undocumented decision is a future mystery. Connect feedback to roadmap. When customers ask, why don't you have feature X? The product plugin can help you research whether that's a common request, what the implementation would involve and where it should sit in your priorities. This turns reactive customer conversations into strategic product conversations. Review your specs after building. Once you build a feature, compare the result to the original spec. Where did you deviate? Was the deviation an improvement or a compromise? This feedback loop makes your future specs better.
