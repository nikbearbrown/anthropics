Chapter 10. Building your own plug-ins. The official plug-ins cover common professional functions, but your work isn't generic. You have specific processes, recurring workflows, and domain knowledge that no off-the-shelf plug-in can capture. That's where custom plug-ins come in. Building a custom plug-in sounds technical. It isn't. A anthropic built-to-tool called plug-in creates specifically for this purpose. You describe your workflow in plain language, and it constructs the plug-in for you. If you can explain how you do something, you can build a plug-in that helps clawed do it with you. Why custom plug-ins matter? Every business has processes that are uniquely theirs. Maybe you have a specific way you onboard clients, a sequence of emails, documents, and check-ins that you've refined over years. Maybe you review deliverables against a particular checklist. Maybe you write proposals that follow a proven structure. These processes live in your head, or in a document somewhere. When you do them, they work well because you've internalized the steps and the standards. When you try to delegate them to an employee, a contractor, or an AI, the results are inconsistent because the knowledge hasn't been transferred. A custom plug-in is that transfer. It encodes your process, the steps, the standards, the decision points, and to something clawed can follow consistently. Once built, it works every time, the same way, without you having to explain the process from scratch. For solar-proners, this is especially powerful. You are the business. Your methods are the business's methods. A custom plug-in preserves and scales your expertise. How plug-in create works? Plug-in create is itself a plug-in. Install it from the plug-in sidebar. Once active, it guides you through building a custom plug-in by asking you questions about what you want the plug-in to do. The process is conversational. You describe your workflow, your standards, and your preferences. A custom create structures this information into the components of a plug-in. Skills that encode your domain knowledge, slash commands that trigger specific actions, and connectors that link to relevant tools. You don't write code, you don't edit configuration files. You have a conversation about how you work, and a plug-in emerges from that conversation. What goes into a plug-in? Slinging the anatomy helps you build better ones. Skills are the domain expertise. For a proposal writing plug-in, the skills might include your proposal structure, your pricing logic, your standard terms, and your approach to scoping projects. These are written as clear instructions that Claude follows. Slash commands are the shortcuts. Slash new proposal might trigger your full proposal workflow. Slash client onboard might kick off your onboarding sequence. Each command maps to a specific capability you want quick access to. Connectors link the plug-in to tools and data. If your proposal plug-in needs access to your pricing spreadsheet, a connector makes that link. If your onboarding plug-in needs to create documents in a specific folder, a connector handles the file access. Plugins are file-based. They're essentially organized sets of instructions and configurations stored as text. This means they're easy to share, easy to modify, and easy to understand if you ever want to peek under the hood. Building your first custom plug-in. Start with something you do repeatedly and would benefit from consistency. Client onboarding. Project kickoff, content review, weekly reporting, anything that follows a recognizable pattern. Here's how the process works in practice. Say you want to build a plug-in for your client onboarding process. Step 1. Describe the process. Open co-work with plug-in, create active, and say, I want to build a plug-in for my client onboarding workflow. When I sign a new client, I need to create a project folder with standard subfolders. Draft a welcome email with project timeline. Generate a kickoff questionnaire tailored to their project type and create a project tracking document. The process varies slightly depending on whether it's a design project, a development project, or a strategy engagement. Step 2. Answer questions. Plug-in create asks you to elaborate. What goes in each subfolder? What does your welcome email typically include? What questions are in the kickoff questionnaire? How does the tracking document differ by project type? Step 3. Review and refine. Plug-in create builds the plug-in based on your answers. You test it with a real scenario. Enboard a new development client called Meridian Design and see whether the result matches what you do manually. Adjust anything that's off. Step 4. Use it. Your onboarding plugin is now available. Every time you sign a client, type slash client onboard and your entire process executes, consistently, completely, and in minutes instead of hours. What to build plugins for? Look for workflows where. You follow the same steps every time. If the process is consistent enough that you could write it down, it's a plugin candidate. Consistency matters. If every client should get the same quality onboarding, if every proposal should cover the same basis, if every report should follow the same structure, a plugin ensures that consistency. You'd rather not think about it. The administrative parts of your work, the setup, the formatting, the standard communications are perfect for plugins. Save your thinking for the work that actually requires thought. You've wished you could clone yourself. The phrase, I just wish I could hand this off, usually describes a plugin opportunity. The process you delegate, if you had someone to delegate to, is the process a plugin can handle. Sharing and evolving. Once you've built a plugin, you can share it with team members, with peers, or with the broader community through the plugin directory. If you've built something useful for your industry, others in similar roles would likely benefit. One should evolve. After using one for a few weeks, you'll notice what works and what's missing. Update the plugin to reflect your improved understanding. The best plugins are living documents of your best practices, refined through use. The directory and community. Beyond the 11 official plugins and what you build yourself, a growing ecosystem of community created plugins is emerging. The plugin directory at Claude.com slash plugins includes submissions from other users who've built plugins for specific industries, roles, and workflows. Before building something from scratch, check whether someone has already built something similar. A community plugin customized to your specifics is often faster than starting from nothing. After 11, combining plugins. Individual plugins are useful. Plugin combinations are where the real leverage appears, and where the team of specialists metaphor stops being a metaphor, and starts being an accurate description of what's happening. When you use multiple plugins together, Claude coordinates between them, drawing on different domains of expertise within a single conversation. This is closer to how a real team works than anything a solo operator normally has access to. You don't go to your marketing director, get a report, then walk it over to your sales lead, and ask for their take. You sit them in a room together and say, here's the situation. How should we approach this?
