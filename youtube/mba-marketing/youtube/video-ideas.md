## Candidate 01 — The Pepsi Paradox: Brand Changes the Taste

- Source: `mba-marketing/chapters/05-consumer-markets-and-purchasing-behavior.md`
- Topic: MARKETING
- Hook: In a blind taste test, half of people prefer Pepsi over Coke — but tell them it's Coke and that number jumps to 75%. The label literally changes what they taste.
- Key case: 2003 Baylor fMRI study — blind condition ~50% Pepsi preference; labeled condition 75% Coke preference, with medial prefrontal cortex (brand/identity region) activating only in the labeled condition
- The Question: If two drinks are chemically almost identical, how can knowing the brand name produce a measurably different brain response?
- Core idea: Brand doesn't add information about the product — it triggers a separate cognitive process (cultural identity, memory, belonging) that runs parallel to sensory processing. The experience of taste is constructed after sensory input is filtered through that identity layer.
- Visual object: Split-brain diagram — left side "sensory signal only," right side "sensory signal + brand identity layer" — showing two different output values from the same input
- Manim move: split → duplicate (two processing paths from single input) → compare (output values)
- Example seed: A barista serves the same espresso in two cups: one labeled "house blend," one labeled "single-origin Ethiopia Yirgacheffe." Customers rate the labeled cup 30% higher on complexity. Same coffee, different story — different experience.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: c2v (fMRI brain scan stills), raster (Pepsi/Coke logo)
- Prerequisites: None — fully self-contained consumer story
- Exclusions: Full five-stage buying process, Maslow hierarchy, all four buying-behavior quadrants
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 02 — V=B/P: Why Gatorade Has Owned 67% of Its Market for 55 Years

- Source: `mba-marketing/chapters/02-marketing-and-customer-value.md`
- Topic: MARKETING
- Hook: Gatorade and Powerade have nearly identical ingredients. Powerade is cheaper. Gatorade still holds 67.7% market share vs. Powerade's 13.7% — and has for more than 50 years.
- Key case: Gatorade (67.7% share) vs. Powerade (13.7% share) in the sports drink category; both are electrolyte solutions with comparable formulations
- The Question: If value = Benefits ÷ Price, and both products deliver the same functional benefit at a lower price for Powerade, why does Gatorade win?
- Core idea: The V=B/P formula has four benefit layers — functional, monetary, social, and psychological. Gatorade's 55-year lead is built on social (athlete identity) and psychological (belonging to a culture) benefits that Powerade's lower price cannot offset. Competing on price alone attacks only one term in the denominator.
- Visual object: Two-column value stack — Gatorade vs. Powerade — with four benefit rows; social and psychological rows light up green for Gatorade, grey for Powerade, price row lights green for Powerade
- Manim move: accumulate (stack benefit layers) → compare (two columns side by side)
- Example seed: A recreational runner buys Gatorade before a 5K even though the store-brand electrolyte drink is $0.50 cheaper. Asked why, she says "Gatorade just feels right." That feeling is the product — the formula is just the container.
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: c2v (market share chart), geo (none needed)
- Prerequisites: None
- Exclusions: Full 4Ps system, three-nested-environments model, ethics boundary detail
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 03 — The CLV Reversal: Why Your Most Profitable Segment Might Be Your Worst Investment

- Source: `mba-marketing/chapters/03-strategic-planning-in-marketing.md`
- Topic: MARKETING
- Hook: A company's Gen Z segment generates $34,000 in annual profit per customer — the highest of any segment. But it's still the worst investment. Here's the math.
- Key case: Gen Z: $34,000/year profit, 2-year retention → $66,400 CLV. Baby Boomers: $28,000/year profit, 10-year retention → $280,000 CLV. A single 2-year retention extension for Gen Z → $270,400 CLV (highest segment).
- The Question: If Gen Z produces the most profit per year, why does it rank last in total customer lifetime value — and what single change flips the entire ranking?
- Core idea: Customer Lifetime Value = Annual Profit × Retention Years. Annual profit per customer is only one term. Retention multiplies it. A segment with high annual value but low retention can rank below a segment with moderate annual value and high retention — and a small retention investment rewrites the entire priority ranking.
- Visual object: Bar chart race — three segments, two bars each (annual profit vs. CLV) — showing rank reversal as retention years change
- Manim move: transform (annual profit ranking → CLV ranking shows reversal) → accumulate (years stacking up to show CLV building)
- Example seed: Imagine hiring two salespeople: one closes $34K deals every 2 years, then clients leave; the other closes $28K deals and clients stay 10 years. Who do you invest in? The answer flips depending on which metric you're tracking.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: c2v (bar charts)
- Prerequisites: Basic familiarity with profit concepts
- Exclusions: Full BCG matrix, full Ansoff matrix, SWOT, SMART objectives detail
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 04 — Amazon Prime's Price Paradox: Raise the Price, Keep the Customer

- Source: `mba-marketing/chapters/15-pricing-products-and-services.md`
- Topic: MARKETING
- Hook: Amazon raised the price of Prime three times — $79 to $99 to $119 to $139, a 76% total increase — and membership grew to 200 million. Customers called each increase a good deal.
- Key case: Amazon Prime pricing history: 2005 $79 → 2014 $99 → 2018 $119 → 2022 $139. Membership: ~25M in 2013 → 200M+ in 2022. Each increase accompanied by added benefits (video streaming, music, pharmacy, grocery delivery).
- The Question: Standard economics says higher prices reduce demand. Why did three consecutive price increases produce record membership growth, and why did customers feel better about the deal each time?
- Core idea: In the V=B/P formula, price is only the denominator. Amazon systematically grew the benefits bundle (B) faster than it raised price (P), so perceived value increased even as the dollar cost rose. Captive pricing compounds this: once a customer uses Prime for shipping, each added service has near-zero marginal cost to them — B keeps growing while P is fixed until the next increase.
- Visual object: Two-axis graph — Price line rising in steps, Benefits line rising faster — showing the gap between them widening at each increase
- Manim move: accumulate (benefits stacking per year) → compare (price step vs. benefits curve)
- Example seed: A gym raises its monthly fee from $30 to $45, but adds a sauna, towel service, and nutrition coaching. Members complain for a week, then call it the best gym deal in the city. The price went up; the value went up more.
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: c2v (price/membership chart)
- Prerequisites: Basic understanding of supply and demand
- Exclusions: Full five-step pricing process, break-even analysis, psychological pricing tactics detail
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 05 — The 4P Coherence Trap: Why One Wrong Signal Makes Everything Cheap

- Source: `mba-marketing/chapters/11-product-promotion-price-and-place.md`
- Topic: MARKETING
- Hook: A premium handcrafted knife sells for $89 on Amazon, right next to a $11 version with the same product photos and keywords. The $89 knife gets one-star reviews complaining about price. Nothing is wrong with the knife — everything is wrong with where it's being sold.
- Key case: Premium knife on Amazon — high price, commodity placement, identical promotional signals to budget competitors. Customers read "overpriced commodity" regardless of actual product quality.
- The Question: If product quality is identical, why does changing the place of sale change how customers perceive the product's value?
- Core idea: The 4Ps (Product, Price, Promotion, Place) are a signaling system. Customers use all four signals together to form a value judgment. When one P sends an incompatible signal — a premium product in a commodity channel — the contradictory signal overrides the others. The customer doesn't investigate further; they apply the cheapest available explanation.
- Visual object: Four-square grid (the 4Ps) with arrows pointing to a single "customer value perception" output — one square highlighted red showing signal contradiction collapsing the output
- Manim move: collapse (four signals combining into one perception) → split (showing which signal dominates when they conflict)
- Example seed: A Michelin-starred chef opens a food truck. The food is identical to the restaurant, but the line is half-full and Yelp reviewers call it "expensive for a food truck." Same product, same price, different place — different value verdict.
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: c2v (Amazon screenshot-style mock)
- Prerequisites: Basic familiarity with marketing mix concept
- Exclusions: Full macro/micro/internal environment model, full product life cycle
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 06 — The Buying Center: Why "The Buyer" Is the Last Person You Need to Convince

- Source: `mba-marketing/chapters/06-business-markets-and-purchasing-behavior.md`
- Topic: MARKETING
- Hook: In 86% of large and medium B2B purchases, the final decision is made by a group — not one person. Targeting "the buyer" means you've already lost the other five people shaping the outcome.
- Key case: Germany procurement study — 86% of major purchasing decisions at large/medium firms made by groups of 2–20 people. Six distinct roles: Initiator, Influencer, Gatekeeper, Buyer, Decider, User — each with different information needs and different veto points.
- The Question: If your sales process treats B2B purchasing as a single-person decision, how much of the buying center are you leaving unpersuaded?
- Core idea: B2B purchases involve a buying center — a temporary coalition of people with different roles, different motivations, and different information needs. A salesperson who reaches only the designated "buyer" has persuaded one node in a six-node network. The gatekeeper can block access; the decider holds final authority; the user can torpedo post-sale adoption.
- Visual object: Network diagram — six labeled nodes (roles) with arrows showing information and authority flows — one node highlighted as "where most sales efforts land" vs. the full network
- Manim move: spread (nodes appearing to show full buying center) → trace (path of typical sales contact vs. full influence map)
- Example seed: An IT vendor spends six months building a relationship with the IT manager (buyer). The CFO (decider) kills the deal in the final review because no one spoke to him about ROI. The sale wasn't lost in month six — it was never going to close without the decider in the room from month one.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: c2v (org chart / network diagram)
- Prerequisites: Basic understanding of B2B vs. B2C distinction
- Exclusions: Full eight-stage buying process, all four buy classes, derived demand mechanics
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 07 — Starbucks Australia: When the Winning Playbook Fails

- Source: `mba-marketing/chapters/09-marketing-in-a-global-environment.md`
- Topic: MARKETING
- Hook: Starbucks used the exact same expansion strategy that worked in more than 60 countries, entered Australia, lost $105 million, and closed 61 stores in a single year. The strategy wasn't wrong. The market was already solved.
- Key case: Starbucks Australia 2000–2008 — rapid expansion (87 stores), same menu/format as global template, $105M losses, mass closure of 61 stores in 2008. Australia had a mature, independent espresso cafe culture established before Starbucks arrived.
- The Question: If a market-entry strategy succeeded in 60 other countries, what makes a single market immune to it?
- Core idea: Global expansion strategies are built on assumptions about the competitive baseline — that the target market lacks the product category Starbucks is introducing. Australia's cafe culture made locals more sophisticated about espresso than Starbucks's menu, not less. When the target market already has a high-quality, locally rooted version of your product, standardization is a liability, not an asset.
- Visual object: World map with Starbucks success dots (60+ countries) and one red X on Australia — then zoom in to show Australia's existing cafe density before 2000
- Manim move: spread (success dots appearing globally) → collapse (zoom to Australia showing market already occupied)
- Example seed: A popular American burger chain enters Japan confident in its formula. Japan already has high-end burger culture with local chains. The American chain's "innovation" is ordinary to the local customer. Market-entry assumptions that held in 59 other countries dissolve in market 60.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: geo (world map, Australia), raster (Starbucks closure news)
- Prerequisites: Basic understanding of global market entry
- Exclusions: Full Hofstede five dimensions, all six entry modes, full standardization vs. adaptation framework
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 08 — The Service-Profit Chain: Bad Customer Service Is Almost Never a Customer Service Problem

- Source: `mba-marketing/chapters/14-services-the-intangible-product.md`
- Topic: MARKETING
- Hook: When a customer has a terrible service experience, the impulse is to retrain the frontline employee. But the failure usually happened three layers back — in how the company treats its own staff.
- Key case: Wired Coffee Bar case — variable service quality tracked to inconsistent internal service quality (staffing, training, tools), not individual employee attitude. Service-profit chain: internal service quality → employee satisfaction → employee retention → service value → customer satisfaction → customer loyalty → profit growth.
- The Question: If customer satisfaction is the goal, why do the most important levers sit inside the organization — invisible to the customer?
- Core idea: The service-profit chain is a causal sequence: internal service quality (how well the organization supports employees) determines employee satisfaction, which determines service consistency, which determines customer experience. Fixing the customer-facing symptom without fixing the internal cause produces temporary improvement. The root cause is always upstream.
- Visual object: Seven-link chain diagram — left end "Internal Service Quality," right end "Profit Growth" — with a break highlighted in the middle link and an arrow showing where most organizations intervene vs. where the break actually is
- Manim move: trace (following causal chain left to right) → collapse (showing the break point) → transform (fixing upstream vs. downstream)
- Example seed: An airline's gate agents are consistently rated hostile. The airline sends them to a customer service training. Scores improve briefly, then fall back. Six months later an internal audit finds gate agents lack the system access to resolve problems themselves — they're apologizing for failures they can't fix. The training was the wrong intervention.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: c2v (chain diagram)
- Prerequisites: Basic understanding of service vs. product distinction
- Exclusions: Full IHIP properties, full Gap Model, Services Marketing Triangle detail
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 09 — The Discount-Seeking Trap: How Sales Promotions Train Customers to Never Pay Full Price

- Source: `mba-marketing/chapters/18-the-promotion-mix-personal-selling-and-sales-promotion.md`
- Topic: MARKETING
- Hook: A retailer runs a "20% off" sale every six weeks. Customers love it. Full-price sales collapse. The retailer hasn't run a promotion — they've redefined their price.
- Key case: Recurring sales promotion cycle — when discounts run with enough frequency, customers learn to time purchases to the next discount window. Full-price purchase rate falls. The discount stops being a promotion and becomes the de facto price, permanently compressing margin.
- The Question: If discounts drive volume, why does running them repeatedly make a brand less profitable than not running them at all?
- Core idea: Sales promotions work by creating urgency through scarcity of the deal. When the deal recurs on a predictable schedule, urgency disappears — customers simply wait. The promotion has trained a new purchasing behavior (delay until discount) that persists even when the marketer wants to restore full-price selling. The "sale price" becomes the reference price in the customer's mind.
- Visual object: Timeline bar — full-price periods (tall green bars) alternating with discount periods (shorter bars) — showing full-price bars shrinking over successive cycles as customers migrate to discount windows
- Manim move: decay (full-price revenue bars shrinking across cycles) → accumulate (discount windows growing as proportion of sales)
- Example seed: A clothing retailer runs a Black Friday sale every year. After five years, regular-season sell-through drops 40%. Customers have learned: wait for November. The retailer hasn't just discounted once — they've restructured when customers believe it's rational to buy.
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: c2v (timeline/bar chart)
- Prerequisites: Basic understanding of pricing and promotions
- Exclusions: Full seven-step selling process, full sales force management, trade-oriented vs. consumer promotions taxonomy
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 10 — Dollar Shave Club and the Adopter Sequence: Launch to the Right People or Launch to No One

- Source: `mba-marketing/chapters/13-maintaining-a-competitive-edge-with-new-offerings.md`
- Topic: MARKETING
- Hook: 50–80% of new products fail. Products with a unique benefit vs. competition have five times the success rate. But even uniquely differentiated products fail if they launch to the wrong 2.5% of the market first.
- Key case: Dollar Shave Club — disruptive differentiation (subscription razor delivery vs. $4+ Gillette blades), targeted early adopters willing to try subscription commerce, scaled to $1B Unilever acquisition in 8 years. Contrast: innovators (2.5%) will try anything; early adopters (13.5%) need to see the idea work; early majority (34%) won't move until it's established — crossing the chasm between early adopters and early majority is where most launches die.
- The Question: If your product is genuinely better, why does the sequence of who you sell to first determine whether you succeed or fail?
- Core idea: Rogers' diffusion of innovations shows that adoption spreads through distinct social groups in sequence — each group only adopts after watching the previous group succeed. Launching to innovators without a strategy to cross to early adopters strands the product in a tiny niche. The product doesn't spread because each successive group is waiting for the previous one's endorsement, which never comes at sufficient scale.
- Visual object: Bell curve with five adopter segments labeled and colored — with a vertical "chasm" line between early adopters and early majority — and an arrow showing where most new product launches stall
- Manim move: spread (adoption curve filling left to right) → collapse (showing stall at the chasm) → transform (Dollar Shave Club crossing the chasm via YouTube video reach)
- Example seed: A startup builds a genuinely superior password manager and launches at a tech conference. Innovators love it. But the early majority — small business owners — have never heard of it and don't trust a product they've never seen used by someone like them. Sales plateau. The product didn't fail; the sequence failed.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: c2v (adoption curve bell chart), raster (Dollar Shave Club launch video still)
- Prerequisites: Basic understanding of product launch and marketing channels
- Exclusions: Full seven-stage NPD process, IP protection detail, all five new-product categories
- Score: 8/10
