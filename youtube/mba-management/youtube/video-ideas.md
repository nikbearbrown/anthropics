## Candidate 01 — The Slot Machine Beats the Bonus Program

- Source: `mba-management/chapters/04-learning-and-reinforcement.md`
- Topic: MANAGEMENT
- Hook: A slot machine that always loses money creates more persistent behavior than a bonus program that guarantees rewards — and managers unknowingly use the losing strategy every day.
- Key case: Skinner's variable-ratio vs. fixed-interval extinction curves: FR/FI schedules extinguish within minutes of reward removal; VR schedule produces responding that persists for hours.
- The Question: Why does an unpredictable reward with terrible expected value produce stronger, longer-lasting behavior than a reliable reward with positive expected value?
- Core idea: Variable-ratio reinforcement schedules tie reward probability to response count, not time, so the organism can never predict when responding will "stop paying." Fixed schedules create a predictable post-reward pause — the organism "knows" no reward is coming immediately after one just arrived, so motivation dips on cue.
- Visual object: Four extinction curves (FR / FI / VR / VI) on the same axes — response-rate over time after reward removal; the VR curve is a plateau while FR crashes immediately.
- Manim move: compare (four simultaneous curves), then decay (FR and FI drop while VR stays flat)
- Example seed: A sales manager switches from monthly quota bonuses (fixed interval) to a random spot-prize wheel spun when any deal closes. Reps stop the post-quota slump because no one knows when the wheel will spin next.
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: c2v
- Prerequisites: basic familiarity with rewards/incentives; no prior OB required
- Exclusions: classical conditioning, Pavlov, OB Mod five-step process, punishment schedules
- Score: 10/10

## Candidate 02 — Pay Kills the Passion (Overjustification Effect)

- Source: `mba-management/chapters/04-learning-and-reinforcement.md`
- Topic: MANAGEMENT
- Hook: Adding pay to a task someone loves doesn't increase their motivation — it replaces it. Remove the pay, and they care less than they did before you ever offered it.
- Key case: Deci's puzzle experiment: participants who were paid to solve intrinsically interesting puzzles played with them less during a free-choice period after payment stopped than participants who were never paid at all.
- The Question: If someone enjoys their work, why does paying them for it reduce their long-run engagement?
- Core idea: External rewards shift the perceived cause of behavior from internal ("I do this because I enjoy it") to external ("I do this because I get paid"). When the external reason disappears, internal motivation has already been crowded out — the person has re-attributed their interest to the reward, not to themselves.
- Visual object: Three-line time-series chart: "never paid" line stays flat/high; "paid throughout" line stays high while paid but may decline; "paid then stopped" line drops below baseline after payment ends.
- Manim move: trace (three lines drawn simultaneously over time), then split (highlight the drop-below-baseline segment)
- Example seed: A software engineer spends evenings contributing to an open-source project she loves. Her company offers to pay her for those same contributions as part of her job. Three months later the stipend is cut. She now contributes less than she did before the stipend existed.
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: c2v
- Prerequisites: none
- Exclusions: full SDT framework, OB Mod, punishment, Herzberg two-factor theory
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 03 — Same Math, Opposite Choice (Kahneman's Framing Effect)

- Source: `mba-management/chapters/06-perception-and-managerial-decision-making.md`
- Topic: MANAGEMENT
- Hook: Show investment professionals two mathematically identical options and they will choose opposite ones — depending solely on whether the outcome is described as a gain or a loss.
- Key case: Kahneman's framing experiment with finance professionals: "keep $240K certain" vs. "coin flip for $250K" (gain frame) → most choose certain; reframe as "lose $760K certain" vs. "coin flip to lose $750K or $1M" → most choose gamble. Same numbers. Opposite decisions.
- The Question: How can a purely verbal reframe — with zero change in probabilities or dollar amounts — reverse a rational decision-maker's preference?
- Core idea: Losses loom roughly twice as large as equivalent gains (loss aversion). Framing a fixed amount as a "certain loss" triggers avoidance behavior and risk-seeking; framing it as a "certain gain" triggers acceptance and risk-aversion. The math is identical; the emotional loading is opposite.
- Visual object: Two parallel decision trees with identical terminal nodes ($240K / coin flip $0 or $250K); left tree labeled in green (gain frame), right tree labeled in red (loss frame); arrows show the majority choice flipping.
- Manim move: compare (side-by-side trees), then transform (relabel numbers from gain frame to loss frame while keeping values identical), then split (show the majority choice arrow reversing)
- Example seed: A hospital administrator frames a policy choice as "this plan saves 200 of 600 patients for certain" vs. "this plan lets 400 of 600 patients die for certain." Doctors recommend the first framing and oppose the second — even after being shown the arithmetic is the same.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: c2v
- Prerequisites: basic probability intuition helpful but not required
- Exclusions: full prospect theory, System 1/System 2 dual-process model, anchoring, availability heuristic
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 04 — Diverse Teams Lose, Then Win (The 17-Week Crossover)

- Source: `mba-management/chapters/05-diversity-in-organizations.md`
- Topic: MANAGEMENT
- Hook: Diverse teams consistently underperform homogeneous teams — but only for the first few weeks. Given enough time and the right conditions, the result flips completely.
- Key case: Watson et al. 17-week longitudinal study: homogeneous teams outperformed at week 5; no difference by week 9; diverse teams outperformed at week 17. The crossover is real, measurable, and time-dependent.
- The Question: Why do diverse teams start worse and finish better — and what conditions determine whether the crossover actually happens?
- Core idea: Early performance costs come from communication friction and norm-negotiation. The advantage accrues over time as diverse problem-solving repertoires activate on novel sub-problems. But the crossover only appears when three conditions are met: genuine task interdependence, psychological safety, and integration-focused leadership. Without those, diverse teams pay the early cost without collecting the late gain.
- Visual object: Two-line performance-over-time chart with a crossover at ~week 13; homogeneous line starts high and plateaus; diverse line starts low, dips, then rises above.
- Manim move: trace (two lines drawn simultaneously across 17 weeks), then accumulate (annotation bubbles appear at crossover point marking the three conditions)
- Example seed: A product team building a genuinely novel medical device: weeks 1–4 feel chaotic compared to the all-engineers team next door. By month four, the diverse team has solved three interface problems the homogeneous team hadn't anticipated.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: c2v
- Prerequisites: basic familiarity with teams/diversity concept
- Exclusions: surface/deep/hidden diversity taxonomy, Bertrand/Mullainathan resume audit, legal compliance framing, DEI policy
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 05 — Google Spent Two Years Learning It's Not Who's on the Team

- Source: `mba-management/chapters/10-understanding-and-managing-work-teams.md`
- Topic: MANAGEMENT
- Hook: Google analyzed 180+ teams expecting to find that the best teams had the smartest people. They found the opposite: who is on the team matters far less than how the team operates.
- Key case: Project Aristotle: Google's People Analytics team spent two years testing every compositional variable (talent, tenure, personality, demographic mix). None predicted performance. Psychological safety — whether members felt safe taking interpersonal risks — was the single strongest predictor.
- The Question: If individual talent doesn't explain team performance, what does — and why does the answer make high performers uncomfortable?
- Core idea: Teams are not the sum of individual capabilities; they are emergent systems shaped by interaction norms. Psychological safety allows the full distribution of knowledge and error signals to surface rather than be suppressed. High individual talent in low-safety environments is often worse than moderate talent in high-safety environments, because mistakes and minority views stay hidden.
- Visual object: Scatter plot with "average member talent" on x-axis and "team performance" on y-axis — no correlation visible; then color-code points by psychological safety level — high-safety cluster appears in top right even at moderate talent levels.
- Manim move: spread (scatter plot populates with dots), then split (color overlay appears showing safety clustering)
- Example seed: A surgical team at a hospital. The team with the most decorated individual surgeons has worse patient outcomes than a team with slightly less credentialed surgeons who openly flag concerns during operations.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: c2v
- Prerequisites: basic team/group concept
- Exclusions: Tuckman stages, Hackman's four factors, cohesion, team composition theory details
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 06 — The Clerk Dies Younger Than the Executive (Whitehall's Real Finding)

- Source: `mba-management/chapters/18-stress-and-well-being.md`
- Topic: MANAGEMENT
- Hook: The lowest-grade British civil servants had three times the cardiovascular mortality of the highest-grade civil servants — even after controlling for income, diet, and lifestyle. The critical variable wasn't money. It was control.
- Key case: Whitehall Studies (Marmot et al.): 18,000+ UK civil servants followed over decades. Grade predicts mortality in a smooth gradient — not a step between employed and unemployed, but a continuous gradient through the entire hierarchy. Karasek Demand-Control model: high demand + low control = physiological damage regardless of absolute income.
- The Question: Why does having a lower-stress, more predictable job kill you faster than having a higher-stress, more demanding one — and what does that imply for how we design work?
- Core idea: Autonomy and control are not perks — they are physiological regulators. Chronic exposure to situations where effort and outcomes are decoupled (you work but cannot affect results) activates sustained cortisol and sympathetic nervous system responses that accumulate into cardiovascular damage. Income moderates some risk factors; control moderates the stress-health pathway directly.
- Visual object: Gradient bar showing civil service grades (Permanent Secretary → Clerical) with mortality rate bars rising as grade descends — a staircase effect, not a cliff between employed and unemployed.
- Manim move: accumulate (grade bars appear left to right, each taller than the last), then scan (horizontal line sweeps to show the gradient is continuous)
- Example seed: Two customer service reps, same salary, same workload. Rep A follows a fixed script with no authority to resolve complaints. Rep B can exercise judgment and authorize refunds. After five years, Rep A's blood pressure is measurably higher.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: geo (UK map overlay optional), c2v
- Prerequisites: basic understanding of stress concept
- Exclusions: Maslach burnout three-component model, EAP/wellness program critique, full Karasek model axes
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 07 — A Meaningless Label Is Enough to Make You Discriminate

- Source: `mba-management/chapters/09-group-and-intergroup-relations.md`
- Topic: MANAGEMENT
- Hook: Tajfel told strangers they preferred Klee over Kandinsky — a label he randomly assigned. Minutes later, those strangers were allocating real money in ways that favored their "group" and penalized the other. No history. No competition. No interaction. Just a label.
- Key case: Tajfel's minimal group paradigm: participants assigned to meaningless aesthetic categories immediately allocated resources to maximize the in-group advantage over the out-group — even when it meant reducing absolute in-group gain to increase relative lead.
- The Question: What is the minimum condition required to trigger intergroup discrimination — and why does the answer make almost every organizational category dangerous?
- Core idea: Group identity does not require history, scarcity, or prior conflict. The mere act of categorization activates social identity mechanisms: people shift self-esteem to the group's status, which creates motivation to see the in-group as superior. Labels in organizations — team names, department colors, building floors — are never merely labels.
- Visual object: Matrix showing allocation choices: two rows (in-group / out-group), two columns (maximize in-group absolute vs. maximize relative advantage); arrows showing that participants chose relative advantage even at cost to their own group's absolute total.
- Manim move: split (matrix populates), then transform (highlight the dominated-strategy choice that reveals the in-group favoritism mechanism)
- Example seed: A company renames its two product divisions "Alpha" and "Beta" for an internal competition. Within three months, Alpha and Beta teams stop sharing technical documentation — not because anyone told them to, but because the label made them a team.
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: c2v
- Prerequisites: none
- Exclusions: Robbers Cave study details, realistic group conflict theory, social loafing/Ringelmann
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 08 — Why Bad News Dies on the Way Up (The MUM Effect)

- Source: `mba-management/chapters/11-communication.md`
- Topic: MANAGEMENT
- Hook: By the time "the seals will fail at 36°F" reached the people who could stop the Challenger launch, it had become "Thiokol has reviewed engineering concerns and recommends proceeding." No one lied. The message just transformed at every level.
- Key case: Challenger disaster communication cascade: Thiokol engineers' specific warning about O-ring failure temperature was softened at each hierarchical relay until it registered as a resolved concern rather than a launch-blocking risk. The MUM effect — people suppress or soften bad news — operates at every level simultaneously.
- The Question: Why do organizations systematically destroy the accuracy of bad news as it travels upward — and what structural features make this worse?
- Core idea: Messengers of bad news are associated with the bad news itself (shoot the messenger). Each relay node rationally softens the message to manage their own reputational risk, not out of malice. The result is a low-pass filter on negative signals: urgent warnings degrade into mild concerns before reaching decision-makers.
- Visual object: Five-level vertical cascade diagram — original message at bottom, transformed message at top; each level shows the verbatim message getting hedged, shortened, and reframed; the final version at top is barely recognizable.
- Manim move: accumulate (messages appear level by level, each visibly edited from the previous), then scan (vertical highlight shows the cumulative transformation)
- Example seed: A frontline nurse documents a drug interaction risk in a patient chart. The charge nurse mentions "a note about medications" to the attending. The attending tells the chief resident there was "a documentation question" resolved. The risk never reaches the prescribing doctor.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: c2v
- Prerequisites: basic organizational hierarchy concept
- Exclusions: Shannon-Weaver model, media richness theory, Allen Curve, grapevine accuracy statistics
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 09 — The Broken Water Pipe That Ended a War (Superordinate Goals)

- Source: `mba-management/chapters/09-group-and-intergroup-relations.md`
- Topic: MANAGEMENT
- Hook: Sherif spent weeks turning two groups of boys into bitter enemies. Lectures about cooperation did nothing. Forced contact made it worse. Then the camp's water supply broke down — and both groups had to fix it together. The hostility dissolved.
- Key case: Robbers Cave experiment (Sherif, 1954): after engineering intense intergroup rivalry and hostility, Sherif attempted reconciliation through information sharing (failed), positive contact (failed), then introduced superordinate goals requiring genuine interdependence — a broken water line both groups needed fixed. Conflict dissolved across multiple joint tasks.
- The Question: What actually resolves entrenched intergroup conflict — and why do the most intuitive interventions (education, contact, dialogue) consistently fail?
- Core idea: Intergroup conflict is maintained by separate goal structures, not ignorance or animosity. Information and contact don't change goals — they sometimes intensify competition by increasing salience. Only shared goals that neither group can achieve alone restructure the interdependence and shift identity from "us vs. them" to "us together."
- Visual object: Three-panel sequence: (1) two groups facing each other with hostility markers; (2) a mediator between them presenting information — hostility unchanged; (3) both groups facing a broken pipe together — hostility arrows redirect toward shared problem.
- Manim move: transform (panel sequence), then rotate (hostility arrows pivot from inter-group to shared-problem orientation)
- Example seed: Two rival engineering teams at a tech company share a codebase. Mandated "cross-team knowledge sessions" change nothing. Then a production outage requires both teams to jointly debug — and within two days, informal collaboration is ongoing.
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: c2v
- Prerequisites: basic group/team concept
- Exclusions: Tajfel minimal group paradigm, Ringelmann effect, French & Raven power bases
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 10 — The Most-Used Personality Test Predicts Almost Nothing

- Source: `mba-management/chapters/02-individual-and-cultural-differences.md`
- Topic: MANAGEMENT
- Hook: MBTI is administered to 2.5 million people a year, costs organizations hundreds of millions globally, and has a test-retest reliability so low that up to half of people get a different type when retested five weeks later.
- Key case: MBTI test-retest reliability: ~50% of respondents receive a different four-letter type when retested after 5 weeks. Predictive validity for job performance: near zero. Big Five (OCEAN), by contrast, shows consistent validity coefficients across decades of research: conscientiousness r≈0.30 with job performance across virtually all job types.
- The Question: Why does an organization keep using a tool that fails its own consistency standard — when a better-validated alternative exists and is freely available?
- Core idea: MBTI persists because it generates satisfying, non-threatening typologies (Barnum/Forer effect) that feel accurate and provide a shared vocabulary. The Big Five produces dimensional scores that are actionable for selection and development but lack the narrative appeal of letter combinations. Organizational tools are selected for palatability as well as validity.
- Visual object: Side-by-side validity comparison bar chart: MBTI (near zero) vs. Big Five conscientiousness (r≈0.30) vs. structured interview (r=0.51) vs. work sample (r=0.54) — all labeled clearly.
- Manim move: compare (bars appear side by side), then accumulate (labels and r-values populate)
- Example seed: An HR team retests their leadership cohort with MBTI five weeks after initial assessment. Forty percent of the cohort has a different type. The debrief consultant explains this is normal — "people grow."
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: c2v
- Prerequisites: familiarity with MBTI concept (most viewers will have taken it)
- Exclusions: Big Five full OCEAN dimensions, Hofstede cultural dimensions, EQ validity debates
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 11 — Unstructured Interviews Feel Better and Work Half as Well

- Source: `mba-management/chapters/17-human-resource-management.md`
- Topic: MANAGEMENT
- Hook: Decades of research show that structured interviews predict job performance 2.5 times better than unstructured ones. Most companies still use unstructured interviews — because they feel more valid even though they aren't.
- Key case: Schmidt & Hunter (1998) meta-analysis of selection validity: unstructured interview r=0.20, structured interview r=0.51, work sample r=0.54. The gap has been known and replicated since the 1980s. Adoption of structured interviewing remains low because interviewers prefer the feeling of "reading" a candidate conversationally.
- The Question: Why would rational hiring managers systematically choose a method they know is less accurate — and what does this reveal about how organizations actually make decisions?
- Core idea: Confidence in a judgment and accuracy of a judgment are largely independent. Unstructured interviews produce high interviewer confidence (the interviewer generated the conversation and feels they understand the person), which is mistaken for high validity. Structured interviews constrain interviewer autonomy, which feels like it reduces insight — even as it dramatically improves prediction.
- Visual object: Two-bar comparison: r=0.20 (unstructured) vs. r=0.51 (structured), with a third bar r=0.54 (work sample) for reference; below each bar, "% of companies using this method" — inverse relationship visible.
- Manim move: compare (bars), then split (usage-rate annotation appears below validity bars, showing the inversion)
- Example seed: A startup founder interviews 40 candidates for a key hire with a freewheeling conversation. She feels confident she can "read people." She hires someone she clicks with. The hire fails within six months — a pattern that repeats until an HR consultant implements structured scoring rubrics.
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: c2v
- Prerequisites: basic hiring/recruitment concept
- Exclusions: Kirkpatrick four levels, GE forced ranking, nine-box grid, full selection validity hierarchy
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 12 — The Teacher Who Was Told to Expect a Miracle (Pygmalion Effect)

- Source: `mba-management/chapters/03-perception-and-job-attitudes.md`
- Topic: MANAGEMENT
- Hook: Rosenthal and Jacobson told teachers that randomly selected students had just shown unusual academic potential. Those students actually gained IQ points by year's end — with no other intervention. The teachers' false belief became a true outcome.
- Key case: Rosenthal & Jacobson (1968) Pygmalion in the Classroom: researchers told teachers certain students had scored high on a "Harvard Test of Inflected Acquisition." The test was fictional; the students were randomly chosen. End-of-year IQ tests showed the designated students gained significantly more than controls — especially in early grades.
- The Question: How does a false belief about someone's potential change that person's actual measured ability — and what mechanism runs through the gap?
- Core idea: Expectations change behavior without awareness: teachers gave "bloomers" more challenging material, more warmth, more specific feedback, and longer wait times when they hesitated. Students responded to differential treatment. The belief → treatment → outcome → confirms belief loop is self-sealing. The dangerous corollary: negative expectations run the same loop downward.
- Visual object: Four-arrow feedback loop: "Teacher belief (random)" → "Teacher behavior (more challenge, warmth, feedback)" → "Student outcome (IQ gain)" → "Belief confirmed" — with a dashed red version showing the negative loop simultaneously.
- Manim move: accumulate (loop arrows appear one at a time), then duplicate (negative version appears alongside positive, mirroring the structure)
- Example seed: A manager is told a new hire was a "star recruit." She gives the new hire stretch assignments and detailed feedback. He performs well and gets promoted. A different new hire — equally capable at baseline — is described as "average." He gets routine tasks. He stays average.
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: c2v
- Prerequisites: none
- Exclusions: attribution theory (FAE/self-serving bias), full perceptual process model, job satisfaction correlations
- Score: 8/10
