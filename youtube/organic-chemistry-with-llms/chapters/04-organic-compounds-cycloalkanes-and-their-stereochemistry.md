# Chapter 4 — Organic Compounds: Cycloalkanes and Their Stereochemistry

*The geometry of a six-membered ring is responsible for an enormous amount of organic chemistry — including, eventually, the shape of every sugar, steroid, and pharmaceutical you'll ever care about.*

In 1885 Adolf von Baeyer proposed that ring strain in cycloalkanes should follow a simple geometric rule. The internal angle of an equilateral triangle is 60°. The internal angle of a square is 90°. The internal angle of a regular pentagon is 108°. As you build larger and larger rings, the internal angle approaches 180°. The "ideal" angle for a tetrahedral carbon is 109.5°. So Baeyer predicted that ring strain should be highest in cyclopropane (60° vs. 109.5° = strained), drop dramatically in cyclopentane (108° vs. 109.5° = nearly relaxed), and increase again for medium rings (where the internal angle would have to exceed 109.5°).

The prediction is right for the small rings and wrong for the medium and large ones. Cyclohexane, where Baeyer's flat-hexagon model predicts an internal angle of 120° and substantial strain, is in fact almost completely strain-free. Cyclopentane, predicted to be the most relaxed, is actually slightly strained. Rings of 14 carbons or more, predicted by Baeyer to be very strained, are in fact strain-free.

The mistake was assuming the rings are flat. Real cycloalkanes pucker. They twist out of the planar geometry to relieve other kinds of strain at the cost of bending the bond angles. The lowest-energy three-dimensional conformation of cyclohexane is a beautiful structure called the *chair*, which has every C–C–C bond angle exactly at 109.5° and every adjacent C–H bond perfectly staggered. There is no ring strain at all — none from angles, none from torsion, none from steric clashes between substituents. Cyclohexane in the chair conformation is as relaxed as an open-chain alkane.

This chapter is about the geometric consequences of putting carbons into rings. The story has three threads — angle strain (Baeyer's idea, partially right), torsional strain (the same eclipsing-vs-staggered story from Chapter 3), and steric strain (groups crowding each other in space). Once you can balance the three, you can predict which conformation a substituted cyclohexane will adopt — and that prediction sets up the chemistry of cyclic systems for the rest of the book.

## Cycloalkanes and how to name them

The cycloalkanes are saturated cyclic hydrocarbons. They follow the formula CₙH₂ₙ — two fewer hydrogens than the corresponding open-chain alkane CₙH₂ₙ₊₂, because forming a ring uses up one C–H bond per carbon on each end of the chain that joins. Cyclopropane is C₃H₆, cyclobutane is C₄H₈, cyclohexane is C₆H₁₂.

Naming substituted cycloalkanes follows rules similar to alkane naming:

1. Count the carbons in the ring. That sets the parent name: cyclo + alkane prefix.
2. If the ring has more carbons than any attached substituent, use the ring as the parent. If a substituent has more carbons, use the substituent as the parent and treat the ring as a substituent.
3. Number around the ring to give substituents the lowest possible locants.
4. List substituents alphabetically.

So 1,2-dimethylcyclohexane is a cyclohexane with two methyl groups, on adjacent carbons. *cis*-1,3-dimethylcyclopentane is a cyclopentane with two methyl groups, on carbons 1 and 3, both on the same face of the ring.

Cycloalkanes are mostly inert, like their open-chain cousins. The chemistry-relevant features are geometric. Two stand out:

First, cycloalkanes have *less rotational freedom* than open-chain alkanes. The C–C bond in ethane can rotate freely; the analogous bond in cyclopropane cannot rotate at all without breaking the ring. As ring size increases, rotational freedom returns, but for the small rings (3–6 carbons), rotation around individual ring bonds is constrained.

Second, cycloalkanes have *two faces* — when you draw a planar (or near-planar) ring, substituents can sit above or below the plane. A 1,2-dimethyl cyclopropane can have both methyls on the same face (*cis*) or on opposite faces (*trans*). Cis and trans 1,2-dimethylcyclopropane are different compounds with different properties — they are *stereoisomers* (same connectivity, different spatial arrangement). Cis–trans isomerism is widespread in cyclic compounds, including the biological molecules that come up later — sugars, steroids, prostaglandins.

## Three kinds of strain

Before the cyclohexane discussion, three strain mechanisms to know:

**Angle strain** comes from forcing bond angles away from their ideal value. An sp³ carbon prefers 109.5°. Cyclopropane forces 60°. The 49.5° deviation costs energy. To accommodate the angle, the bonds in cyclopropane have to bend — the orbitals can no longer point directly along the line between nuclei, so the overlap is poorer than in a normal C–C bond. The bonds are weaker (255 kJ/mol vs. 370 kJ/mol for normal C–C) and more reactive than typical alkane bonds, which is why cyclopropanes participate in some reactions that ordinary alkanes don't.

**Torsional strain** is the eclipsed-vs-staggered story from Chapter 3. Bonds on adjacent carbons prefer to be staggered (60° apart in projection) and resist being eclipsed (0° apart). Each H ↔ H eclipsing interaction costs about 4 kJ/mol; CH₃ ↔ H costs about 6 kJ/mol; CH₃ ↔ CH₃ costs about 11 kJ/mol.

**Steric strain** comes from atoms or groups physically crowding each other in space. Two methyl groups in a gauche arrangement (60° apart, both staggered) cost about 3.8 kJ/mol because the methyls are close enough to bump into each other even without eclipsing. Steric strain is geometric "you can't put two things in the same space at the same time" energy.

Most ring conformations involve some combination of these three. The lowest-energy conformation is the one that minimizes the total strain energy.

## Why cyclopropane and cyclobutane are strained

Cyclopropane has total ring strain of about 115 kJ/mol. About 60 kJ/mol is angle strain (the 60° vs. 109.5° geometric problem). The rest is torsional strain — because cyclopropane is forced into a flat triangle, every adjacent C–H bond is eclipsed with the next one. Three pairs of eclipsed C–H interactions, plus the bent bonds, plus the angle strain, all stack up. Cyclopropane's high reactivity is the consequence: ring-opening reactions are favored because they relieve all three strain components at once.

Cyclobutane has total ring strain of about 110 kJ/mol — almost as much as cyclopropane, despite the larger angle. Why? Because the four carbons could in principle sit in a flat square (90° angles, slightly less angle strain than 60°), but a flat square forces all the C–H bonds into eclipsed arrangements (eight pairs of eclipsings, since each carbon has two H's that eclipse with the next). The molecule resolves the trade-off by puckering slightly out of plane: one carbon sits about 25° above the plane of the other three. This adds back a little angle strain but reduces torsional strain enough that the puckered structure is the lowest-energy conformation.

Cyclopentane has 26 kJ/mol of total strain — much less. The molecule resolves things by adopting an *envelope* conformation, in which four carbons are roughly coplanar and the fifth is above or below. The envelope ripples around the ring rapidly at room temperature, so on average no single carbon sits in the "out" position. Bond angles are close to 109.5°, and torsional eclipsing is largely avoided, but not perfectly — there are still some H ↔ H interactions that cost energy.

## Cyclohexane: the chair conformation

Cyclohexane has *zero* ring strain. Among rings of any size, six is geometrically magic.

The reason is the chair conformation. In the chair, three carbons sit slightly above a reference plane and three sit slightly below, alternating around the ring. Every C–C–C angle is exactly at the tetrahedral value of 109.5°. Every C–H bond is perfectly staggered with the C–H bonds on adjacent carbons. There is no angle strain, no torsional strain, and (for unsubstituted cyclohexane) no steric strain.

To draw a chair from scratch, follow these conventions:

1. Draw two parallel lines slanting down to the right, slightly offset vertically. These are two of the C–C bonds.
2. Connect their right ends with a line slanting up to the right, to a point above and to the right.
3. Connect their left ends with a line slanting down to the left, to a point below and to the left.
4. Add the final two bonds to close the ring.

Once you have the chair skeleton, every carbon has two C–H bonds: one "axial" and one "equatorial."

**Axial bonds** point straight up or straight down, parallel to a vertical axis through the center of the ring. They alternate as you go around the ring — up, down, up, down, up, down.

**Equatorial bonds** point outward, roughly in the plane of the ring (well, parallel to one of the C–C bonds across the ring). They also alternate, but their pattern is offset from the axial: each carbon has one axial pointing one direction and one equatorial pointing roughly the opposite.

This axial/equatorial distinction is the reason cyclohexane chemistry is so interesting. The two positions are *not* equivalent. Substituents in axial vs. equatorial positions experience different steric environments.

## The ring flip

Cyclohexane has another chair conformation, indistinguishable from the first by symmetry but with one important difference: every carbon's axial and equatorial bonds have swapped places. The carbon that pointed an axial-H *up* in the first chair points it *equatorial-out* in the second chair. The carbon that pointed an equatorial-H *out* in the first chair points it *axial-down* in the second.

This *ring flip* happens through an intermediate boat conformation (which is less stable, by about 23 kJ/mol — boat conformations have torsional strain from eclipsed bonds and a steric "flagpole" interaction between the two H's at the ring's bow and stern). The total barrier for chair-to-chair interconversion in cyclohexane is about 45 kJ/mol — high enough that the molecule lives in one chair or the other most of the time, low enough that the interconversion is fast at room temperature (millions of times per second).

For unsubstituted cyclohexane, the two chairs are energetically equivalent. The molecule spends equal time in each.

For monosubstituted cyclohexane — say, methylcyclohexane — they are not equivalent. The methyl group prefers to be in an equatorial position. In the axial position, the methyl points straight up (or down) and ends up close to the two axial H's three carbons away (the C3 and C5 positions in a cyclohexane numbered from the methyl carbon). These are *1,3-diaxial interactions*. They're geometrically equivalent to the gauche butane interaction — the methyl is gauche to the C2–C3 bond and gauche to the C5–C6 bond — and each costs about 3.8 kJ/mol. Two such interactions on an axial methyl: 7.6 kJ/mol.

So methylcyclohexane prefers the equatorial-methyl conformation by 7.6 kJ/mol over the axial-methyl conformation. Using the Boltzmann distribution at room temperature, this corresponds to about a 95:5 ratio at equilibrium. The molecule spends 95% of its time with the methyl equatorial.

This 1,3-diaxial argument is one of the most useful predictive tools in organic chemistry. The energy cost of putting a substituent axial depends on the size of the substituent — small atoms (F: 0.5 kJ/mol; Cl, Br: 1.0 kJ/mol) cost little, methyl-sized groups (CH₃: 3.8 kJ/mol per interaction × 2 = 7.6 total) cost moderate amounts, and large groups (*tert*-butyl: > 20 kJ/mol) cost so much that they essentially lock the ring in the conformation that puts them equatorial.

This cost is called the **A-value** of a substituent — the energy difference between axial and equatorial conformations for a given group on cyclohexane. A-values are tabulated in textbooks; the larger the A-value, the more strongly the group prefers equatorial.

| Substituent | A-value (kJ/mol) |
|---|---|
| F | 0.5 |
| Cl, Br | 1.0 |
| OH | 4.2 |
| CH₃ | 7.6 |
| CH₂CH₃ | 7.5 |
| CH(CH₃)₂ | 9.2 |
| C(CH₃)₃ | > 20 |
| C₆H₅ | 12.6 |

For disubstituted cyclohexanes, you have to think carefully. Two substituents can be *cis* (both up, both down — both on the same face) or *trans* (one up, one down — on opposite faces). For each cis or trans pair, the conformation that places more groups equatorial is preferred. Sometimes both substituents can be equatorial; sometimes only one can. The conformation that minimizes the total axial-substituent burden wins.

A worked case: *cis*-1,2-dimethylcyclohexane. Both methyls on the same face. In one chair, one methyl is axial and one is equatorial. In the flipped chair, the same: one axial, one equatorial (the assignments swap). The two chairs are equivalent in energy, and the molecule spends equal time in each. Total axial penalty per chair: 7.6 kJ/mol.

*trans*-1,2-dimethylcyclohexane. Both methyls on opposite faces. In one chair, both are equatorial (lowest energy). In the flipped chair, both are axial (high energy, 15.2 kJ/mol of axial penalty plus a strong methyl-methyl 1,3-diaxial interaction). The diequatorial chair dominates almost completely.

This is why *trans*-1,2-dimethylcyclohexane is more stable than *cis*-1,2-dimethylcyclohexane: the trans isomer has access to a fully diequatorial conformation that the cis isomer doesn't.

For 1,3-disubstitution: cis-1,3 can place both equatorial; trans-1,3 has to place one axial.

For 1,4-disubstitution: cis-1,4 has to place one axial; trans-1,4 can place both equatorial.

Memorize the pattern of which (cis or trans) puts both equatorial for each (1,2-, 1,3-, 1,4-) and the rest follows.

## Why this matters

The cyclohexane conformational analysis isn't a one-time exercise. It comes back constantly.

In Chapter 11, the E2 elimination requires anti-periplanar H and X. On a cyclohexane, anti-periplanar means trans-diaxial. The reaction can fire only when both H and the leaving group are axial, which means the molecule has to first adopt the right conformation. This explains the 200-fold rate difference between menthyl and neomenthyl chloride.

In Chapter 17, the chemistry of alcohols often depends on whether the −OH is axial (more accessible to attack) or equatorial (less accessible).

In Chapter 25, simple sugars like glucose exist as cyclic hemiacetals — six-membered rings — and their conformations control which face an enzyme can grab, which −OH can be protonated, and how the molecule interacts with proteins. Glucose's preference for the all-equatorial chair (β-D-glucopyranose) is the reason it's so stable and so widespread in biology.

In Chapter 27, steroids — multi-ring systems with three fused cyclohexanes — adopt specific conformations dictated by ring fusion geometry. The chemistry of cholesterol, testosterone, and cortisone all depends on which substituents are axial vs. equatorial at specific ring junctions.

The conformational analysis is the same in all these cases. The molecule chooses the conformation that minimizes total strain energy. Strain energy is the sum of angle, torsional, and steric components. Most cyclohexane substituents prefer equatorial positions because axial positions create 1,3-diaxial steric interactions.

## Bicycles and beyond

Briefly: many natural products and pharmaceuticals contain fused or bridged ring systems. *Decalin* (bicyclo[4.4.0]decane) is two cyclohexanes sharing one C–C bond. The fusion can be cis or trans, and the two have very different conformational behavior. *trans*-decalin is rigid — both rings are locked in chairs with the two ring-junction C–H bonds axial and on opposite faces, so neither ring can flip without breaking the system. *cis*-decalin is flexible — the two rings can interconvert, with the ring junction allowing some motion.

Steroid skeletons are four fused rings — three cyclohexanes and one cyclopentane. The fusions are mostly trans, locking the system into a flat-ish shape with one face above the other. Substituents projecting up (β) and down (α) determine almost everything about how the steroid interacts with proteins. The chemistry of cortisone, testosterone, and cholesterol is, mechanistically, alcohol chemistry plus some other functional-group chemistry, but the *selectivity* of every step is decided by which face of the rigid ring system is exposed.

## What this chapter does

Baeyer was right that small rings are strained and large rings might be too. He was wrong that medium rings are strained. The reason was a flat-rings assumption that real cycloalkanes don't honor: they pucker, twist, or chair, choosing whatever three-dimensional geometry minimizes total strain.

For cyclohexane, the chair conformation has zero strain. Axial and equatorial positions are not equivalent; substituents prefer equatorial because 1,3-diaxial interactions cost 3.8 kJ/mol per gauche-equivalent contact, and most substituents have at least two such interactions in axial orientation. The ring flips between two chair conformations, and the population of each is determined by how many substituents end up axial vs. equatorial.

This conformational logic, once learned, applies to almost every cyclic system you'll meet for the rest of organic chemistry. The next chapter introduces stereochemistry at tetrahedral centers — a different topic, but one that uses the same kind of careful three-dimensional reasoning.
