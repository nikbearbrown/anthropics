We saw in [\#](#) that the extent of dissociation of a carboxylic acid
HA in an aqueous solution buffered to a given pH can be calculated with
the Henderson--Hasselbalch equation. Furthermore, we concluded that at
the physiological pH of 7.3 inside living cells, carboxylic acids are
almost entirely dissociated into their carboxylate anions, RCO2--.

*Henderson--Hasselbalch equation:*

$$\text{pH~=~p}K\text{a~+~log}\frac{\text{[A}^{\text{–}}\text{]}}{\text{[HA]}}\quad\text{so}\quad\text{log}\frac{\text{[A}^{\text{–}}\text{]}}{\text{[HA]}}\text{~=~pH~–~p}K\text{a}$$

What about amine bases? In what form do they exist at physiological pH?
As the amine (A-- = RNH2), or as the ammonium ion (HA = RNH3+)? Let's
take a 0.0010 M solution of methylamine at pH = 7.3, for example.
According to [\#](#), the p*K*a of methylammonium ion is 10.64, so from
the Henderson--Hasselbalch equation, we have

$$\begin{array}{l}
{l\text{og}\frac{\text{[RNH}_{\text{2}}\text{]}}{\text{[RNH}_{\text{3}}{}^{\text{+}}\text{]}}\text{~=~pH~–~p}K_{\text{a}}\text{~=~7}\text{.3~–~10}\text{.64~=~–3}\text{.34}} \\
{\frac{\text{[RNH}_{\text{2}}\text{]}}{\text{[RNH}_{\text{3}}{}^{\text{+}}\text{]}}\text{~=~antilog(–3}\text{.34)~=~4}\text{.6~} \times \text{~10}^{\text{–4}}\quad\text{so}\quad\text{[RNH}_{\text{2}}\text{]~=~(4}\text{.6~} \times \text{~10}^{\text{–4}}\text{)[RNH}_{\text{3}}{}^{\text{+}}\text{]}}
\end{array}$$

In addition, we know that

$$\text{[RNH}_{\text{2}}\text{]~+~[RNH}_{\text{3}}{}^{\text{+}}\text{]~=~0}\text{.0010~M}$$

Solving the two simultaneous equations gives \[RNH3+\] = 0.0010 M and
\[RNH2\] = 5 × 10--7 M. In other words, at a physiological pH of 7.3,
essentially 100% of the methylamine in a 0.0010 M solution exists in its
protonated form as methylammonium ion. The same is true of other amine
bases, so we always write cellular amines in their protonated form and
amino acids in their ammonium carboxylate form to reflect their
structures at physiological pH.
