# Chapter 27 — Biomolecules: Lipids

*Lipids are defined not by structure but by behavior — they're whatever doesn't dissolve in water. The category includes the energy-storing fats in your body, the cholesterol in your blood, the steroid hormones that control growth and reproduction, and the membrane bilayers that hold every cell together.*

The cell membrane is a double layer of *phospholipids* — molecules with a hydrophilic head (a phosphate-containing group) and two long hydrophobic hydrocarbon tails. The lipid bilayer is about 5 nm thick. It selectively allows nonpolar molecules to pass while blocking ionic species and most polar ones. The membrane proteins embedded in it (about 30% of the bilayer's mass) regulate the traffic of specific molecules and ions. Without this lipid bilayer, no cells. Without cells, no life as we know it.

Lipids are the most structurally diverse of the major biomolecule classes. The category includes fatty acids and their triester storage forms (triglycerides), phospholipids that build membranes, steroids that act as hormones and structural components, terpenoids that include vitamin A and carotenoids and a vast array of plant-derived natural products, and several smaller categories. The unifying property is solubility in nonpolar solvents — lipids dissolve in chloroform, hexane, ether, but not in water.

This chapter covers the major classes and the basic chemistry of each.

## Fatty acids

A fatty acid is a long-chain carboxylic acid: CH₃(CH₂)ₙCOOH. Most natural fatty acids have even numbers of carbons (because they're built two carbons at a time by fatty acid synthase, Chapter 23). Common ones:

| Common name | C count | Unsaturation | bp / mp |
|---|---|---|---|
| Palmitic acid | 16:0 | 0 (saturated) | mp 63 °C |
| Stearic acid | 18:0 | 0 | mp 70 °C |
| Oleic acid | 18:1 (Δ9) | 1 (cis) | mp 13 °C |
| Linoleic acid | 18:2 (Δ9,12) | 2 (both cis) | mp –5 °C |
| α-Linolenic acid | 18:3 (Δ9,12,15) | 3 (all cis) | mp –11 °C |
| Arachidonic acid | 20:4 | 4 (cis) | mp –50 °C |
| EPA | 20:5 (ω-3) | 5 (cis) | liquid |

The notation 18:1 (Δ9) means 18 carbons total, 1 double bond, with the double bond starting at C9 from the carboxyl end. The 18:1 cis Δ9 fatty acid is oleic acid; the trans isomer (called elaidic acid) is rare in nature but produced industrially by partial hydrogenation of vegetable oils.

The general trends:
- Saturated fatty acids are higher melting (more closely packed alkyl chains).
- Unsaturated cis fatty acids are lower melting (the cis double bond introduces a kink that prevents close packing).
- More double bonds → lower melting point.
- Trans isomers melt higher than cis (less kinked), close to saturated equivalents.

**Saturated fats** dominate animal fat (lard, butter). They're typically solid at room temperature.

**Unsaturated fats** dominate vegetable oils (olive, soybean, corn). They're typically liquid at room temperature.

**Trans fats** are made industrially by partial hydrogenation of unsaturated oils. Margarine and shortening were traditionally made this way to give them solid texture without the saturated-fat content of butter. The trans fats produced as byproducts of partial hydrogenation are now known to raise LDL cholesterol and lower HDL, increasing cardiovascular disease risk. They've been largely banned or phased out (FDA banned new uses in food in 2015).

**Omega-3 fatty acids** have their first double bond at the third carbon from the methyl end (counting backwards). The most common: EPA (eicosapentaenoic acid, 20:5) and DHA (docosahexaenoic acid, 22:6). Found in fatty fish (salmon, mackerel, sardines). Strongly correlated with reduced cardiovascular disease and possibly with brain function.

## Triglycerides

The energy storage form of fatty acids. A triglyceride (also called a triacylglycerol) is glycerol (1,2,3-propanetriol) with all three OH groups esterified to fatty acids:

$$\text{CH}_2-\text{OOC-R}_1$$
$$\text{CH}-\text{OOC-R}_2$$
$$\text{CH}_2-\text{OOC-R}_3$$

The three R groups can be the same (simple triglyceride) or different (mixed triglyceride). In animal fats, the three positions tend to have specific fatty acid identities — saturated fatty acids predominate at sn-1 and sn-3, unsaturated at sn-2. (The "sn-" nomenclature is a stereochemistry-aware numbering system specific to glycerolipids.)

Triglycerides store more energy per gram than carbohydrates or proteins (about 9 kcal/g vs. 4 kcal/g) because they're more reduced — fatty acid carbons are mostly in the form of CH₂, while carbohydrate carbons are partially oxidized as C–OH. Burning the more-reduced lipid releases more energy.

The chemistry of triglycerides:

**Hydrolysis (saponification).** Triglyceride + 3 NaOH → glycerol + 3 R-COO⁻Na⁺ (sodium salts of the three fatty acids). The sodium fatty acid salts are *soaps*. Saponification has been the standard soap-making process for thousands of years.

**Hydrogenation.** Vegetable oil + H₂/Pt → solid fat. The C=C double bonds in the unsaturated fatty acids are reduced to give saturated fatty acid tails, raising the melting point. Used to make margarine and shortening.

**Hydrolysis to make biodiesel.** Triglyceride + 3 CH₃OH (with NaOH catalyst) → glycerol + 3 fatty acid methyl esters. The fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs) are biodiesel. They burn similarly to petroleum diesel and can replace it in diesel engines with minimal modification.

**Biological lipase digestion.** In the body, dietary triglycerides are hydrolyzed by pancreatic lipases to give monoglycerides and free fatty acids, which are then absorbed across the intestinal lining and reassembled into chylomicrons for transport.

## Phospholipids

The major component of cell membranes. A phospholipid has glycerol (or sphingosine) with two fatty acid chains and one phosphate-containing head group.

The most common are *glycerophospholipids*: glycerol with the sn-1 and sn-2 OHs esterified to fatty acids, and the sn-3 OH esterified to a phosphate that's then linked to a small polar molecule (choline, ethanolamine, serine, or inositol). The structure is amphiphilic — hydrophobic fatty acid tails and a hydrophilic phosphate head.

Phosphatidylcholine (with choline at the head) is the most abundant glycerophospholipid in animal membranes. Phosphatidylserine, phosphatidylethanolamine, phosphatidylinositol are also common.

**Sphingolipids** use sphingosine instead of glycerol. The structure is similar: two long alkyl chains and a polar head group. Sphingomyelin (with phosphocholine) is abundant in nerve cell membranes.

In water, phospholipids self-assemble into bilayers. The hydrophobic tails cluster in the middle (away from water), and the hydrophilic heads face out into the aqueous environment. The bilayer is the structural element of all biological membranes.

Membrane proteins are embedded in this bilayer. Some span the membrane (transmembrane proteins, often used as channels and receptors); others are anchored to one side. The bilayer's fluidity (how easily molecules can move within and across it) is regulated by:
- Cholesterol content (a steroid that intercalates among phospholipids).
- Saturation level of fatty acid tails (more saturated → less fluid).
- Temperature.

## Steroids

A *steroid* has a four-ring carbon skeleton: three six-membered rings (A, B, C) fused with a five-membered ring (D). The basic skeleton is *cyclopentanoperhydrophenanthrene*, but most chemists call it the "steroid skeleton" and don't worry about the formal name.

Common steroids:

**Cholesterol.** Found in animal cell membranes (about 30% of mammalian membrane lipid content). Modulates membrane fluidity. Total in adult human body: about 100 g. Source: ~30% from diet, ~70% biosynthesized in the liver.

**Cortisol.** Glucocorticoid hormone, produced by the adrenal cortex. Regulates glucose metabolism and the stress response. Synthetic versions (prednisone, hydrocortisone) are widely used as anti-inflammatory drugs.

**Aldosterone.** Mineralocorticoid hormone, also produced by the adrenal cortex. Regulates sodium and potassium handling by the kidneys.

**Testosterone, estradiol, progesterone.** Sex hormones, produced by the gonads. Synthetic versions are used in oral contraceptives and hormone replacement therapy.

**Bile acids.** Cholesterol metabolites secreted by the liver into the small intestine. Emulsify dietary fats so they can be absorbed.

**Vitamin D.** A steroid produced in skin from cholesterol on UV exposure. Regulates calcium absorption.

The chemistry of steroids is the chemistry of alcohols, ketones, alkenes, and rings — covered in earlier chapters. The interesting features are stereochemistry-related: steroids have many chirality centers (typically 7+), and most natural steroids are single stereoisomers. The biosynthesis of steroids from a cholesterol-like skeleton goes through a remarkable cascade of cation-driven cyclizations and rearrangements (briefly mentioned in Chapter 11 as biological SN1 chemistry).

## Terpenoids

The largest single class of natural products. Terpenoids are built from *isoprene units* (5-carbon C₅H₈ pieces). Isoprene itself, 2-methyl-1,3-butadiene, is the structural building block: terpenoids of various sizes are formed by joining specific numbers of isoprene units.

Classification by isoprene count:
- **Monoterpenes** (C₁₀, two isoprenes): geraniol (rose scent), limonene (citrus), pinene (pine), menthol.
- **Sesquiterpenes** (C₁₅, three isoprenes): farnesol, abscisic acid (a plant hormone).
- **Diterpenes** (C₂₀, four isoprenes): vitamin A (retinol), abietic acid (rosin).
- **Triterpenes** (C₃₀, six isoprenes): squalene (a cholesterol precursor).
- **Tetraterpenes** (C₄₀, eight isoprenes): β-carotene (orange of carrots), lycopene (red of tomatoes).
- **Polyterpenes**: natural rubber (cis-polyisoprene).

The biosynthesis of terpenoids starts from a 5-carbon precursor — *dimethylallyl pyrophosphate* (DMAPP) — and its isomer *isopentenyl pyrophosphate* (IPP). These two combine via SN1-like reactions (Chapter 11) to give larger and larger units. The cyclizations and rearrangements that give specific terpenoid structures involve cation chemistry of the most baroque kind.

## Eicosanoids

A class of 20-carbon signaling lipids derived from arachidonic acid. Includes:
- *Prostaglandins*: regulate inflammation, blood clotting, smooth muscle contraction.
- *Thromboxanes*: platelet activation.
- *Leukotrienes*: inflammation, allergic responses.

Aspirin, ibuprofen, and other NSAIDs (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs) work by inhibiting cyclooxygenase (COX), the enzyme that synthesizes prostaglandins from arachidonic acid. By blocking prostaglandin synthesis, these drugs reduce inflammation and pain (which prostaglandins help signal).

## Soap and detergent action

The amphiphilic structure of soap (long hydrocarbon tail + ionic head) gives it cleaning power. In water, soap molecules form *micelles* — small spherical aggregates with hydrophobic tails inside and hydrophilic heads outside. Oils, grease, and fat-soluble dirt get trapped in the hydrophobic interior of the micelles and are carried away with the water.

This is the same physics that underlies cell membranes, just applied to dirt. The lipid bilayer of a membrane is like a flattened version of a micelle, with the hydrophobic interior accommodating membrane proteins instead of soap-encapsulated grease.

## Lipid signaling

Beyond their structural role, many lipids serve as signals.

- **Phosphoinositides** (phosphatidylinositol with various phosphates added). PIP2 (phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate) is cleaved by phospholipase C in response to certain receptor signals, releasing IP3 (inositol trisphosphate) and DAG (diacylglycerol). IP3 triggers calcium release from intracellular stores; DAG activates protein kinase C.

- **Endocannabinoids** (anandamide, 2-arachidonoylglycerol). Lipid signals that bind to the cannabinoid receptors. Modulate pain, appetite, mood. Targeted by Δ9-THC, the active component of cannabis.

- **Sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P).** Regulates lymphocyte trafficking. Targeted by some immunosuppressant drugs.

- **Thromboxanes and leukotrienes** (above). Inflammation and clotting.

Lipid signaling is a large and growing field. The molecules involved are derivatives of fatty acids and phospholipids, modified through specific enzymatic chemistry that uses the standard organic functional group reactions.

## What this chapter does

Lipids are defined by physical property (insoluble in water, soluble in nonpolar solvents) rather than by a unifying chemical structure. The major classes:

- **Fatty acids and triglycerides**: long-chain carboxylic acids and their triester storage forms with glycerol. Energy storage. Saturated vs. unsaturated trans/cis matters for melting point and biological effect.

- **Phospholipids**: glycerol or sphingosine with two fatty acid chains and a phosphate head group. The amphiphilic structure forms membrane bilayers.

- **Steroids**: four-ring skeletons including cholesterol, sex hormones, glucocorticoids, bile acids, vitamin D.

- **Terpenoids**: built from isoprene units (C₅), in sizes from monoterpenes (C₁₀) to polyterpenes (rubber). Includes pigments, vitamins, plant defenses.

- **Eicosanoids**: 20-carbon signaling lipids (prostaglandins, thromboxanes, leukotrienes) from arachidonic acid. Targets of NSAIDs.

The chemistry is mostly chemistry from earlier chapters: ester hydrolysis (saponification), hydrogenation (margarine production), nucleophilic substitution (cation chemistry of terpenoid biosynthesis), oxidation/reduction at carbonyls, and the geometric arguments (cis-trans, stereochemistry of steroids) that come up repeatedly throughout organic chemistry.

The next chapter (28) covers nucleic acids, the last major class of biomolecules.
