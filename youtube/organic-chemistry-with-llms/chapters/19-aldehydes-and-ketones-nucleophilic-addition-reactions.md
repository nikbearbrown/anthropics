# Chapter 19 — Aldehydes and Ketones: Nucleophilic Addition Reactions

*The carbonyl group is the most studied functional group in organic chemistry. It's also the most reactive — every nucleophile in this book has a way to add to it, and every addition gives a different product.*

About 20 million tons of formaldehyde (CH₂O) are produced industrially every year, mostly by catalytic oxidation of methanol over a silver catalyst. The formaldehyde then goes into urea-formaldehyde resins (used to bond particle board and laminated wood products), phenol-formaldehyde resins (Bakelite, the first synthetic plastic), and various other polymer feedstocks. Acetone (CH₃COCH₃) is produced at similar scale, mostly as a co-product of phenol from cumene oxidation; it's then used as a solvent and as a feedstock for methyl methacrylate (the polymer of acrylic plastics).

These two simple molecules — formaldehyde and acetone — are the simplest aldehyde and ketone, and their chemistry is the foundation for almost every more complex carbonyl reaction. The carbonyl group (C=O) is at the center of organic chemistry: it's an intermediate in many syntheses, a structural feature of most biological molecules, and the entry point for several major reaction classes.

This chapter is the first of four on carbonyl chemistry. Chapter 20 covers carboxylic acids and nitriles. Chapter 21 covers carboxylic acid derivatives (esters, amides, anhydrides, acid chlorides). Chapters 22 and 23 cover α-substitution and condensation reactions. Together, these four chapters are the largest single reactivity domain in this book.

## The carbonyl group

The C=O double bond consists of one σ bond and one π bond. The carbonyl carbon is sp² hybridized, with three other atoms (R, R′, and O) at angles of approximately 120°. The π bond uses one p orbital from each of the two atoms. The oxygen has two lone pairs in its remaining sp² orbitals.

The carbonyl group is *strongly polarized*. Oxygen is much more electronegative than carbon, so the π electrons are pulled toward oxygen. The σ electrons of the C=O bond are also slightly pulled. The carbon is δ⁺ — markedly so — and the oxygen is δ⁻.

This polarity is what makes the carbonyl reactive. Nucleophiles attack the δ⁺ carbon (the *electrophilic* center). Bases pick up the δ⁻ oxygen (acting as a Brønsted base, accepting H⁺) or its lone pair (acting as a Lewis base). Both types of approach are common.

Resonance structures: the polarization can be described as a contribution from a charge-separated form, R₂C(+)–O⁻, in which a full positive sits on carbon and a full negative on oxygen. The actual molecule is a hybrid between the neutral form (R₂C=O) and this charge-separated form. The contribution from the charge-separated form is what gives the carbonyl carbon its electrophilic character.

## Aldehydes vs. ketones

An *aldehyde* has at least one H on the carbonyl carbon: R–CHO (with R an alkyl, aryl, or H itself). Examples: formaldehyde (HCHO), acetaldehyde (CH₃CHO), benzaldehyde (PhCHO).

A *ketone* has two carbon substituents on the carbonyl carbon: R₂C=O. Examples: acetone (CH₃COCH₃), 2-butanone (CH₃COCH₂CH₃), cyclohexanone, acetophenone.

Naming follows familiar rules. For aldehydes, use the suffix *-al* on the parent chain (the carbonyl carbon is always C1). Methanal (formaldehyde), ethanal (acetaldehyde), 2-butenal (crotonaldehyde, CH₃–CH=CH–CHO). For aldehydes attached to rings, the parent is named with *-carbaldehyde*: cyclohexanecarbaldehyde.

For ketones, use the suffix *-one*. Number to give the carbonyl carbon the lowest locant: 2-butanone (CH₃COCH₂CH₃), 3-pentanone, cyclopentanone, acetophenone (the most common common name; IUPAC: 1-phenylethan-1-one).

Aldehydes are higher in oxidation state than alcohols but lower than carboxylic acids. Ketones are higher in oxidation state than alcohols but cannot easily be further oxidized (no H on the carbonyl carbon to lose). This difference — aldehyde easily oxidizes to acid, ketone resists oxidation — is one of the key chemical differences between the two.

## The nucleophilic addition mechanism

The dominant reaction of aldehydes and ketones is *nucleophilic addition* across the C=O double bond. A nucleophile attacks the electrophilic carbonyl carbon; the carbon becomes sp³ (with the new bond to Nu); the π bond's electrons move onto the oxygen, leaving a tetrahedral intermediate with O⁻ (often called a *tetrahedral alkoxide intermediate*).

The first step:

$$\text{R}_2\text{C}{=}\text{O} + \text{Nu}^- \longrightarrow \text{R}_2\text{C}(\text{Nu}){-}\text{O}^-$$

This is the universal first step for nucleophilic additions. From here, what happens next depends on the nucleophile and conditions.

For most additions, the next step is *protonation* of the alkoxide oxygen to give the neutral product:

$$\text{R}_2\text{C}(\text{Nu}){-}\text{O}^- + \text{H}^+ \longrightarrow \text{R}_2\text{C}(\text{Nu}){-}\text{OH}$$

So the overall transformation is C=O + Nu–H → R₂C(Nu)(OH). The π bond has been broken; two new bonds (one to Nu, one to H from the proton source) have formed.

The trajectory: the nucleophile approaches the carbonyl carbon at an angle of about 105° from the C=O axis (the Bürgi–Dunitz angle). This angle results from the electronic structure: the nucleophile's lone pair has to overlap with the π* (LUMO) of the carbonyl, which is centered slightly behind the carbon, away from the oxygen.

The carbonyl is *more reactive* in aldehydes than in ketones. The reason is partly electronic (alkyl groups in ketones are weakly electron-donating, slightly reducing the carbon's δ+) and partly steric (an aldehyde's CHO has more accessible carbonyl carbon than a ketone's C(R)R'). The order: formaldehyde > simple aldehydes > simple ketones > aryl-conjugated ketones.

## Specific nucleophilic additions

The chapter goes through a series of additions. Each one has a specific nucleophile and gives a specific product. Pattern: Nu attacks C, alkoxide intermediate, protonation gives final product (sometimes with subsequent elimination of water for certain nucleophiles).

### Hydration

Water adds across the C=O to give a *hydrate* (also called a *gem-diol* — two OH groups on the same carbon).

$$\text{R}_2\text{C}{=}\text{O} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{R}_2\text{C}(\text{OH})_2$$

The reaction is reversible. For most aldehydes and ketones, the equilibrium lies far on the side of the carbonyl (the hydrate is unstable). Acetone is about 0.1% hydrated in water. Formaldehyde, however, is over 99% hydrated; it exists in aqueous solution almost entirely as methanediol (CH₂(OH)₂). Chloral (Cl₃CCHO) is also nearly fully hydrated due to electron-withdrawal stabilization of the gem-diol.

Hydration is acid- or base-catalyzed (it's slow without catalysis). Acid catalysis: the carbonyl is protonated to make it more electrophilic; water attacks; deprotonation gives the gem-diol. Base catalysis: hydroxide attacks the carbonyl directly; the alkoxide is protonated by water.

### Cyanohydrin formation

HCN (or NaCN + acid) adds to a carbonyl to give a *cyanohydrin*.

$$\text{R}_2\text{C}{=}\text{O} + \text{HCN} \longrightarrow \text{R}_2\text{C}(\text{OH})(\text{CN})$$

The cyanide is the nucleophile. In water, the equilibrium favors the cyanohydrin (CN is electron-withdrawing, which stabilizes the gem-diol/cyanohydrin product). The reaction is useful synthetically: cyanohydrins can be hydrolyzed to give α-hydroxy acids, or reduced (LiAlH₄) to give amino alcohols, or further chemistry.

### Grignard addition

A Grignard reagent (R'–MgX) is a strong nucleophile (R'-) and adds to the carbonyl to give an alkoxide, which is then protonated on workup to give an alcohol.

$$\text{R}_2\text{C}{=}\text{O} + \text{R'MgX} \longrightarrow \text{R}_2\text{C}(\text{OMgX})(\text{R'}) \xrightarrow{\text{H}^+/\text{H}_2\text{O}} \text{R}_2\text{C}(\text{OH})(\text{R'})$$

This is the canonical method for making alcohols with a new C–C bond. From a ketone + Grignard, you get a tertiary alcohol. From an aldehyde + Grignard, secondary alcohol. From formaldehyde + Grignard, primary alcohol. This is one of the most-used reactions in synthesis.

### Hydride reduction

Sodium borohydride (NaBH₄) and lithium aluminum hydride (LiAlH₄) are hydride donors. They reduce aldehydes to primary alcohols and ketones to secondary alcohols.

$$\text{R}_2\text{C}{=}\text{O} + \text{H}^- \longrightarrow \text{R}_2\text{C}(\text{OH})\text{H}$$

NaBH₄ is mild — selective for aldehydes and ketones over other carbonyl groups (it doesn't reduce esters, amides, or carboxylic acids efficiently). LiAlH₄ is more aggressive — it reduces almost any carbonyl-containing functional group.

The trajectory of hydride attack is the same as other nucleophiles: 105° approach from above the C=O plane. The result is sp³ carbon with H, R, R, OH attached.

For chiral starting materials, hydride can attack from either face of the C=O, giving a mixture of stereoisomers. Chiral reducing agents (CBS catalyst, BINAL, BINAP-Ru complexes) give enantioselective reductions where one face is preferred. This is the basis of much of asymmetric reduction chemistry.

### Imine formation

Primary amines (RNH₂) add to carbonyls. The initial step gives a tetrahedral hemiaminal (R₂C(NHR)(OH)), which then loses water to give an *imine* (also called a *Schiff base*, R₂C=NR).

$$\text{R}_2\text{C}{=}\text{O} + \text{R'NH}_2 \longrightarrow \text{R}_2\text{C}{=}\text{NR'} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$

The reaction is acid-catalyzed and reversible. Optimal conditions are mild acid (pH 4–5): some protonation activates the carbonyl, but not so much that the amine is fully protonated and inactive as a nucleophile.

Imines are important biologically. The aldehyde pyridoxal phosphate (the active form of vitamin B6) forms imines with the amino groups of substrate amino acids in many enzymatic reactions, activating the substrate for further chemistry. Many drugs have imine intermediates in their syntheses.

### Enamine formation

Secondary amines (R₂NH) react with carbonyls similarly, but the final loss of water from the carbinolamine intermediate has to give an enamine (since there's no H on the nitrogen to lose):

$$\text{R}_2\text{C}{=}\text{O} + \text{R'}_2\text{NH} \longrightarrow \text{R}_2\text{C}{=}\text{NR'}_2... \text{wait}$$

Actually: R₂CH–C(=O)R' + R₂NH → R₂C=C(NR₂)R' + H₂O. The secondary amine attaches to the carbonyl, then water leaves with a β-H of the adjacent C, giving an enamine (the C=C is now in the α/β position, with the amine on the original carbonyl carbon).

Enamines are useful as nucleophiles. The nitrogen lone pair pushes electron density into the C=C, giving the β-carbon nucleophilic character. Enamines react with alkyl halides at the β-carbon (a carbon-carbon bond-forming reaction). The Stork enamine alkylation, developed by Gilbert Stork in the 1950s, is a classic synthetic method.

### Acetal formation

Two equivalents of an alcohol react with a carbonyl (with acid catalysis) to give an *acetal* (R₂C(OR')₂).

$$\text{R}_2\text{C}{=}\text{O} + 2\,\text{R'OH} \rightleftharpoons \text{R}_2\text{C}(\text{OR'})_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$

The mechanism goes through a hemiacetal intermediate (one alcohol added, R₂C(OH)(OR')) and then through an oxocarbenium-ion intermediate (after water leaves) to give the acetal.

The reaction is reversible. Acetals form when water is removed from the equilibrium (using a Dean-Stark trap, drying agent, or other means). Acetals hydrolyze back to the carbonyl in aqueous acid.

Acetals are *protecting groups* for carbonyls. If you want to do chemistry on another part of the molecule and need to keep the carbonyl out of the way, convert it to an acetal first, do the chemistry, then hydrolyze the acetal back to the carbonyl. The acetal is inert under most basic conditions and most nucleophilic substitutions; it only opens up under acid hydrolysis.

A common variant: use 1,2- or 1,3-diols to give cyclic acetals (1,3-dioxolanes from ethylene glycol; 1,3-dioxanes from 1,3-propanediol). Cyclic acetals are even more stable than acyclic ones, and they're the standard protecting groups for ketones.

### Wittig reaction

A *phosphonium ylide* (R'-PPh₃-CR₂⁻, with the negative on carbon) reacts with a carbonyl to give an alkene. This is the *Wittig reaction*, developed by Georg Wittig (Nobel Prize 1979).

$$\text{R}_2\text{C}{=}\text{O} + \text{R}'_2\text{C}{=}\text{PPh}_3 \longrightarrow \text{R}_2\text{C}{=}\text{CR}'_2 + \text{Ph}_3\text{PO}$$

Mechanism: the ylide's carbanion attacks the carbonyl carbon. The resulting betaine cyclizes to a four-membered oxaphosphetane intermediate, which then fragments to give the alkene and triphenylphosphine oxide.

The Wittig reaction is the cleanest way to make a specific alkene from a ketone or aldehyde with a defined position of the new C=C bond. It's used heavily in synthesis.

### Haloform reaction

Methyl ketones (R-CO-CH₃) react with a halogen (Br₂, I₂) under basic conditions to first halogenate the methyl group (giving R-CO-CX₃), then break the C–C bond between the two carbons to give a carboxylic acid and a haloform (CHX₃).

$$\text{R}{-}\text{CO}{-}\text{CH}_3 + 3\,\text{X}_2 + 4\,\text{HO}^- \longrightarrow \text{R}{-}\text{COO}^- + \text{CHX}_3 + 3\,\text{X}^- + 3\,\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

The CHX₃ (haloform) is a precipitate in aqueous solution (chloroform, bromoform) or a yellow solid (iodoform). The iodoform test (with NaOH/I₂) is a classic qualitative test for methyl ketones — a yellow precipitate of CHI₃ confirms the presence of a CH₃CO group.

## A summary of the carbonyl additions

The pattern across all of these:

1. Nucleophile adds to the carbonyl carbon (sp³ alkoxide intermediate).
2. Either: the alkoxide is protonated (giving an OH) and we stop. Or: water (or a similar leaving group) departs, often in a second step, giving a different functional group (imine, enamine, acetal, alkene, etc.).

The summary table:

| Nucleophile | Product | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| H₂O | gem-diol | mostly equilibrium, favored only for formaldehyde and EWG-stabilized cases |
| HCN | cyanohydrin | useful intermediate |
| R'MgX, R'Li | alcohol | new C–C bond |
| H⁻ (NaBH₄, LiAlH₄) | alcohol | reduction |
| R'NH₂ | imine | with loss of water |
| R'₂NH | enamine | with loss of water |
| ROH (×2) | acetal | with loss of water; protecting group |
| R'CH=PPh₃ | alkene | Wittig reaction |
| HSR' | thioacetal | analog of acetal |

For each, the mechanism is broadly the same. The variations come from what happens after the alkoxide intermediate forms — direct protonation gives an alcohol; loss of water can give an imine, enamine, or acetal; further rearrangement gives an alkene.

## Conjugate addition (1,4-addition)

When an α,β-unsaturated carbonyl compound (R-CO-CH=CH-R') is attacked by a nucleophile, two outcomes are possible:

- *1,2-addition*: the Nu goes onto the carbonyl carbon, giving a tetrahedral alcohol after protonation. Same as for a regular ketone.
- *1,4-addition* (also called *Michael addition* or *conjugate addition*): the Nu goes onto the β-carbon of the C=C, with the negative charge ending up on the carbonyl oxygen (delocalized through the conjugated system). After protonation, the result is a saturated carbonyl compound with the Nu attached at the β-position.

The regioselectivity depends on the nucleophile:
- Hard, charged nucleophiles (RMgX, RLi, NaBH₄) prefer 1,2-addition.
- Soft, neutral, or stabilized nucleophiles (amines, RS⁻, malonate anions, organocuprates R₂CuLi) prefer 1,4-addition.

Michael addition is one of the most useful reactions for forming new C–C bonds in conjugated systems. It's the basis of many natural-product syntheses and pharmaceutical syntheses.

## What this chapter does

The carbonyl group (C=O) is sp² at carbon, polarized with C δ⁺ and O δ⁻, and reactive toward nucleophilic addition. Nucleophiles attack the carbon, form an alkoxide intermediate, and either get protonated (giving an alcohol-like product) or expel water (giving imines, enamines, acetals, alkenes).

The major nucleophiles and products:
- H₂O → gem-diol (rarely useful)
- HCN → cyanohydrin
- R'MgX or R'Li → alcohol with new C–C bond
- NaBH₄ or LiAlH₄ → alcohol via hydride reduction
- R'NH₂ → imine (Schiff base)
- R'₂NH → enamine
- 2 R'OH → acetal (with cyclic-diol variant)
- R'CH=PPh₃ → alkene (Wittig reaction)

α,β-unsaturated carbonyls (enones) can react via 1,2-addition (carbonyl) or 1,4-addition (β-carbon, Michael addition), with the choice determined by the nucleophile.
