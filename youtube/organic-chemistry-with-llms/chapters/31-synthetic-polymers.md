# Chapter 31 — Synthetic Polymers

*Most of the materials of modern life are polymers. Plastics, fibers, foams, rubbers, paints, adhesives, and coatings are all built from small monomers connected into long chains, with properties that depend on the monomer chemistry, the chain length, and the way the chains are arranged.*

In 1907 Leo Baekeland produced the first fully synthetic polymer, Bakelite, by condensing phenol with formaldehyde under heat and pressure. Bakelite was an immediate commercial success — durable, electrically insulating, and moldable. The age of synthetic polymers had begun. Over the next century, polymer science would create most of the materials we live with: polyethylene grocery bags, polypropylene yogurt containers, polystyrene cups, PET water bottles, polyester clothing, nylon stockings, polyurethane foam mattresses, polyvinyl chloride pipes, Teflon non-stick coatings.

The global polymer industry produces about 400 million tons of plastics per year. The molecular chemistry of synthetic polymers is, fundamentally, the chemistry we've covered in the rest of this book — applied repeatedly. Each polymerization step is one chemical reaction (addition or condensation). The interesting chemistry is in the *mechanisms of chain growth*, the *control of chain length and structure*, and the *physical properties* that emerge from connecting many small reactive units into long chains.

This is the final chapter of the book. It pulls together polymer chemistry, briefly references the major classes, and points toward the connections between polymer structure and bulk material properties.

## How polymers are classified

By **synthesis method**:

- **Chain-growth polymers**: built by chain-reaction polymerization. An initiator adds to a single monomer, generating a reactive end. That end adds to another monomer. The chain grows by repeating addition reactions, one monomer at a time.

- **Step-growth polymers**: built by step-growth polymerization. Each step is a separate condensation reaction (like ester or amide formation) between monomer functional groups. Chains grow gradually through random pairing of growing chains and individual monomers.

By **physical behavior**:

- **Thermoplastics**: melt and re-solidify when heated. Examples: polyethylene, polypropylene, polystyrene, PVC, nylons, PET. Most everyday plastics.

- **Thermosets**: cure into a permanent cross-linked network on heating. Don't re-melt. Examples: epoxy resins, melamine resins, vulcanized rubber. Generally harder, more rigid, and more chemically resistant than thermoplastics.

- **Elastomers**: stretchy, can be deformed substantially and recover when released. Examples: vulcanized rubber, polyurethanes, silicone rubber. Rubber bands, tires, and gaskets.

## Chain-growth polymerization

The polymerization of an alkene monomer (a "vinyl monomer") goes through three phases: initiation, propagation, termination.

**Initiation.** A reactive species (a radical, cation, or anion) is generated. For radical polymerization, an initiator like benzoyl peroxide ((PhC(=O)O)₂) decomposes at modest temperature to give two PhC(=O)O• radicals.

**Propagation.** The reactive species adds to the C=C of a monomer, generating a new reactive species at the chain end. That end adds to another monomer, and so on. Each step is a conjugate addition (Chapter 19) — the chain end's radical (or cation, or anion) attacks the monomer's π bond.

**Termination.** Two reactive ends combine, or a chain transfer reaction caps an end. The chain stops growing.

**Three flavors of chain-growth polymerization:**

**Free radical polymerization.** The most common. Works for almost any vinyl monomer (styrene, acrylates, vinyl chloride, ethylene, methyl methacrylate, etc.). The initiating radical adds to the C=C; the resulting carbon radical adds to another monomer; and so on. Termination by radical-radical recombination or by chain transfer.

Polystyrene, polyethylene (low-density), polyvinyl chloride, polymethyl methacrylate (Plexiglas, Lucite) are all radical polymers.

**Cationic polymerization.** Works for monomers with electron-donating groups (alkenes with alkyl, alkoxy, or amine substituents). The initiator is a Lewis acid (BF₃, AlCl₃) that adds H⁺ or another electrophile to the monomer. The resulting carbocation adds to more monomer, growing the chain. Used for polyisobutylene (a soft, rubbery polymer used in butyl rubber for inner tubes).

**Anionic polymerization.** Works for monomers with electron-withdrawing groups (acrylates, acrylonitrile, styrene). The initiator is a strong nucleophile (BuLi, organolithium). The resulting carbanion adds to more monomer, propagating the chain. Used for "living polymers" — the chains can be capped with specific end groups by adding a desired electrophile at the end.

## Chain stereochemistry

When a vinyl monomer with a substituent (R) on one end of the C=C polymerizes, the polymer chain has a stereocenter at every other carbon. The arrangement of these stereocenters affects polymer properties enormously.

Three configurations:

- **Atactic** (random): the R groups are on random sides of the chain. Atactic polymers tend to be amorphous (no crystalline order), often soft and gummy.

- **Isotactic** (all on same side): all R groups on one side of the polymer backbone. Isotactic polymers are highly crystalline — chains pack regularly. Often hard, strong, high melting.

- **Syndiotactic** (alternating): R groups alternate sides. Also crystalline.

Atactic polypropylene is a sticky, useless gum. Isotactic polypropylene is a hard, useful plastic widely used in packaging. The difference is in the catalysts used: a Ziegler-Natta catalyst (TiCl₃ + Et₃Al) gives isotactic polypropylene by stereoregular addition; a free radical initiator gives atactic.

The Ziegler-Natta catalyst was the breakthrough that made stereoregular polymerization practical. Karl Ziegler and Giulio Natta shared the 1963 Nobel Prize in chemistry for this work.

## Major chain-growth polymers

**Polyethylene (PE).** From ethylene. Two main forms:
- LDPE (low-density polyethylene): made by free-radical polymerization at high pressure. Highly branched. Soft, flexible. Used in plastic bags, films, squeeze bottles.
- HDPE (high-density polyethylene): made by Ziegler-Natta or metallocene catalysis. Linear, with little branching. Hard, rigid. Used in milk jugs, detergent bottles, pipes.

About 80 million tons per year, the largest-volume synthetic polymer.

**Polypropylene (PP).** From propylene. Made by Ziegler-Natta or metallocene catalysis. Isotactic. Used in food containers, fibers, automobile parts. About 70 million tons per year.

**Polystyrene (PS).** From styrene. Made by free-radical polymerization. Atactic. Used in disposable cups, packaging, foam (with blowing agents). About 25 million tons per year.

**Polyvinyl chloride (PVC).** From vinyl chloride. Made by free-radical polymerization (suspension or emulsion). Used in pipes, window frames, vinyl flooring, electrical insulation. About 50 million tons per year.

**Polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA).** From methyl methacrylate. Free-radical. Crystal-clear, used in Plexiglas, dental fillings, contact lenses.

**Polyacrylonitrile (PAN).** From acrylonitrile. Used in fibers (Acrilan, Orlon). Acrylic fibers.

**Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE, Teflon).** From tetrafluoroethylene. Free-radical. Extreme chemical and thermal resistance. Non-stick coatings, gaskets.

**Polybutadiene, polyisoprene.** From conjugated dienes. Mostly free-radical or anionic. Synthetic rubbers.

## Step-growth polymerization

Each step is an independent condensation. Common patterns:

**Polyamides (nylons).** A diacid + a diamine → polyamide + water. Each step is amide formation (Chapter 21). Nylon-6,6 is from hexamethylenediamine + adipic acid. Nylon-6 is from caprolactam (a cyclic amide that opens during polymerization).

Nylons are used in fibers (clothing, carpets), engineering plastics (gears, bearings), and ropes.

**Polyesters.** A diol + a diacid → polyester + water. Each step is ester formation. PET (polyethylene terephthalate) is from ethylene glycol + terephthalic acid. PBT (polybutylene terephthalate) similarly.

PET is used in soda bottles, polyester clothing fibers, thin films.

**Polyurethanes.** A diol + a diisocyanate → polyurethane. Each step is urethane formation (an amine + a carbonyl-isocyanate). Used in foams (mattresses, insulation), elastomers, adhesives, coatings.

**Polycarbonates.** A diol + phosgene (or a carbonate equivalent) → polycarbonate. Lexan is the trade name. Used in eyeglass lenses, CD/DVD discs, bullet-resistant glass.

**Phenol-formaldehyde resins (Bakelite).** Phenol + formaldehyde → highly cross-linked thermoset. The first synthetic plastic.

**Epoxy resins.** Diepoxide + diamine → cross-linked thermoset. Used as adhesives, coatings, and structural composites.

The molecular weight in step-growth polymerization typically reaches large values only when the reaction is essentially complete (>99% conversion). At lower conversions, the chains are too short to be useful.

## Cross-linking and curing

Some polymerization processes deliberately leave functional groups on chains that can cross-link with other chains, forming a three-dimensional network instead of a linear or branched polymer.

**Vulcanization** of rubber (Charles Goodyear, 1839). Natural rubber (cis-polyisoprene) is heated with sulfur. The S-S bonds form between chains at the C–H positions adjacent to the C=C bonds, cross-linking the rubber. The result is harder, more elastic (recovers shape after deformation), and resistant to heat. All modern rubbers (tires, gaskets, elastic bands) are vulcanized.

**Curing** of epoxy resin. The diepoxide and diamine form a network: each amine has two N–H bonds, each can open one epoxide ring, and the resulting cross-linked structure is rigid and strong. Used in fiber composites (epoxy + glass fiber → fiberglass; epoxy + carbon fiber → carbon-fiber composites used in aircraft and bicycles).

**Curing** of polyurethane. Excess diisocyanate provides cross-linking through urea formation (water reacts with isocyanate to give CO₂ + amine, which then reacts with another isocyanate). Used in rigid foams.

The amount of cross-linking determines mechanical properties. Lightly cross-linked polymers are elastic (rubber). Heavily cross-linked are rigid (Bakelite, epoxy).

## Plastic recycling and biodegradability

Most plastics are not easily recycled. The reasons:
- Mixing different plastic types complicates the chemistry.
- Many additives (colorants, plasticizers, stabilizers) make recycling difficult.
- Some plastics degrade during recycling (chains break, properties decline).

Plastics are typically classified into seven recycling categories (the numbered triangle on bottle bottoms):
1. PET (recyclable; bottles)
2. HDPE (recyclable; jugs)
3. PVC (rarely recycled)
4. LDPE (rarely recycled)
5. PP (sometimes recycled)
6. PS (rarely recycled)
7. Other (mixed)

In practice, only about 9% of plastic produced has been recycled; the rest is landfilled or incinerated.

**Biodegradable polymers** are an active area of research. PHA (polyhydroxyalkanoate) and PLA (polylactic acid) are commercial biodegradable polyesters made from renewable feedstocks. They degrade in compost or sea water over months to years. Adoption is growing but still small relative to traditional plastics.

The environmental impact of plastics is one of the major concerns of contemporary chemistry. Microplastics are ubiquitous in oceans, rivers, soils, and even human tissues. Plastic pollution is a global problem with no clear solution at scale.

## Polymer physical properties

The properties of a polymer depend on:

**Chain length** (degree of polymerization). Longer chains generally give higher melting points, higher strength, more entanglement. Below a critical chain length, the polymer doesn't have useful properties.

**Crystallinity**. Polymers with regular structure (isotactic, linear) crystallize when the chains pack regularly. Crystalline regions are stronger and stiffer; amorphous regions are softer and more flexible. Many polymers are partially crystalline (e.g., HDPE is about 80% crystalline).

**Glass transition temperature (Tg)**. Below Tg, an amorphous polymer is glassy and brittle. Above Tg, it's rubbery and flexible. The Tg depends on the molecular structure — flexible chains with weak intermolecular interactions have low Tg (and the polymer is rubbery at room temp); rigid chains with strong intermolecular interactions (hydrogen bonds, large dipoles) have high Tg.

**Melting temperature (Tm)**. The crystalline portions melt at Tm. Above Tm, the entire polymer is amorphous and processable.

**Cross-linking**. As discussed, cross-linking turns thermoplastic polymers into thermosets. Cross-link density affects elasticity and stiffness.

## Polymerization and its limits

A polymer's "molecular weight" is actually a *distribution* — chains have different lengths. Common measures:
- Number-average molecular weight (M_n): the simple mean of chain weights.
- Weight-average molecular weight (M_w): a weighted mean that emphasizes longer chains.
- Polydispersity index (PDI = M_w / M_n): a measure of distribution width. PDI = 1 means all chains the same length (ideal); PDI = 2 is typical for free-radical polymerization; PDI > 5 indicates very broad distribution.

Living polymerizations (anionic, controlled radical methods) can give PDI close to 1 — narrow distribution, well-defined chains. This is important for applications where uniform properties matter.

## Biopolymers and synthetic biology

Many biological polymers (proteins, polysaccharides, nucleic acids) have synthetic uses or analogs. Modern synthetic biology uses tools from molecular biology to make polymers in cells:

- Recombinant proteins as biomaterials.
- Engineered bacteria producing biopolyesters (PHA family).
- Spider silk proteins expressed in tobacco plants and bacteria for high-performance fibers.
- DNA-based materials for nanofabrication.

These methods overlap with the standard polymer chemistry but use cellular machinery to build the polymers.

## What this chapter does

Synthetic polymers are large molecules made by repeating polymerization of smaller monomers. Two general mechanisms:

- **Chain-growth polymerization**: an initiator generates a reactive end; the end adds to monomers one at a time; eventually terminates. Used for vinyl polymers (PE, PP, PS, PVC, PMMA, etc.). Three flavors: free radical, cationic, anionic.

- **Step-growth polymerization**: each step is a separate condensation between monomer functional groups (esterification, amide formation, urethane formation). Used for nylons, polyesters, polyurethanes, epoxies.

Polymer properties depend on chain length, crystallinity, glass transition temperature, melting point, and cross-linking. Stereoregularity (isotactic vs. atactic) dramatically affects crystallinity and physical properties. Ziegler-Natta and metallocene catalysts give stereoregular polymers; free-radical methods give atactic polymers.

Major polymer classes:
- Thermoplastics (PE, PP, PVC, PS, nylon, PET) — melt and re-solidify.
- Thermosets (epoxy, Bakelite, melamine) — cross-linked, don't re-melt.
- Elastomers (rubbers, polyurethanes) — stretchy, recoverable.

Biodegradability and recycling are major contemporary concerns. Most plastics are not recycled at scale; environmental impact is significant.
