# Chapter 24 — Amines and Heterocycles

*Nitrogen is the second most common element in biological molecules after carbon. Most pharmaceuticals are amines. The chemistry of the lone pair on nitrogen is what makes them work.*

About 75% of FDA-approved small-molecule drugs contain at least one amine group. Amines are in caffeine, nicotine, morphine, cocaine, dopamine, adrenaline, histamine, serotonin, and most antihistamines, antibiotics, antidepressants, and stimulants. They're in essentially every neurotransmitter and most peptide hormones. The amino acids that build proteins are amines. The nucleobases in DNA and RNA are heterocyclic amines.

The reason amines are so prevalent is in their reactivity. The nitrogen lone pair makes amines basic (acceptable to protons), nucleophilic (donating to electrophiles), and able to participate in hydrogen bonding. The C–N bond is polar but stable. Nitrogen accommodates positive charge well (as an ammonium ion). All of this together makes amines structural and functional centerpieces of biological chemistry.

This chapter covers amines (R-NH₂, R₂NH, R₃N, R₄N⁺) and the major heterocyclic amines (pyridine, pyrrole, imidazole, indole, and related N-containing aromatic rings). The chemistry of amines as bases, as nucleophiles, in coupling reactions, and in heterocycle chemistry comes together to give a picture of nitrogen functional-group reactivity.

## Structure and naming

An amine is N with three R groups (some of which can be H or aryl) plus a lone pair. The nitrogen is sp³ hybridized, with a tetrahedral arrangement of bonds and lone pair. Bond angles are about 107° (slightly compressed from 109.5° because the lone pair takes more space than a bond).

Classification:
- *Primary* (1°): R-NH₂ (one C attached to N, two H's). Methylamine, ethylamine, aniline.
- *Secondary* (2°): R₂N-H (two C's, one H). Dimethylamine, *N*-methylaniline.
- *Tertiary* (3°): R₃N (three C's, no H). Trimethylamine, pyridine.
- *Quaternary* (4°): R₄N⁺ (four C's, positive charge, ammonium ion). Tetraethylammonium chloride.

Note that this classification is by the *N*, not by the C of the C–N bond. A tertiary amine has three groups on N. (Compare to alcohols: a tertiary alcohol has three groups on the C bearing the OH, not three on the O.)

Naming amines: the IUPAC suffix is *-amine* on the parent alkyl group:
- Methanamine = methylamine = CH₃NH₂
- Ethanamine = ethylamine
- Cyclohexanamine = cyclohexylamine

For substituted amines, locants on N are denoted with a capital N: *N*-methylaniline, *N*,*N*-dimethylaniline.

For primary amines on a chain or ring, "amino" can be used as a substituent name: 2-aminobutane, 4-aminocyclohexanol.

## Properties

The N–H bonds in primary and secondary amines hydrogen-bond, but less strongly than O–H bonds in alcohols. Nitrogen is less electronegative than oxygen, so the N–H is less polarized, and hydrogen bonding is weaker.

Boiling points reflect this: ethylamine bp 17 °C; ethanol bp 78 °C; ethane bp –89 °C. The amine boils much higher than the alkane (because of hydrogen bonding) but lower than the alcohol.

Tertiary amines with no N–H bonds don't hydrogen-bond as donors (they can still accept H-bonds at the lone pair), so their boiling points are lower than primary or secondary amines of similar mass.

Amines have characteristic, often unpleasant smells. Trimethylamine (the smell of rotting fish) is a metabolic product. Putrescine and cadaverine (named for what they smell of — bacterial decomposition) are diamine products of decarboxylation of certain amino acids.

## Basicity

Amines are basic: the lone pair on N can accept a proton.

$$\text{R}_3\text{N} + \text{H}^+ \rightleftharpoons \text{R}_3\text{N-H}^+$$

The product is an *ammonium ion*. The pKa values referred to are for these ammonium ions (i.e., the ammonium's pKa equals the equilibrium for the ammonium ion donating a proton; this is the conjugate acid of the amine):

| Compound | pKa of conjugate acid | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| NH₃ | 9.26 | reference |
| methylamine | 10.66 | alkyl amine, slightly more basic than NH₃ |
| dimethylamine | 10.73 | even more so |
| trimethylamine | 9.81 | down because of solvation effects |
| ethylamine | 10.81 | |
| aniline (PhNH₂) | 4.63 | far less basic, lone pair conjugates into ring |
| pyridine | 5.25 | sp² N, lone pair in sp² orbital |
| pyrrole | ≈ 0 | lone pair is part of aromatic π system |

Alkyl amines are roughly 100 times more basic than ammonia (pKa of conjugate acid is higher by 1–2 units), because the alkyl groups stabilize the ammonium cation by hyperconjugation.

Aromatic amines (anilines) are much less basic, by 5+ pKa units. The reason: the lone pair on N can delocalize into the aromatic ring through resonance, reducing its availability for protonation. When you protonate the amine, you lose that resonance stabilization.

Pyridine is even less basic than aliphatic amines because its lone pair sits in an sp² orbital (more s-character, lower energy, less available). Pyrrole is essentially nonbasic because protonating its N would destroy the aromaticity of the ring (the lone pair is part of the 6-electron aromatic π system).

## Synthesis of amines

Several major routes:

**SN2 of alkyl halide with NH₃ or amine.** R–X + NH₃ → R–NH₃⁺ + X⁻. Often gives a mixture (because the product amine is a better nucleophile than NH₃ and reacts further). Used for symmetric quaternary ammonium salts (with excess R–X).

**Reduction of nitriles.** R-CN + LiAlH₄ → R-CH₂-NH₂. Adds two H's, gives a primary amine with one extra carbon than the starting halide (since the cyanide came from R-X + NaCN). Net: R-X → R-CN → R-CH₂-NH₂.

**Reduction of nitro compounds.** Ar-NO₂ + Sn/HCl (or H₂/Pt) → Ar-NH₂. The standard route to anilines from nitrobenzenes (Chapter 16).

**Reductive amination.** Carbonyl + amine + reducing agent (NaBH₃CN or H₂/Pt) → amine. The carbonyl forms an imine first, then the imine is reduced to give the amine. Useful for making secondary and tertiary amines from a primary or secondary amine and an aldehyde or ketone. Doesn't suffer from the over-alkylation problem of direct SN2.

**Hofmann rearrangement.** R-CONH₂ + Br₂/NaOH → R-NH₂ + CO₂. Converts a primary amide to a primary amine with one fewer carbon.

**Curtius rearrangement.** R-CO-N₃ (acyl azide) + heat → R-N=C=O (isocyanate) → R-NH₂ (after hydrolysis of the isocyanate). Like Hofmann, gives a primary amine with one fewer carbon than the starting acyl azide.

**Gabriel synthesis.** Phthalimide (cyclic imide) + KOH → potassium phthalimide; then SN2 with R-X; then hydrolysis or hydrazinolysis releases the primary amine. Avoids the over-alkylation problem and gives clean primary amines.

## Reactions of amines

Amines are nucleophiles, bases, and reducing agents. The major reactions:

**Acid-base.** Reactions with acids give ammonium salts (often crystalline, easily isolated). Many drugs are sold as the hydrochloride salt because the salt is more water-soluble and more crystalline than the free amine. Codeine HCl, morphine HCl, etc.

**Nucleophilic substitution and addition.** Amines react with alkyl halides (SN2 to give substituted amines), with carbonyls (forming imines or enamines, Chapter 19), with carboxylic acid derivatives (making amides, Chapter 21).

**Acylation.** Primary or secondary amines react with acyl halides, anhydrides, or activated esters to give amides. This is the reaction that builds peptide bonds in proteins, and it's also widely used in synthesis to install amide linkages.

**Hofmann elimination.** A quaternary ammonium hydroxide R₄N⁺OH⁻, on heating, gives an alkene by E2 elimination. The elimination prefers the *less substituted* alkene (Hofmann's rule, the opposite of Zaitsev's rule). Used historically to determine amine structures by sequential degradation; rarely used in modern synthesis.

**Diazotization (for primary aromatic amines).** Ar-NH₂ + HNO₂ → Ar-N₂⁺ (diazonium ion). Aryldiazonium ions are powerful electrophiles in subsequent reactions: they can be replaced by halides (Sandmeyer reaction), by hydroxide (to give phenol), by nitrile, or by H (with H₃PO₂ to give the unsubstituted arene). Diazonium chemistry is a rich source of aromatic substitution products that can't be made directly.

**Coupling with diazonium salts.** Aryldiazonium salts can be used as electrophiles in electrophilic aromatic substitution on activated arenes (anilines, phenols). The product is an *azo compound* (Ar-N=N-Ar'). Many synthetic dyes are azo compounds — methyl orange, sunset yellow, etc.

## Heterocyclic amines

Heterocycles are ring systems containing one or more atoms other than carbon (most commonly N, O, or S). Nitrogen-containing heterocycles are by far the most important in biology and medicinal chemistry.

The major N-heterocycles:

**Pyridine** (six-membered ring, one N replacing a CH). Aromatic, with N in the sp² plane and its lone pair in a sp² orbital pointing outward (not part of the π system). Modestly basic (pKa of conjugate acid 5.25). Used as a base and solvent in many reactions. The structure of niacin (vitamin B3) and many alkaloids.

**Pyrrole** (five-membered ring, one N, two C=C bonds). Aromatic with 6 π electrons (2 from each C=C, 2 from N's lone pair). The N's lone pair is part of the aromatic π system, so it's not available for protonation — pyrrole is nonbasic. Pyrroles are in heme (iron-binding cofactor of hemoglobin and cytochromes), in chlorophyll (Mg-binding cofactor of plant pigments), and in many alkaloids.

**Imidazole** (five-membered ring, two N's, two C=C bonds). Has both pyrrole-type and pyridine-type N's: one N has its lone pair in the π system (pyrrole-like, not basic), the other has its lone pair in an sp² orbital outside the π system (pyridine-like, basic, pKa of conjugate acid about 7). The presence of one basic and one less-basic N makes imidazole the perfect side chain of histidine — its pKa of about 6 means histidine residues in proteins can act as both proton donor and acceptor at physiological pH.

**Furan** (five-membered ring, one O). Aromatic with 6 π electrons (2 from each C=C, 2 from one O lone pair). The other O lone pair is in the sp² plane.

**Thiophene** (five-membered ring, one S). Aromatic with 6 π electrons. Common in pharmaceuticals.

**Indole** (fused six-membered + five-membered, one N in the 5-ring). Aromatic. The basis of tryptophan (the amino acid), serotonin, melatonin, LSD, DMT, and many other natural products. The most pharmacologically prevalent heterocycle.

**Pyrimidine** (six-membered ring, two N's at 1,3-positions). Aromatic. The basis of cytosine, thymine, and uracil — three of the four bases of DNA/RNA.

**Purine** (fused 5- and 6-membered, four N's). Aromatic. The basis of adenine and guanine — the other two bases of DNA/RNA.

The combination of pyrimidines and purines as DNA/RNA bases is one of the most striking facts in biology: the entire genetic code is written in five heterocyclic rings (A, G, C, T in DNA; replace T with U for RNA). The base-pairing chemistry — A-T, G-C — depends on hydrogen-bonding patterns that are precisely tuned by the nitrogen positions in these heterocycles.

## Heterocycles in pharmaceuticals

Looking at the top 100 best-selling drugs, you'll find:

- Imidazoles (omeprazole, fluconazole, ketoconazole — antifungals and PPIs)
- Indoles (sumatriptan, ondansetron, zolmitriptan, indomethacin)
- Pyridines (esomeprazole, nicotine, niacin)
- Pyrimidines (caffeine, theophylline, sulfa drugs, antifolate agents)
- Quinolines (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin — antibiotics)
- Benzimidazoles (omeprazole, mebendazole)
- Triazoles (fluconazole, itraconazole)

Building these rings is a major focus of medicinal chemistry. Modern transition-metal-catalyzed methods (Suzuki coupling on heterocyclic halides, C-H activation, etc.) have made heterocycle synthesis dramatically more efficient over the past 20 years.

## Synthesizing pyridines and pyrroles

Several classic heterocycle syntheses:

**Hantzsch pyridine synthesis.** Aldehyde + 2 β-ketoester + NH₃ → dihydropyridine, which can be oxidized to pyridine. A multicomponent reaction that builds the 6-membered ring with two ester substituents.

**Knorr pyrrole synthesis.** α-amino ketone + β-ketoester (with base) → substituted pyrrole. The two carbonyls condense and the amine forms part of the 5-membered ring.

**Paal-Knorr pyrrole synthesis.** 1,4-diketone + R-NH₂ → 2,5-disubstituted pyrrole. Direct condensation; an N replaces an O in the ring.

**Skraup quinoline synthesis.** Aniline + glycerol + oxidant + acid → quinoline. The aniline provides the N for the new ring.

These older "named" reactions are still used, but modern routes often go through transition-metal-catalyzed cyclizations or cycloadditions. The synthesis of any specific heterocycle is usually a research project in itself, with the choice of route depending heavily on which substituents you need.

## What this chapter does

Amines (R-NH₂, R₂NH, R₃N, R₄N⁺) are nitrogen analogs of alcohols and ethers, with substantial lone-pair-driven chemistry. They're basic (alkyl amines with pKa of conjugate acid ≈ 10-11; aryl amines ≈ 4-5; pyridine ≈ 5; pyrrole ≈ 0), nucleophilic, and capable of hydrogen bonding (when N-H is present).

Synthesis routes include SN2 with nitrogen nucleophiles, reduction of nitriles or nitro compounds, reductive amination, and several rearrangement reactions (Hofmann, Curtius). The Gabriel synthesis is the canonical clean route to primary amines without polyalkylation.

Major reactions: acid-base chemistry (forming ammonium salts), nucleophilic addition (to carbonyls, forming imines or enamines), nucleophilic acyl substitution (forming amides), Hofmann elimination, and diazotization (for aromatic primary amines).

Heterocyclic amines — pyridine, pyrrole, imidazole, indole, pyrimidine, purine, and others — are among the most important structural motifs in biological molecules and pharmaceuticals. They're aromatic (when 4n+2 π electrons), with nitrogen sometimes contributing its lone pair to the π system (pyrrole-type, non-basic) and sometimes not (pyridine-type, basic).

The remaining chapters (25–31) cover biomolecules — sugars, amino acids, lipids, nucleic acids, metabolic pathways. Most of these involve amines as core structural elements, and the chemistry of amines comes back repeatedly as we walk through how these molecules are made and how they react.
