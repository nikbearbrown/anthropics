# Chapter 17 — Alcohols and Phenols

*Alcohols are everywhere — in your body, in your fuel tank, in your medicine cabinet. Their chemistry is half of organic synthesis: half the time you're making them, half the time you're transforming them into something else.*

About 50 billion gallons of methanol are produced industrially each year — most of it from the catalytic reduction of carbon monoxide with hydrogen, then used as a fuel additive, a feedstock for formaldehyde and other chemicals, and a building-block solvent. About 25 billion gallons of ethanol are produced annually, mostly by fermentation of sugars or starches, used as fuel, in beverages, and as a chemical feedstock. The annual production of all alcohols globally is in the hundreds of billions of pounds, dwarfing most other functional-group classes.

This chapter is about alcohols (R–OH, where R is an sp³ carbon) and phenols (Ar–OH, where Ar is an aromatic ring). The two are related — both have the H–O–C structural motif — but their chemistry differs enough that we treat them somewhat separately. Phenols are about a million times more acidic than alcohols, for instance, because the phenoxide anion is stabilized by resonance into the aromatic ring while alkoxide ions have no comparable stabilization. This single fact governs much of phenol's chemistry.

The chapter covers structure, naming, properties (especially acidity), preparation methods (most of which we've seen in earlier chapters), and the major reactions that *consume* alcohols — converting them to ethers, esters, alkenes, or carbonyls.

## Structure and naming

An alcohol is R–OH where R is an sp³ carbon. The oxygen is sp³ hybridized, with two lone pairs and two bonds (one to C, one to H). The C–O–H bond angle is about 108.5°, close to the tetrahedral 109.5°. Alcohols can rotate freely about the C–O bond.

Like other functional groups, alcohols are classified by the carbon they're attached to:
- *Primary* (1°): the C–OH carbon has one other C attached (and two H's).
- *Secondary* (2°): two other C's attached (one H).
- *Tertiary* (3°): three other C's attached (no H's).

This 1°/2°/3° classification matters because it strongly affects reactivity, especially in oxidation reactions.

Naming alcohols: replace the final -e of the parent alkane name with -ol. So methane → methanol, ethane → ethanol, etc. For more complex compounds, number the chain to give the OH the lowest locant.

A few common names persist:
- methanol = methyl alcohol
- ethanol = ethyl alcohol
- 2-propanol = isopropyl alcohol
- 2-methyl-2-propanol = *tert*-butyl alcohol

Phenols are named with -phenol as the parent. Hydroxyl substituents on a benzene ring are sometimes given common names too (cresol = methylphenol, hydroquinone = 1,4-dihydroxybenzene, etc.).

When there are two OH groups, the suffix is -diol: 1,4-pentanediol, 1,2-cyclohexanediol. Three OH groups gives triol.

## Properties: hydrogen bonding and boiling points

The O–H of an alcohol is polar, with a partial negative on the oxygen (lone pair–rich) and a partial positive on the hydrogen. Alcohols *hydrogen bond* — the H of one molecule attracts the lone pair of another, creating an attractive interaction worth about 20 kJ/mol per H-bond.

This is why alcohols have boiling points much higher than alkanes of comparable molecular weight:
- methane (CH₄, MW 16): bp −161 °C
- methanol (CH₃OH, MW 32): bp 65 °C — far higher
- water (H₂O, MW 18): bp 100 °C — also far higher

The H-bonding network in liquid alcohol takes substantial energy to break. Hydrogen bonding also explains why alcohols are miscible with water (water can hydrogen bond to the alcohol's OH and form a continuous network). Higher alcohols (with longer alkyl chains) become less water-soluble as the alkyl portion gets larger and the hydrogen-bonding contribution to overall interaction energy shrinks.

## Acidity

Alcohols are weakly acidic — pKa around 16, similar to water (pKa 15.74). The conjugate base, an *alkoxide* (RO⁻), is a strong base.

Substituents affect acidity:
- *Alkyl groups* slightly *decrease* alcohol acidity. *tert*-butyl alcohol has pKa 18, methanol 15.5. The reason is solvation: alkoxide ions are stabilized by surrounding water molecules; bulky alkyl groups around the O⁻ block solvation, destabilize the anion, and make the alcohol less acidic.
- *Electron-withdrawing groups* increase acidity. 2,2,2-trifluoroethanol (CF₃CH₂OH) has pKa 12.43; nonafluoro-tert-butyl alcohol ((CF₃)₃COH) has pKa 5.4. The strongly electron-withdrawing CF₃ groups stabilize the alkoxide by pulling negative charge toward themselves, making the alcohol more acidic by orders of magnitude.

Phenols are remarkably more acidic than alcohols — pKa about 10 instead of 16. The conjugate base, a phenoxide ion, is stabilized by resonance: the negative charge can delocalize from the oxygen into the ring at three different ortho/para positions. This delocalization is worth about 40 kJ/mol of stabilization. The alcohol's alkoxide has no comparable resonance stabilization.

Substituents on phenol affect acidity:
- *Electron-withdrawing groups* (especially at ortho and para positions) increase acidity. Each NO₂ group decreases pKa by about 3 units. Picric acid (2,4,6-trinitrophenol) has pKa 0.4 — about as acidic as nitric acid.
- *Electron-donating groups* decrease acidity. *p*-Methoxyphenol has pKa 10.2; *p*-aminophenol has pKa 10.46.

| Compound | pKa |
|---|---|
| ethanol | 16.0 |
| water | 15.74 |
| phenol | 10.0 |
| *p*-nitrophenol | 7.15 |
| picric acid | 0.4 |

A practical consequence: phenoxide ions can be generated with sodium hydroxide. Hydroxide is a strong enough base to deprotonate phenols (pKa 10 vs. water 15.7 — favorable by 5–6 pKa units). Alkoxides require much stronger bases — sodium hydride (NaH), sodium amide (NaNH₂), or alkali metal in alcohol.

## Preparing alcohols: known reactions

We've already seen many ways to make alcohols. Pulling them together:

**From alkenes:**
- Acid-catalyzed hydration (Markovnikov, Chapter 8): H₂SO₄ + H₂O.
- Oxymercuration/demercuration (Markovnikov, no rearrangement, Chapter 8): Hg(OAc)₂ + H₂O, then NaBH₄.
- Hydroboration/oxidation (anti-Markovnikov, Chapter 8): BH₃ then H₂O₂/HO⁻.

**From alkyl halides:** SN2 with hydroxide (Chapter 11). Limited to primary and secondary halides, since tertiary go E2 instead.

**From carbonyl compounds (Chapter 19, looking ahead):**
- Reduction of aldehydes and ketones with NaBH₄ or LiAlH₄ → primary or secondary alcohols.
- Addition of Grignard reagents to aldehydes, ketones, and esters → primary, secondary, or tertiary alcohols.
- Addition of organolithium reagents likewise.

**From epoxides:** Ring-opening with nucleophiles.

A typical synthesis question gives you a target alcohol and asks you to design a sequence. The toolkit is large; the strategy depends on what other functional groups need to be present in the product.

## Reactions of alcohols

Alcohols are a hub functional group. Many synthetic operations either make or use alcohols. The major reaction classes:

### Acid-base chemistry

Generating alkoxides:
- Strong base (NaH, NaNH₂, or alkali metal): R–OH + Na → R–ONa + ½ H₂ (vigorous, often violent for Na in higher alcohols).
- For phenols, NaOH is sufficient.

Alkoxides are nucleophilic and basic. They participate in:
- *Williamson ether synthesis*: alkoxide + primary alkyl halide → ether (SN2 mechanism). RO⁻ + R′X → R–O–R′. Limited to primary halides because secondary or tertiary substrates undergo E2 elimination instead.
- Alkoxides as bases in eliminations and other reactions.

### Conversion to alkyl halides

R–OH + HX → R–X + H₂O. Mechanism depends on the substrate.

For *tertiary alcohols*: the protonation gives a tertiary carbocation, and X⁻ captures it (SN1). Works at room temperature with HCl, HBr, or HI.

For *primary or secondary alcohols*: HX alone is sluggish. Better reagents include:
- SOCl₂: clean conversion to alkyl chloride, byproducts SO₂ and HCl. Mechanism via a chlorosulfite intermediate.
- PBr₃: clean conversion to alkyl bromide.
- TsCl + base: converts OH to a tosylate, which is then displaced by halide in a separate SN2 step.

Stereochemistry varies: SN1 routes scramble (cation intermediate), SOCl₂ has its own intricate stereochemistry (often retention), tosylate routes go through clean SN2 with inversion.

### Dehydration to alkenes

R–CH(OH)–CH₂–R′ + H⁺ (catalyst) → R–CH=CH–R′ + H₂O.

Mechanism: protonation of OH gives a good leaving group (H₂O); loss of water forms a carbocation; a β-proton is then lost to give the alkene. This is an E1 mechanism for tertiary alcohols.

Strong acid (concentrated H₂SO₄ or H₃PO₄) and heat are typical conditions. Tertiary alcohols dehydrate at lower temperatures than secondary, which dehydrate at lower temperatures than primary. (Primary alcohols are reluctant to dehydrate by E1 because primary carbocations are too unstable; under harsh enough conditions they will dehydrate, often via E2-like mechanisms or rearranged cations.)

The product alkene is typically the more substituted one (Zaitsev's rule). Rearrangements are common when the initial cation is less stable than a possible rearranged cation — hydrides or alkyl groups migrate to give the more substituted product.

### Oxidation

Alcohols are oxidized to carbonyl compounds (Chapter 19).

- **Primary alcohols** → aldehydes (RCH₂OH → RCHO) → carboxylic acids (RCHO → RCOOH). Stopping at the aldehyde stage requires a controlled reagent like *PCC* (pyridinium chlorochromate); going through to the acid uses stronger oxidants like CrO₃ in aqueous acid, or KMnO₄.

- **Secondary alcohols** → ketones (R₂CHOH → R₂CO). Many oxidants work: PCC, CrO₃, Na₂Cr₂O₇, KMnO₄.

- **Tertiary alcohols** are generally not oxidized (no H to lose at the OH-bearing carbon). Oxidants that do something to tertiary alcohols typically degrade the molecule.

The mechanism of chromium-based oxidation: a chromate ester forms first (ROH + CrO₃ → ROCrO₃H, with a leaving-group oxygen on chromium); the C–H bond on the OH-bearing carbon is then lost simultaneously with the C–OCrO₃H bond breaking, in an E2-like process. The chromium is reduced (Cr(VI) → Cr(IV) → Cr(III)), and the carbon goes up in oxidation state.

Modern alternatives include the *Swern oxidation* (DMSO + oxalyl chloride + base) and the *Dess-Martin periodinane*, both of which give cleaner reactions than chromate-based oxidations and avoid the heavy metal contamination.

### Reactions specific to phenols

Phenols can:
- Undergo electrophilic aromatic substitution at ortho/para positions (Chapter 16). The OH is a strong activator and a strong ortho/para-director, so phenols react with electrophiles much faster than benzene.
- Be oxidized to *quinones* — six-membered rings with two C=O groups in a 1,4 relationship (or 1,2 for ortho-quinones). Quinones are important in biological electron-transport chains (coenzyme Q is a substituted quinone), in dyes, and in some industrial processes.
- Be deprotonated by hydroxide and used as nucleophiles in ester or ether syntheses.

The resonance argument for phenoxide stability also applies to other resonance-stabilized phenolic anions, including those used in food preservatives (like BHT, butylated hydroxytoluene) where the phenol acts as a radical scavenger by donating an H to neutralize a chain-propagating radical.

## Diols

Diols (two OH groups in one molecule) are made by:
- Dihydroxylation of alkenes (KMnO₄ cold/dilute, or OsO₄ — Chapter 8). Gives cis-1,2-diols.
- Hydrolysis of an epoxide (which itself is made by mCPBA epoxidation of an alkene). Gives trans-1,2-diols.

Diols are useful as:
- Solvents (ethylene glycol = 1,2-ethanediol, antifreeze).
- Polymer precursors (ethylene glycol + terephthalic acid → polyethylene terephthalate, the polymer of soda bottles).
- Protecting groups for ketones and aldehydes (acetal formation, Chapter 19).

A 1,2-diol can be cleaved to give two carbonyl compounds with HIO₄ (periodic acid) or Pb(OAc)₄ (lead tetraacetate). The reaction is analogous to ozonolysis: it splits the C–C bond between the two OH's. Each carbon goes from C–OH to C=O.

## What this chapter does

Alcohols (R–OH on sp³ C) and phenols (Ar–OH) are the most-produced functional group class in industrial chemistry. Their structure is a polarized O–H bond, with hydrogen-bonding capability that elevates their boiling points and makes lower alcohols miscible with water.

Alcohols have pKa around 16; phenols pKa around 10 (a million-fold more acidic, due to resonance stabilization of the phenoxide anion). Substituents shift these pKa values predictably — electron-withdrawing groups make alcohols and phenols more acidic, electron-donating groups less.

Alcohols are made from alkenes (hydration in three flavors), from alkyl halides (SN2 with HO⁻), and from carbonyl compounds (reduction or Grignard addition, Chapter 19). They're consumed by:
- Conversion to halides (HX, SOCl₂, PBr₃, TsCl).
- Williamson ether synthesis (alkoxide + RX → ether).
- Dehydration to alkenes (acid + heat).
- Oxidation to aldehydes, ketones, or carboxylic acids.
- And in special cases (phenols), electrophilic aromatic substitution and oxidation to quinones.

Diols and triols extend the chemistry, giving cleavable C–C bonds (with periodate) and useful protecting groups.

Almost every multi-step synthesis you'll meet involves an alcohol at some stage — either as a starting material, as an intermediate, or as the final functional group of the target. The next chapter (18) handles ethers and epoxides, which are alcohols with the H replaced by an alkyl group, plus thiols and sulfides (the sulfur analogs).
