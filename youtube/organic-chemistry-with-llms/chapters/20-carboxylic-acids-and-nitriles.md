# Chapter 20 — Carboxylic Acids and Nitriles

*A carboxylic acid sits at the highest oxidation state of carbon you'll meet in everyday organic chemistry. Once a carbon is at the level of CO₂H, almost every direction is downhill — and that downhill chemistry is what builds esters, amides, peptides, and most of the rest of biological organic matter.*

The name "carboxylic acid" comes from "carbonyl + hydroxyl" — it's a functional group with both a C=O and a C–OH on the same carbon. The combination produces a chemistry that is genuinely different from either parent functional group alone. Carboxylic acids are far more acidic than alcohols (pKa ≈ 5 vs. 16). They form unusually strong hydrogen-bonded dimers in solution. Their reactions are dominated by *nucleophilic acyl substitution* — a different mechanism from the nucleophilic addition we just saw for aldehydes and ketones.

This chapter covers carboxylic acids and their close cousins, the nitriles (R–C≡N). The nitrile carbon is at the same oxidation level as a carboxylic acid carbon: both have three bonds to electronegative atoms (a CO₂H has two C–O bonds plus one to OH; a CN has three C–N bonds via the triple bond). Hydrolysis of a nitrile gives a carboxylic acid, and many other transformations can interconvert them.

The next chapter (21) covers the *carboxylic acid derivatives* — esters, amides, acid chlorides, anhydrides — which are formed by replacing the OH of a carboxylic acid with a different group. Together, Chapters 20 and 21 cover the entire chemistry of "carbonyl with a leaving group attached."

## Structure and properties

A carboxylic acid is R–CO₂H, where the central carbon is sp² hybridized, with three coplanar bonds (to R, to =O, to –OH). Both C–O bonds are shorter than a typical C–O single bond — about 130 pm for the C–O of the OH, and 120 pm for the C=O. This is partial double-bond character on both — resonance contributes a charge-separated structure with the negative on the C=O oxygen and the positive on the OH oxygen.

Hydrogen bonding is intense in carboxylic acids. They form *cyclic dimers* in pure liquid (and even in dilute solution) — two molecules face-to-face, with each one's OH hydrogen-bonded to the other's C=O oxygen. The dimer has two strong H-bonds, which roughly double the apparent molecular weight from the perspective of intermolecular interactions. The result is unusually high boiling points: acetic acid boils at 118 °C, compared to 79 °C for ethanol (similar molecular weight, but with only one H-bond per molecule).

## Naming

The IUPAC suffix is *-oic acid*, with the carboxyl carbon as C1. So:
- Methanoic acid (formic acid, HCOOH)
- Ethanoic acid (acetic acid, CH₃COOH)
- Propanoic acid (CH₃CH₂COOH)
- Hexanoic acid (the 6-carbon caprylic acid)

For acids attached to rings, the parent is named with -*carboxylic acid*: cyclohexanecarboxylic acid.

Common names persist for many simple acids: formic (from Latin *formica*, ant — formic acid is in ant venom), acetic (from *acetum*, vinegar), butyric (from *butyrum*, butter — found in rancid butter), valeric (from valerian root), caproic (from *caper*, goat — found in goat milk fat), and so on.

The IUPAC system handles dicarboxylic acids with the suffix *-dioic acid*: hexanedioic acid (adipic acid, used to make nylon-6,6). Tricarboxylic acids: citric acid (2-hydroxy-1,2,3-propanetricarboxylic acid). Aromatic acids: benzoic acid (PhCOOH), terephthalic acid (1,4-benzenedicarboxylic acid, used to make polyester).

## Acidity

Carboxylic acids are weakly acidic — pKa about 5 — much more acidic than alcohols (pKa ≈ 16) or phenols (pKa ≈ 10). The reason is in the conjugate base.

The carboxylate anion (R–CO₂⁻) is stabilized by resonance: the negative charge can sit on either oxygen, with the C–O bonds becoming equivalent in the hybrid. There are two equivalent contributing resonance structures. The full negative charge is spread evenly over both oxygens. The carboxylate is thus much more stable than an alkoxide (which has the negative on a single O with no resonance stabilization).

The energetic effect is large — about 40 kJ/mol of stabilization (roughly 7 pKa units worth). This explains the 11 pKa-unit difference between an acid (5) and an alcohol (16).

Substituents shift carboxylic acid acidity:
- Electron-withdrawing groups (especially when α to the carbonyl) increase acidity. Trichloroacetic acid (Cl₃CCOOH) has pKa 0.7, much more acidic than acetic acid (pKa 4.76). The three Cl groups stabilize the carboxylate through induction.
- Electron-donating groups decrease acidity slightly (alkyl substitution).
- Aromatic substitution on benzoic acid: electron-withdrawing groups (NO₂, halides) increase acidity; electron-donating groups (OMe, NH₂) decrease it. The pattern follows the electronic logic for the carboxylate resonance structure.

## Preparing carboxylic acids

Several common routes:

**Oxidation of alcohols.** Primary alcohols → aldehydes → carboxylic acids, using strong oxidants (CrO₃ in aqueous acid, KMnO₄, H₂CrO₄). The aldehyde is an intermediate; under standard oxidation conditions it goes through to the acid because the aldehyde gem-diol (from hydration) is the actual oxidation substrate.

**Oxidation of alkylbenzenes.** A toluene (or any alkyl-substituted benzene) is oxidized at the benzylic position by KMnO₄ or other strong oxidants to give a benzoic acid (or substituted benzoic acid). The alkyl group is degraded to a CO₂H, regardless of how long it was originally.

**Hydrolysis of nitriles.** RCN + H₂O (acid or base) → RCOOH. The mechanism goes through an amide intermediate: RCN → RCONH₂ → RCOOH.

**Carboxylation of Grignard reagents.** R–MgX + CO₂ → R–COO⁻ MgX⁺ → (after acid workup) R–COOH. Because Grignards are easily made from alkyl halides, this gives a one-carbon-extension method for going from R–X to R–COOH. The CO₂ is added as gas or solid (dry ice).

## Reactions of carboxylic acids

Carboxylic acids react in three main ways:

1. As Brønsted acids — proton transfer to bases.
2. As electrophiles at the carbonyl carbon — but only via nucleophilic acyl substitution, where the OH (or its protonated form, water) is the leaving group.
3. As starting materials for making derivatives (esters, amides, acid chlorides, etc.) — Chapter 21.

Acid-base chemistry is straightforward: acids react with bases (NaOH, NaHCO₃, NH₃, amines) to give the salt. Carboxylate salts are typically water-soluble (especially for short-chain acids), even when the parent acid isn't very soluble.

Reaction with base also opens the door to carboxylate as a *nucleophile*. Carboxylate ions can attack alkyl halides (SN2) to give esters. They can also do alkylations of α-positions (but usually that's done after esterification — Chapter 22).

## Direct conversions of carboxylic acids

To make an ester or amide, a direct route is:

**Fischer esterification.** R–COOH + R'–OH + acid catalyst → R–CO₂–R' + H₂O. Reversible. The reaction proceeds through protonation of the carbonyl oxygen, attack by the alcohol, proton transfer, loss of water, deprotonation. To drive the equilibrium toward the ester, remove water (Dean-Stark, drying agent) or use excess of one reagent.

**Direct amidation.** R–COOH + R'–NH₂ → R–CONH–R' + H₂O. Not generally favorable at room temperature because the amine first protonates the acid (the salt has no nucleophilic amine and no electrophilic carbonyl), so it sits as the salt and doesn't react. To get amide formation directly, you typically need very high temperature (160 °C+) or a coupling reagent like DCC (dicyclohexylcarbodiimide) that activates the acid.

In practice, most lab syntheses go *via* an acid chloride or activated ester rather than directly from the carboxylic acid. The acid is first converted to a more reactive intermediate (Chapter 21) which then reacts cleanly with the desired nucleophile.

## Reduction of carboxylic acids

LiAlH₄ reduces carboxylic acids to primary alcohols. The intermediate is an aldehyde, which is further reduced under the reaction conditions to give the alcohol. The net result: R–COOH → R–CH₂–OH.

NaBH₄ does *not* reduce carboxylic acids efficiently — it's not strong enough to reduce the high-oxidation-state carbonyl that's already partially deprotonated. Use LiAlH₄ when you need to reduce an acid; use NaBH₄ when you specifically want to *not* reduce an acid present in a substrate.

Borane (BH₃·THF) is selective for carboxylic acids — it reduces them but doesn't reduce esters or other carbonyl groups. Useful when an acid needs to be reduced in the presence of an ester elsewhere in the molecule.

## Decarboxylation

Special carboxylic acids — those with a substituent two carbons away from the COOH that can stabilize the resulting carbanion — undergo *decarboxylation* on heating, losing CO₂ and giving the substituent's carbon as a new C–H.

Examples include β-ketoacids (R-CO-CH₂-COOH → R-CO-CH₃ + CO₂) and malonic acid derivatives (HOOC-CH₂-COOH → CH₃-COOH on heating). The driving force is the stability of the carbanion intermediate, which is stabilized by the adjacent carbonyl.

This is the basis of the *malonic ester synthesis* — a method for making substituted carboxylic acids by alkylating diethyl malonate (CH₂(CO₂Et)₂), saponifying to the dicarboxylic acid, and decarboxylating one of the COOHs to give the substituted acid (Chapter 22).

## Nitriles

A nitrile (R–C≡N) is a triple bond between carbon and nitrogen. The carbon is sp, the bond is short and strong, and the carbon has substantial δ⁺ character because nitrogen is electronegative.

Naming: -*nitrile* suffix on the parent chain (the nitrile carbon is C1). So acetonitrile (ethanenitrile, CH₃CN), propanenitrile (CH₃CH₂CN), benzonitrile (PhCN).

Nitriles are made by:
- SN2 of an alkyl halide with cyanide ion (CN⁻). R–X + NaCN → R–CN + NaX. Standard procedure for making nitriles in the lab.
- Dehydration of amides: R–CONH₂ → R–CN with strong dehydrating agents (P₂O₅, SOCl₂, POCl₃).

Nitriles undergo nucleophilic additions at the C of the C≡N, similar to (but not identical to) the carbonyl chemistry we've seen.

**Hydrolysis.** R–CN + H₂O (acid or base) → R–COOH (via the amide intermediate R–CONH₂). The full hydrolysis sequence gives the carboxylic acid; you can stop at the amide if you control the conditions carefully.

**Reduction.** LiAlH₄ reduces a nitrile all the way to a primary amine: R–CN → R–CH₂–NH₂. The mechanism goes through an imine intermediate. DIBAL-H (diisobutylaluminum hydride) at low temperature can stop at the imine, which then hydrolyzes to give an aldehyde — a useful aldehyde synthesis.

**Grignard or organolithium addition.** R–CN + R'–MgX → R–C(=N–MgX)–R' (an iminium intermediate), which on aqueous workup gives R–C(=O)–R' (a ketone via hydrolysis of the iminium). This is a useful ketone synthesis.

## Spectroscopy

Carboxylic acids: distinctive IR bands at 2500–3300 cm⁻¹ (broad O–H, often very broad due to dimer H-bonding) and 1700–1725 cm⁻¹ (C=O). The combination is diagnostic — broad O–H plus carbonyl in the right region = carboxylic acid.

¹H NMR: the COOH proton appears at δ 10–12, very downfield. The chemical shift depends on concentration (because of H-bonding with neighboring molecules) and conditions, but is unmistakable.

¹³C NMR: carbonyl carbon at δ 170–185.

Nitriles: IR band at 2200–2260 cm⁻¹ (sharp, medium to strong, distinctive — only triple-bonded C≡N or C≡C absorb in this region). ¹H NMR shows nothing distinctive (the nitrile carbon has no H). ¹³C NMR: nitrile carbon at δ 110–120.

## What this chapter does

Carboxylic acids (R-CO₂H) are weakly acidic (pKa ≈ 5) due to resonance stabilization of the carboxylate anion. They form strong hydrogen-bonded dimers, giving them unusually high boiling points relative to their molecular weight.

Carboxylic acids are made by oxidation of primary alcohols, oxidation of alkylbenzenes, hydrolysis of nitriles, and carboxylation of Grignard reagents.

Their reactions include:
- Acid-base chemistry (deprotonation by NaOH, NaHCO₃, amines).
- Nucleophilic acyl substitution to form derivatives (esters, amides, acid chlorides, anhydrides — Chapter 21).
- Reduction by LiAlH₄ to give primary alcohols (NaBH₄ doesn't reduce acids).
- Decarboxylation (for β-ketoacids and similar substrates).

Nitriles (R-C≡N) are at the same oxidation level as carboxylic acids. They're made by SN2 of cyanide on alkyl halides, or by amide dehydration. They undergo:
- Hydrolysis to carboxylic acids (via amide).
- Reduction to amines (LiAlH₄) or aldehydes (DIBAL at low T).
- Grignard or organolithium addition to give ketones.

The next chapter (21) takes the carboxylic acid as starting material and surveys the four major derivatives: esters, amides, acid chlorides, and anhydrides. The unifying mechanism is *nucleophilic acyl substitution* — in which a nucleophile attacks the carbonyl, and a leaving group departs to give a new carbonyl-containing product.
