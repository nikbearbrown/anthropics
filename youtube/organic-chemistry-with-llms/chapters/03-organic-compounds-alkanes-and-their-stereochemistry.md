# Chapter 3 — Organic Compounds: Alkanes and Their Stereochemistry

*Alkanes are the boring chapter on purpose — the unreactive backbone everything interesting hangs off of. Get comfortable here, because the conformations and the naming you learn are the same ones you'll use in every chapter that follows.*

There are 75 different alkanes with the molecular formula C₁₀H₂₂. Seventy-five different molecules, all carbon and hydrogen, all with exactly the same atoms in the same numbers, distinguished only by the order in which the atoms are connected. Some are straight chains, some branch once, some branch many times. Some have boiling points around 170 °C, others closer to 100 °C. Each is a real compound that has been isolated, purified, and characterized.

This is the first thing to understand about organic chemistry: connectivity matters. The same handful of atoms (C, H, N, O, S, halogens) reassembled in different orders gives you essentially every molecule of biological or industrial interest. Methane and 100-million-carbon DNA are made from the same toolkit. Glucose and fructose have identical molecular formulas (C₆H₁₂O₆) and behave completely differently. The only thing that makes one a sugar your body can metabolize easily and the other a sugar that needs separate enzymatic machinery is the order of bonds.

The simplest way into this is the alkanes — the family of carbon–hydrogen compounds with only single bonds. Alkanes have almost no chemistry to speak of. They burn (every fuel from natural gas to gasoline is mostly alkanes), and that's about it. They don't react with acids, bases, oxidants, reductants, nucleophiles, or electrophiles under normal conditions. The C–C and C–H bonds are too strong and too nonpolar for anything useful to happen.

But alkanes are exactly where you learn the foundations: how to name molecules systematically, how to recognize structural isomers, how to think about the three-dimensional shapes molecules adopt when single bonds rotate. The naming system you learn here applies to every other functional group in the book. The conformational arguments you learn here will reappear in cyclohexane chemistry (Chapter 4), in stereochemistry (Chapter 5), in elimination reactions (Chapter 11), and again in any molecule large enough that its shape matters.

## What functional groups do, and what they don't

Before we settle into alkanes, one organizing idea. Almost all organic chemistry is the chemistry of *functional groups* — small, recurring arrangements of atoms (C=O, OH, NH₂, COOH, halides, etc.) that determine how a molecule reacts. The carbon-hydrogen scaffolding mostly doesn't react. The functional group does.

This is why we can talk about "the chemistry of alcohols" or "the chemistry of carboxylic acids" without specifying the rest of the molecule. Methanol (CH₃OH) and 1-octanol (CH₃(CH₂)₇OH) and cholesterol (a steroid alcohol with 27 carbons) all undergo the same kinds of reactions at their –OH group, in the same way, by the same mechanisms. The hydrocarbon backbone is along for the ride.

A handful of common functional groups, together with their suffixes in IUPAC nomenclature:

| Group | Structure | Suffix | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| alkene | C=C | -ene | ethene (ethylene) |
| alkyne | C≡C | -yne | ethyne (acetylene) |
| alcohol | –OH | -ol | ethanol |
| amine | –NH₂ | -amine | methanamine |
| aldehyde | –CHO | -al | ethanal |
| ketone | C(=O)– | -one | propanone (acetone) |
| carboxylic acid | –COOH | -oic acid | ethanoic acid (acetic acid) |
| ester | –CO₂R | -oate | methyl ethanoate |
| amide | –C(=O)NR₂ | -amide | ethanamide |
| nitrile | –C≡N | -nitrile | ethanenitrile |
| halide | –X | none | chloromethane |
| thiol | –SH | -thiol | methanethiol |
| ether | –O– | none | dimethyl ether |

Most of the chapters in this book pick one or two of these and unpack their chemistry. The functional group is the protagonist. The alkane backbone is the stage.

Within the alkane family, three structural features deserve attention because they recur everywhere: chain length, branching, and three-dimensional shape.

## Naming alkanes

Methane, ethane, propane, butane — the first four alkanes have historical names, used everywhere and worth memorizing. Beyond that, IUPAC nomenclature uses Greek-derived prefixes for the chain length:

| Carbons | Prefix | Alkane |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | meth- | methane |
| 2 | eth- | ethane |
| 3 | prop- | propane |
| 4 | but- | butane |
| 5 | pent- | pentane |
| 6 | hex- | hexane |
| 7 | hept- | heptane |
| 8 | oct- | octane |
| 9 | non- | nonane |
| 10 | dec- | decane |

These prefixes show up in the naming of every other functional group. Pentanoic acid is the five-carbon carboxylic acid. Hexanal is the six-carbon aldehyde. The numerical prefix counts the carbons in the *parent chain* (the longest continuous chain in the molecule), and the suffix names the functional group.

Branched alkanes are named by:

1. Find the longest continuous chain of carbons. That's the parent.
2. Number the parent chain from the end that gives substituents the lowest possible numbers.
3. Identify each substituent (each branch) by its own alkane prefix plus the suffix *-yl* (so a methyl group is –CH₃, an ethyl group is –CH₂CH₃, and so on).
4. Assemble the name as: position-of-substituent + name-of-substituent + parent + suffix.

A worked example. Take this skeletal structure: a five-carbon main chain with a methyl group on the second carbon. Longest chain: 5 carbons → pentane. Numbering: starting from the end nearest the methyl group, the methyl is at position 2 (rather than position 4 from the other end). Substituent: methyl. Name: 2-methylpentane.

Another. Six-carbon main chain, with two methyl groups, one on carbon 2 and one on carbon 3. Name: 2,3-dimethylhexane. The "di" prefix indicates two identical substituents.

A third. Four-carbon chain with a methyl on carbon 2. Wait — is that the longest chain? Drawing it carefully shows the longest chain is still four (butane). Methyl on carbon 2: 2-methylbutane. (Some chemists still use the older name *isopentane* for this, and one carbon shorter — 2-methylpropane — is *isobutane*. The trivial names "iso-" "neo-" "sec-" and "tert-" appear often enough to be worth recognizing, but IUPAC names are unambiguous and preferred.)

A handful of common substituent names you'll see often:

| Substituent | Structure | Common name |
|---|---|---|
| methyl | –CH₃ | – |
| ethyl | –CH₂CH₃ | – |
| propyl | –CH₂CH₂CH₃ | n-propyl |
| isopropyl | –CH(CH₃)₂ | – |
| butyl | –CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃ | n-butyl |
| sec-butyl | –CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃ | sec-butyl |
| isobutyl | –CH₂CH(CH₃)₂ | isobutyl |
| tert-butyl | –C(CH₃)₃ | tert-butyl |

## Constitutional isomers, and what changes between them

Two molecules that have the same molecular formula but different connectivity are *constitutional isomers* (sometimes called *structural isomers*). Butane and isobutane are constitutional isomers of C₄H₁₀ — same atoms, different bonds.

The number of constitutional isomers grows fast with chain length. Methane through propane each have only one isomer (no choice in how to connect the atoms). Butane has two. Pentane has three. The growth is approximately exponential:

| Carbons | Number of constitutional isomers |
|---|---|
| 1 | 1 |
| 2 | 1 |
| 3 | 1 |
| 4 | 2 |
| 5 | 3 |
| 6 | 5 |
| 7 | 9 |
| 8 | 18 |
| 9 | 35 |
| 10 | 75 |
| 15 | 4,347 |
| 20 | 366,319 |
| 30 | 4 × 10⁹ |

The combinatorial explosion is one reason why organic chemistry is such a big field. Even just for hydrocarbons, the number of possible structures grows faster than you can practically enumerate, and once you start adding heteroatoms (oxygen, nitrogen, halogens), each one can sit on different positions and create new isomers.

Constitutional isomers are different compounds. They have different boiling points, different melting points, different densities, often different colors, often different reactivities. The shape and arrangement of atoms determines all the macroscopic properties.

A simple lesson from the alkane data: branching lowers the boiling point. Pentane (no branches) boils at 36.1 °C. Isopentane (2-methylbutane, one branch) boils at 27.85 °C. Neopentane (2,2-dimethylpropane, two branches) boils at 9.5 °C. Why? Boiling point is determined by intermolecular forces. For nonpolar alkanes, those forces are dispersion (van der Waals) interactions, which depend on how much surface area neighboring molecules can present to each other. A straight chain stretches out and packs efficiently. A branched isomer is more compact and presents less surface — neighbors don't get as much grip on each other. Less grip means lower boiling point.

This is the first instance of a recurring theme: structure dictates physical properties. Chapter by chapter, we'll see structure dictate reactivity too.

## Carbon classification: 1°, 2°, 3°, 4°

Chemists classify carbons by how many other carbons they're bonded to:

- *Primary (1°)*: bonded to one other carbon
- *Secondary (2°)*: bonded to two other carbons
- *Tertiary (3°)*: bonded to three other carbons
- *Quaternary (4°)*: bonded to four other carbons

Hydrogens are classified by the carbon they're attached to: a hydrogen on a primary carbon is a "primary hydrogen," and so on. Quaternary carbons obviously have no hydrogens.

This classification matters because the chemistry at a primary carbon often differs from the chemistry at a tertiary carbon. The SN1 vs. SN2 split in Chapter 11 is the cleanest example: primary substrates react by SN2, tertiary substrates react by SN1, and the difference is partly steric (sterics matter most at primary vs. tertiary) and partly electronic (carbocation stability is highest for tertiary). Naming functional groups also uses this classification: primary alcohols, secondary alcohols, tertiary alcohols all have different reactivity patterns.

## Conformations: rotation around single bonds

The single bond in ethane (C–C in CH₃CH₃) is a sigma bond — head-on overlap of two sp³ orbitals. The bond is cylindrically symmetric around the bond axis. That symmetry has a structural consequence: the two methyl groups can rotate relative to each other around the C–C bond without breaking the bond.

But not all rotational positions are equally favorable. As one CH₃ rotates relative to the other, the three C–H bonds on one carbon move past the three C–H bonds on the other. There are two extreme arrangements.

In the *staggered* conformation, the C–H bonds on the front carbon are exactly between the C–H bonds on the back carbon, when viewed down the C–C bond. The hydrogens are 60° apart.

In the *eclipsed* conformation, each front C–H is directly aligned with a back C–H, when viewed down the C–C bond. The hydrogens are 0° apart (at least in projection).

The eclipsed conformation is higher in energy — by about 12 kJ/mol (2.9 kcal/mol) for ethane. The reason is *torsional strain*: the bonding electrons of the eclipsed C–H bonds repel each other when forced into close proximity. There are three pairs of eclipsed C–H bonds in ethane's eclipsed conformation, so the strain works out to about 4.0 kJ/mol per H–H eclipsing interaction. (This number is not literal-physical-truth for a single interaction in isolation, but it works as a useful additive estimate.)

We draw conformations using *Newman projections*. A Newman projection looks down the C–C bond axis. The front carbon is shown as a point at the center; its three bonds extend out at 120° intervals. The back carbon is shown as a circle; its three bonds extend from the edge of the circle. By comparing the angles between front and back bonds, you can see whether the conformation is staggered (bonds offset by 60°) or eclipsed (bonds aligned at 0°).

For ethane, all rotational positions are either staggered or eclipsed; there are no other distinguishable conformations. The energy as a function of rotation angle (the *dihedral angle* or *torsional angle* between the front and back C–H bonds) is a sinusoid: minima at 60°, 180°, 300° (all staggered), maxima at 0°, 120°, 240° (all eclipsed).

For propane, the same picture, slightly modified: one C–H on the front carbon is replaced by a CH₃, but the analysis is the same — staggered minima, eclipsed maxima. The eclipsing interactions are now CH₃ ↔ H instead of three H ↔ H, and the energy barrier is slightly higher (14 kJ/mol vs. 12 for ethane). From this we can extract a value for the CH₃–H eclipsing interaction: 14 − 2(4.0) = 6.0 kJ/mol per CH₃ ↔ H interaction.

For butane (CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃), the conformational analysis around the central C–C bond is more interesting. The two ends of butane are now CH₃ groups, not Hs, and rotating around the central bond doesn't just swap H ↔ H eclipsing for CH₃ ↔ H — it can produce CH₃ ↔ CH₃ eclipsing in certain rotational positions.

Specifically, butane has four named conformations as you rotate from 0° to 360°:

- *anti* (180°): the two methyls 180° apart, all hydrogens staggered. The lowest energy conformation. Used as the reference (ΔE = 0).
- *gauche* (60° and 300°): the two methyls 60° apart, staggered. About 3.8 kJ/mol above anti — the cost of bringing two methyls relatively close, even staggered. This is *steric strain*, distinct from torsional strain.
- *eclipsed (CH₃-H)* (120° and 240°): methyl eclipsed with hydrogen. About 16 kJ/mol above anti.
- *eclipsed (CH₃-CH₃)* (0°): two methyls fully eclipsed. About 19 kJ/mol above anti — the highest energy conformation.

From the gauche-vs-anti energy gap (3.8 kJ/mol) you can extract a value for the gauche interaction: about 3.8 kJ/mol per pair of gauche methyls. This is the energy cost of having two methyls 60° apart, both still staggered. From the fully eclipsed CH₃ ↔ CH₃ value (19 kJ/mol total minus 2 × 4.0 for the two H ↔ H eclipsings), about 11 kJ/mol per CH₃ ↔ CH₃ eclipsing interaction.

These additive values let you predict the conformational behavior of any open-chain alkane. They also become essential in the next chapter, when we start putting carbons into rings and find that the same kinds of strain control which ring conformations are stable.

## Why alkanes barely react

To close the chapter, the most important single fact about alkane reactivity: there isn't much.

Alkane C–C bonds are about 350 kJ/mol. Alkane C–H bonds are about 410 kJ/mol. Both are very strong. Both are essentially nonpolar (carbon and hydrogen have nearly equal electronegativities). There's no electron-rich site for an electrophile to attack. There's no electron-poor site for a nucleophile to attack. There's no leaving group ready to depart.

The one reaction alkanes do at industrial scale is *combustion*: alkanes plus O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O + heat. The methane example:

$$\text{CH}_4 + 2\text{O}_2 \longrightarrow \text{CO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + 890 \text{ kJ/mol}$$

The reaction is wildly exothermic. It's also kinetically slow at room temperature (you can store methane in a tank, mixed with air, indefinitely) until you supply enough activation energy with a spark or a flame. Once started, the chain propagates through radical intermediates that consume the alkane and the oxygen rapidly until one runs out. Combustion is what powers most of human civilization — heating, electricity generation, transportation — and it's all alkane chemistry.

Alkanes will also react with halogens (Cl₂, Br₂) in the presence of light or heat, in a radical chain mechanism (Chapter 6 and 10). And they undergo industrial cracking and reforming reactions at high temperature with catalysts. Beyond those few examples, alkanes are spectators in most of organic chemistry.

So why study them? Two reasons. First, the IUPAC naming system was built around alkane skeletons; learning to name alkanes is learning to name everything. Second, the conformational analysis you do for alkanes — counting eclipsing interactions, identifying gauche vs. anti relationships, predicting which conformer dominates — is the same analysis you'll do for cyclohexanes, for elimination transition states, for cyclic and acyclic stereochemistry, and for biological macromolecules. It's the apparatus for thinking about molecular shape.

## What this chapter does

Alkanes are inert. That fact, by itself, isn't very interesting. What's interesting is that alkanes are the scaffolding for everything else. The naming system (parent chain, substituents, position numbers) extends to every other functional group. The classification (primary, secondary, tertiary, quaternary) controls reactivity in functional-group chemistry. The conformational analysis (staggered, eclipsed, gauche, anti) is what cyclohexanes, eliminations, and stereochemistry all rely on.

Once you can take a structure, name it, identify its constitutional isomers, classify each carbon, and predict its lowest-energy conformation, you've internalized the apparatus. The functional groups can now hang off the scaffolding, and each one becomes its own short story.

The next chapter takes the conformational analysis from open-chain alkanes to rings. The same energetic accounting (torsional strain, steric strain) applies, but the geometry is constrained by the ring closure, and the result is a set of preferred ring conformations that explain a remarkable amount of chemistry.
