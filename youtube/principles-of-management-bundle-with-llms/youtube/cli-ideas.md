## Candidate 01 — Build a Motivation Philosophy Doc for a Fictional Startup with Claude
- Source: principles-of-management-bundle-with-llms/chapters/07-work-motivation-for-performance.md + LLM Exercise (Founder Handbook project)
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: Most startup motivation systems are either "passion substitutes for pay" or "equity vesting as a retention threat" — and the chapter names both as the myths founders are most likely to fall into. Claude builds the handbook section that forbids them.
- The artifact: a synthesized ~1,200-word "Motivation Philosophy" section for a fictional startup, structured with four subsections (content reading, expectancy-theory process design, goal-setting cadence, myth correction) plus a "What this philosophy explicitly forbids" closer — rendered as a Remotion animated document reveal, paragraph by paragraph.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Build a 'Motivation Philosophy' section for a 12-person B2B SaaS startup operating handbook. Use Herzberg's two-factor model to separate hygiene factors (base pay at market, reliable tech stack, clear job scope) from motivators (ownership of outcomes, visible impact). Apply Vroom's expectancy theory to our primary behavior: shipping features on time — walk through whether engineers believe (a) their effort will produce working code, (b) working code will produce a positive review, (c) positive review will produce career growth. Then specify our OKR cadence (quarterly, bottom-up with founder approval, difficult-but-attainable, feedback every 2 weeks). End with 200 words on what this philosophy explicitly forbids."`
- Read / check: Verify the two-factor model is correctly applied (hygiene factors prevent dissatisfaction but do not produce motivation; motivators produce engagement but only after hygiene needs are met); verify the expectancy chain has all three links (E: effort→performance, I: performance→reward, V: reward is valued); verify the OKR cadence specifics are internally consistent (quarterly cadence + biweekly feedback is compatible; daily feedback + annual OKRs is not).
- Human supplies (Claude can't): The fictional company's specific context (industry, role mix, compensation structure) must be provided by the human — Claude generates a generic startup if the context is absent. **A more compelling video uses a real company the human has access to**, with real compensation bands and actual team structure. The synthetic version is labeled as illustrative. Nothing requires external data collection.
- Output medium: Remotion animated document reveal (section headers appearing, then paragraphs fading in, with a final "Forbidden Moves" callout box).
- The change: re-run the prompt for a nonprofit organization — same framework, different hygiene/motivator balance (mission replaces equity; autonomy replaces variable pay) — and overlay the two outputs side by side to show how the same theory produces different policies in different contexts.
- Teardown angle: Motivation theory is not HR boilerplate — it is a specification for a system. The "What this forbids" section is the operative discipline: naming the failure modes explicitly before they happen is the difference between a philosophy and a wish list.
- Exclusions: organizational culture measurement, 360-degree feedback design, compensation benchmarking methodology.
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 02 — Build a Performance Appraisal System Design with Claude
- Source: principles-of-management-bundle-with-llms/chapters/08-performance-appraisal-and-rewards.md + LLM Exercise (Founder Handbook)
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: GE's "vitality curve" stack-ranking was abandoned in 2015 because it damaged long-term performance. Claude builds the appraisal system that avoids the same five rater biases that broke GE's — and names the bias explicitly for each countermeasure.
- The artifact: a synthesized "Performance and Rewards" handbook section — appraisal method selection (BARS vs. MBO vs. check-ins), a five-bias countermeasure table, and a total-rewards philosophy — rendered as a Remotion animated decision-tree leading to the method choice, then a bias countermeasure table appearing row by row.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Design a performance appraisal system for a 15-person product team at a Series A startup. Choose between BARS, MBO, and Adobe-style check-ins — defend the choice for this stage. Then build a bias countermeasure table: for each of the five rater biases (halo, recency, central tendency, leniency, confirmatory), name (a) where it's most likely at this company and (b) the structural countermeasure. Then specify the total-rewards philosophy: base pay (market data source?), equity vesting, recognition cadence. State what the company will NOT do — stack-ranking, forced curves, or using ratings as the sole compensation input."`
- Read / check: Verify the appraisal method choice is defended with reasons specific to early-stage companies (BARS has best validity but high cost; check-ins suit fast-moving teams; MBO requires concrete goal infrastructure); verify each countermeasure is structural (process change) not attitudinal (training alone); verify the forbidden list (stack-ranking, forced curves) matches the documented failures from GE and Microsoft cases in the chapter.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): The fictional company's actual role descriptions and current compensation levels must be supplied by the human for a specific output. **For authenticity, real comp band data (from Pave, Levels.fyi, or Radford) makes the total-rewards section credible.** Without it, Claude uses generic frameworks. The human must know their team's actual structure.
- Output medium: Remotion animated decision tree (appraisal method selection) + bias-countermeasure table building row by row.
- The change: add a calibration session design — Claude specifies the agenda, participants, decision rules, and guardrails for the quarterly cross-manager calibration meeting that counteracts central tendency and leniency biases.
- Teardown angle: Appraisal systems fail not because of the method but because of the biases the method doesn't correct for. The countermeasure table makes the failure modes structural — which means they can be designed out rather than trained away.
- Exclusions: compensation survey methodology, executive compensation design, legal compliance for appraisal documentation.
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 03 — Research Leadership Style Fit with Claude
- Source: principles-of-management-bundle-with-llms/chapters/12-leadership.md + LLM Exercise (Founder Handbook)
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: Shackleton's 28 men all survived Antarctica because he matched his leadership behavior to the situation — and the research says the same leader who saves a crisis team destroys a high-performing steady-state team. Claude builds the contingency rules that tell you which style applies when.
- The artifact: a synthesized "Leadership Philosophy" handbook section (founder default style, three situation-contingency rules, leadership pipeline spec, LMX safeguards) — rendered as a Remotion animated decision-matrix: situation → recommended style → specific behavior the founder will perform.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Write the 'Leadership Philosophy' section of an operating handbook for a 20-person B2B SaaS startup. Apply Fiedler's contingency model to identify: (1) the founder's natural leadership style from their Big Five profile [provide: high C, moderate E, high O, moderate A]; (2) which situations call for task-oriented behavior (crisis, new hire onboarding) vs. relationship-oriented (high-performing steady-state team); (3) Hersey-Blanchard situational leadership: which three role types at this company are currently low-readiness (and need telling/coaching) vs. high-readiness (and need delegating). Then write LMX safeguards: how does the founder prevent in-group/out-group asymmetry from becoming structural advantage?"`
- Read / check: Verify the Fiedler style classification is correctly derived from the Big Five profile (high C founders trend task-oriented/transactional; high E trend transformational); verify the situational leadership readiness classifications are role-specific (a senior engineer is high-readiness on technical tasks, possibly low-readiness on cross-functional influence); verify the LMX safeguards are structural (rotation policies, transparent criteria) not just aspirational.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): The founder's actual Big Five profile, or a specific team structure with named roles and readiness levels, must be supplied by the human. **The more specific the context, the less generic the output.** A synthetic "average startup founder" produces useful teaching material but weaker operational advice.
- Output medium: Remotion animated decision matrix (situation → style → specific behavior, rows appearing one by one) + LMX safeguard checklist.
- The change: show what happens when the founder's natural style mismatches the situation — e.g., a high-relationship-oriented founder in a crisis requiring directive behavior — and Claude writes the explicit protocol for overriding default style.
- Teardown angle: There is no one leadership style that works everywhere. The contingency rules are the discipline that prevents a founder from defaulting to their comfort zone in situations where it will fail. Naming the mismatch explicitly before it happens is the operational value.
- Exclusions: leadership assessment tools (StrengthsFinder, MBTI), charismatic leadership theory in depth, organizational politics playbook.
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 04 — Build a Decision Bias Audit with Claude
- Source: principles-of-management-bundle-with-llms/chapters/06-perception-and-managerial-decision-making.md + LLM Exercise
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: The chapter lists nine cognitive biases that corrupt managerial decisions — availability heuristic, escalation of commitment, groupthink, anchoring, framing, representative heuristic, optimism bias, hindsight bias, and satisficing. Claude audits a real decision against all nine.
- The artifact: a synthesized bias audit for a specific managerial decision (e.g., "continue funding a failing product line for another quarter") — a structured table showing which biases are active, the specific evidence of each, and a debiasing protocol — rendered as a Remotion animated table with bias names appearing, evidence populating, and debiasing steps fading in.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Audit this managerial decision for cognitive bias: 'We have invested $2M in our enterprise product line. Q3 revenue was $180K against a $600K target. We are deciding whether to fund another quarter at $500K or pivot to our SMB product.' For each of the nine biases (availability, escalation of commitment, groupthink, anchoring, framing, representative, optimism, hindsight, satisficing): (1) Is this bias active in this decision? Cite the specific evidence from the scenario. (2) What is the debiasing protocol? Use only structural interventions (pre-mortems, reference class forecasting, devil's advocate) — not attitude changes."`
- Read / check: Verify escalation of commitment is identified (the $2M sunk cost is the textbook trigger); verify anchoring is flagged if the $600K target is treated as the reference point for evaluating $180K; verify that debiasing protocols are structural (pre-mortem is a process change; "try to be less biased" is not); check that at least 4–5 biases are genuinely active in the scenario (a good scenario will trigger multiple biases simultaneously).
- Human supplies (Claude can't): **A real decision scenario produces a much more useful audit than a synthetic one** — the human should use an actual current or recent decision from their organization. The synthetic "failing product" scenario is illustrative and clearly labeled. No external data collection is required; the audit works from whatever context the human provides.
- Output medium: Remotion animated bias audit table (bias name → active Y/N → evidence → debiasing protocol, columns appearing left to right per row).
- The change: run the same decision through a structured decision-analysis protocol (decision matrix with explicit criteria and weights) to show how formal method reduces the number of biases that can operate — then compare the decision that comes out of the biased process vs. the structured process.
- Teardown angle: Cognitive biases are not character flaws — they are predictable, nameable failure modes in how humans process information under uncertainty. Naming them in a specific decision is the first step to structural correction. The debiasing protocol is not willpower; it is process design.
- Exclusions: formal decision analysis (decision trees, expected utility), behavioral economics deep-dive, organizational politics and power.
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 05 — Research Communication Failure Modes in Organizations with Claude
- Source: principles-of-management-bundle-with-llms/chapters/11-communication.md + LLM Exercise
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: Most organizational communication failures are not about information content — they are about channel selection, encoding mismatches, and the ten barriers the chapter names. Claude maps which failures are happening and what fixes them.
- The artifact: a synthesized communication audit for a fictional or real organizational scenario — a table of active communication barriers (noise, filtering, selective perception, information overload, semantic ambiguity, cultural differences, emotional state, credibility gaps, status differences, poor listening) with a channel-selection recommendation matrix — rendered as a Remotion animated barrier table plus a 2×2 (message complexity × relationship familiarity) channel-selection guide.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Audit the communication system for a 30-person remote engineering organization. They use Slack for everything — decisions, announcements, feedback, performance conversations. For each of the ten communication barriers, assess: (1) Is this barrier elevated by the current all-Slack architecture? (2) What is the specific failure mode (give an example scenario)? (3) What channel change reduces this barrier? Then build a channel-selection matrix: for message types [sensitive feedback / urgent decision / project update / team celebration / complex technical design], recommend the appropriate medium and justify with communication theory."`
- Read / check: Verify information overload is flagged for all-Slack environments (documented in the chapter and in organizational behavior research); verify that channel-selection recommendations match media richness theory (face-to-face for ambiguous/sensitive messages; text for simple/routine); verify that each barrier is genuinely elevated by the described scenario and not just a generic warning.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): A real organizational context (actual team size, actual channel stack, real communication failures that have occurred) makes the audit operationally useful rather than generically educational. **For authenticity, the human should describe specific recent communication failures** and let Claude diagnose which barriers produced them.
- Output medium: Remotion animated barrier table + channel-selection 2×2 matrix appearing.
- The change: re-run the audit for a fully in-office synchronous organization — show how the barrier profile shifts (information overload drops; filtering increases; status differences amplify) and how the channel recommendations change.
- Teardown angle: Channel selection is not a preference — it is a risk management decision. The wrong channel for a high-stakes message increases the probability of barrier activation. The audit makes the risk visible before the message is sent.
- Exclusions: public relations and external communication, crisis communication protocols, presentation design methodology.
- Score: 7/10

## Candidate 06 — Research Negotiation Strategy Design with Claude
- Source: principles-of-management-bundle-with-llms/chapters/14-conflict-and-negotiations.md + LLM Exercise
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: Most negotiations are either positional (both sides anchor and fight for concessions) or unprepared (one side discovers their BATNA only after they've made an irreversible commitment). Claude builds the negotiation playbook before you walk in.
- The artifact: a synthesized negotiation brief for a specific scenario (salary negotiation, vendor contract, partnership term-sheet) including BATNA, ZOPA, opening anchor, concession sequence, and three likely opponent moves with prepared responses — rendered as a Remotion animated brief with sections revealing.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Build a negotiation preparation brief for the following scenario: I am negotiating a software vendor contract renewal. My current spend is $180K/year. I have a competing offer from a vendor at $140K but their product is 20% less capable. My BATNA is switching (cost: $60K implementation + 3 months disruption). The vendor's likely BATNA is losing us (we represent ~2% of their ARR). Build: (1) my ZOPA calculation; (2) opening anchor with justification; (3) three concession trades (what I give up, what I ask for in return); (4) three likely opponent moves and my prepared response to each."`
- Read / check: Verify the ZOPA is correctly computed (ZOPA spans from my resistance point to their resistance point; if there is no overlap, the deal should not happen); verify the opening anchor is above my resistance point and grounded in a rationale (not arbitrary); verify each concession trade is asymmetric in the right direction (I give up low-value items; I ask for high-value items); check that opponent moves include both positional and interest-based tactics.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Real negotiation scenarios require actual numbers (current spend, competitor pricing, switching costs, contract terms). **The human must supply accurate figures from their actual situation** — Claude's output quality degrades sharply when the inputs are generic. The illustrative scenario above is labeled as such; a real-scenario video requires real context the human provides.
- Output medium: Remotion animated negotiation brief (BATNA → ZOPA → anchor → concession sequence → opponent-move responses, sections appearing).
- The change: add a principled negotiation layer (Fisher and Ury's interests-vs-positions framework) — Claude re-analyzes the same scenario to identify what the vendor's underlying interests are (recurring revenue? case study? product feedback?) and designs a deal structure that creates value on both sides rather than just splitting on price.
- Teardown angle: Negotiation preparation is asymmetric advantage — the side that has computed their BATNA, ZOPA, and concession sequence before the first meeting dominates the side that has not. Claude collapses the preparation work that most people skip because it feels tedious.
- Exclusions: multi-party negotiation theory, labor law and collective bargaining, international/cross-cultural negotiation specifics.
- Score: 7/10

## Candidate 07 — Build a Team Diagnostic with Claude
- Source: principles-of-management-bundle-with-llms/chapters/10-understanding-and-managing-work-teams.md + LLM Exercise
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: Tuckman's forming-storming-norming-performing model says every team goes through the same stages — but most managers can't tell which stage their team is in, so they apply the wrong intervention.
- The artifact: a synthesized team diagnostic report for a described team — stage classification (with evidence), trust level assessment, role-clarity gap analysis, and three specific interventions matched to the current stage — rendered as a Remotion animated diagnostic card with stage meter, trust gauge, and intervention checklist.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Diagnose this team: 8-person cross-functional product team, together 7 months, shipped one product. Symptoms: engineers and designers frequently re-litigate decisions in Slack after they're made; one senior engineer dominates standups; two members have stopped contributing in retrospectives; output quality is high but velocity has dropped 20% over 8 weeks. Classify the team's Tuckman stage with evidence. Assess trust level (structural, knowledge-based, identification-based). Identify the three highest-leverage interventions for this stage."`
- Read / check: Verify the Tuckman stage classification is grounded in the described symptoms (re-litigating decisions and velocity drop is classic storming; the pattern does not fit norming); verify trust classification is correctly applied (structural trust = role clarity; knowledge-based = competence confidence; identification-based = shared values alignment); verify interventions are stage-appropriate (storming teams need conflict-resolution process design, not team-building activities).
- Human supplies (Claude can't): **Real team diagnostic data (actual team symptoms, meeting observations, retrospective artifacts) makes the output useful rather than generic.** The fictional scenario above is illustrative. A manager who supplies actual observable behaviors gets a more specific and actionable diagnosis. No external data collection is required beyond observation.
- Output medium: Remotion animated diagnostic card (stage meter + trust gauge + intervention checklist revealing).
- The change: re-run the diagnostic after the three interventions have been applied (simulated, with described behavior changes) and show how the stage classification shifts — demonstrating that the diagnostic is not static and that interventions change the evidence base.
- Teardown angle: Teams are not fixed objects — they are processes in a stage. The right intervention depends entirely on which stage the team is in. Applying a norming-stage intervention (shared rituals, identity building) to a storming team makes the conflict worse, not better. The diagnostic is what prevents the wrong medicine.
- Exclusions: agile team methodology deep-dive, remote team specifics, team composition optimization (skills matrix).
- Score: 7/10
