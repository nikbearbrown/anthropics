# Chapter 5 — Your Cognitive Audit: Mapping Your Own Work to the Tiers

**One-line:** The cognitive audit is the ongoing practice of asking, for every task you do, whether doing it yourself is building your judgment or whether delegating it is eroding it.

## Chapter overview

This chapter is the practical core of Part 1. Chapters 3 and 4 gave you the map (the seven-tier taxonomy) and the boundary concept (the phase gate). This chapter gives you the method for applying both to your actual work — not a hypothetical professional's work, yours. By the end, you will have a completed cognitive audit of your ten most common tasks, a short list of your highest-value protected work, and a one-page AI Use Discipline document that governs how you use AI going forward.

The audit is not a one-time exercise. It is a recurring practice — something you revisit quarterly as your tools change, your role evolves, and the frontier moves. But you have to start somewhere, and this chapter is where you start.

---

## Opening case

In the early 1990s, K. Anders Ericsson and his colleagues at Florida State University published a study that changed how researchers thought about expertise. They studied violinists at a Berlin music academy — elite students, good students, and students studying to be music teachers — and tried to understand what distinguished the best from the rest. The finding was not what most people expected. The best violinists were not practicing more kinds of things. They were practicing one specific way: deliberate practice, structured specifically to challenge their current edge, with feedback tight enough to reveal failure within seconds. [verify — Ericsson, Krampe, and Tesch-Romer 1993]

The students who improved the most were not the ones who played the most hours. They were the ones who spent the most hours in practice designed to be hard enough to produce errors and specific enough to correct them. The students who hit a plateau were the ones who spent their practice time playing things they already knew how to play well.

This is the central mechanism behind the cognitive audit. The question is not whether you are doing a lot of work. It is whether the work you are doing is the kind that builds the judgment your career depends on — or whether you are, professionally speaking, playing the pieces you already know, while delegating the difficult passages that would have made you better.

AI creates a new version of this problem. When AI is available, the path of least resistance is to delegate everything that feels like effort: drafting, summarizing, formatting, option-generating, first-pass analysis. Some of that is exactly right — those are Tier 1 tasks that AI handles competently and that do not build judgment through repetition. But some of what feels like effort is the cognitive friction that builds the skill. When you delegate the difficult passages to AI, the skill atrophies. You may not notice for months. You will notice eventually.

The cognitive audit is the instrument for telling the difference.

## Core content

### 1. What the audit asks

The cognitive audit is a structured process for evaluating your current work against the seven-tier taxonomy. It asks six questions for each task:

1. **What tier is this task?** Where does it sit on the taxonomy — Tier 1 (pattern recognition, generation, formatting) through Tier 7 (wisdom, accountability, judgment under genuine uncertainty)?
2. **How frequently does this task occur?** Daily, weekly, monthly, or rarely?
3. **Is it currently delegated to AI?** Yes, partially, or no?
4. **Should it be delegated?** Given the tier, the stakes, and the judgment it builds, what is the right answer?
5. **What judgment does performing this task build?** What skill or knowledge are you developing when you do this yourself?
6. **What is the risk if it is delegated?** None, low, moderate, or high — and specifically, what erodes?

The audit does not tell you what your field values. It tells you how to find and protect it. The answers to question 5 (what judgment does performing this task build?) are the ones that require your domain knowledge, and they are the ones that matter most.

Here is the audit table. Complete it for your ten most common work tasks before reading the next section.

| Task | Tier | Frequency | Currently delegated? | Should be delegated? | Judgment it builds | Risk if delegated |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Task 1] | [1–7] | [Daily/Weekly/Monthly] | [Yes/Partial/No] | [Yes/Partial/No] | [Skill built by doing this yourself] | [None/Low/Moderate/High] |

Work through the table before reading on. The rest of this chapter is more useful if you have actual entries to think about.

### 2. The mechanism: why delegation choices compound

Donald Schön, the organizational theorist who wrote *The Reflective Practitioner* in 1983, made an observation that is easy to misunderstand. He said that professionals do not primarily learn by studying theory and then applying it — they learn by doing, noticing the gap between what they expected and what happened, and adjusting in real time. He called this "reflection-in-action": the expert does not stop to analyze; they adjust as they work, and the adjustment is inseparable from the expertise. [verify — Schön 1983]

This has a specific implication for AI use. If AI is doing the drafting, the analysis, and the first-pass generation, the professional is no longer in the loop at the moment when reflection-in-action happens. They are reviewing output rather than producing it. Reviewing is a different cognitive activity from producing. It catches some errors, but it does not build the same judgment. The professional who spends years reviewing AI output rather than making the first move develops a different — and often thinner — kind of expertise than the professional who spent those years in the productive struggle.

Gary Klein's research on expert decision-making adds another layer. In *Sources of Power* (1998), Klein studied firefighters, military commanders, and other experts who make high-stakes decisions under time pressure. What he found was that experts do not primarily use analytical reasoning — comparing options, running through decision trees. They pattern-match: they see a situation, and something about it either does or does not fit patterns they have seen before. When it does not fit, they notice. That anomaly detection — the sense that something is off before they can say what — is the most valuable thing an experienced professional has.

Klein's anomaly detection is learned from encountering anomalies. If AI is producing output that smooths over the anomalies — because AI is very good at making things look coherent even when they are not — the professional never develops the pattern library that makes the sense-of-wrongness available. The audit's question about "what judgment does this task build" is, in part, asking: is this task building your anomaly detection, or is it Tier 1 work that does not require that kind of judgment?

Robert Bjork's research on desirable difficulties extends this further. In a 2011 chapter with Elizabeth Bjork, he documented the counterintuitive finding that harder practice produces better long-term retention and transfer than easier practice, even when the harder practice feels less productive in the moment. The conditions that make practice feel easy — spacing, cueing, fluency — are often the conditions that make the learning shallow. [verify — Bjork and Bjork 2011] 

The professional implication: when AI makes a task easier, that ease can be the signal that you are in territory where the learning was happening before. The question is not whether to use AI — it is whether doing the task yourself, in this case, was building something you still need.

### 3. Identifying your compounding tasks

Not all protected tasks are equally worth protecting. Some Tier 4–7 tasks are high-leverage: doing them yourself builds judgment that improves everything else you do. These are your compounding tasks.

A compounding task has three features. First, it requires judgment that is specific to your domain and your context — not general reasoning, but the kind of knowing that comes from years in this field with these clients in this market. Second, doing it yourself produces feedback that is specific enough to teach you something: not just "it went okay" but "I saw X, and that meant Y, and I handled it with Z, and here is what happened." Third, the judgment it builds makes your other work better — it is not just useful for this task, it is transferable to many tasks.

Examples of compounding tasks across different professional domains:

- **In consulting:** diagnosing the real problem behind the stated problem. Clients rarely present the actual issue; they present a symptom or a preferred solution. The judgment to see through the presentation and formulate the real question is built by being wrong about it and having to recover. AI can help research options once you have formulated the problem. It cannot formulate the problem for you without losing the learning.

- **In software development:** deciding which abstraction is right for a new system. Architecture decisions are not primarily about correct answers; they are about tradeoffs that only become visible in production, over time, as requirements change. The developer who designs a dozen systems and lives with the consequences develops an intuition for which abstractions age well. That intuition is built by experiencing the consequences. An architecture chosen by AI, accepted without the developer's independent judgment, does not produce the feedback that builds the intuition.

- **In real estate:** reading whether a deal is going to close based on cues that are not in the data. Financing contingencies, emotional states of buyers and sellers, the pace of communication, the questions that are and are not asked — experienced agents learn to read these as signals. That reading is built by being in hundreds of transactions and noticing which signals predicted what. AI can analyze the data. It cannot build the pattern library from the lived experience of the transactions.

These are all Tier 4–7 tasks. They all require judgment that is specific to the domain, produces feedback that teaches, and makes the professional better at adjacent work. They are worth protecting.

The audit's job is to identify which tasks in *your* work are like these — and which tasks you are currently performing yourself that are actually Tier 1 work that AI handles competently and that do not build your judgment through repetition.

### 4. The deskilling risk register

The audit also produces a shorter, more uncomfortable document: the deskilling risk register. This is the list of tasks you have already delegated — or are close to delegating — where the delegation is eroding a capability you still need.

The register asks: for each AI delegation in your current workflow, is the skill you are no longer practicing one that you will need when AI is wrong, when AI is unavailable, or when stakes are high enough that someone needs to own the judgment personally?

Some delegations are clean: you delegated a Tier 1 task that produces no judgment through repetition, and there is no skill atrophying. Formatting a report, generating a summary, producing variations on a design concept within a defined brief — these are clean delegations.

Some delegations are risky: you delegated a task that felt like Tier 1 work but was actually building anomaly detection, domain pattern recognition, or the kind of judgment that makes you better at the Tier 4–7 work. The risk is not obvious, because the output still looks fine. What has eroded is the next level — the capacity to evaluate the output correctly when it is wrong.

The most honest deskilling risk register for most professionals who have been using AI tools for a year or more will have at least one entry: something they used to do themselves that they are less sure they could do well today, because they have not done it recently.

That entry is not a reason for guilt. It is a reason for a deliberate reclamation decision.

### 5. Writing your AI Use Discipline document

The audit produces three outputs: a list of tasks to delegate, a list of tasks to protect (your compounding tasks), and a list of delegations to reconsider (the deskilling risk register). The AI Use Discipline document is the document that turns those three lists into a standing operating procedure for your work.

The document is one page. It has three sections:

**Section 1: What I delegate to AI.** A specific list of the tasks in your workflow that AI handles, with a note on the handoff condition for each. Not a category ("research") but a specific task ("AI aggregates search results for a given query; I evaluate source reliability and decide what to use").

**Section 2: What I protect.** A specific list of your compounding tasks — the Tier 4–7 work that you perform yourself because doing it builds the judgment your expertise depends on. For each, a brief note on what judgment it builds and why delegating it would erode that judgment.

**Section 3: My reclamation decisions.** For any delegation in the deskilling risk register, a specific decision about what to take back, how often to practice it yourself, and what the purpose of that practice is.

The document does not need to be shared with your employer, though it can be. It does not need to be long. It needs to be honest, specific, and revisited quarterly — or whenever your tools change significantly.

The act of writing it is valuable independent of the document itself. Writing forces precision in a way that thinking does not. The professional who writes "I protect the task of diagnosing the real client problem" and then writes why has done a different kind of thinking than the professional who vaguely intends to "keep the important work." The document makes the intention into a specification. The specification can be evaluated. Vague intentions cannot.

## Worked example: a mid-career UX designer's cognitive audit

Here is a worked audit for a specific professional archetype: a UX designer, nine years into their career, who has been using AI generation tools for design concepts and copy for eighteen months.

**Step 1: List the ten most common tasks.**

1. Generating initial concept variations for a new brief
2. Writing UX copy for interface elements
3. Running usability testing sessions with participants
4. Synthesizing usability findings into recommendations
5. Writing the project brief — defining what the work should accomplish
6. Reviewing and selecting from generated concept options
7. Presenting recommendations to stakeholders
8. Conducting competitive analysis and design benchmarking
9. Writing detailed interaction specifications for developers
10. Facilitating alignment sessions with product and business stakeholders

**Step 2: Map to tiers and assess delegation.**

| Task | Tier | Freq | Currently delegated? | Should be delegated? | Judgment built | Risk if delegated |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Initial concept variations | 1 | Daily | Yes | Yes | Minimal — variation generation is AI-tier work once the brief exists | Low |
| UX copy for interface elements | 1 | Daily | Partial | Yes, with review | Copy pattern recognition; can retain by reviewing critically | Low |
| Usability testing sessions | 3 | Weekly | No | No | Reading participant behavior, detecting unstated confusion, building user-mental-model intuition | High |
| Synthesizing usability findings | 4-5 | Weekly | Partial | Partial (AI aggregates; I interpret) | Causal analysis: why users behave as they do, not just what they do | High |
| Writing the project brief | 5 | Weekly | No | No | Problem formulation — the judgment that makes all subsequent work point at the right thing | Very high |
| Reviewing/selecting concepts | 4 | Daily | No | No (AI generates, human selects) | Evaluation judgment, brief-fitness assessment, client-context matching | Moderate |
| Presenting to stakeholders | 6 | Weekly | No | No | Reading the room, managing disagreement, owning the recommendation | High |
| Competitive analysis | 1-2 | Monthly | Partial | Yes, heavily | Pattern cataloguing — AI handles well; my value is in the interpretation | Low |
| Interaction specifications | 4 | Weekly | Partial | Partial | Precision, edge-case thinking, developer-communication judgment | Moderate |
| Facilitation with stakeholders | 6 | Monthly | No | No | Navigating competing interests, building consensus, institutional navigation | High |

**Step 3: Identify the compounding tasks.**

Looking at the table, the compounding tasks for this designer are:
- Writing the project brief (problem formulation — the single highest-leverage task)
- Synthesizing usability findings (causal interpretation, not just aggregation)
- Facilitating stakeholder alignment (institutional navigation, consensus-building)

These three tasks are where the designer's nine years of experience compounds. They are also the tasks that AI is worst at — not because they are magic, but because they require context, judgment, and accountability that AI structurally lacks.

**Step 4: Identify the deskilling risk.**

The designer has been delegating initial concept generation to AI for eighteen months. That is appropriate — it is Tier 1 work. But they have also been using AI to aggregate usability findings into themes and occasionally to draft synthesis sections of research reports. On reflection, the synthesis is not a Tier 1 task. The aggregation is; the interpretation is not. The designer notes this in the risk register: "I am generating synthesis language from AI aggregations and editing it rather than forming the interpretation myself. I may be losing the causal reasoning step."

**Step 5: Write the AI Use Discipline document.**

*What I delegate:* Initial concept variations from a written brief; copy for interface elements (with my review and revision); competitive analysis aggregation; data cleaning and formatting for research reports; slide deck formatting and layout.

*What I protect:* Brief writing — I always write the brief myself, before any generation begins; usability synthesis — I form the interpretation before looking at AI-generated themes; stakeholder facilitation — no AI in the room; the selection and rationale decision — when I choose a concept direction, I write the rationale myself.

*Reclamation:* I am taking back the synthesis step. Starting this quarter, I will write the interpretation section of usability reports before reviewing any AI-generated themes. I will compare my interpretation to the AI aggregation afterwards and note the differences. This is my deliberate practice for keeping the causal reasoning sharp.

## Key terms

- **Cognitive audit:** A recurring structured review of your current tasks mapped to the tier taxonomy, producing decisions about what to delegate, what to protect, and what to reclaim. Not a one-time worksheet — a quarterly practice.
- **Compounding task:** A task that, performed yourself, builds the judgment that makes your other work better. Usually Tier 4–7. High-leverage to protect. Examples: problem formulation, synthesis interpretation, stakeholder facilitation.
- **Deskilling risk register:** The list of AI delegations in your current workflow where the delegation is eroding a capability you will still need when AI is wrong, unavailable, or insufficient.
- **AI Use Discipline document:** The one-page written specification of what AI does in your workflow, what you protect, and what you have deliberately reclaimed. The standing operating procedure for your AI use.
- **Reflection-in-action:** Schön's term for the learning that happens during professional work — adjusting in real time based on feedback from the task itself. Delegating the task removes the learner from the feedback loop.
- **Desirable difficulty:** The finding (Bjork and Bjork) that harder practice produces more durable skill than easier practice, even when it feels less productive in the moment. When AI makes something effortless, ask whether the effort was building something.

## What would change my mind

I would revise this chapter if evidence emerged that passive AI use — reviewing AI-generated output without engaging in the productive struggle of generating it yourself — produced equivalent long-term expert judgment to deliberate practice with feedback. The current evidence from expertise research (Ericsson et al.), reflective practice theory (Schön), and naturalistic decision making (Klein) gives no basis for that assumption. I would also revise if domain-specific research showed that, in a particular profession, the audit tasks identified as compounding are not in fact skill-building — that the judgment they appear to build is not the judgment that distinguishes expert performance. That would require field-specific evidence I do not currently have.

## Still puzzling

- How should an employee handle the audit when their employer's AI policy rewards speed over deliberate practice? The cognitive audit identifies what to protect; it does not resolve the organizational incentive problem.
- Which Tier 1 tasks are secretly apprenticeship tasks for early-career professionals — tasks that should be kept human for junior practitioners even when AI can handle them, because doing them builds foundational judgment? The audit framework does not distinguish by career stage.
- How often should the audit be repeated? The answer probably depends on how fast the tools are changing in your field, but a general rhythm has not been validated.
- When a compounding task has been delegated for long enough that the skill has atrophied, how should reclamation work — cold re-entry or graduated re-exposure? The deliberate practice literature suggests structured re-exposure with feedback, but field-specific guidance is limited.
