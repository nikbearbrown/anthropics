# Chapter 3 — What AI Actually Can and Cannot Do: The Seven Tiers

**One-line:** The seven-tier taxonomy is the map, from tasks where AI is superhuman to tasks where human accountability remains irreducible.

## Chapter overview

The previous two chapters established what is happening in the labor market and why. This chapter gives you a tool to apply that understanding to your own work. The seven-tier taxonomy maps the full range of human cognitive work against what AI can and cannot do — from the tasks where AI is decisively better than any human (Tier 1) to the tasks where human judgment, accountability, and wisdom are structurally irreducible (Tier 7). By the end of this chapter, you will be able to define each tier in plain language, classify tasks from your own work into the taxonomy, and identify which tiers dominate your current daily work. The taxonomy is not a status ladder. It is a delegation map.

## Learning objectives

- Define the seven cognitive tiers in plain language, with at least one professional example per tier.
- Classify professional tasks into the taxonomy.
- Identify which tiers dominate your current daily work and what that means for your delegation decisions.

---

## The lying calculator

Imagine a calculator correct 85 percent of the time and confidently wrong the other 15 percent. The display looks identical either way. Who catches the errors? Not the fastest typists. The people who catch them are the ones with enough mental arithmetic to feel that 247 times 6 is not 3,200 — before they can prove it. They do not know the exact answer. They know the shape of the answer, and the shape is wrong.

This is a thought experiment, but the dynamic is real. Large language models produce confident, fluent output whether that output is correct or not. The only people reliably positioned to catch the errors are the ones who have built enough independent knowledge to notice when something is off before they run the verification.

Every profession has its version of mental arithmetic. The lawyer who has read thousands of contracts feels in two seconds when an indemnification clause is strange. The structural engineer knows when a load number looks wrong before confirming it. The physician knows when something is clinically atypical before the diagnostic system flags it. That felt knowledge — domain intuition built through years of doing the work directly — is the foundation of the protected tiers. The taxonomy is the map that tells you where that knowledge lives and which tasks maintain it.

## 1. How to use the taxonomy

The taxonomy is pragmatic, not philosophical. It does not claim to describe the final nature of human uniqueness. It describes the current operational division of labor — where AI reliably outperforms humans, where humans reliably outperform AI, and why — organized to be useful for a working professional deciding what to delegate.

Two structural notes before the tiers:

First, the tiers are not a hierarchy of worth. Tier 7 is not more important than Tier 1. A professional who does only Tier 7 work without Tier 1 infrastructure accomplishes nothing. The tiers describe types of cognitive operation, not levels of prestige.

Second, most professional tasks mix tiers. Writing a client recommendation involves Tier 1 (drawing on research), Tier 4 (deciding whether the research applies to this situation), Tier 5 (reasoning about what will happen if the client acts on it), and Tier 7 (signing your name to it). The operative question is: which tier is load-bearing in this task — and what is the minimum tier at which a human must remain present for this to be done responsibly?

## Tier 1 — Pattern and Association: Where AI Is Superhuman

**What it is:** Recognizing patterns, generating variations, classifying inputs, summarizing content, matching templates, translating between formats. The cognitive operation is: given this input distribution, produce an output consistent with it.

**What AI does here:** Current language models are genuinely superhuman at Tier 1 tasks when the input distribution is familiar. A model trained on professional writing generates professional writing faster and at greater volume than any human. A model trained on code produces syntactically correct, conventionally structured code faster than any programmer types. The critical qualifier is "familiar distribution" — when the task deviates from what the model has seen before, the pattern-generation mechanism continues producing fluent output, but that output may no longer be correct. The output looks the same. The failure is harder to detect.

**Professional examples:** First draft of a standard report. Meeting-transcript summary. Five email subject-line variations. Boilerplate proposal from a template. Customer inquiry classification. Technical-to-plain-language translation. All high quality, high speed.

**The delegation signal:** If you spend significant time on Tier 1 tasks at your career stage, you are competing with a machine — and losing hours you could spend on the tasks that build and maintain your judgment. Delegate Tier 1 aggressively, with a Tier 4 verification guardrail. Chapter 4 covers how to design that guardrail.

---

## Tier 2 — Embodied and Sensorimotor: Where Physics Bounds AI

**What it is:** Tasks requiring physical presence, sensory integration, manual dexterity, or real-world observation.

**What AI does here:** Robotic systems handle highly structured physical environments, but general embodied intelligence — a plumber in a century-old building, a surgeon adapting to unexpected tissue, a physical therapist sensing muscle resistance — remains hard. The physical world does not submit to language model generation.

**For mid-career knowledge workers:** Tier 2 is not usually the primary source of professional value in the occupations this book focuses on. Where it matters is in knowledge work with physical components — site inspections, patient contact, materials assessment. But the more load-bearing tiers for a marketing manager, financial analyst, or software developer are 4 through 7. Physical presence is protected for now; what you reason about from physical observation is where Tiers 4–7 take over.

---

## Tier 3 — Social and Personal: Where Stakes and Relationship Matter

**What it is:** Work done through genuine human relationships with accumulated history and real stakes. Trust built over time. The specific knowledge of particular individuals.

**What AI does here:** AI social simulation handles transactional interactions well — customer service, routine inquiries, first-contact exchanges. But genuine relationship work — where years of shared history are load-bearing, where the other person's trust in you is not transferable to a tool, where your knowledge of them specifically changes what you say — is not meaningfully automated.

**Professional examples:** A long-term client relationship where the professional knows the client's decision-making style, their risk tolerance, their history of good and bad calls, and what they actually need to hear. A negotiation between parties whose dynamics cannot be read from a transcript. A mentorship conversation at a specific inflection point.

**The key mechanism:** Trust built over time is not generatable on demand. A professional who has worked with a client for twelve years holds something a tool that met the client yesterday cannot replicate. The Tier 3 protection argument is strongest for relationships where depth, history, and genuine stakes matter.

---

## Tier 4 — Metacognitive and Supervisory: Where AI Cannot Audit Itself

**What it is:** Knowing when to distrust output, including one's own output and especially AI output. Formulating the right problem before solving it. Auditing reasoning for consistency and plausibility. Knowing what you do not know. The cognitive operation is: evaluate the quality of thinking, not just the content of its outputs.

**What AI does here:** Current AI systems have no reliable self-auditing capability. They produce output based on training distribution — right or wrong, the format is identical. Asking an AI to check its own work produces another sample from the same distribution, not an independent audit. When models express uncertainty, that signal is generated from training data about what uncertain outputs look like; it does not reliably correspond to actual epistemic state. [verify — check calibration literature for current frontier models]

Kahneman and Klein's 2009 *Conditions for Intuitive Expertise* establishes when expert intuition is reliable: valid environments with rapid, clear feedback. A chess player's intuition is trustworthy because chess provides immediate corrective feedback over thousands of games. Professional knowledge work usually provides slower, noisier feedback — but the intuition built through actual consequences is still more reliable than an AI confidence signal calibrated to output distribution rather than correspondence with reality.

**Professional examples:** A senior consultant deciding the data supports one conclusion but the situation calls for a different question entirely — reframing rather than executing. A physician seeing an AI diagnostic recommendation that is technically within normal range but anomalous for this specific patient's baseline. A brand strategist recognizing that a campaign concept is technically on-brief but will land wrong in the current cultural moment.

**Why this is the load-bearing tier for this book:** Every Tier 1 delegation requires a Tier 4 check — a human with sufficient domain knowledge to evaluate whether the output is right for this situation. The erosion of Tier 4 capability is the most common form of deskilling: professionals retain the responsibility but lose the judgment to exercise it. Maintaining Tier 4 requires continuing to do Tier 5–7 work that keeps the domain knowledge current. A professional who delegates all Tier 1 and only reviews outputs — without doing direct higher-tier thinking — is exercising their Tier 4 against a weakening base.

## Tier 5 — Causal and Counterfactual: Where AI Is a Fluent Parrot

**What it is:** Reasoning about causes, not just patterns. Asking "why does this happen?" rather than "what happens when this is present?" Reasoning about what would happen if something changed — counterfactual analysis. Designing interventions based on causal models. The cognitive operation is: build a model of how the world works, not just a model of what the world looks like.

**What AI does here:** Judea Pearl and Dana Mackenzie's 2018 *The Book of Why* explains the core distinction: statistical pattern-finding identifies correlations and generates predictions, but causal reasoning requires a model of the underlying mechanism. An AI can predict market patterns under normal conditions. It cannot reason about what would happen if a central bank changed its policy in an unprecedented way — counterfactual causal reasoning about novel interventions is not pattern matching. A model can summarize what happened in previous restructurings. It cannot reason about whether this restructuring, with these people, in this institutional context, will follow or break the pattern.

**Professional examples:** A strategist reasoning about whether a new market entry will change competitor behavior (counterfactual causal). A physician deciding that the standard treatment protocol does not apply to this patient because of a specific mechanism the protocol was not designed to account for (causal override of statistical recommendation). A financial advisor explaining to a client why the correlation they observed in the data does not justify the investment thesis they are proposing (separating pattern from cause). A lawyer constructing an argument about why the precedent does not apply because the causal chain that made the precedent reasonable is absent from the current case.

**The operational boundary:** AI outputs for Tier 5 questions can be useful inputs for human causal reasoning. They can surface relevant patterns, flag historical cases, and structure the question. But accepting AI Tier 5 output without independent human causal reasoning — without someone who actually has a model of how this specific situation works — is delegating strategy to a sophisticated pattern matcher that does not know the difference between a correlation and a cause.

---

## Tier 6 — Collective and Distributed: Where AI Has No Skin in the Game

**What it is:** Work that happens through teams, institutions, shared norms, and organizational culture. Negotiation. Consensus-building. Political judgment within an organization. Understanding what an institution can and cannot do given its history, its culture, and its stakeholders. The cognitive operation is: work within and through a distributed social system, not just from outside it.

**What AI does here:** Edwin Hutchins's 1995 *Cognition in the Wild* established that thinking is not only in one person's head — it is distributed across people, artifacts, procedures, and environments. A ship's navigation team has no single navigator; knowledge is distributed across instruments, roles, and coordinated interaction. Much professional knowledge work is like this.

AI can participate in some aspects: drafting documents, summarizing stakeholder positions, modeling scenarios. But actually navigating the institution — knowing which stakeholder's concern is decisive, understanding which history makes a proposal viable or toxic, building the relationships that allow a decision to be implemented rather than merely announced — requires genuine participation with genuine stakes. Karl Weick's *Sensemaking in Organizations* (1995) reinforces this: organizations act under ambiguity, and the sensemaking that precedes action is irreducibly social and political. An AI tool can produce analysis; it cannot participate in the process by which an organization decides what the analysis means.

**Professional examples:** A manager knowing which conversations to have in which order to build the coalition that makes a restructuring possible. A lawyer in a negotiation reading the room. A consultant navigating a client firm's actual power structure, which differs from the org chart.

**Why this tier resists delegation:** You can only have skin in the game by being in it. An AI conducting a negotiation on your behalf is not negotiating — it is generating text addressed to another party. The counterparty's trust and willingness to concede depends on knowing they are dealing with a person with genuine stakes. That cannot be outsourced.

---

## Tier 7 — Existential and Wisdom: Where Accountability Is Irreducible

**What it is:** The decisions you sign your name to. Judgment about what is worth doing, what values should govern a choice when multiple values conflict, when to proceed and when to stop. The professional courage to say no. The wisdom to know when you are in over your head. The cognitive operation is: take genuine responsibility for a consequential decision under conditions where the costs of being wrong are real and fall on real people.

**What AI does here:** Essentially nothing consequential — not because AI cannot generate text about values and wisdom, but because the text is not accountable. When a physician makes a decision that harms a patient, the physician bears it. When an AI generates a recommendation that harms a patient, the AI bears nothing. The accountability that makes Tier 7 work meaningful is inseparable from the genuine stakes of the decision-maker.

Dreyfus and Dreyfus's 1986 *Mind over Machine* argued that the highest stages of expert performance require holistic, situation-specific judgment that cannot be captured in rules. AI has exceeded that argument in some domains — some expert-level pattern recognition has been surpassed. But the Dreyfus claim holds at the level of accountability: the expert who acts faces the consequences, and that structure cannot be replicated by a tool.

**Professional examples:** A physician who deviates from the treatment protocol because something about this patient is wrong — and stands behind it if the decision goes badly. A lawyer who refuses to pursue a strategy that would likely win but would be unconscionable. A manager who overrides an AI staffing recommendation and owns the outcome.

**The irreducibility of Tier 7:** Use AI to inform these decisions — surface considerations, model consequences, articulate trade-offs. But you cannot delegate the decision itself. "The AI recommended it" is not a full explanation for a consequential action; it is accountability abdication.

## Worked example: a professional workday, eight tasks mapped

Priya is a content strategist, twelve years in at a B2B marketing firm. Here are eight tasks from her Tuesday, mapped to the taxonomy:

| # | Task | Tier | Ruling | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Generate five email subject-line versions for the campaign launch | 1 | **Delegate** | Pattern variation on established templates; AI faster, output verifiable |
| 2 | Summarize fifty-page customer research report into a one-page brief | 1 + 4 check | **Delegate with verification** | AI summarizes; Priya checks for what the AI misweighted — the insight buried on page 47 that matters most for this brief |
| 3 | Write the strategic brief: what the campaign must accomplish, what the audience needs to feel, what success looks like | 5 + 7 | **Protect absolutely** | Causal reasoning (what will move this audience?) plus accountable commitment (what is the brand promising?); the brief also gates all subsequent AI delegation |
| 4 | Prepare the campaign performance report for quarterly review | 1 | **Delegate** | Data formatting, chart generation, narrative templating |
| 5 | Decide whether to proceed with the campaign given ambiguous news that broke this morning | 5 + 6 + 7 | **Protect** | Causal (how does the news change reception?), institutional (which stakeholders need to weigh in?), accountable (Priya owns the call to go or pause) |
| 6 | Schedule a meeting with four internal stakeholders and two agency contacts | 1 | **Delegate** | Pure logistics |
| 7 | Advise a junior colleague on handling a client who wants something technically achievable but strategically wrong | 3 + 4 + 6 | **Protect** | Requires knowing this colleague's development stage (Tier 3), diagnosing the actual problem with the request (Tier 4), and navigating institutional dynamics without damaging the client relationship (Tier 6) |
| 8 | Decide whether to renew the agency contract or bring the capability in-house | 5 + 6 + 7 | **Protect** | Causal analysis of cost-capability trade-offs, stakeholder navigation, consequential and difficult-to-reverse judgment |

---

## The delegation map in practice

Across eight tasks: delegate four (Tasks 1, 2 with verification, 4, 6), protect four (Tasks 3, 5, 7, 8). Task 2 is the interesting case — delegable, but the Tier 4 verification step is not optional.

Notice what AI handles: variations, formatting, summaries, coordination. Notice what Priya owns: the brief, the strategic decisions, the mentorship, the judgment calls, the accountability.

By delegating Tier 1 tasks, Priya recovers roughly four hours a week. She reinvests that time in Tier 5–7 work — deeper strategy, more mentorship, more client-facing judgment. That shift is not just efficiency. Every additional Tier 5–7 task she does builds the judgment that makes her Tier 4 auditing sharper, which makes her Tier 1 delegations safer — a compounding loop. The professional who delegates Tier 1 and protects Tier 4–7 is not just more efficient. They are building an advantage that accumulates.

## What to do this week

Return to the list of ten tasks from Chapter 2. For each task, classify it using the tier framework:

- Tier 1 if the task is primarily pattern generation, summarization, variation, or formatting.
- Tier 4 if the task requires knowing when to distrust output or formulating the right problem.
- Tier 5 if the task requires causal or counterfactual reasoning.
- Tier 6 if the task requires navigating institutions, teams, or stakeholder relationships.
- Tier 7 if the task requires accountable judgment with genuine stakes.

If more than half of your tasks are Tier 1, your current task bundle is weighted toward work that is under AI pressure. That is not a crisis — it is a signal to restructure.

If you are currently delegating Tier 4, 5, 6, or 7 work to AI, take at least one of those tasks back this week. Not because AI cannot produce the output, but because doing the task yourself is what maintains the capability to evaluate the output when AI generates it.

## Key terms

| Tier | Name | AI status | Signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Pattern and Association | Superhuman | Delegate aggressively |
| 2 | Embodied and Sensorimotor | Constrained by physics | Protected where physical presence is required |
| 3 | Social and Personal | Simulates, does not replace | Protected where history and trust are load-bearing |
| 4 | Metacognitive and Supervisory | Cannot self-audit | The phase gate tier — most critical to protect |
| 5 | Causal and Counterfactual | Fluent parrot | Strategy, diagnosis, design: human work |
| 6 | Collective and Distributed | No skin in the game | Institutional navigation: human work |
| 7 | Existential and Wisdom | Not accountable | Decisions you sign your name to: irreducibly human |

**The load-bearing question for any task:** Which tier is this task at its core — and what is the minimum tier at which a human must remain present for this to be done responsibly?

## What would change my mind

**Tier 4:** I would revise this boundary if AI systems became reliably calibrated self-auditors — producing accurate uncertainty signals corresponding to actual epistemic states rather than to training data about what uncertain outputs look like. Current calibration research does not support this. [verify — check calibration literature for current frontier models]

**Tier 5:** I would revise this if AI systems demonstrated reliable causal reasoning on genuinely novel situations outside training distribution. Current systems do not distinguish correlation from causation in the way the Tier 5 definition requires. Pearl's causal hierarchy remains the right frame for this boundary.

**Tier 7:** I would revise this if a legal or regulatory framework emerged assigning genuine accountability to AI systems — not just liability to deployers, but accountability structured like professional accountability, with consequences falling on the decision-making entity. That framework does not exist.

## Still puzzling

- Chain-of-thought models produce text that looks like metacognition. Does it cross the Tier 4 boundary — is the uncertainty signal genuine, or is it trained mimicry of uncertain-sounding output?
- Can organizations distribute accountability (Tier 6) so widely across committees and review processes that no individual meaningfully holds Tier 7, and if so, what happens to professional responsibility?
- Which tier boundaries move fastest as AI becomes agentic — capable of multi-step action in the world rather than single-turn text generation? Tier 6 appears most exposed.
