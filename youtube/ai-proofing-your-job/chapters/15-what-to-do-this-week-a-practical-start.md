# Chapter 15 — What to Do This Week: A Practical Start

**One-line:** The framework is complete; this chapter is the Monday-morning implementation plan.

## Chapter overview

This is the book's landing. The framework has been built — the seven-tier taxonomy, the phase gate, the cognitive audit, the AI Use Discipline document. The case studies have shown what the division of labor looks like in practice across six industries. This chapter does one thing: it turns all of that into a specific sequence of actions that a professional can begin this week, without prerequisites, without waiting for a better moment.

The chapter is deliberately short on theory and long on sequence. If you want to revisit the mechanism, go back to Chapters 3 through 5. If you want to start, this is the chapter.

## Learning objectives

- Complete the six-step implementation sequence, starting Monday.
- Choose one task to delegate and one to reclaim this week.
- Schedule a monthly review of your AI Use Discipline.
- Know what to do when your employer's AI policy conflicts with your professional judgment.

## Opening case

A communications director named Priya finished reading a framework book about professional development on a Sunday evening, felt genuinely motivated, and went to bed planning to change things on Monday. On Tuesday she was in meetings. On Wednesday she had a deadline. By Friday the book was on her shelf and she had not implemented a single thing. She liked the book. She remembered the ideas. Nothing was different.

This is not a failure of motivation. It is a failure of specification. Priya had a goal — "use AI better" — but no implementation intention: the specific, context-anchored plan that Peter Gollwitzer's research shows is the difference between intentions that produce behavior change and intentions that do not. An implementation intention is not "I will work on this." It is "When I sit down at my desk Monday morning, I will open a blank document and write the ten tasks I spent the most time on last week." That specificity — the time, the place, the action — is what bridges the gap between reading a framework and changing your work.

This chapter is written as a set of implementation intentions. Each step names what you do, when you do it, and how long it takes. The sequence is designed for a professional who has a full workload and forty-five minutes of discretionary time this week. You do not need more than that to start.

## Core content

### 1. Why insight is not enough

The gap between understanding a framework and changing behavior is well-documented. Gollwitzer's 1999 work on implementation intentions showed that people who planned the specific when, where, and how of a behavior were significantly more likely to follow through than people who only formed a goal-level intention. The mechanism is that implementation intentions are stored as action-triggering mental links — when the context occurs, the action fires — rather than as general aspirations that must be actively retrieved and applied under pressure.

Lally et al.'s 2010 research on habit formation found that new behaviors take repeated context-specific practice to become habitual — and that the first repetition is the hardest because it requires the most deliberate attention. The value of the first week of implementation is not the outcome. It is making the behavior happen once, in context, so that it has a foundation to build on.

Wrzesniewski and Dutton's 2001 work on job crafting supports the idea that professionals can redesign their own task boundaries without waiting for organizational permission. You do not need your employer to approve a cognitive audit. You do not need your organization to mandate an AI Use Discipline before you write one. The task boundary between what AI does in your work and what you do is yours to specify.

The common failure mode for framework books is that readers understand the framework but leave implementation as a second step they get to "when things settle down." Things do not settle down. The sequence in this chapter is designed to happen during the work week as it actually is — not during a hypothetical future week with more margin.

### 2. The six-step sequence

The sequence below is designed to take approximately forty-five minutes of active work, distributed across one week. The order matters: each step builds the input for the next.

---

**Step 1 — The task list (Monday, 15 minutes)**

Open a blank document. Write the ten tasks you spend the most time on in a typical work week. Not job description language — actual tasks. Not "strategic planning" — "the weekly report I write that summarizes project status for the director." Not "client relations" — "the calls I take when a client has a problem and needs to be talked through it."

Specificity is the point. Generic task descriptions produce generic audits. Specific task descriptions produce specific delegation and protection decisions.

This is the raw material for everything that follows.

---

**Step 2 — The tier classification (Monday or Tuesday, 15 minutes)**

For each of your ten tasks, assign a tier from the taxonomy in Chapter 3. You do not need to agonize over exact tier numbers. The meaningful classification is binary: is this task primarily pattern-matching, summarizing, formatting, or generating variations (Tier 1, delegate), or does it require plausibility auditing, causal reasoning, institutional navigation, or personal accountability (Tiers 4–7, protect)?

If you have not read Chapter 3, the shorthand question is: would I know if the AI's output was wrong before I could verify it? If yes — if you have the domain expertise to catch the error — the task has significant Tier 4–7 content, and it belongs in your protection scope. If no — if you would only know the output was wrong after checking it against a source — the task is more fully inside AI's frontier.

Circle the Tier 1 tasks. These are your delegation candidates. Put a star next to the tasks with the highest Tier 4–7 content — these are your compounding tasks, the ones where doing them yourself builds the judgment that makes all your other work better.

---

**Step 3 — The AI Use Discipline (Tuesday or Wednesday, 30 minutes)**

Write your one-page AI Use Discipline using the template from Chapter 14. Use your ten tasks and the tier classification to populate it:

- Your delegation scope comes from the Tier 1 tasks you circled.
- Your protection scope comes from the starred Tier 4–7 tasks.
- Your handoff conditions should reference the specific verification step for each delegated task.
- Your data boundary should reflect your employer's policy if one exists, and a conservative default if it does not.
- Your accountability statement should be one sentence that you could say out loud to a client or a manager without discomfort.

Thirty minutes is enough to produce a working first draft. It will not be perfect. It does not need to be perfect. It needs to be specific enough to use.

Print it or keep it visible on your screen. You will revise it after you use it.

---

**Step 4 — Delegate one Tier 1 task (Wednesday or Thursday)**

Pick one task from your circled Tier 1 list. Not the most important one — pick one that is low-stakes enough that if the AI output is wrong, the cost of catching it is low. Delegate it this week.

Before you open the AI tool, read your AI Use Discipline. Confirm that this task is in your delegation scope. Write a one-sentence specification of what you want the output to accomplish. Use the tool. Apply your handoff condition before accepting the output.

Then record one sentence: what did you verify, and what did you find?

This is not about efficiency. It is about doing the practice once, in context, so you know what it feels like when it is working correctly. The first delegation is a training run, not a production run.

---

**Step 5 — Reclaim one Tier 4–7 task (Thursday or Friday)**

Pick one task you have been delegating to AI — or avoiding, or compressing into a quick review — that belongs in your protection scope. Do it this week without AI assistance.

This is the deliberate friction practice described in Chapter 12. Ericsson et al.'s research on deliberate practice shows that expertise requires scheduled friction — the tasks that maintain the judgment, not just the tasks that produce the output. If you have been letting AI draft your client assessments, write one by hand this week. If you have been letting AI structure your analysis, structure it yourself first.

The output may be slower. It may be less polished. That is not the point. The point is that doing the task yourself once a week keeps the underlying capability accessible — keeps you able to catch the AI's errors because you still know how to do the work yourself.

Record one sentence: what did you notice that you would not have noticed if the AI had drafted this?

---

**Step 6 — Schedule the monthly review (Friday, 5 minutes)**

Open your calendar. Put a recurring 30-minute block on the last Friday of each month. Title it "AI Use Discipline Review."

In that block, you will answer three questions: Has my delegation scope changed based on what I learned this month? Have my handoff conditions held, or have I been skipping them under pressure? Do my protection scope decisions still reflect where my judgment actually matters?

Scheduling the review is the most important administrative step in the sequence because it converts the AI Use Discipline from a one-time exercise into a living document. A discipline document that is never reviewed becomes shelf-ware within two months. A document that is reviewed monthly becomes a record of how your practice is developing.

Put it on the calendar before you close this book.

### 3. The most common failure modes

**Failure mode 1: Writing the discipline document and not enforcing it.**

This is the most common failure mode. The document gets written, gets filed, and the next AI interaction happens exactly the way previous ones did — by feel, under deadline pressure, without opening the document first.

The fix is visibility. If the document is on your screen or printed next to your keyboard, you will see it when you open an AI tool. If it is in a folder you rarely open, it will not change your behavior. The document's physical location matters more than its content quality during the first month.

**Failure mode 2: Completing the audit and not acting on it.**

The task list gets written. The tiers get assigned. The classification reveals that you are doing more Tier 1 work yourself than you need to, and delegating more Tier 4–7 work than is safe. And then nothing changes.

This happens when the audit is treated as insight rather than as the input to a decision. The audit is not complete until you have named one task to delegate and one task to reclaim. Not "I should probably delegate some of these" — name the task. Name when you will do it.

**Failure mode 3: Trying to implement everything at once.**

The cognitive audit, the AI Use Discipline, the compounding practice, the monthly review, the deliberate friction exercises from Chapter 12 — readers who try to implement all of it simultaneously implement none of it. Behavior change research is consistent: smaller, more specific changes have higher completion rates than comprehensive transformation plans.

The six steps in this chapter are designed to be complete as a starting point, not as the full implementation. Do the six steps. Evaluate what happened. Add one more practice in month two.

**Failure mode 4: Waiting for conditions that are not prerequisites.**

"I'll do the cognitive audit when I have a slower week." "I'll write the discipline document once I know what tools we're using at work." "I'll start the monthly review once the current project wraps."

None of these conditions are prerequisites. The cognitive audit takes fifteen minutes and can be done on a normal Tuesday. The discipline document can be written before your employer has an AI policy — that is the point of it. The monthly review can start before the practice is fully formed.

The only condition that matters is that you start before the motivation from reading this book dissipates. That window is approximately seventy-two hours.

### 4. When employer policy conflicts with your professional judgment

Most employers are still developing their AI use policies. In 2025, a large number of organizations have issued guidance that is either too broad ("use AI responsibly") or too restrictive ("no AI in client communications") to map cleanly onto individual professional practice. And some employers have explicit policies that conflict with what a domain expert would specify as the right phase gate.

If your employer's AI policy is more restrictive than your AI Use Discipline, you follow the employer policy. This is not a principled argument — it is a practical one. Employer policies that restrict AI use are rarely trying to harm your professional judgment; they are usually trying to manage liability and data security risks that are real. Compliance with a cautious employer policy is compatible with practicing the cognitive audit, building your compounding task practice, and developing your evidence portfolio. None of those require using AI tools in ways your employer has prohibited.

If your employer's AI policy is less restrictive than your AI Use Discipline — if your employer allows AI use that your professional judgment says should have a tighter gate — you do not have a compliance problem. You have a professional judgment call. Your discipline document specifies the gate you hold for your work, regardless of what the employer policy permits. You are not obligated to delegate up to the limit of what policy allows. The policy defines the ceiling. Your discipline defines where you actually stand.

If there is a genuine conflict — your employer is mandating AI use in a situation where your professional judgment says the gate should be stricter — document the professional concern. Write a short note to yourself, or to your manager if the relationship allows, describing what you believe the right phase gate is and why. Then comply with the policy while looking for a permitted way to preserve the judgment task. Most professional accountability frameworks — legal, medical, financial advisory, architectural — include provisions for documented professional dissent. Using those provisions is not insubordination. It is practicing the Tier 7 accountability that makes domain expertise valuable.

## Worked example: one week

Here is what the six steps look like for a specific professional — a marketing manager named Daniel, twelve years in brand communications at a consumer goods company — working through the sequence.

**Monday, 8:30 a.m.** Daniel writes his ten tasks: weekly campaign performance report for the director, client briefing documents for new product launches, competitive analysis summaries, ad copy variations for A/B testing, brand voice review of AI-generated social content, vendor communication and scheduling, the monthly brand strategy memo that goes to leadership, approval decisions on visual assets, crisis response communications, and weekly team check-in facilitation.

**Tuesday, before lunch.** Daniel classifies. The campaign performance report: mostly data formatting and summarization — Tier 1, delegate. Competitive analysis summaries: Tier 1 if the sources are defined, but he typically adds a market interpretation layer — mixed, delegate the summary, protect the interpretation. A/B copy variations: Tier 1, delegate. Brand voice review of AI-generated content: Tier 4 — this is plausibility auditing, and it requires knowing the brand well enough to catch off-voice content. Protect. The monthly brand strategy memo: Tier 5-7 — causal reasoning about where the brand should go. Protect and star. Crisis response communications: Tier 7 — accountability, timeliness, client-specific. Never delegate the substance. Vendor communication and scheduling: Tier 1, delegate.

**Wednesday morning, 45 minutes.** Daniel writes his AI Use Discipline. Delegation scope: performance reports, competitive summaries (data layer), copy variations, scheduling, vendor communications. Protection scope: brand strategy memo, crisis communications, visual asset approval, brand voice reviews. Handoff condition for performance reports: "I verify that the numbers match the source data before the report goes to the director." Handoff condition for copy variations: "I evaluate each variation against the brief objective before approving it for testing." Data boundary: no unreleased product information, no consumer research data outside the approved internal system. Accountability statement: "All communications and recommendations that reach clients or leadership carry my professional judgment and my name." He prints it and tapes it to his monitor.

**Wednesday afternoon.** Daniel uses AI to draft the weekly performance report. He reads his discipline document before opening the tool. He writes a one-sentence specification: "Summarize campaign performance from the attached data for the last two weeks, focusing on the three metrics in the brief." He reviews the output, checks the numbers against the source data (his handoff condition), corrects one figure the AI pulled from the wrong week, and sends the report. He records: "Caught a data-pull error — the AI used the previous period's figures for one metric."

**Friday morning.** Daniel writes the monthly brand strategy memo himself, without opening any AI tool. It takes longer than his usual practice. He notices that his thinking is slower and more specific than when he lets AI structure the argument first — and he notices two strategic questions that the AI's usual framework would have skimmed past. He records: "Found two questions about competitive positioning that I would not have surfaced if AI had framed the analysis."

**Friday, 4:45 p.m.** Daniel opens his calendar and sets a recurring 30-minute block on the last Friday of each month: "AI Use Discipline Review."

Total active time: approximately 50 minutes, distributed across the week. The campaign performance report is running faster. The brand strategy memo is running deeper. The discipline document is visible on his monitor. The review is on the calendar.

That is a week's work. Not a transformation — a start.

## Key terms

- **Implementation intention:** A specific, context-anchored plan of the form "When [situation X] occurs, I will do [action Y]." Gollwitzer's research shows that implementation intentions are substantially more effective at producing behavior change than goal-level intentions alone. "I will use AI better" is a goal. "When I open an AI tool on Monday morning, I will read my AI Use Discipline before typing the first prompt" is an implementation intention.

- **Job crafting:** Wrzesniewski and Dutton's term for the process by which professionals proactively reshape the task, relational, and cognitive boundaries of their work. Relevant here because the AI Use Discipline document is an act of job crafting: you are specifying which tasks belong to you and which belong to the tools, without waiting for the organization to specify it for you.

- **Monthly review:** The recurring practice of revisiting your AI Use Discipline to answer three questions: Has my delegation scope changed? Have my handoff conditions held under pressure? Do my protection scope decisions still reflect where my judgment actually matters? The review converts a one-time document into a living practice.

- **Compounding task:** A task where doing it yourself builds the judgment that makes your other work better — not just produces the output, but develops the capability. Compounding tasks are your highest-value protected tasks. They are the reason the protection scope exists. Reclaiming one compounding task per week, and doing it without AI, is the maintenance practice for the expertise that earns the premium.

## What would change my mind

I would revise this chapter if evidence emerged that professionals reliably changed AI-use behavior after reading frameworks without specific implementation steps — that insight alone produced durable behavior change. Most behavior-change research, across multiple domains and methodologies, says it does not. If that evidence reversed, the argument for specificity and scheduling would need to be reconsidered.

I would also revise the employer-policy section if professional accountability frameworks evolved to give employers greater legitimate authority over individual professional judgment in AI contexts. The current section assumes that professional accountability frameworks (legal, medical, financial advisory) maintain the primacy of individual professional judgment even when employer policy is permissive. If those frameworks shift, the section should reflect it.

---

## Still puzzling

- What support do readers need when they lack the autonomy to redesign their task boundaries — in highly constrained roles, highly structured organizations, or fields with tight regulatory control? The framework assumes a degree of professional autonomy that not every reader will have. The implementation sequence needs a lower-autonomy variant.
- How should teams coordinate individual AI Use Discipline documents? If ten people on a team each have a slightly different protection scope, the team's collective accountability boundary becomes unclear. There is probably a team-level layer needed above the individual document — a shared phase gate for team outputs — but this book does not address team-level implementation.
- Which metric best shows the framework is working? Word count of evidence portfolio entries? Number of AI errors caught? Speed of compounding task performance over time? The framework predicts that protected judgment builds, but identifying how to measure that buildup for an individual professional remains open.
