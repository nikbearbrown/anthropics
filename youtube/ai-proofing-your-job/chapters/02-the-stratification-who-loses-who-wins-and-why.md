# Chapter 2 — The Stratification: Who Loses, Who Wins, and Why

**One-line:** AI exposure does not predict displacement: tasks inside the jagged frontier contract, tasks outside it expand, and mid-career experts must know which is which.

## Chapter overview

The previous chapter established that the displacement narrative is too blunt to be useful. This chapter makes the stratification pattern concrete. The same AI tools that are eliminating certain roles are creating expanded demand for others — and the difference is not industry, not seniority in the abstract, and not whether someone uses AI. The difference is task composition: which tasks are inside the jagged frontier (where AI performs well) and which are outside it (where human judgment expands in value). By the end of this chapter, you will be able to explain why two professionals in the same field with the same tools can be on opposite career trajectories, apply task-level thinking to your own role, and identify which of your regular work tasks are likely to contract in value and which are likely to grow.

## Learning objectives

- Explain the jagged technological frontier and what it means for your specific work.
- Apply task-level exposure thinking to professional work.
- Identify which tasks in your role are likely to contract or expand under AI pressure.

## Two analysts, opposite trajectories

James and Sarah joined the same financial services firm within a year of each other. They have similar titles, similar salaries, and, as of eighteen months ago, access to the same AI-assisted analysis tools.

James's week looks like this: he runs the standard market summary reports each morning using templates that the AI fills from structured data feeds. He generates the required charts, formats the client-facing documents to the house standard, and sends them by ten o'clock. By noon he is working through the next set of summaries. His output volume is up. His manager notices the efficiency. James has found his rhythm.

Sarah's week looks different. She spends less time on the summaries — she lets the AI handle those — and more time on the edge cases the templates were never designed for. A long-term client's portfolio contains a position that the standard model is misclassifying because of a technical accounting adjustment; Sarah knows this because she has been watching the same sector for nine years and the number looks wrong. A second client is asking whether to rebalance in light of a development that is not yet reflected in any of the data feeds; Sarah is writing the analysis from scratch because there is no template for a situation without precedent. A third situation requires explaining to a nervous client, over the phone, why the model's recommendation is correct even though it feels counterintuitive given recent news. That conversation cannot be templated.

By Friday, James looks more productive on every quantitative metric the firm tracks. Sarah looks less productive. She has produced fewer documents and logged fewer completed tasks.

In eighteen months, when the firm reorganizes in response to the AI tools' maturation, James's role is reclassified. The summaries he was producing are now generated automatically. The value-add the firm once attributed to his quick turnaround now belongs to the tool. Sarah's role is expanded. The firm needs more people who can handle the situations the tool cannot, and Sarah has been getting better at exactly those situations while James has been getting more efficient at the work the tool is taking over.

The AI tools did not create this divergence. They revealed it. The divergence was always there, in the task composition of each analyst's week.

## 1. The task-level lens

The conceptual foundation for understanding stratification is the task-level framework developed by David Autor, Frank Levy, and Richard Murnane in their 2003 Quarterly Journal of Economics paper, *The Skill Content of Recent Technological Change*.

Their central argument is that technology changes work by task content, not by occupation. Routine tasks — work that follows explicit rules, can be described in a procedure, and produces a verifiable output — are more exposed to automation than nonroutine tasks. Nonroutine tasks come in two varieties: nonroutine manual (physical tasks requiring adaptability, like a plumber fitting pipes in an old house) and nonroutine cognitive (judgment tasks requiring interpretation, inference, and context-sensitivity, like a financial analyst deciding which scenario a client's situation most closely resembles).

The task-level lens changes the question. Instead of asking "is my job at risk?" — a question that produces an occupation-level exposure score and a lot of anxiety — it asks: "which tasks in my job are routine, and which are nonroutine?" The exposure to automation is not uniform across a job. It is concentrated in the routine task component.

For knowledge workers in 2024 and 2025, "routine" has to be understood more broadly than it was in 2003. When Autor, Levy, and Murnane wrote, routine cognitive tasks meant things like bookkeeping, data entry, and rule-based sorting. Today, a large language model can handle a much wider range of cognitive tasks that would have counted as nonroutine in 2003: drafting professional communications, summarizing complex documents, generating plausible analysis from structured data, producing code from natural language specifications. The frontier has moved.

But the underlying logic has not changed. The tasks inside the frontier — where AI reliably performs well — are still the ones that follow recognizable patterns, operate on well-structured inputs, and can be evaluated against verifiable criteria. The tasks outside the frontier — where AI performs poorly or fails in ways that are hard to detect — still require the things that were always hardest to automate: contextual interpretation, causal reasoning, institutional knowledge, genuine accountability.

## 2. The jagged frontier, explained

The language of the "jagged technological frontier" comes from Fabrizio Dell'Acqua and colleagues' 2023 field experiment at Boston Consulting Group. Their finding — that AI tools improved performance dramatically inside the frontier and degraded it outside — introduced a metaphor that captures something the occupation-level exposure scores missed.

A smooth frontier would mean that AI capability improves uniformly as it gets more powerful. A software developer would be, say, 60 percent exposed today and 70 percent exposed next year. You could plan around a gradient.

The actual frontier is jagged. AI is superhuman at some tasks and below-human at others, often within the same professional domain, sometimes within the same document. A consultant's job might contain a task that an AI handles better than any human — synthesizing a large body of market research into a structured analysis — sitting directly next to a task where AI routinely fails — deciding whether the client's situation is actually similar enough to the cases in that market research for the analysis to apply. Both tasks happen in the same work session. One belongs to AI. One does not.

The jaggedness matters for two reasons. First, it means you cannot manage your AI exposure at the occupational level. You have to go task by task. A blanket rule — "I use AI for all my writing" or "I use AI for all my research" — will inevitably drag Tier 4–7 judgment tasks into AI handling because the categories ("writing," "research") span the frontier.

Second, and more insidiously, the jagged frontier means that the tasks where AI fails are often the tasks where its output looks most credible. AI is excellent at producing confident, well-structured text. It produces confident, well-structured text about situations it does not understand in the same format as confident, well-structured text about situations it handles correctly. The reader of the output — the professional reviewing the AI's work — must independently know enough to catch the difference. That is the metacognitive supervision task: Tier 4 in the taxonomy introduced in Chapter 3.

## 3. Inside the frontier: what contracts

The clearest current illustration of inside-frontier contraction comes from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Outlook Handbook. As of the 2024–2025 projections, "computer programmers" — defined by BLS as workers who translate software designs into working code, debug programs, and maintain existing systems — show employment projected to decline roughly 6 percent through 2034. [verify — check exact BLS figures and projection years before final publication] Meanwhile, "software developers" — defined as workers who design software applications, analyze user needs, and oversee development teams — show employment projected to grow approximately 15 percent through the same period.

Same field. Same fundamental industry. AI tools in both cases. Opposite trajectories.

The difference is task composition. A computer programmer's job, as BLS defines it, is concentrated in tasks that sit inside the jagged frontier: translating documented specifications into working code, testing against documented requirements, maintaining existing codebases according to established standards. These are rule-following tasks on well-structured inputs. AI code generation tools do exactly these tasks, often better and always faster than a human.

A software developer's job, as BLS defines it, is concentrated in tasks outside the frontier: understanding what a system needs to do (problem formulation), deciding how to structure the architecture (design judgment), working with non-technical stakeholders to clarify requirements (interpretation and communication), and owning the outcome when a system behaves unexpectedly in production (accountability and diagnosis). These tasks require the things AI cannot reliably supply.

The same pattern appears in marketing — where content production is contracting as AI handles first drafts at scale, while strategy and brief-writing are expanding. It appears in legal work — where document review and template drafting are compressing while negotiation, judgment, and advocacy are not. It appears in real estate — where standardized property analysis and listing descriptions are moving to AI, while the negotiation, the advice about a specific client's situation, and the fiduciary judgment about whether a particular deal serves a particular person's actual interests remain outside the frontier.

The pattern is not industry-specific. It is structural: wherever a job contains a significant routine cognitive task component, that component is under pressure regardless of what the rest of the job involves.

## 4. Outside the frontier: what expands

The expansion side of stratification is harder to see because it is less dramatic. Job titles do not change. Promotion cycles move slowly. The evidence shows up in salary data and in the composition of what successful mid-career professionals are actually spending their time on.

The Eloundou and colleagues 2023 working paper from OpenAI and the University of Pennsylvania, *GPTs are GPTs: An Early Look at the Labor Market Impact Potential of Large Language Models*, analyzed exposure at the task level using O*NET task statements. Their finding is useful but requires the caveat the research notes flag: exposure measures the potential of AI to touch a task, not the likelihood of AI replacing a worker. A task that is highly exposed to AI might still require a human who can specify what the AI should do, verify whether the output is right, and take responsibility for the consequences of using it. The human role changes; it does not disappear.

What expands outside the frontier is the demand for what might be called interpretive integration: the work of taking AI outputs — which are often individually correct in narrow ways — and determining whether they apply, what they mean, how they connect, and what to do because of them. This is not a single task type. It shows up differently in every field:

In medicine, a diagnostic AI system may flag a scan with high confidence. The physician's job is not just reading the flag — it is integrating the AI's observation with the patient's history, the patient's preferences, the clinical context, and the physician's own assessment of what the AI is likely to miss in this specific case. That integration is outside the frontier.

In law, contract review AI can identify non-standard clauses with high accuracy. The lawyer's job is assessing whether the non-standard clause matters for this client in this transaction, what the counterparty's intent is likely to be, and how to address it in negotiation. That judgment is outside the frontier.

In engineering, AI can generate code that passes all its tests. The senior engineer's job is knowing whether the tests cover the right cases, whether the architecture will hold up as the system scales, and what the code does in edge cases that nobody thought to test. That systems-level judgment is outside the frontier.

In all these cases, the AI has done real work. Its contribution is genuine. But the interpretive integration step — which requires domain expertise, contextual knowledge, and accountability — has not been automated. It has, in many cases, become more important because AI-generated outputs have multiplied the volume of material that needs integrating.

## 5. The diamond workforce: why entry-level matters for mid-career

The stratification pattern produces what economists sometimes call the "diamond workforce" shape. The bottom of the skill distribution — workers concentrated in routine task bundles, whether cognitive or manual — faces pressure from AI. The top of the distribution — workers concentrated in outside-frontier tasks — expands. The middle is where the interesting complexity lives, because middle-skill workers have mixed task bundles.

There is a second-order effect that mid-career professionals should understand even if it does not immediately apply to them. The contraction of entry-level and junior roles has implications for the mid-career professional over a five-to-ten-year horizon.

The professional judgment that makes a senior expert valuable was built through experience. That experience accumulated precisely because the professional spent years doing the "lower-level" tasks: running analyses, writing first drafts, doing the production work that is now being automated. The feedback from doing that work — getting it wrong, seeing the consequences, learning to calibrate — is what produced the expert intuition that now operates at the senior level.

If the junior task pipeline contracts significantly — if the tasks that used to train the next generation of analysts, developers, consultants, and designers are now handled by AI — then the pipeline producing the next generation of senior experts also changes. This is sometimes called the "apprenticeship ladder problem," and it is genuinely unresolved. [verify — check whether there is established research on this; it appears in commentaries but may not be in peer-reviewed literature yet]

For the current mid-career professional, the implication is not panic. It is awareness: the experience base you built through years of doing production work is genuinely valuable, precisely because the pathway that builds it is becoming less common. That makes your judgment — built the hard way, through experience — a scarcer input than it was a decade ago.

## 6. The task bundle problem in practice

The clearest practical failure mode is treating occupation-level exposure as an individual verdict.

If a labor market analysis says that "marketing managers" face moderate AI exposure, what does that mean for a specific marketing manager? Almost nothing without a task-level decomposition. One marketing manager might spend 70 percent of her time on content production, briefing writers, and campaign reporting — tasks that are heavily inside the frontier. Another marketing manager might spend 70 percent of his time on brand strategy, stakeholder alignment, and budget allocation — tasks that are largely outside the frontier. The occupation-level score applies poorly to both of them. It applies well to neither.

The Eloundou et al. O*NET analysis helps here because it goes to the task level. What it cannot do — and what only the individual professional can do — is tell you which tasks in your specific job bundle are routine and which are not. Standard O*NET task lists are descriptions of what occupations typically do, not what any specific professional actually does on Tuesday. The task audit is irreducibly personal.

A useful sorting exercise: take the ten tasks you do most frequently. For each task, ask two questions.

First: can the output be checked by a rule or a template? If a client rejects the output, can you explain why it was wrong in terms of explicit criteria? If yes, the task is probably near or inside the frontier.

Second: does accepting the output require knowledge you have that is not in the data — knowledge about this specific client, this specific institution, this specific market history, this specific professional context? If yes, the task is probably outside the frontier.

The tasks that fail the first test and pass the second are your protected tasks. They are the work where your expertise is genuinely irreplaceable — not because AI cannot generate an output, but because evaluating that output requires knowing something AI has no access to.

## Worked example: ten tasks, sorted

Consider a mid-career human resources professional — ten years in, director level at a mid-size company. Her ten most common tasks:

| Task | Inside/Outside frontier | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Generating first-draft job descriptions from hiring manager inputs | Inside | Follows established templates; output is verifiable against requirements |
| Screening resumes against role criteria | Inside (mostly) | Pattern matching against stated criteria; high volume |
| Reporting HR metrics to leadership | Inside | Data formatting and standard analysis |
| Advising a hiring manager on whether a candidate's salary expectation is reasonable | Outside | Requires knowledge of this company's bands, this market's current dynamics, this hiring manager's constraints |
| Handling an employee relations situation involving a performance dispute | Outside | Requires reading interpersonal context, institutional history, legal exposure, management relationships |
| Designing the compensation structure for a new role | Outside | Requires strategic judgment, internal equity knowledge, market data interpretation |
| Scheduling and coordinating interview panels | Inside | Logistics and communication templates |
| Deciding whether to escalate a situation to legal | Outside | Requires judgment, risk tolerance, institutional knowledge, accountability |
| Summarizing candidate interview notes | Inside | Synthesis of structured inputs |
| Advising a senior leader on a restructuring's human impact | Outside | Requires knowledge of individuals, relationships, organizational culture — things that are not in any dataset |

Five inside, five outside — roughly. The HR professional who spends most of her time on the inside-frontier tasks is efficiently doing work that will face significant compression. The one who manages the transition to spending most of her time on the outside-frontier tasks is positioned well.

The lesson is not that inside-frontier tasks should be abandoned now. They still need to be done, and judgment about which AI output is acceptable still requires someone who understands the task. The lesson is that the career value is concentrating in the outside-frontier tasks, and professionals who are building and maintaining those capabilities are accumulating advantage while professionals who are not are accumulating risk.

## What to do this week

List your ten most common work tasks. For each one, apply the two-question test: Can the output be checked by a rule or template? Does accepting the output require knowledge specific to your context that is not in any dataset?

Circle the tasks that fail the first test and pass the second. Those are outside the frontier. Those are the tasks that will expand in value as AI handles more of the inside-frontier work.

If fewer than three tasks circle, you are in a task bundle that is heavily concentrated inside the frontier. That is not a permanent verdict — it is a signal to deliberately restructure your time toward the outside-frontier work that your seniority positions you to do.

## Bridge

We now know that stratification is real, that it operates at the task level, and that the inside/outside frontier distinction is the relevant variable. What we need next is a vocabulary precise enough to actually classify tasks — a map we can use for our own work, not just a description of the pattern. That vocabulary is the seven-tier taxonomy.

---

## Key terms

**Task bundle:** The mix of distinct task types inside one job title. Two workers with the same title can have very different task bundles — and therefore very different exposure to AI pressure — depending on how their actual work is structured.

**Inside the frontier:** Work AI can handle with bounded risk and verifiable output: pattern-following, routine cognitive operations that produce outputs checkable against explicit criteria. The work is real and necessary; it is also the work that compresses in labor-market value as AI tools handle it faster and cheaper.

**Outside the frontier:** Work requiring contextual judgment, causal reasoning, institutional knowledge, or genuine accountability — tasks where the evaluative standard is not an explicit rule but an understanding of what the situation actually requires. This is where human expertise expands in value under AI pressure.

**Stratification:** Unequal outcomes within the same occupation or field, driven by differences in task composition rather than the presence or absence of AI tools. Two people in the same field with access to the same tools can be on opposite career trajectories because of what they are spending their time on.

**Occupational exposure:** An estimate of how much of an occupation's task bundle AI could potentially handle, given current AI capabilities. Useful as a rough map of pressure; misleading when treated as a verdict about an individual professional's replacability.

## What would change my mind

I would revise this chapter if occupation-level AI exposure metrics became reliable predictors of individual wage and employment outcomes without task-level decomposition. The current evidence still requires the task-level step — exposure scores routinely fail to predict which workers within an occupation fare well or poorly. If that changed, the task-bundle argument would lose much of its practical value.

I would also revise the apprenticeship ladder claim if evidence emerged that AI tools are effective at building professional judgment in junior workers — that someone can develop expert intuition through AI-augmented work as effectively as through doing the work directly. The current evidence on skill acquisition under AI assistance suggests the opposite direction [verify — check Bastani et al. or related work on AI and learning in context], but this is an active research area.

## Still puzzling

- How should workers classify tasks when their employer's metrics reward inside-frontier production volume, creating a structural incentive to do the work that is least career-protective?
- Which junior tasks must remain human specifically because doing them builds the pattern recognition that makes senior judgment possible — and which can be safely automated without sacrificing the apprenticeship function?
- How often should a professional remap the frontier? The jagged boundary is advancing; a task outside the frontier today may be inside it in two years. What is a reasonable audit cadence?
- Are there fields where the frontier is advancing so quickly that the "outside-frontier" work is also under pressure, and where does that leave the professionals in those fields?
