# Applied Ethics: When Philosophy Meets Real Stakes

## TL;DR

Applied ethics is the discipline that emerges when philosophical principles meet specific human dilemmas under real consequences. It grew as an academic field in response to documented failures—most notably the Tuskegee Syphilis Study—and now works to anticipate and reason through the moral stakes of medicine, technology, business, and our relationship to the living world.

---

## Chapter Opening: The 1972 Revelation

In 1965, Peter Buxtun, an investigator for the U.S. Public Health Service, opened a file cabinet in a federal office and began reading records. What he found was a 33-year-old experiment that had been running in the background of American medicine since 1932. The U.S. Public Health Service had recruited 399 Black sharecroppers from rural Alabama—men who already had latent syphilis, a bacterially transmitted infection that progresses slowly without treatment—and told them they were receiving free medical care. They were not told they had syphilis. They were not told they were part of an experiment. They were told they had "bad blood," a euphemism the recruiters knew would mean different things to different people. The actual aim was to document how the disease progressed in Black men compared to White men. The study needed untreated subjects to observe the disease's natural course.

By 1947, penicillin had become the standard cure for syphilis. It was cheap, it was effective, it was available. The men in the Tuskegee study did not receive it. They continued to receive no treatment. Some died. Many developed neurosyphilis, a condition that can destroy the nervous system. Wives were infected. Children were born with congenital syphilis. The experiment continued.

In 1965, when Buxtun read those files, he tried to raise an alarm inside the institution. Supervisors told him the study would continue until all the men were dead and could be autopsied. Buxtun waited seven years. In 1972, he leaked the documents to a journalist. On July 25, 1972, Jean Heller's story broke in the *Washington Star*. The next day, the Associated Press ran it nationally. Within months, the federal study was halted. The men, who had been lied to for four decades, sued. The settlement reached $10 million—roughly $25,000 per man, per year of deception and damage.

The Tuskegee Syphilis Study became the hinge on which American bioethics turned. Within two years of the public revelation, Congress passed the National Research Act (1974). Within four years, a blue-ribbon commission had issued the Belmont Report (1979), which established four foundational principles for all research involving human subjects: respect for persons (informed consent, protection of privacy), beneficence (maximize benefit, minimize harm), nonmaleficence (do no harm), and justice (fair distribution of research benefits and burdens). These principles did not emerge from philosophical theory alone. They emerged from a government that had finally been caught conducting 40 years of medical torture in plain sight, and a public that demanded a reckoning.

Applied ethics as an academic discipline began in that reckoning. It is what happens when philosophical reasoning meets real harm under real stakes. It is not a set of answers. It is a discipline for asking the right questions when the stakes are not hypothetical.

## Concept 1: Bioethics—When Does Life Begin, and When Does It End?

### Cold Open

Karen Ann Quinlan was 21 years old when she stopped breathing in 1975. She had taken alcohol and tranquilizers at a party. Exactly what happened is unclear; she may have aspirated her own vomit, or her breathing may have simply stopped. She was rushed to the hospital in a coma. She remained in a coma for months. Brain scans showed permanent damage to the cerebral cortex—the part of the brain that makes a person conscious. She was breathing only because a machine was doing the work for her. Her parents asked the hospital to remove the machine. The hospital refused. The parents sued. The case went to the New Jersey Supreme Court.

The question the court faced was straightforward in form but impossible in substance: Who decides when life should end? The court ruled that the right to refuse life-sustaining treatment was a constitutional right rooted in the right to privacy. The machine could be removed. Karen Ann Quinlan was removed from the ventilator in 1976. She continued to breathe on her own—contrary to expectations—and lived nine more years in a persistent vegetative state, never regaining consciousness, until an infection killed her in 1985. The case itself was over, but the question remained: What counts as a life worth preserving? Who gets to decide?

### Mechanism: Personhood, Autonomy, and the Four Principles

Bioethics addresses ethical issues that emerge in medicine, biology, and biotechnology. The core questions it asks are about personhood, autonomy, and the distribution of medical goods. Where does personhood begin? Where does it end? Who decides what counts as a life? The answers matter because they determine who gets protected by rights, who gets included in moral consideration, and whose interests count when there is a conflict.

Mary Anne Warren, writing in 1973, proposed that personhood is not a property that appears suddenly at some magic moment. Instead, she identified five criteria that cluster together in paradigmatically human beings: consciousness and the capacity to feel pain; reasoning and the capacity to solve new problems; self-awareness and self-conception; self-motivated activity that is self-directed; the capacity to communicate messages whose content is indefinite and not limited in topic or type. A fetus, she argued, does not meet any of these criteria. An adult human meets all five. A newborn meets some but not all. Warren's move is to specify what "personhood" actually means before arguing about who has it. Once you see what the word does—it tracks entities that can suffer, reason, know themselves, act on their own decisions, and communicate—you can have a different conversation about abortion. It's no longer abstract. It's about whether a particular organism at a particular stage has these capacities.

But Warren's specification does something else too. If you define personhood by these capacities, you run into immediate problems. A person in a permanent coma does not have consciousness. A person with severe dementia may lose self-awareness. A person with locked-in syndrome cannot communicate. If personhood requires these capacities, then people who lose them—through accident, age, or illness—would no longer be persons, and their rights could be stripped away. This is a genuine tension in Warren's view, and it reveals why applied ethics must be constantly revisited. The same move that protects abortion rights (show the fetus lacks these capacities) threatens the rights of people in specific medical states.

Don Marquis, responding to Warren in 1989, took a different approach. He argued that what makes killing wrong is not that it harms a person—it's that it deprives an entity of a future. A fetus has a future. It has potential. It will, if uninterrupted, develop into a being with all the capacities Warren describes. That potential itself is valuable. Killing a fetus deprives it of that future. This is what makes abortion wrong—not something metaphysical about ensoulment or the moment of conception, but the simple fact that you have ended a trajectory.

The trade-off between these views is explicit: Warren privileges capacities present now; Marquis privileges potential futures. Warren's view protects medical autonomy and a person's right to decide about their own body. Marquis's view protects developing life. Both are trying to honor something real. They cannot both be fully satisfied simultaneously.

The Belmont Report's four principles cut across this tension by refusing to pick one winner. Instead, it says: when you're making a decision about a human being's medical care, you must simultaneously honor autonomy (their right to choose), beneficence (your duty to help them), nonmaleficence (your duty not to harm them), and justice (fair distribution of benefits and burdens). These principles often conflict. Applied ethics is the discipline of making those conflicts visible and reasoning through them.

### Trade-off: Autonomy vs. Beneficence

When an elderly patient refuses a lifesaving intervention—a blood transfusion, a surgery that would extend life—what do you do? Respect their autonomy and let them refuse, or override their choice because you believe you know what will benefit them? Medical ethics says: respect autonomy. But that choice is only possible if autonomy itself has been elevated as a principle worth protecting. In other medical traditions, beneficence (doing what's best for the patient) might win. Applied ethics makes this trade-off visible and forces the reasoning to be explicit.

### Worked Example: Nancy Cruzan's Feeding Tube

Nancy Cruzan was 25 years old when a car accident left her in a persistent vegetative state in 1983. Like Karen Ann Quinlan, she was not conscious. Unlike Quinlan, she was not on a ventilator. She was being fed through a tube inserted through her nose into her stomach. Her parents asked that the tube be removed so she could die. The hospital refused. The case went to the U.S. Supreme Court.

The Supreme Court's 1990 decision in *Cruzan v. Director, Missouri Department of Health* ruled that the right to refuse life-sustaining medical treatment—including artificial nutrition and hydration—was a constitutionally protected liberty interest. But here's the crucial move: the Court said that while a competent person could refuse treatment, an incompetent person could only refuse treatment if there was "clear and convincing evidence" of what they would have wanted. The burden of proof was placed on the family. Nancy Cruzan's feeding tube was removed. She died 12 days later.

This case works through the tension between autonomy and beneficence. Nancy Cruzan could not choose for herself. Her parents chose for her, based on their understanding of her values. The Court required extraordinary proof that this was what she would have wanted. The principle at work was: autonomy is valuable, but when autonomy cannot be exercised, we must be very cautious about making decisions on someone else's behalf.

### Common Misconception: "Brain Death = No Personhood"

Many people think that if someone is brain-dead, they have no moral status and it's obviously right to remove them from life support. But brain death is not the only state of unconsciousness. A person in a coma may recover. A person in a persistent vegetative state will not recover consciousness but is still alive and still has a heartbeat and brain stem function. A person in a minimally conscious state might show occasional signs of awareness. These are not the same condition. Applied ethics requires you to be precise about what state a person is actually in before you can reason about what to do. The mere fact that someone is unconscious does not automatically settle anything. You still must ask: What did this person value? What would they have wanted? Do their interests still matter now that they cannot express them? These are not medical questions. They are ethical questions, and they require different reasoning.

## Concept 2: Animal and Environmental Ethics—Do Other Lives Matter?

### Cold Open

In 1995, the Northern Rockies Ecosystem Protection Act proposed reintroducing wolves to Yellowstone National Park and central Idaho. For decades, wolves had been hunted to near extinction. Ranchers whose cattle and sheep grazed on public lands would now have to contend with a predator. Some cattle would be killed. Some ranchers would go out of business. The wolves themselves had no voice in the decision. The native Shoshone and Nez Perce, whose lands were being discussed, had limited voice. The decision was made by federal agencies and shaped by legal arguments about whose interests mattered—the interests of the livestock industry, the interests of the ecosystem (abstracted as "ecological health"), the interests of the predator species itself. Applied ethics had to ask: Who counts? Whose flourishing matters?

### Mechanism: From Instrumental to Intrinsic Value

For most of Western history, the natural world was understood to have instrumental value only—value as a means to human ends. A forest had value if it could be logged. An animal had value if it could be hunted or eaten. A river had value if it could power a mill. This view is anthropocentric: human beings are the only things with intrinsic value (value in themselves), and everything else matters only insofar as it serves human purposes.

Peter Singer's 1975 book *Animal Liberation* challenged this view directly. Singer argued that the capacity to suffer is what matters morally. If a being can suffer, its suffering counts morally. A pig can suffer. A cow can suffer. A chicken can suffer. Therefore, their suffering counts morally in the same way that human suffering counts. The fact that they are not human is irrelevant—it's species prejudice, a form of bias as morally arbitrary as racial prejudice. Singer called this "speciesism." Once you see it, you cannot unsee it. If you believe suffering matters morally (and most people do), then you cannot exclude a being from moral consideration just because it belongs to a different species. What matters is the capacity to suffer, not the species membership.

Tom Regan, writing in *The Case for Animal Rights* (1983), pushed further. He argued that animals are not just beings that can suffer; they are "subjects-of-a-life." They have beliefs, desires, memory, a sense of the future, an ability to initiate action, a sense of self, and the capacity to suffer. Beings with these capacities have inherent worth. They deserve to be treated as ends in themselves, not merely as means. This is a stronger claim than Singer's. It's not just that animal suffering matters; it's that animals have rights.

Aldo Leopold, in *A Sand County Almanac* (1949), proposed something different still: an environmental ethic that extends moral consideration to ecosystems, not just individual animals. He argued that "a thing is right when it tends to preserve the integrity, stability, and beauty of the biotic community; it is wrong when it tends otherwise." This shifts the unit of moral consideration from the individual animal to the system. A decision might harm individual wolves but benefit the ecosystem as a whole. An environmental ethic rooted in Leopold's land ethic would ask: what maintains the health of the system?

Arne Naess, a Norwegian philosopher, formalized this in "deep ecology" (1973). He argued that all living things have intrinsic value—not just animals, not just beings that can suffer, but all of nature. Humans are not separate from nature; we are part of an interconnected whole. Our actions ripple through this whole. Our moral obligation is to recognize our place within the system and act in ways that preserve and protect the diversity and integrity of life.

### Trade-off: Individual Rights vs. Ecosystem Health

These frameworks conflict directly. Singer cares about reducing animal suffering. Leopold cares about ecosystem integrity. These can point in opposite directions. If wolves are reintroduced to Yellowstone, some individual wolves will suffer (they will be hunted, will starve, will fight). But the ecosystem will be more biodiverse and resilient. If you protect individual wolf rights, you cannot cull wolves to manage population. If you prioritize ecosystem health, individual wolves become expendable for the sake of the system. Deep ecology says all lives matter equally; individual action matters less than system health. These are genuine tensions. Applied ethics does not resolve them; it makes them visible and forces the reasoning to be explicit.

### Worked Example: The Wolf Reintroduction Conflict

When gray wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone in 1995, livestock losses were initially a major concern. Over 25 years, Yellowstone wolves have killed approximately 40-50 cattle and sheep per year (out of millions on nearby ranches). The ecosystem benefits have been documented: vegetation recovery, changes in elk behavior, increased scavenging by other species from wolf kills. Some ranchers have been financially harmed. Some endangered ecosystems have recovered.

Now ask the question using Singer's framework: The wolves' right to live and the livestock's right not to be killed both matter morally. The decision to reintroduce wolves causes some animals to suffer (wolves killed by ranchers, livestock killed by wolves, some wolves that starve). Did the decision respect everyone's interests?

Now ask it using Leopold's framework: The reintroduction has increased ecosystem integrity and stability. That's what matters. The suffering of individual animals is secondary to the health of the system.

Now ask it using Regan's framework: Wolves are subjects-of-a-life. Cattle are subjects-of-a-life. Both have inherent worth. The decision favors wolves over cattle. Is that justified?

Each framework directs your moral attention differently. Applied ethics requires you to see the conflict and reason through which considerations matter most in this specific case.

### Common Misconception: "Nature Is a Balance That Must Be Preserved"

Many people think ecosystems are in perfect balance and that human intervention always makes things worse. But ecosystems are dynamic, not static. Climate change, invasive species, and human habitat destruction have already altered every major ecosystem on Earth. The "original" state that conservationists often want to restore may itself have been shaped by indigenous management practices that colonizers erased. There is no pristine nature to return to. Applied ethics requires you to ask: what state are we aiming for, and why? Who decides? What trade-offs are we making?

## Concept 3: Technology, Business, and Global Justice—Who Bears the Burden?

### Cold Open

In 1970, Milton Friedman published an essay in the *New York Times* titled "The Social Responsibility of Business Is to Increase Its Profits." He argued that corporations have one obligation: to maximize shareholder returns. This became the cornerstone of modern corporate theory. Everything else—labor conditions, environmental impact, community health—is the government's job to regulate. Business's job is profit.

In the same year, a different question emerged: What obligation does a wealthy person have to a stranger in extreme poverty? Peter Singer posed it as a thought experiment: Imagine you pass a shallow pond. A child is drowning in it. You could wade in and pull the child out, but you would ruin your clothes and shoes. Do you have an obligation to save the child? Everyone says yes. Your expensive shoes do not matter; a child's life does. Now imagine the child is not in front of you but in Bangladesh, and the cost of saving the child (through effective aid organizations) is the same—the price of your shoes. Do you have the same obligation? Singer argued you do. The distance and the abstract nature of the relationship does not change the moral math.

These two questions—Friedman's about what corporations owe shareholders, Singer's about what the wealthy owe the poor—define the tensions in applied ethics around technology, business, and global justice.

### Mechanism: Stakeholder Theory and Institutional Justice

Friedman's position is that corporations are profit-maximizing entities and should stay in their lane. The government regulates them. End of story. But this has a problem: governments are often captured by the industries they regulate, or they move slowly while technology moves fast. By the time a regulation catches up, the damage is done.

Stakeholder theory, developed in contrast to Friedman's shareholder primacy, argues that corporations have obligations not just to shareholders but to all stakeholders—employees, customers, communities, the environment. This is more complicated but arguably more honest about what corporations actually do. A corporation that pollutes affects neighbors. A corporation that pays poverty wages affects workers and their families. A corporation that deploys a biased algorithm affects people it has never met but whose lives it changes. If you acknowledge this impact, you cannot say the corporation has no responsibility for it.

Thomas Pogge, in *World Poverty and Human Rights* (2002), posed the problem in terms of institutions. He argued that the global institutional order—trade agreements, intellectual property regimes, currency systems, borders—is not neutral. It actively maintains poverty and inequality. We (citizens of wealthy nations, beneficiaries of these institutions) are not just failing to help the poor; we are actively harming them through the systems we benefit from. This is different from Singer's drowning child. It's not about individual charity. It's about collective responsibility for unjust institutions.

AI ethics adds another layer. An algorithm is written by human beings with biases. It is deployed in a system shaped by power imbalances. It makes decisions that affect real people. Who is responsible when it goes wrong? The company that deployed it? The engineer who wrote it? The executive who decided to use it without adequate testing? Applied ethics requires you to trace responsibility through complex systems where no one person intended the harm but harm results anyway.

### Trade-off: Efficiency vs. Equity

Business ethics constantly faces this trade-off. A company can maximize profit by cutting labor costs, outsourcing to countries with minimal regulations, using the cheapest materials. This often harms workers, consumers, or the environment. A company can choose to pay fair wages, use safe materials, and operate transparently. This costs more and reduces profit margins. Which obligation wins? Friedman says profit. Stakeholder theory says you must balance multiple stakeholders' interests. Applied ethics says: you must be explicit about what you are choosing and why, and you must be able to defend that choice.

### Worked Example: The Moral Machine Project

In 2014, MIT researchers launched the Moral Machine project. They built an online platform where people could watch simulated autonomous vehicle scenarios and choose what the car should do. The scenarios were dilemmas: a car is going to hit someone. Should it swerve left and hit an elderly person? Swerve right and hit a child? Hit one person or five? Go straight and hit a child or swerve and hit the driver?

Over four years, 40 million people from 233 countries made moral decisions. The results, published in *Nature* in 2018, revealed something fascinating: people's moral preferences varied by culture, but only somewhat. There was broad agreement that you should minimize the number of deaths. There was broad agreement that hitting a child is worse than hitting an elderly person (most people want to protect children). But significant variation appeared when you asked about hitting a pedestrian versus a passenger, or when you introduced factors like law-breaking (should the car hit a person who jaywalked?).

The project pulls together all three concepts. It's bioethics (whose life counts more?). It's environmental ethics (your choice affects others you'll never meet). It's technology ethics (who codes the car's decision into its algorithm?). It's business ethics (who profits from autonomous vehicles?). It's global justice (the algorithm affects people in Bangladesh differently than people in Boston). And it reveals that there is no single right answer—but there is definitely a wrong process: hiding the decision, pretending it's purely technical, refusing to be explicit about the moral choices being made.

### Common Misconception: "Technology Is Neutral"

People often say technology is neutral—it's just a tool, and what matters is how you use it. But this is false. Every technology embodies decisions about what matters. A facial recognition system that works well on light skin but not dark skin embodies a decision about whose face matters. An algorithm trained on historical hiring data that penalizes women embodies the past discrimination in that data. A social media feed that amplifies rage embodies a decision about what gets engagement. Applied ethics requires you to ask: What decisions are embedded in this technology? Whose interests does it serve? Who bears the costs?

## Integration: The Autonomous Vehicle and the Moral Machine

Now put all three concepts together. An autonomous vehicle making a split-second decision about whom to hit is doing bioethics (personhood and the value of life), environmental ethics (affecting others you'll never meet), technology ethics (who coded the choice), business ethics (who profits), and global justice (the same car behaves differently in different regulatory environments).

The Moral Machine project collected 40 million decisions from 233 countries. It's the largest cross-cultural study of moral preferences ever conducted. It shows that human beings, asked to make these tragic choices, tend to converge on some principles—protect children, minimize deaths—but disagree on others. The disagreement is not random. Cultural contexts shape moral intuitions.

Here's the crucial applied-ethics move: once you have this data, what do you do with it? A car manufacturer could use the data to code the algorithm to match the moral preferences of the culture where the car will operate. A car sold in the U.S. would prioritize differently than a car sold in India. This sounds respectful of local values. But it creates a problem: if a car is shipped to a new country, does its moral programming change? More fundamentally: should corporations be coding moral choices into algorithms at all? Should humans be making this choice, not machines?

The Belmont Report's four principles apply here too. Respect for persons means the passengers and pedestrians should somehow have a say in what the algorithm does—but they don't, because they don't know the car exists or what it might do to them. Beneficence means the car should be designed to help people—but which people? Nonmaleficence means do no harm—but some harm is inevitable, and the car must choose who bears it. Justice means fair distribution—but is it fair that wealthy people get safer cars while poor people get cheaper cars with different algorithms?

The MIT researchers did not propose a solution. They made the decision-making visible. That is the work of applied ethics: making visible the moral choices hidden inside technical, business, and policy decisions, so that the reasoning can be explicit and defensible rather than hidden and discovered later by journalists.

The autonomous vehicle is not hypothetical. Companies are testing them. The algorithms already exist. The decisions are already being coded. Applied ethics asks: Who decided what matters? Can that decision be defended? Whose voice was heard?

## Chapter Summary

Applied ethics is what happens when philosophical principles meet real human dilemmas with real stakes. It emerged as an academic discipline in response to the Tuskegee Syphilis Study—a case where a government agency conducted 40 years of medical torture in plain sight and had to be caught by a leaked document and a journalist's story. The Belmont Report's four principles—respect for persons, beneficence, nonmaleficence, justice—became the foundation for how medical research involving human subjects could be evaluated.

Bioethics asks: When does personhood begin and end? What capacities must a being have to count morally? How do we balance autonomy (a person's right to choose) against beneficence (a caregiver's duty to help)? These questions have no permanent answers, only frameworks for reasoning through them case by case. The cases are not abstract: they are about whether a woman can have an abortion, whether an elderly person can refuse lifesaving treatment, whether a family can remove life support from an unconscious relative.

Animal and environmental ethics ask: Does the capacity to suffer entitle a being to moral consideration? Do we have obligations to ecosystems as systems, not just to individual animals? If a decision harms individual wolves but restores an ecosystem, which matters more? These questions force a reckoning with the assumption that only humans matter. Once you grant that suffering counts morally, you cannot exclude it just because it belongs to a non-human animal. Once you see ecosystems as valuable in themselves, not just as resources, you have new obligations.

Technology, business, and global ethics ask: Who does a corporation owe obligations to—only shareholders, or all stakeholders? If the global institutional order maintains poverty, do wealthy people bear responsibility for that? When an algorithm encodes a moral choice, who should decide what that choice is? These questions are not about individual virtue. They are about responsibility inside systems and institutions.

Applied ethics does not settle these questions. It is not a source of answers. It is a discipline for asking the right questions, making hidden choices visible, and forcing the reasoning to be explicit instead of hidden. When philosophers do applied ethics well, they make it harder to hide behind technical language or claim that something is "just how the world works." They ask: Who decided that? Can that decision be defended? Whose interests were considered? Who was left out?

## Connections Forward

Applied ethics leads naturally into political philosophy (Chapter 11). Political philosophy asks: What makes a system of governance legitimate? What are governments responsible for? How should power be distributed? Applied ethics provides the foundation: you cannot answer these questions without asking who counts morally, whose interests matter, and how conflicts between people's interests should be resolved. The frameworks developed in applied ethics—respect for persons, justice, beneficence—become the tools for reasoning about political systems. Should a government prioritize efficiency or equity? Majority preferences or minority rights? Market freedom or redistribution? These are applied-ethics questions with political implications.
