# 2. How to Think Like a Philosopher

**TL;DR:** Philosophy is a skill, not just a subject. Learning to read actively, construct arguments carefully, and research honestly are the three load-bearing moves. The brain is built for speed, not truth — critical thinking is the deliberate work of slowing down, checking your assumptions, and following the evidence.

---

## Chapter Opening: The Paradox That Broke a Book

June 16, 1902. Gottlob Frege is in the final stages of printing *Grundgesetze der Arithmetik*, Volume II — his life's work, a thousand pages proving that arithmetic rests on pure logic. Three years of writing. Careful argument after careful argument. The book is about to go to the printer when a letter arrives from Bertrand Russell, a 30-year-old British mathematician he has corresponded with briefly.

Russell writes that he has found a problem. A genuine logical paradox that sits at the foundation of Frege's system. Russell frames it simply: consider the set of all sets that do not contain themselves as members. Call this set R. Now, does R contain itself? If R is a member of itself, then by definition it is a set that contains itself, so R cannot be a member of R. If R is not a member of itself, then by definition it meets the condition for membership, so R must be a member of R. 

It is a paradox. Inescapable.

Frege's reply came back by return mail. "Your discovery of the contradiction caused me the greatest surprise and, I would almost say, consternation," he wrote, "since it has shaken the basis on which I intended to build arithmetic." A single counterexample — a single logical move — had demolished the foundation of a lifetime of work. Not because Frege was careless. Not because he lacked intelligence. But because he had not thought through the consequences far enough. He had built carefully on an assumption that seemed solid until someone asked the right question.

This is what critical thinking looks like when it works. Someone reads your argument. Really reads it. Thinks through its implications. Looks for what you might have missed. And says: here. Here is what breaks.

Learning to do that — both for others' arguments and your own — is what this chapter teaches. This is the machinery of being a philosopher. It is not mystical. It is not beyond you. It is a set of habits you can practice and improve at.

**Three chapter titles to consider:**

1. The Machinery of Critical Thinking — Reading, Argument, and the Habits of Truth-Seeking
2. How to Think Straight When Your Brain Works Against You
3. Reading Philosophy, Making Arguments, Finding Truth: A Working Guide

**Prerequisites:** You have read Chapter 1 (Introduction to Philosophy). You understand what philosophers do and what methods they use.

## Concept 1: Your Brain Builds Models Faster Than the Truth Can Catch Up

### The mechanism: How perception works

You probably think you see the world. What you actually do is something far stranger. Your brain takes a sketch — incomplete, blurry, full of guesses — and projects a finished picture onto the world, so completely that the picture and the world feel like the same thing.

David Eagleman, a neuroscientist at Stanford, calls this the "newspaper editor" model of perception. Imagine a newspaper editor at midnight. She doesn't print every single event that happened that day. She prints a summary — the events that changed things, that mattered, that her readers would want to know about. She highlights what moved. She ignores what stayed still. The paper is not a complete picture of the world. It is a *relevant* picture, edited for consequence.

Your brain does exactly this. When you look out a window, you do not receive a photograph. You receive a story — what is moving, what is new, what might be dangerous, what you care about. The rest gets dropped. You have blind spots in your visual field. Your peripheral vision is hazy. Colors shift wildly depending on light and distance. Yet you experience a world that is vivid, complete, and three-dimensional. That is not what is out there. That is what your brain projects *for you*.

Here is where it gets consequential: your brain does not just project the world. It projects your beliefs, your fears, your wants. When you are afraid, the shadows look darker. When you want something to be true, the evidence looks stronger. This is not a moral failure. This is how neurons work. The same system that lets you navigate a room while thinking about dinner also leads you to see what you expect to see, to miss what contradicts you, to find reasons to believe what you already believe.

Antonio Damasio, a neuroscientist at USC, spent decades studying patients with brain injuries. He made a counterintuitive discovery: emotions are not the enemy of reason. Emotions *are* part of reason. When your brain processes information, emotional signals guide what matters. Fear, hope, disgust — these are not static in your head. They are active. They shape what you notice and how you interpret it. The mistake is to think you can eliminate emotion from thinking. You cannot. The goal is to notice that emotion is there, to reflect on *why* you are feeling it, and to ask whether the feeling is a guide to truth or a trap.

This matters because of what psychologists call the "substitution heuristic." When you face a hard question — *Is this person trustworthy? Will this investment work out? Is this argument sound?* — your brain does not work through it step by step. Instead, it substitutes an easier question. *Do I like this person? Does this investment feel good? Does this argument feel right?* Your brain answers the easier question and treats that answer as if it answered the hard one. That feels efficient. It is efficient. It is also wrong more often than you would think.

**The trade-off:** Speed versus accuracy. Your brain evolved to make split-second decisions about survival — which direction to run, who to trust, what to eat. That speed is valuable. It is also the enemy of truth. The faster you think, the more likely you are to be wrong when the problem is genuinely hard. This is the central trade-off of critical thinking: you must slow down.

**A worked example:** You are reading an argument for a philosophical position you disagree with. You immediately feel resistance. The position seems wrong. But pause here. That feeling tells you something — perhaps that the position conflicts with something you believe, or with something you witnessed, or with something you were taught. It does not tell you whether the position is actually wrong. The emotion is data. The question "Why do I feel resistant?" is worth asking. But the answer to that question is not the same as the answer to "Is this argument sound?" You have to do the second work separately.

**Common misconception:** People often think that becoming a critical thinker means learning to suppress emotion and rely purely on logic. This is backward. Emotions are part of your cognitive system. The work is to notice them, understand where they come from, and ask whether they are helping you or misleading you. Damasio's studies show that people *without* emotional input actually become worse at reasoning, not better. They take longer to decide, and they decide poorly. The goal is not to eliminate emotion but to think *alongside* it.

## Concept 2: Reading Philosophy Means Following the Argument, Not Just the Words

### The mechanism: How to track a philosophical argument

Most students read philosophy the way they read news articles. They start at the beginning and move through to the end, trying to absorb information as they go. This works for some genres. It does not work for philosophy.

Philosophy is made of *arguments*. An argument is not a disagreement. It is a structure: claims that support other claims, evidence that backs up conclusions, objections that make you rethink your position. When you read philosophy, you are not absorbing information. You are tracing the machinery of thought. If you read linearly, you will lose the thread.

Here is what you actually do:

**Pre-read first.** Before you open the text, spend five minutes on the scaffolding. What is the title? When was it written? Who is the author? What do you know about this person's other work? Read the table of contents or skim the section headings. Look at the bibliography — not to read it, but to get a sense of what conversation the author is joining. Look for the author's own thesis statement. In academic papers, this is usually in the abstract or the introduction. In books, it might take longer to find, but look for phrases like "I will argue," "This chapter demonstrates," or "The thesis of this work is." This pre-reading is not busy work. It gives you a mental scaffold to hang the details on.

**Read fast the first time.** Do not linger. Your goal is to understand the *flow* of argument, not to master every detail. Move purposefully through the material. Use what you learned in pre-reading to fill gaps. Flag things you do not understand for follow-up, but do not stop to resolve them. You are tracing the spine of the argument.

**Then read close.** Now you slow down. This is where critical thinking lives. Read slowly enough that you can think alongside the author. When the author makes a claim, ask: where is the evidence? Is the evidence adequate? What would have to be true for this argument to work? What would falsify it? Write objections in the margin, even if you are not sure. Force yourself to articulate what bothers you.

There is one principle that makes all of this possible: the **principle of charity**. When you encounter an argument that is unclear or seems wrong, assume first that the author is putting forward a rational, thoughtful view. Assume the author has thought about obvious objections. Your job is to find the interpretation that makes the most sense of what is being said — not to find the easiest way to dismiss it.

This does not mean ignoring problems. It means looking for the strongest version of the argument before criticizing it. This is the opposite of "strawmanning" — which is when you construct a weaker version of an argument to easily defeat it. Russell did not strawman Frege. He took Frege's system at its best and showed where it broke.

One more move: pay attention to the *dialectic*. This is a fancy word for a simple thing: philosophers often present multiple views, not all of them their own. You will read a claim, then an objection to the claim, then a response to the objection. Do not assume that every argument in the text is what the author actually believes. Track which strand the author endorses. Mark it. This takes practice, but it is the difference between understanding what a philosopher says and understanding what a philosopher *believes*.

**The trade-off:** Active reading takes more time than passive reading. You cannot skim a philosophical text the way you can skim a news article. The payoff is understanding that stays with you, arguments you can think with, and the ability to write about what you have read. But yes — it is slower.

**A worked example:** John Searle publishes "Minds, Brains, and Programs" in 1980. He wants to argue that a computer running a program does not have genuine understanding, even if its outputs are indistinguishable from human understanding. He introduces the "Chinese Room" — imagine you are in a locked room. Chinese symbols come through a slot. You have a rulebook (the program) that tells you which symbols to write in response. You follow the rules perfectly. Chinese speakers outside the room think you understand Chinese. But inside, you do not understand anything — you are just following syntax, not grasping meaning.

When you read this, do pre-read work: Who is Searle? What is he responding to? (Answer: Alan Turing's imitation game, and the idea that if a machine can pass the Turing test, it has mind.) What is he trying to prove? (Answer: that computational processes do not constitute understanding because they are syntactic, not semantic.) Then read fast: follow the argument. Then read close: Does the example actually prove what he claims? What would someone who disagreed say? (They would say: understanding *is* the right kind of syntactic process. Or: something about the whole system understands, even if the person in the room does not.)

**Common misconception:** Students often think that reading philosophy well means being able to summarize what the author said. This is not the goal. The goal is to be able to think *with* the author, to understand their reasoning deeply enough that you can continue it, extend it, or criticize it. Summarizing is a step along the way. But if you read Searle and the only thing you can do is repeat his words back, you have not done the work.

## Concept 3: Arguments Have Structure; Learning the Structure Teaches You to Build and Evaluate Them

### The mechanism: Premises and conclusions

An argument is not a fight. It is a structure. It looks like this:

**Premise:** A claim that you are asking the reader to accept (for the sake of the argument, or as established fact).

**Conclusion:** A claim that you derive from the premise.

The simplest argument is three lines:

1. All humans are mortal. (Premise)
2. Socrates is human. (Premise)
3. Therefore, Socrates is mortal. (Conclusion)

This argument is *valid*. That means: *if* the premises are true, *then* the conclusion must be true. There is no way for the premises to be true and the conclusion false.

But validity is not the same as *soundness*. An argument is sound if it is valid *and* the premises are actually true. If I say:

1. All philosophers are invisible. (Premise)
2. Plato is a philosopher. (Premise)
3. Therefore, Plato is invisible. (Conclusion)

This argument is valid — the form is perfect. But it is not sound, because the first premise is false.

This distinction matters. When you evaluate an argument, you have to ask two questions: (1) Does the reasoning work? (Is it valid?) (2) Are the starting points true? (Are the premises sound?) Many bad arguments fail on the first count. But some of the most dangerous arguments are valid but built on false premises. Propaganda often works this way. The reasoning is flawless. The premises are lies.

There are other forms of argument. Here is one:

1. If it is raining, the ground is wet. (Premise)
2. It is raining. (Premise)
3. Therefore, the ground is wet. (Conclusion)

This is called "modus ponens," and it is valid. But here is a trap:

1. If it is raining, the ground is wet. (Premise)
2. The ground is wet. (Premise)
3. Therefore, it is raining. (Conclusion)

This is invalid. The ground could be wet for other reasons — a sprinkler, dew, someone with a hose. The conclusion does not follow from the premises, even though it seems like it should.

Philosophers also use inductive arguments, where the premises do not guarantee the conclusion but make it *more likely*:

1. Every swan I have ever seen is white. (Premise)
2. All my observations of swans have been made in similar conditions. (Premise)
3. Therefore, all swans are white. (Conclusion)

This is a reasonable inference based on the premises. But it is not valid in the strict sense, because new evidence could overturn it. (And it does: black swans exist in Australia.)

Now, here is the critical move: watch for *fallacies* — arguments that seem to work but do not. Two common ones:

**Ad hominem:** "That argument is wrong because the person who made it is a hypocrite" or "because I don't trust them." This attacks the person, not the argument. The argument itself might be sound even if the person is untrustworthy.

**Straw man:** You take an opponent's argument, make it weaker (by misrepresenting it), and then defeat the weak version. You have not actually answered the strong version. This is the opposite of the principle of charity.

**The trade-off:** Learning the structure of arguments takes time. You have to slow down and ask: what are the premises? What is the conclusion? Are they actually connected? Once you develop this habit, you cannot read casually anymore — you will always be tracking structure. But this is not a cost. This is the point.

**A worked example:** Here is a classic philosophical argument (from Thomas Hobbes, modified slightly):

"Without a sovereign authority to enforce laws, people live in a state of nature. In the state of nature, every person is at war with every other. Life is 'solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short.' Therefore, we need government."

Let me trace this:

- Premise 1: Without sovereign authority, people are in a state of nature.
- Premise 2: In the state of nature, life is war-like and miserable.
- Premise 3: [Implied] Government prevents this misery.
- Conclusion: We need government.

Is this valid? If all premises are true, does the conclusion follow? Yes. 

Is it sound? That requires asking: Are the premises actually true? This is where philosophers disagree. Some say the state of nature was never actually like this — humans are cooperative. Some say government creates misery of its own. Some say the argument proves too much — maybe we need *some* authority, but not the absolute sovereignty Hobbes favors. The structure of the argument is sound. The premises are what get disputed.

**Common misconception:** Students often think that if an argument is logically valid, it has proved its conclusion. But validity only means the reasoning is correct. Soundness requires that the premises are true. And in philosophy, the premises are *always* debatable. Your job is not to find arguments that are beyond criticism. Your job is to understand the argument well enough to know where someone would criticize it.

## Integration: From Reading to Arguing to Writing

Let me walk you through the full cycle using John Searle's Chinese Room. You are assigned to read it, understand it, and write a paper on whether you think Searle's argument works.

**Reading:** You pre-read Searle's 1980 paper. You learn that Searle is responding to a growing belief that computers could have minds if they passed the Turing test. You read the paper fast, tracking the argument. You close-read it, asking at each step: What is he proving? What assumptions is he making?

The key claim emerges: "Syntax is not sufficient for semantics." In other words: following rules about symbols (what a computer does) is not the same as understanding what the symbols mean (what a mind does).

**Breaking down the argument:**

- Premise 1: A computer that runs a program only manipulates symbols according to syntactic rules.
- Premise 2: Understanding meaning is not the same as manipulating symbols.
- Premise 3: Therefore, a computer running a program, no matter how clever, does not understand.

This is valid — the reasoning works. Now you ask: Are the premises true?

**Finding the objection:** Other philosophers say premise 2 is wrong. They say understanding *is* a kind of symbol manipulation — just very complex. Or they say: the person in the room does not understand, but the whole system (person + rulebook) does.

**Constructing your response:** You have read Searle. You have read responses to Searle. Now you choose: Do you think Searle's argument works? Let's say you decide he has a point, but it is incomplete. You construct a paper:

**Thesis:** "Searle is right that syntax alone does not produce understanding, but his argument does not consider whether a system *could* develop semantics through syntactic processes, given enough complexity and the right environmental feedback."

**Structure:**
1. Explain Searle's argument clearly (using the principle of charity).
2. Show what work the argument does — what claim it actually settles.
3. Introduce an objection (what someone who disagrees would say).
4. Show why you think the objection has merit.
5. But then explain why you think Searle has captured something important, even if the argument is not complete.

**Writing it:** You write with evidence. You quote Searle. You cite the philosophers who object. You show that you have thought through both sides. You acknowledge what you do not know. ("It is unclear whether the Chinese Room argument rules out all computational theories of mind, or only certain versions. More work would be needed to settle this.")

This is the whole cycle. Reading teaches you the structure of arguments. Understanding structure teaches you to build arguments. And writing forces you to think through whether your argument actually works.

## Addressing the Biggest Obstacle: Your Brain Prefers Speed to Truth

Before we move forward, let me be direct about the central problem in critical thinking. Your brain is shaped by evolution to be *efficient*, not *accurate*. Efficiency kept our ancestors alive. Accuracy is something you have to work for.

Psychologist Daniel Kahneman did studies on this. He gave people easy tasks and hard tasks. Easy tasks — like recognizing faces — your brain does instantly, without conscious effort. Hard tasks — like multiplying two-digit numbers in your head — require slow, deliberate attention. What he found is this: we have a strong tendency to avoid the hard tasks. We would rather answer an easier version of the question than solve the actual problem.

This is called the "substitution heuristic." You ask: "Is this argument sound?" But your brain answers: "Does this argument feel right to me?" And it treats the answer to the second question as if it answered the first.

You can resist this. But it requires what psychologists call "metacognition" — thinking about thinking. You have to notice when you are doing it. You have to force yourself to slow down. You have to ask yourself: Am I really evaluating this argument, or am I just checking whether it matches what I already believe?

One specific trap: **confirmation bias**. This is the tendency to search for, interpret, and remember information that confirms what you already believe. Peter Wason ran a classic experiment. He gave people a sequence of numbers (2, 4, 8) and asked them to figure out the rule. People could propose new sequences to test their hypothesis. What he found: people almost always proposed sequences that would confirm their hypothesis. They almost never proposed sequences that would disprove it. This is called "falsification" — you try to prove yourself wrong. And people almost never do it naturally.

In philosophy, this means: when you read an argument, you are likely to focus on the parts that confirm your existing view and skim over the parts that challenge it. You are likely to find the strongest versions of arguments you agree with and the weakest versions of arguments you disagree with.

The antidote is to practice the opposite. When you encounter an argument you disagree with, deliberately construct the strongest version of it. Make it as hard as possible to dismiss. *Then* try to object. When you encounter an argument you agree with, deliberately look for its weaknesses. Ask: what would have to be true for this to fail?

There is one more thing worth knowing. It is called the **Dunning-Kruger effect**. David Dunning and Justin Kruger found that people with little expertise in a domain tend to overestimate their knowledge, while experts tend to underestimate theirs. The novice thinks they understand almost everything. The expert knows how much they do not know.

This matters for philosophy because you are almost certainly a novice in whatever philosophical domain you are studying. Your intuitions will feel strong. You will feel confident that you already understand the problem. But the fact that you feel confident is almost the worst possible sign. The more you learn, the more uncertainty you will feel — not because you are getting worse, but because you are getting better and discovering the complexity you were missing.

This is liberating once you accept it. You do not have to pretend to be confident. In fact, admitting what you do not know is a sign of serious thinking.

## The Three Core Moves for Finding and Evaluating Information

When you research philosophy, you will use the internet. The internet is full of misinformation, half-truths, and well-meaning but unreliable summaries. Information literacy scholar Michael Caulfield developed a method called SIFT — four moves that professional fact-checkers use, and that you can use too.

**Move 1: Stop.** Before you read anything carefully, stop and ask: Do I already know something about this source? Can I recognize a red flag? If you are seeing a claim on social media, if the source is unclear, if the language is inflammatory — stop. Do not assume you will evaluate this fairly after reading it. Your brain will project what it wants to see.

**Move 2: Investigate the source.** Do not just read the website. Search for reviews of the website. Ask: Who wrote this? What are their credentials? Are they affiliated with an institution? What do other people say about them? This is called "reading laterally" — you leave the site and search around it. You might find that the author is a physicist writing about philosophy, or a blogger with no training. Neither means they are wrong, but it changes what you should expect from the source.

**Move 3: Find better coverage.** Check whether other sources say the same thing. Is this claim being covered elsewhere? Do other sources agree? If a claim appears on one obscure website and nowhere else, be skeptical. If it appears in multiple reliable sources, it carries more weight. Not because multiple sources prove something true — they could all be repeating the same error — but because you have more to compare.

**Move 4: Trace claims back to the original source.** If someone quotes a philosopher, find the original quote. Search for the exact words in quotation marks. Follow the citations. A claim that has a source is more trustworthy than a claim that appears without context. A claim that you can trace back to a primary source — the actual paper, the actual book — is something you can evaluate yourself.

These four moves are not foolproof. But they work far better than just reading what feels authoritative. And they work because they force you to slow down, to check, to think laterally instead of linearly.

## Graduated Exercises

### Warm-up

1. **Analyze a simple argument.** Take this claim: "Video games cause violence, because studies show that violent games increase aggressive behavior." Identify: What is the premise? What is the conclusion? What assumption is being made that is not stated? Is the reasoning valid?

2. **Find the hidden emotion.** Read the following passage: "Obviously, anyone with common sense knows that artificial intelligence is a threat to humanity. The media keeps talking about it, so it must be a real danger." What emotional appeals are being used here? What substitution heuristic is at work? What would a more careful version of this claim look like?

3. **Track the dialectic.** Read this passage: "Some philosophers argue that free will is an illusion because our actions are determined by prior causes. However, others respond that determinism is compatible with free will if we define free will carefully. Yet a third group claims that both sides miss something fundamental about how human agency actually works." Which view is the author endorsing? What clues tell you?

### Application

4. **Apply SIFT to a philosophical claim.** Find a claim online about a philosopher (any philosopher). It might be on social media, a blog, or a website. Now apply all four SIFT moves: Stop (is there a red flag?), Investigate the source (who wrote this?), Find better coverage (what do other sources say?), Trace it back (can you find the original?). Write a paragraph explaining what you discovered about the reliability of the original claim.

5. **Construct a small argument.** Choose a philosophical position from a text you have read. State it as a thesis. Identify at least two premises that would support it. Check: Is your reasoning valid? Then think of an objection — how would someone disagree? Write a one-paragraph response to that objection.

6. **Read and respond.** Find a short philosophical text (a few pages). Pre-read it (title, author, date, thesis). Read it fast (track the argument). Read it close (write margin notes with objections). Then write a two-paragraph summary: (a) What is the author arguing? (b) Do you think the argument works? Why or why not?

### Synthesis

7. **Compare two arguments.** Read two different philosophers making opposing claims about the same question. (Example: Descartes arguing that the mind is non-physical vs. a materialist arguing that mind is just brain activity.) Construct the strongest possible case for each. Then explain where you think the disagreement actually lies — what assumption are they disagreeing about?

8. **Find a real counterexample.** Russell's paradox works because it is a counterexample — a case where the thing being said cannot possibly be true. Take a philosophical claim you have read. Try to construct a counterexample. Can you think of a situation that fits all the premises but makes the conclusion false? Write it out clearly.

### Challenge

9. **Evaluate a fallacy in depth.** Find a real argument (online, in a text, in a conversation) that contains a fallacy. Identify which fallacy it is. Explain what makes it a fallacy — what would need to be true for the argument to actually work? Then explain how to construct a stronger version of the same argument that avoids the fallacy.

10. **Trace an assumption.** Take a philosophical claim that seems obviously true to you. Now question it. What assumption is it based on? What would have to be different for the assumption to be wrong? Can you think of a context where the assumption does not hold? Write a page exploring this.

## Summary

Philosophy is a skill. You develop it by doing it: by reading actively, by tracking arguments carefully, by writing your thoughts and testing them against evidence and objection.

Your brain is built for speed, not truth. It projects what it expects to see. It substitutes easier questions for harder ones. It confirms what it already believes. None of this is your fault — it is how brains work. But once you know it, you can work against it. You can slow down. You can force yourself to consider opposing views. You can ask yourself whether you are really thinking through an argument or just checking whether it feels right.

Reading philosophy means pre-reading, fast-reading, and close-reading. It means tracking the dialectic so you know what the author actually believes. It means using the principle of charity — finding the strongest interpretation of an argument before criticizing it. This is slower than passive reading, but it is the only way to actually understand.

Arguments have structure: premises and conclusions. When you understand the structure, you can evaluate whether the reasoning is valid and whether the premises are sound. You can recognize fallacies — arguments that seem to work but do not. And you can build your own arguments, testing them against objections and evidence.

When you research, use the SIFT method. Stop before diving in. Investigate who wrote what you are reading. Find other coverage. Trace claims back to original sources. This is what professional fact-checkers do. It is not foolproof, but it is far better than just trusting what feels authoritative.

The closing move is writing. Writing forces you to think through whether your argument actually works. It is the crucible where your ideas either hold up or fall apart. It is also where you discover what you do not yet understand — and that discovery is the beginning of real philosophy.

## Connections Forward

These skills will be tested throughout the rest of this book. When you read about metaphysics, epistemology, ethics, you will need to track arguments, recognize assumptions, construct counterexamples. You will need to slow down, check yourself, and resist the brain's pull toward speed. In Chapter 3, we turn to some of the oldest questions humans have asked — what is real, what can we know, how should we live. You will be better equipped to think about them now that you understand the machinery of critical thinking.

## What Would Change My Mind

If new evidence showed that the brain's inferential shortcuts are *more* reliable than critical reflection in evaluating complex philosophical arguments, this chapter's central premise would need revision. Currently, the evidence suggests the opposite — that systematic effort to slow down and check reasoning produces more accurate conclusions.

## Still Puzzling

I do not yet fully understand *why* humans find confirmation bias so compelling. It seems evolutionarily adaptive for simple decisions, but the mechanism by which it persists even when people are explicitly taught about it remains unclear.

   - Doesn't follow a discrete rule book — it's a neural network.

   - Operates at a scale (175B+ parameters) far beyond what
     Searle considered.
   How does each of Searle's premises hold or fail when applied

5. **Take a position.** State your current verdict:
   - Does the Chinese Room argument succeed against Claude?
   - If yes, what's the strongest reason to think so?
   - If no, what's the strongest objection that defeats it?
   - If unclear, name the empirical or conceptual question that
     would resolve it.

End with: name one assumption Searle's argument shares with
your starting position from Chapter 1's Charter. Surfacing
