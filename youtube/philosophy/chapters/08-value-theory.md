# What Makes Something Good? The Machinery of Value

## Three Suggested Titles

1. **The Open Question: Why "Good" Cannot Be Reduced**
2. **What We Actually Mean by Value: From Pleasure to Meaning**
3. **The Problem of the Good: How We Distinguish What Matters**

## TL;DR

Value theory asks what makes something good or valuable, and whether value is objective or subjective. The core puzzle is the open-question argument: for any claim that good equals some measurable property (pleasure, desire-satisfaction, biological flourishing), we can always ask "But is that actually good?"—and the question remains genuinely open. This chapter traces that puzzle through theories of well-being, the fact-value distinction, aesthetic judgment, and the search for meaning.

---

## Chapter Opening: Cambridge, 1903

George Edward Moore, a British philosopher in his early thirties, published a small book called *Principia Ethica*. It was not long. It was not difficult to read. But it contained an argument that has haunted ethical theory ever since—an argument so clean, so apparently obvious once stated, that philosophers have spent the last century trying to escape it.

Moore's point was this. Imagine someone claims: "Good means pleasurable." Simple. Elegant. Measurable. We can study pleasure scientifically. We can compare quantities. We can build a moral theory on it.

But then Moore invites you to test this claim. Take any instance of pleasure—eating a favorite meal, lying in the sun, the warmth of a friend's company. Now ask yourself: "This is pleasurable. But is it *good*?" The question does not collapse. You do not laugh at yourself for asking it. The question remains open. You might answer yes, or you might say "No, I was just being self-indulgent. It wasn't actually good for me." You might answer that something can be pleasurable and still be bad—self-destructive pleasure, for instance, or the cruel satisfaction of seeing someone humiliated.

This is Moore's open-question argument. For any definition of "good" in terms of natural properties (pleasure, desire-satisfaction, flourishing, evolutionary fitness), the question "But is X actually good?" remains genuinely open. It is not like asking "Is water actually H2O?" That question *does* collapse. Once you know water is H2O, you have settled the matter. But "Is pleasure good?" does not collapse the same way. There is a logical gap.

This gap—this persistent ability to question whether the thing you have identified *really is* good—points to something strange about the nature of value itself. Either goodness is not reducible to any natural property, or our whole way of thinking about goodness is confused. Either way, value is not as simple as we often assume.

## Concept Section 1: The Fact-Value Distinction, and Why the Gap Won't Close

Let me start with the simplest claim: facts and values are different kinds of statements.

A fact is a description of how things are. "It is raining." "Water boils at 100 degrees Celsius." "Human beings have twenty-three pairs of chromosomes." You can check these claims against the world. You can observe them. You can measure them. They are true or false based on how reality is arranged.

A value is a judgment about how things *should* be, or what is good or important or beautiful. "You should help people in need." "That painting is beautiful." "Kindness is important." These statements do not describe the world as it is. They describe the world as someone thinks it *ought* to be. They express a judgment about what matters.

This distinction is called the fact-value distinction, or the is-ought problem. David Hume, the Scottish Enlightenment philosopher, noticed it first in the 1700s. In his *Treatise of Human Nature*, he observed that every moral system he had read made the same logical leap. Philosophers would spend chapters describing facts about human nature, or God, or society. Then, at some crucial moment, they would switch. They would move from statements of fact ("This is how people behave") to statements of value ("This is how people *ought* to behave"). The shift was imperceptible, but it was logically stark. "Is" and "ought" are not the same thing. You cannot derive an "ought" from an "is" alone.

This is not just pedantry. It points to something real. You can agree with someone on all the facts and still disagree about the values.

Consider a concrete case. Two people might agree on every empirical fact about monogamy: that it is rare in nature, that it creates emotional satisfaction in humans, that it comes with costs and benefits. They agree on the data. Yet one concludes "Monogamy is good," and the other concludes "Monogamy is merely conventional, not intrinsically good." They have the same facts. They reach opposite values.

Or take a biological fact: humans are competitive creatures, shaped by evolution to pursue reproductive fitness. This is empirically defensible. Some people conclude from this fact: "Therefore, cooperation and altruism are illusions or tricks evolution played on us." Others conclude the opposite: "We are also creatures shaped to cooperate, and cooperation is as 'natural' as competition." Same fact, opposite values.

The fact-value distinction tells us why these disagreements are not settled by more data. You cannot run an experiment that decides the question. You cannot measure a value. You can measure pleasure, but you cannot measure whether pleasure is good. You can measure wealth, but you cannot measure whether wealth is valuable. This is the core problem of value theory: values are *not facts*, and yet we speak about them as if they matter deeply.

**The trade-off is real and unavoidable.** If values were reducible to facts—if "good" simply meant "pleasurable" or "desired" or "evolutionarily fit"—then we could study value scientifically, measure it, build policy on it with certainty. But as Moore showed, there is always a logical gap. We can always ask "But is that actually good?" The question does not collapse into fact.

On the other hand, if values are completely subjective—if "good" means only "I personally like this"—then there is no ground for moral disagreement at all. Your values would be true for you, my values true for me, and no one could be wrong. But we do not believe that. We think some moral positions *are* wrong. We think slavery is not just "disvalued by me"—we think it is actually bad, regardless of whether anyone values it.

**A worked example: The naturalistic fallacy in popular debate.** In contemporary discussions of gender and sexuality, we often hear the claim "This is natural, therefore it is good" or "This is unnatural, therefore it is bad." Heterosexual reproduction is natural (factual claim). Therefore it is good? Not obviously. Appendicitis is natural too. So is disease, infant mortality, aggression, and infanticide (all observed in nature). Many things are natural and bad. Many things are artificial and good (antibiotics, literacy, civil rights). The naturalistic fallacy is the move from "X occurs in nature" to "X is good." The fact does not entail the value.

**A common misconception.** People often think that pointing out the fact-value distinction means values are merely subjective, a matter of opinion, not worth serious debate. This is a mistake. The fact-value distinction says that values *are not facts*. It does not say they are arbitrary or baseless. You can have very serious, evidence-informed reasons for your values without those reasons being reducible to factual claims. I value knowledge. I value honesty. I value the reduction of suffering. These values are not *facts*, but they are not arbitrary either. They are commitments I have reasoned toward, tested against my life, and found to cohere with other things I believe.

## Concept Section 2: What Is Valuable—Intrinsic Value, Extrinsic Value, and the Structure of Mattering

Now that we have separated facts from values, let me ask a more precise question: What kinds of things can have value?

When we call something "valuable," we often mean it is useful—it gets us something we want. A hammer is valuable because it drives nails. A vaccine is valuable because it prevents disease. A friendship is valuable because it brings joy and support. This is *extrinsic value*—value something has *for the sake of something else*. The hammer is a means to an end. Its value depends on whether it serves that end well.

But not everything we value is a means. Some things seem to matter *in themselves*, not as instruments. Happiness, for instance. If someone asks "What is happiness for?" the question seems strange. You do not pursue happiness in order to achieve something else. You pursue it because happiness, itself, is good. This is *intrinsic value*—value something has *for its own sake*.

This distinction opens a philosophical puzzle: Where does the chain end? If everything valuable is either a means to something else or intrinsically good, then there must be *something* that is intrinsically good. Otherwise, we have an infinite chain: the hammer is good for driving nails, driving nails is good for building shelter, building shelter is good for survival, survival is good for... and we never reach bottom. That seems incoherent. There must be something that matters in itself.

But here is where the disagreement begins. Philosophers do not agree on what that intrinsic good is.

Some say it is pleasure. Epicurus, the ancient Greek philosopher, claimed that pleasure was the only intrinsic good. But notice: this is not what people think "Epicurean" means. It does not mean endless indulgence in sensory excess. Epicurus himself lived simply. He valued intellectual conversation over fancy food. He reasoned that wild desires—for wealth, status, luxury—cause unhappiness. The goal was ataraxia: tranquility, freedom from pain and fear. Pleasure, for him, was the state of being without acute suffering, plus the intellectual satisfactions of friendship and philosophy. Even so, he made a choice: pleasure is the bottom value. Everything else is good insofar as it produces pleasure.

Others say the intrinsic good is desire-satisfaction. What makes a life good is that the person gets what they want. If you desire knowledge, then knowledge is good for you. If you desire friendship, friendship is good for you. The theory is elegantly simple: goodness is getting what you actually value.

But both of these theories face the same problem: counterexample. Robert Nozick, in 1974, invented the "experience machine" thought experiment to challenge them both. Imagine a machine so sophisticated it can simulate any experience you desire. You plug in, and you live out an elaborate fantasy. You are a famous novelist, beloved and admired. You have deep friendships. You solve important problems. You experience beauty, connection, achievement. The machine is so perfect that you are completely unaware you are in it. Everything feels real.

Would you plug in?

Most people say no. This reveals something. If pleasure or desire-satisfaction were the only things that matter, you should plug in. The machine would give you both in abundance. But something matters *besides* your internal states. You care that your accomplishments are *real*, not simulated. You care that your friendships are with *actual people*, not illusions. You care that what you do actually *matters* in the world, not just in your own mind.

This suggests that there are objective goods—things that are good for you independently of whether you desire them or whether they make you feel good. Knowledge is good for you even if you don't want it. Health is good for you even if you're not aware you have it. Friendship is good for you even if you don't consciously savor it. These are goods you can miss or fail to achieve even while experiencing pleasure.

The ancient Greek philosophers called this *eudaimonia*—flourishing. It is often translated as "happiness," but that misses the sense. Eudaimonia is not a feeling. It is a state of excelling, of becoming what you are equipped to become, of thriving according to your nature. A knife flourishes when it cuts well. A plant flourishes when it grows strong in good soil. A human flourishes when they develop their capacities—reason, compassion, creativity, virtue. You might not *feel* happy while flourishing. You might be struggling, sacrificing, learning. But you are living well.

**The trade-off between theories of value cuts deep.** If pleasure or desire-satisfaction is the bottom value, then we have a clear, measurable goal. We know what to optimize for. We can build policy around maximizing happiness. But we lose the intuition that some things matter independently of how we feel about them. If objective goods are real—if knowledge and virtue and friendship are intrinsically good—then we have something to aspire to. But we have also lost the simplicity. Now we must ask: Which objective goods matter most? What do we do when they conflict?

**A worked example: Should you save your drowning child or a stranger's drowning child?** You can save only one. Most people save their own child. A pure utilitarian—someone who believes pleasure or desire-satisfaction is the only good—struggles with this. Both children have equal claims to happiness. If you can only save one, you should save the one whose life will produce more pleasure or satisfy more desires. But most of us think you *should* save your child, even if the stranger's child would suffer less overall. Why? Because our relationships are themselves intrinsically good. Your special bond to your child has value in itself, not reducible to the happiness it produces. But now we face pressure. Does this mean we can be partial to our loved ones at the expense of everyone else? How do we balance special relationships against universal obligations?

**A common misconception.** People often think that saying something has intrinsic value means it cannot be analyzed or explained. "Beauty is in the eye of the beholder, so we cannot say anything more about it." But this misses the point. Intrinsic value is a logical claim, not a mystical one. It means: this thing is good in itself, not just a means to something else. You can still study it, explain it, trace how it relates to other goods. Intrinsic value does not mean unexplainable. It means not reducible to other values.

## Concept Section 3: Aesthetic Value and the Meaning of a Life

Now let me shift the perspective. We have asked what is valuable in the abstract. But people do not live in abstraction. They live in particular moments, particular places, particular relationships. And much of what they value is aesthetic—beauty, art, the texture of everyday life.

The question "What is beautiful?" seems innocent. But as philosophers have discovered, it conceals a deeper puzzle: Is beauty objective or subjective? Does beauty exist in the object, or in the eye of the beholder?

For Plato, beauty was objective. Beauty existed as a transcendent form—an ideal, unchanging pattern that particular beautiful things approximate. When you see a beautiful person, you are seeing a *reflection* of the Form of Beauty. The form is real; particular beautiful things are imperfect copies. This is why beauty can move us deeply. When we encounter it, we are glimpsing the eternal.

For Enlightenment philosophers like David Hume, this was too mystical. Hume observed that beauty is obviously subjective. Different cultures value different aesthetics. Different historical periods do too. What medieval Europeans found beautiful, contemporary Europeans might find ugly. Beauty varies with taste, and taste is shaped by upbringing, education, temperament. So beauty cannot be objective.

Yet Hume noticed something else: our tastes can be educated and refined. A critic with educated taste makes judgments that others come to accept. Fashion changes, but some works persist—some paintings remain beautiful across centuries. Some poetry remains moving. This suggests that while individual judgments vary, there might be something about human perception and human nature that allows certain judgments to rise above mere preference.

The German philosopher Immanuel Kant tried to split the difference. For Kant, judgments of beauty are subjective—they express how a particular person feels about an object, not a property of the object itself. Yet they are not arbitrary. When you say "This painting is beautiful," you expect others to agree. You are not just reporting your private sensation, the way you might report "I like chocolate." You are making a claim that carries universality.

Here is Kant's insight: the experience of beauty is *disinterested*. When you enjoy beauty, you are not enjoying it for what it can do for you. You are not thinking "How will this benefit me?" You are simply attending to it, letting it present itself. This disinterest, this freedom from desire, is what gives aesthetic judgment its claim to universality. You are not advocating for your personal preference; you are inviting others to share a particular way of attending to something.

This matters because it changes how we think about value. Aesthetic value is not about maximizing pleasure or satisfying desires. It is about a particular kind of attention, a particular quality of presence. And that presence can matter—it can be part of a flourishing life—without promising any payoff beyond itself.

This connects to a larger question: What makes a life meaningful? Susan Wolf, a contemporary philosopher, has argued that meaning in life comes from *fitting fulfillment*—pursuing an objective good that you are genuinely invested in. It is not enough to desire something. It is not enough to achieve it and feel good. Real meaning requires that the thing you pursue is *actually worth pursuing*, and that you are the kind of person who can recognize that worth.

A person obsessed with counting blades of grass might achieve their goal, might feel satisfied. But most of us would say their life is not meaningful. Why? Because counting blades of grass is not objectively valuable. It does not develop human capacities, it does not connect you to others, it does not express understanding or create beauty. By contrast, a scientist pursuing a cure for disease, or an artist creating something that will outlive them, or a parent raising children with care and attention—these lives have meaning because they are devoted to things that are *genuinely worth doing*.

This introduces the question of meta-ethics: Is there a fact of the matter about what is objectively valuable? Or are all values ultimately grounded in human desires and preferences? Some philosophers, called moral realists, argue that there are objective moral facts. There are things that are genuinely good, independent of whether anyone desires them. Others, called anti-realists, argue that all values ultimately reduce to human sentiments, preferences, and feelings. The disagreement is fundamental. It affects how we think about moral disagreement, about culture, about progress.

**The trade-off is between objectivity and humility.** If moral values are objective, we have firm ground for saying certain practices are wrong, certain principles are true. But we must also grapple with the problem of moral knowledge: how do we *know* objective values? What faculty perceives them? How do they differ from scientific facts? If moral values are subjective—grounded in human desires and feelings—then we avoid this problem. We ground ethics in something we understand: human psychology. But we lose the ability to say that someone *morally disagrees with us* is wrong. We can only say they *feel differently*.

**A worked example: The conflict between cultural values and universal principles.** Female genital mutilation is a cultural practice in some communities, valued as a marker of identity and belonging. From within that cultural framework, it is seen as a good thing, an important ritual. From outside, many view it as a harmful violation of women's bodily autonomy. Can we say it is wrong, or must we say that value is relative to each culture? If we say it is wrong, we are making a claim about objective value—that bodily autonomy matters regardless of cultural perspective. But this seems to impose our own value framework on another. If we say it is merely different, not wrong, we seem to abandon the possibility of moral progress or genuine ethical critique. The tension between respecting cultural difference and asserting universal principles is real and not easily resolved.

**A common misconception.** People often think that if something is subjective, it must be arbitrary—a matter of mere opinion, not worth serious discussion. But subjective does not mean arbitrary. Your preference for the taste of coffee over tea is subjective, but it is not arbitrary; it is grounded in your neurophysiology, your experience, your palate. Your values might be grounded in human needs, human psychology, human relationships—and still be constrained, still be subject to reasoning and evidence. I might convince you that a practice you value actually undermines something you value more deeply. Subjective values can still be subject to rational critique.

## Integration and Synthesis: A Painter and Her Practice

Let me bring these strands together through a concrete case: a woman in her fifties who has devoted her adult life to painting.

She is not famous. Her paintings do not sell for large sums. She lives modestly, teaches art part-time, spends mornings in her studio. She works slowly, revising, often dissatisfied with the result. By many measures—by wealth, by conventional recognition—her life might seem narrow, unambitious, even unsuccessful.

Yet she considers her life good. Meaningful. Worth living. Let's trace what that means through the conceptual machinery we have built.

**Is her value intrinsic or extrinsic?** The pleasure of painting brings her satisfaction, but not consistently. Some days she is frustrated, tired, blocked. The painting is extrinsically valuable if it serves something else—if it makes her happy, if it earns money, if it wins approval. But something in her resists this reduction. She continues even when it does not make her happy, even when it earns nothing, even when friends question why she bothers. The painting itself seems to have intrinsic value. It matters *in itself* to make the thing well. The work of attending to color, form, gesture—this has value for its own sake.

**Is she flourishing?** She is not always happy. But is she flourishing? She is developing her capacities—her eye, her technical skill, her ability to see the world deeply. She is not maximizing pleasure. She is becoming more fully herself, more fully the kind of human capable of the kind of attention her practice demands. In that sense, yes: she is eudaimonizing. She is achieving human excellence in a particular domain.

**What is the role of aesthetic value?** What makes a painting beautiful is partly about her responsiveness to visual form—color, composition, gesture. She is attending to something that has no practical payoff. She is not painting to solve a problem or to produce income. She is painting because the visual world calls for attention, because beauty deserves to be made visible. This is disinterested in Kant's sense—not motivated by desire for gain, but by an invitation from the thing itself to attend to it carefully.

**How does she navigate the fact-value distinction?** She knows factually that painting does not make her rich, that it will not make her famous, that the market will likely ignore her work. These are facts she has accepted. Her commitment to painting does not depend on denying them. She has chosen a value—the value of making beautiful things carefully—that stands independent of those facts. Her life witnesses that values are not reducible to facts, that you can accept the facts fully and still organize your life around something that transcends them.

**What does the experience machine reveal?** If she could plug into a machine that simulated the experience of being a successful artist—of seeing her work admired, of the pleasure of recognition—would she? No. She cares that the painting be *real*, that her growth be *genuine*, that the work engage with actual materials in actual time. The experience would be hollow. This tells us that what matters is not just her internal state but her real relationship to the world, her actual development, her genuine contribution.

**What would satisfy her if values were merely subjective?** If all value were grounded only in what she happens to desire or prefer, then her life would be good as long as she felt satisfied. But she knows her own mind well enough to know that she can feel satisfied and still be missing something. She can be pleased with a painting and know it is actually mediocre. She can be pleased with her life and know she is not fully alive. So she resists the reduction of value to feeling. She holds out for something more—for standards that exist beyond her momentary preferences.

**The deeper pattern.** What her life shows is this: A human being can commit to an objective good (making beautiful things well) that she does not fully control, that often brings difficulty rather than pleasure, that she pursues not because it maximizes her satisfaction but because it is *genuinely worth doing*. She accepts the fact-value distinction—she is not confused about why the world does not reward her practice financially. Yet she lives as if certain things *really matter*, as if there are standards beyond personal preference that warrant her attention and sacrifice.

This is how value actually functions in human life. It is not a philosophical puzzle to be solved in the abstract. It is lived. It is the difference between a life that is merely pleasant and a life that is genuinely good.

## Chapter Summary

Value theory investigates the nature of what is good, valuable, and beautiful, and how we know these things.

The fact-value distinction separates descriptive claims about how the world is from evaluative claims about how it should be. David Hume showed that you cannot derive an "ought" from an "is" alone—no amount of empirical data will settle what is good. G.E. Moore's open-question argument demonstrates that for any proposed definition of "good" in terms of natural properties (pleasure, desire-satisfaction, flourishing), the question "But is that actually good?" remains logically open. This suggests that goodness is not reducible to any empirical fact.

Intrinsic value is value something has for its own sake. Extrinsic value is value something has as a means to something else. Philosophers disagree about what is intrinsically valuable: some claim pleasure, others desire-satisfaction, others objective goods like knowledge, virtue, and friendship. The disagreement cannot be settled empirically because value is not a fact. Yet we can reason about values, refine them, test them against our lives and our principles.

Aesthetic value is value we find in beauty, art, and the texture of everyday experience. Judgments of beauty are subjective in that they express how a person perceives something, yet they carry a claim to universality. When we say something is beautiful, we expect others might come to agree. This suggests that beauty is not merely idiosyncratic preference but involves real responsiveness to form, proportion, and presence.

The meaning of a life comes from fitting fulfillment: pursuing something that is genuinely valuable and that you are genuinely invested in. This requires both objective goods (things actually worth pursuing) and subjective commitment (genuine desire to pursue them). Neither alone is sufficient.

The deepest question in value theory concerns moral realism: whether there are objective moral facts independent of human desire, or whether all values ultimately ground in human preferences and psychology. This question cannot be settled by empirical investigation. It requires examining the structure of our moral reasoning, our sense that some things are genuinely wrong, and our need to evaluate whether our own desires are worth having.

### Application Questions

1. Consider a value you hold (fairness, creativity, loyalty, compassion). Is it intrinsic or extrinsic? Can you reduce it to pleasure or desire-satisfaction, or does Moore's open-question argument apply?

2. A person pursues a career in art and earns very little money. From the perspective of extrinsic value (means to other ends like wealth or status), the career seems unwise. But suppose the person claims their life is good. What do you think they mean? Is their claim coherent?

3. Look at a piece of music, visual art, or nature that you find beautiful. Why do you find it beautiful? Is your judgment subjective (based on your taste) or objective (based on properties in the thing itself)? How would Kant explain your response?

### Synthesis Questions

1. Trace the connection between Moore's open-question argument and the experience machine. Both seem to suggest that something matters about our lives besides pleasure or desire-satisfaction. What is that something?

2. A utilitarian believes the right action maximizes pleasure or satisfaction. But most people would not plug into the experience machine, and most people do not always act to maximize their own pleasure. What does this suggest about utilitarian theories of value? Are they wrong about what is good, or wrong about what is good for *human beings specifically*?

3. Suppose someone says: "All values are subjective and grounded only in human preference." Does this claim undermine the possibility of moral progress (society becoming more just, more compassionate)? Or can moral progress be real even if values are subjective?

### Challenge Questions

1. Is there a fact of the matter about whether female genital mutilation is wrong? If values are objective, how do we know them? If values are subjective, how can we say a culture is wrong? Is there a middle ground?

2. Some philosophers argue that meaning requires fitting fulfillment—pursuing an objective good. But how do we recognize an objective good when we find one? What distinguishes pursuing a genuinely valuable goal from being trapped in a culturally-imposed preference that *feels* like a good?

## Connections Forward

The next chapter, normative moral theory, asks what we *ought* to do—the practical implications of value theory. Once you understand what makes something good (intrinsically valuable, conducive to flourishing, beautiful), you can ask which actions are right, which virtues worth cultivating, which societies just.

But notice what we have learned here: we cannot answer those questions by empirical investigation alone. We cannot run an experiment that decides what is good. We must reason. We must examine our values, test them against counterexample, check whether they cohere with our other commitments. We must accept that disagreement is possible even among thoughtful people because we are disagreeing about something that is not a fact—it is a value.

This does not mean moral reasoning is baseless or arbitrary. It means it is *different* from scientific reasoning. It means that to understand ethics, you must understand that values are real in their consequences even if they are not facts about nature. A person can organize their entire life around what is good, can sacrifice comfort and security for a principle, can find meaning in difficulty. These are facts about human life—facts about how value functions. The meaning exists whether or not the universe contains any objective moral facts.

## What Would Change My Mind

If I encountered a rigorous proof that either (1) all moral properties are reducible to natural properties without remainder, or (2) all moral properties are purely subjective and individual, I would revise my understanding of the nature of moral disagreement. Currently, I hold that moral disagreement is real (not merely preference) without yet being certain whether it tracks objective facts or something else. A definitive argument on either side would refocus the entire framework.

I do not yet fully understand what separates genuine aesthetic appreciation from culturally-conditioned preference. When I find a painting beautiful, is my response genuine insight into the thing itself, or is it the internalization of aesthetic values I absorbed from my culture? The experience feels like genuine responsiveness, but so does preference. How do we distinguish them?
