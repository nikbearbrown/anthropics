# Epistemology: What Knowledge Actually Is

## Three Suggested Titles

1. **How We Know: The Gettier Problem and Two Thousand Years of Certainty Undone**

3. **Justified True Belief Isn't Enough: Why Philosophers Had to Rebuild Knowledge from Scratch**

## TL;DR

For 2,300 years, philosophers believed knowledge was simple: justified true belief. In 1963, Edmund Gettier published a three-page paper with two counterexamples that broke that definition entirely. This chapter traces what knowledge actually is, why the definition failed, how philosophers have tried to fix it, and what skepticism teaches us about the limits of what we can ever claim to know.

---

## Chapter Opening: July 1963, Analysis Magazine

Edmund Gettier was a young American philosopher. He worked at Wayne State University. He had written relatively little. What he published in *Analysis* that summer was a paper of exactly three pages titled "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?" It contained no elaborate theory. No new framework. Just two counterexamples—hypothetical cases so simple that any philosophy student could understand them in five minutes.

By the end of the decade, epistemology (the philosophy of knowledge) was in turmoil. By the end of the century, thousands of papers had been written trying to patch the hole Gettier had opened. The definition of knowledge that had survived from Plato through Descartes, Hume, Russell, and everyone in between—knowledge is justified true belief—was dead. And it stayed dead.

What was remarkable was not that Gettier was brilliant. It was that the problem seemed so obvious once he pointed it out. Philosophers began asking themselves: How did we miss this for two thousand years? The answer is instructive. Philosophers had been building their theories by asking the wrong question. They were asking, "What makes a belief true?" and "What makes a belief justified?" But they had not fully asked, "What separates a lucky true belief from genuine knowledge?" That gap—between having all the right ingredients and actually having understanding—is where the entire field of modern epistemology lives.

## Concept Section 1: Knowledge as Justified True Belief — And What Went Wrong

Consider a simple scenario. You walk into my office and I ask, "Do you know that there is a bird on the branch outside this window?"

You look. You see the bird clearly. Sunlight. Normal vision. Normal lighting. You say, "Yes, I know there's a bird there."

I ask you what makes that knowledge. You give me three things:

First, the belief itself. You believe "There is a bird on that branch." Without belief, you could not have knowledge. No one says, "I know that X is true, but I don't believe it." That sounds incoherent.

Second, the truth. The bird is actually there. Your belief matches reality. If you had hallucinated the bird, or if a trick of light had fooled you, then you would not know—you would merely have a false belief. Knowledge requires that you get it right.

Third, justification. You did not guess. You did not have a hunch. You have good reasons for the belief. You saw the bird with your own eyes, in normal conditions, under normal lighting, and your vision is generally reliable. You have evidence. This is what philosophers mean by justification—having adequate reasons that make the belief rational, well-supported, earned rather than lucky.

This simple picture—that knowledge is **belief plus truth plus justification**—is called the Justified True Belief account, or JTB. It comes from Plato's dialogue *Theaetetus*, where Socrates argues that knowledge is "true opinion with an account" (an account being the justification). The definition held for over two thousand years. It was taught in every introductory philosophy course. It felt intuitively right.

Then Gettier broke it.

**Here is the first Gettier case.** A nomad is traveling across the desert in search of water. He crests a dune and sees what appears to be an oasis in the valley below—shimmering water, vegetation, a pool to drink from. He forms the belief: "There is water in that valley." His belief is justified. He has good reason to believe it. He is using his perception, which is generally reliable.

But there is a problem. The oasis is a mirage. There is no water in the valley where the mirage appears.

However—and this is the crucial move—there *is* water in the valley. It is just beneath the surface, hidden under the sand. The mirage is in exactly the wrong place. The nomad is looking at a hallucination caused by heat and light refraction, but his conclusion that there is water in the valley is true.

So the nomad has a belief (there is water in the valley). The belief is true (there is water there). The belief is justified (he has sensory evidence from a reliable source). Yet does he *know* there is water in the valley? No. Not really. He is right by accident. He formed a true belief for the wrong reason. The fact that water happens to exist in that location is a matter of pure luck.

**Here is the second Gettier case, slightly more modern.** A man looks at a stopped clock at exactly the right moment—the moment when the clock *happens* to show the correct time. He thinks the clock is working. He has good reason to think so. He bought it from a reputable store. It has been reliable in the past. He forms the belief: "It is 4:30." And it is 4:30. The belief is true. The justification seems good (he trusted a reliable instrument).

But the clock is broken. It stopped weeks ago. By pure chance, he glanced at it at the exact moment it displayed the actual time. He *knows* what time it is? No. He lucked into a true belief.

**The third case is the hardest.** Henry is driving through a rural area. All around him are barns. He points at one and says, "That is a barn." And he is right. It is a real barn. But what he doesn't know is that he is driving through "fake barn country"—an area being used as a movie set where almost every structure is a fake barn facade. He could have easily pointed at one of the fakes and been wrong. He lucked into pointing at the one real barn.

In all three cases, the person has justified true belief. Yet no one would say they have knowledge. The justification is not properly connected to the truth. There is a gap between having all the right pieces and actually having understanding.

**The trade-off:** The JTB definition was elegant. It was simple. It captured something intuitive about what knowledge requires. But that simplicity came at a cost. Philosophers had not paid attention to the way luck could sneak into the picture. They had not asked hard enough: What happens when a person has good reasons but those reasons point in the wrong direction, yet the conclusion turns out to be true anyway? Once that question was asked, the simple definition shattered.

**A common misconception:** People sometimes think that the Gettier problem means knowledge is impossible, or that it proves skepticism. It does not. Gettier cases are artificial. In ordinary circumstances, when you have justified true belief, you typically have knowledge. But the cases show that justification plus truth is not *sufficient* for knowledge. Something is missing. The question became: What?

## Concept Section 2: Rationalism vs. Empiricism — Two Opposite Paths to Knowledge

But before we can understand what philosophers have tried to add to the JTB definition, we need to examine a deeper question: Where does justification come from in the first place? How do we get reasons? What makes a belief justified?

This is where one of the oldest debates in epistemology begins. It cuts across centuries and civilizations. The question is simple: Does knowledge come primarily from reason or from experience?

René Descartes was sitting in a room heated by a stove, in winter, in Holland in 1641. He decided to doubt everything that could possibly be doubted. He doubted his senses—they had deceived him before. He doubted the external world—he could be dreaming. He even imagined a powerful demon feeding him false experiences. But there was one thing he could not doubt: the fact that he was doubting. "I am thinking, therefore I am," he wrote (*cogito, ergo sum*). That unshakeable truth—the existence of thought itself—could not be questioned. It was known not through the senses but through reason alone. From that foundation, Descartes built his philosophy.

Descartes was a rationalist. He believed that genuine knowledge comes through reason, through the mind's ability to think clearly and distinctly about reality. The senses are unreliable. Experience is fallible. But reason—pure reasoning about what must be true—can give you certainty.

Across the channel, in England and Scotland, a different tradition emerged. David Hume, writing a century later, looked at empirical knowledge and asked: Where does it come from? When you know that the sun will rise tomorrow, that knowledge does not come from pure reason. You cannot deduce it from first principles. It comes from experience. You have observed the sun rising countless times. You have built an expectation based on accumulated observations. This is empirical knowledge. Hume was an empiricist. He believed that knowledge comes primarily through the senses, through experience of the world.

But Hume pushed the empiricist view to its logical limit and found a problem. Consider causality. You see one billiard ball strike another, and the second ball moves. You call the first ball's motion the "cause" and the second ball's motion the "effect." But what is the cause itself? You never perceive causality directly. You perceive one event, then another event. You perceive temporal order and spatial proximity. But the causal link itself—the power of one thing to make another happen—is never observed. Causality is an *inference* you make, not a direct perception. This means empiricism faces a hard question: If knowledge comes from experience, but we cannot experience causality, where does our knowledge of causality come from? How can we ever know that one thing causes another?

This is the problem of induction. Hume stated it clearly: No amount of past observations can ever logically guarantee a future observation. Just because the sun has risen every day of your life does not logically prove it will rise tomorrow. The future is not logically determined by the past. Yet we all live as though it is. We expect the sun to rise. We expect gravity to work the same way tomorrow as it did today. How do we justify these expectations?

Immanuel Kant, a German philosopher, tried to resolve the debate. He agreed with rationalists that certain knowledge must come through the structure of the mind itself. The laws of space, time, and causality are not discovered in experience. They are the *framework* through which experience becomes possible. You cannot experience anything except through space and time. You cannot make sense of anything except by applying causal reasoning. These are not empirical facts about the world. They are the preconditions for any experience at all. Kant called this knowledge *a priori*—knowledge that is prior to experience, independent of it.

But Kant also agreed with empiricists that we learn about the specific facts of the world through experience. Geometry is a priori knowledge (true prior to experience). But knowing that a specific rock is in a specific valley is a posteriori knowledge (it requires experience). Knowledge comes from both sources. Neither reason nor experience alone is enough.

**The trade-off is this:** Rationalism gives you certainty but leaves you disconnected from the world. If all knowledge comes from reason, how can you trust that your reasoning reflects reality? Empiricism gives you connection to the world but leaves you with probability, not certainty. If knowledge comes from experience, you can never move beyond what you have observed to what must be true. Every empirical belief is fallible. The rationalist has certainty but risks irrelevance. The empiricist has relevance but loses certainty.

**A worked example: Mathematical knowledge.** Consider 2 + 2 = 4. Is this knowledge a priori or a posteriori? 

A rationalist says: This is known through reason alone. You need not count objects to know that two objects plus two objects equal four objects. You can reason your way to the conclusion. Therefore it is a priori knowledge.

An empiricist says: Even mathematical knowledge rests on experience. First you learned by counting objects. You observed that two sticks and two sticks made four sticks. From those observations, you abstracted the principle. It is based on experience.

Kant would say: The structure of mathematics is a priori—it is built into how we think. But we apply that structure to empirical objects. So mathematics is both. It is synthetic (it tells us something substantive about the world, not just definitions) and a priori (we know it independent of specific experiences). This is what Kant called synthetic *a priori* knowledge—the most interesting kind.

**A common misconception:** People think rationalism and empiricism are opposed views where only one can be right. In fact, all epistemologists (even modern ones) grant that both reason and experience matter. The debate is about which is fundamental, which is more reliable, and how they work together. Modern epistemology incorporates both. The question has shifted to: How do we weigh them? Which takes priority when they conflict? What justifies us in trusting reason when experience seems to contradict it?

## Concept Section 3: Skepticism — The Challenge That Never Dies

Now we arrive at the deepest question. Forget about Gettier cases for a moment. Forget about the rationalism-empiricism debate. Ask a more basic question: How do you know you are not dreaming right now?

You might say: "I can tell the difference between dreaming and waking. Dreams are chaotic and illogical. This experience is coherent."

But a skilled logician will push back: "When you are dreaming, you also feel like you are having a coherent experience. You believe you are awake while you are dreaming. You cannot tell the difference *from the inside*. So how do you know you are not dreaming now?"

This is not a new worry. Zhuang Zhou, a Chinese Taoist philosopher from the 4th century BCE, expressed it perfectly. He wrote about dreaming he was a butterfly, so vividly that when he awoke, he could not tell whether he had been a man dreaming he was a butterfly, or a butterfly dreaming he was a man. If the experience is indistinguishable, how can you know which is real?

Descartes took the worry further. Dreams are one thing. But what if something more powerful was deceiving you? What if an evil demon—a being of immense power—could reach into your mind and create any experience it wanted? The demon could make you see a world that does not exist. It could make you feel sensations that are not caused by anything real. It could trick you completely and you would have no way to detect the deception. If an evil demon could be feeding you false experiences right now, how can you know anything about the external world?

The modern version of this thought is Hilary Putnam's brain in a vat. Imagine that while you slept, scientists removed your brain and placed it in a vat of nutrients. Your brain is connected to a supercomputer that feeds it experiences. The computer recreates your entire life—all your memories, all your perceptions, everything. From the inside, your experience is identical to normal life. You cannot tell the difference. But nothing you perceive actually exists. The world you think you see is a simulation.

Here is the logical structure of skeptical worry:

1. I cannot rule out the possibility that I am dreaming / being deceived by a demon / am a brain in a vat.
2. If I cannot rule out that possibility, then I am not justified in believing claims about the external world.
3. Therefore, I am not justified in believing anything about the external world.
4. Therefore, I have no knowledge of the external world.

This argument is called global skepticism. It denies the possibility of knowledge in general, particularly about the external world.

There are responses. G. E. Moore, a British philosopher, offered what seems almost offensively simple. He raised his hand and said: "Here is one hand. Here is another hand. Therefore, hands exist. Therefore, skepticism is false." The skeptic is wrong because he claims to know nothing about the external world, but he clearly knows his own hands exist.

Moore's move is clever. He does not try to refute the skeptic's argument on the skeptic's terms. He refuses to grant the skeptic's premise that he must rule out every conceivable alternative. Instead, he says: "I am more confident in my knowledge that I have two hands than I am in any argument that would deny it. If your argument conflicts with my direct knowledge, your argument is wrong, not my knowledge."

But the skeptic has a response too. Someone who accepts the possibility of the skeptical hypothesis (the demon, the brain in the vat) will insist that Moore's claim about his hands does not actually refute anything. The skeptical hypothesis makes that claim uncertain.

One way out is contextualism. The idea is that "knowledge" is not a fixed concept. What counts as knowledge depends on the context. In everyday life, when you are identifying a bird outside your window, you do not need to rule out the hypothesis that you are a brain in a vat. That hypothesis is not relevant to your context. What matters is whether you might be mistaken about identifying the *bird*—whether it is a sparrow or a finch, whether you have good eyesight, etc.

In contrast, if you are in a philosophy class discussing skepticism, the context changes. Now wild hypotheses become relevant. In that context, a higher standard of justification applies.

Different contexts allow different standards. In daily life, you have knowledge of many things. In a philosophy seminar, skepticism has more bite. Both can be true. Knowledge is context-dependent.

**The trade-off:** Skepticism is troubling because it seems to prove that knowledge is impossible. But skepticism is also valuable. It forces us to ask hard questions about justification. It clarifies what we really mean by knowledge. It reveals assumptions we were taking for granted. A philosopher who engages with skepticism will think more clearly about knowledge than one who dismisses it outright. The price is discomfort. The payoff is precision.

**A common misconception:** People sometimes think skepticism is refuted by showing that we have reliable access to the world (through perception, reason, testimony). But skepticism is not really about reliability. The brain-in-a-vat scenario does not claim your perceptions are *unreliable*. It says they are perfectly reliable—at representing the computer's simulation. The problem is that perfect reliability at representing a simulation is still not knowledge of the real world. Skepticism cuts deeper than reliability. It questions whether even perfectly reliable processes give you access to reality.

**A second response to skepticism: Wittgensteinian hinge propositions.** Ludwig Wittgenstein, an Austrian-British philosopher, offered a different kind of response. He noted that in any inquiry, there are some propositions we do not question—we treat them as fixed points, as hinges on which our entire system of reasoning swings. When you are playing chess, you do not question the rules of chess mid-game. When you are investigating a crime, you do not question whether the room where the crime occurred actually exists. These "hinge propositions" are not beyond doubt because we have proven them. They are beyond question because doubting them would make inquiry impossible.

Wittgenstein's insight is that skepticism tries to question a hinge proposition—the proposition that the external world exists. But once you have adopted the project of trying to gain knowledge, you have already committed to the existence of something to have knowledge about. The skeptical hypothesis becomes incoherent within the context of inquiry itself. Not because it is logically refutable, but because accepting it would undermine the very activity the skeptic is engaged in (trying to refute non-skeptics).

This does not satisfy all philosophers, but it points to something important: skepticism has force only within certain contexts. Outside those contexts, the skeptical hypothesis seems irrelevant. This is related to contextualism, but Wittgenstein's point is slightly different—it is about the *internal logic* of the activity of inquiry itself.

## Integration and Synthesis: A Case That Uses All Three

Let me bring these pieces together with a concrete question: How do you know that you are reading a text written by a real human being and not generated by an artificial system?

This is not a silly question anymore. It is a live epistemological problem. Let me trace how it engages all three concepts.

**Start with the JTB framework.** You believe that a human wrote what you are reading. You have justification: the text is coherent, displays knowledge, has a voice and perspective. You could check the publication details, the author's history, institutional affiliation. These would provide evidence. But is the belief true? Is there actually a human author, or is it a statistical model trained on human text? And if you cannot be certain of the truth, do you have knowledge?

**Bring in rationalism vs. empiricism.** An empiricist would say: Check the evidence. Look at the text. Is the reasoning sound? Does the knowledge seem genuine? A rationalist would say: Think about what it means to be a human author. What must be true about consciousness, intention, understanding for something to count as a "human" text? Different epistemological frameworks give you different guidance. The empiricist says: More evidence will resolve this. The rationalist says: You need clarity on what you even mean by "human author" before evidence can help.

**Finally, apply skepticism.** Could a system be indistinguishable from a human author and yet not be human? Yes, by definition. Could you ever rule out that possibility? It depends on your standard. If you require absolute certainty, no. If you are satisfied with "reasonable confidence in context," yes. The skeptical hypothesis—that the author is not human—is consistent with all the evidence you have. So in what sense do you know the author is human?

The resolution is not to abandon knowledge. It is to recognize that knowledge happens at different levels. You can have justified true belief without absolute certainty. You can have understanding without eliminating every conceivable alternative. You can have knowledge in a context where skeptical hypotheses are set aside as irrelevant. The details matter. The precision matters. This is what epistemology teaches.

## Chapter Summary

Epistemology is the philosophy of knowledge and justification. For two thousand years, philosophers believed knowledge was simple: a justified true belief. This definition came from Plato and seemed intuitive. A belief is knowledge when you believe something, it is true, and you have good reasons for believing it.

In 1963, Edmund Gettier showed the definition was incomplete with two counterexamples. In both cases, a person had justified true belief yet did not have knowledge because the justification was disconnected from the truth. The belief was lucky—accidentally right for the wrong reasons.

This launched modern epistemology. Philosophers have spent sixty years trying to clarify what knowledge is. Some added a fourth condition (no false lemmas, no defeaters, sensitivity, safety). Others replaced the justification condition with something more robust (reliabilism—beliefs formed by reliable processes count as knowledge). Others appealed to virtue epistemology—knowledge comes from intellectual virtues like attentiveness, open-mindedness, intellectual courage.

The rationalism-empiricism debate asks where justification comes from. Does it come from reason (rationalism), from experience (empiricism), or from both working in tandem (Kant's synthesis)? Modern epistemology grants that both matter. The question is how they relate when they conflict.

Skepticism challenges the possibility of knowledge itself. If you cannot rule out that you are dreaming, or being deceived, or are a brain in a vat, then how can you have justified beliefs about the external world? The skeptic's argument has the logical structure: You cannot rule out alternative hypotheses, therefore you cannot have knowledge. Responses include Moore's commonsense appeal, internalism (you need only rule out relevant alternatives), and contextualism (knowledge is context-dependent). Skepticism does not disprove knowledge. It clarifies what justification requires.

Beyond traditional epistemology, applied epistemology examines how knowledge works in social contexts. Social epistemology studies how groups form justified beliefs, how testimony creates knowledge, and how power dynamics affect who counts as a reliable source. Epistemic injustice occurs when perspectives are unfairly excluded or when language cannot capture people's experiences.

## Graduated Exercises

### Warm-up Questions

1. Gettier's nomad sees a mirage but there is water beneath the surface. What does this case teach us about the relationship between justification and truth? Why is being right not the same as knowing?

2. Moore raised his hand and said, "Here is one hand." What is he trying to prove against the skeptic? What assumption about knowledge is Moore relying on?

3. Rationalists trust reason. Empiricists trust experience. What does Hume's problem of induction show about empiricism? Why can't past observations logically guarantee future ones?

### Application Questions

1. Consider a case where you learned something from a friend's testimony. You believed it, it turned out to be true, and you had good reason to trust your friend. Did you have knowledge? How would you determine whether the testimony was properly connected to the truth?

2. Suppose a neuroscientist claims that all your beliefs are determined by prior causes in your brain. Is this claim a priori or a posteriori knowledge? What epistemological framework (rationalism, empiricism, or Kant's synthesis) best accounts for how we know this is true?

3. You read a news article about a political event. You believe it, you have justification (the article is from a reputable source), and let us grant that it is true. But what if the article's reasoning was flawed, or it relied on false premises, yet still reached a true conclusion? Would you have knowledge of the event?

### Synthesis Questions

1. The rationalist Descartes began by doubting everything and found that thought itself was certain. The empiricist Hume examined experience and found causality could not be observed. Explain how both philosophers were responding to the question "What can we know?" but arriving at different answers because they trusted different sources of justification.

2. Consider Gettier's fake barn case. Henry forms the belief "That is a barn" by looking at a barn in an area full of fake barns. His belief is justified and true. But most philosophers say he does not have knowledge. How would each of the following respond: (a) a defender of the JTB account before Gettier, (b) someone who adds a "no false lemmas" condition, (c) someone who appeals to contextualism?

3. A brain in a vat has perfectly reliable perceptions—they reliably represent what the computer is feeding it. Yet the brain-in-a-vat has no knowledge of the external world. What does this tell you about whether reliability alone is sufficient for knowledge? What else might be required?

### Challenge Questions

1. Some epistemologists have proposed that knowledge requires a proper causal connection between your belief and the fact that makes it true. But how would you define "proper"? In Gettier's cases, is the connection improper? If so, what makes it improper?

2. Putnam argued that the semantic content of your thoughts (what they are *about*) depends on your external environment, not just your internal mental state. If Putnam is right, can a brain in a vat even have beliefs about the external world, or can it only have beliefs about the computer simulation? What does your answer imply for the skeptic's argument?

3. Applied epistemology studies how knowledge works in social systems. How does the epistemology of testimony (learning from what others tell us) change the picture of knowledge? If most of what you know comes from testimony, does that make knowledge less certain or less objective? Why or why not?

## Connections Forward

The question of knowledge defines the boundaries of all other philosophy. Metaphysics asks what exists. But metaphysics depends on epistemology—it assumes you can have knowledge of what exists. Ethics asks how you should live. But that depends on knowing what the good is, what consequences follow from your actions, what others need. Political philosophy asks about justice and rights. But justice requires knowledge—knowledge of what people deserve, what arrangements are fair, what effects policies produce.

When you examine any philosophical question deeply, you discover an epistemological assumption beneath it. How do we know what is real? How do we know what is good? How do we know what other people are experiencing? These are the questions epistemology lives in. The rest of philosophy builds on the answers—or on the recognition that the answers are harder than we thought.

Epistemology is also the foundation for understanding how inquiry works in science, law, journalism, and everyday reasoning. When a scientist claims to have discovered something, they are making an epistemological claim—they are saying they have knowledge, not just opinion. When a jury must decide whether someone is guilty, they are wrestling with epistemology—what standard of justification is required to move from belief to knowledge that someone committed a crime? When you evaluate a source of information in the digital age, you are practicing epistemology—asking whether this source is reliable, whether it has a proper causal connection to the truth, whether there are defeaters you should consider. The abstract problems in epistemology have immediate practical consequences.

## What Would Change My Mind

If I encountered a persuasive demonstration that the Gettier problem is actually a pseudo-problem—that careful attention to what justification really means (as opposed to what philosophers have carelessly meant by it) dissolves the apparent counterexamples—I would revise my confidence that Gettier identified a genuine gap in the JTB definition.

## Still Puzzling

I do not yet fully understand how reliabilism avoids the problem that a perfectly reliable process can still deliver only accidentally true beliefs (as in the brain-in-a-vat scenario where a simulated brain's perceptions are reliable at representing the simulation but not the external world). There seems to be something about the *connection* between justification and reality that requires more than reliability.
