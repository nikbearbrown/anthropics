# Normative Moral Theory: How to Choose What to Do

## Three Suggested Titles

1. The Machinery of Moral Choice: Consequences, Rules, Character, and Care
2. When You Don't Know What's Right: Five Frameworks That Disagree
3. How Philosophers Learned to Calculate Morality — and Why the Calculation Breaks

---

## TL;DR

Five major approaches to normative ethics have emerged across human philosophy, each focusing on different ingredients for determining what you ought to do: consequences (utilitarian and Mohist), duties or rules (Kantian), character (virtue ethics), relationships (care ethics), and harmony with natural and social order (Confucian, Daoist, and Buddhist). No single framework handles all cases well. Understanding how they work and where they collide is how you develop judgment.

---

## Chapter Opening: The Scene

Immanuel Kant, pacing along a fixed walking route in Königsberg, Prussia, in the 1780s — so punctual that neighbors could set their clocks by him, every day at the same hour — was at work on a question that seemed simple but turned out to collapse the moment you tried to answer it honestly.

What makes an action right?

Not what makes it succeed. Not what makes you happy. Not what your parents taught you to do. But what makes it *right* — morally right, in a way that applies to all rational beings everywhere, in a way that doesn't depend on what you want or what happens next.

Kant was working toward *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785), a text that would insist: an action is right when it accords with a rule you could will every rational being in the universe to follow. Not a rule that happens to work out well. A rule that is *universally binding* by the structure of reason itself.

Across Europe, Jeremy Bentham — Kant's contemporary, though they never knew each other's work — was proposing something that looked radically simpler: every moral decision is a math problem. You list every person affected by your action. You calculate the pleasure or pain your action produces for each of them. You add it up. You choose the action that produces the most pleasure (or the least pain) overall. In *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* (1789), Bentham called this the "felicific calculus" — the calculus of happiness.

Two centuries earlier in ancient China, Confucius had been teaching something different again. He was not interested in universal rules or happiness calculations. He cared about relationships: a son's respect for his father, a ruler's care for his people, the ritual practices that bind society together. The right action flows from your position in a web of human bonds, and from the cultivation of a virtuous character through practice and habit.

By the fifth century BCE, in Greece, Aristotle had arrived at still another view. Everything alive is aimed at something. A knife aims at cutting. A human being — made distinct from other animals by the capacity to reason — aims at *eudaimonia*, a flourishing life. The right action is the one that a person of excellent character would choose after deliberation. Character is built through habit. A virtuous person acts rightly the way a skilled musician plays: from long practice, without conscious calculation.

These are not five theories arriving at the same answer from different directions. They are five frameworks that often produce *conflicting verdicts* on what you ought to do — and understanding why they conflict is how you develop moral judgment.

## Concept Section 1: Consequentialism and Utilitarianism

### The Cold Open

Jeremy Bentham did something that philosophers before him had not quite done: he proposed that morality could be calculated. Not estimated. Not guessed at. *Calculated*.

The principle was radical in its simplicity. An action is right if and only if it produces the greatest good for the greatest number of people. That is all. Not your intentions. Not your character. Not the rules you follow. The consequences, measured in happiness or pleasure.

What makes this work is that happiness is fungible — you can add it up. You can compare a unit of my happiness to a unit of your happiness. They are the same thing. All pleasures count equally. Bentham wrote that "quantity of pleasure being equal, push-pin is as good as poetry" — meaning a simple children's game produces the same pleasure as high art, if the quantity is the same. What matters is the arithmetic, not the refinement.

### The Mechanism: How Utilitarian Calculation Works

Here is how the calculation actually goes:

1. Identify all the people affected by your action.
2. For each person, estimate the happiness (or unhappiness) your action will produce.
3. Add up the total.
4. Choose the action with the highest sum.

This is not a vague instruction. Bentham meant it literally. You map the consequences. You quantify them.

Consider a concrete example. You borrowed a friend's car. You want to return it with a full tank, but you don't know which fuel it uses and cannot reach your friend. Utilitarian reasoning looks like this:

**Option A: Return the car empty.**
- Your friend: unhappiness from having to fill the tank yourself (−3 happiness units, say).
- Your friend: mild frustration at you not being considerate (+0, neutral to negative).
- You: guilt about not trying (−2).
- You: relief at avoiding the risk of using wrong fuel (+1).
- **Total: −4 happiness units.**

**Option B: Ask someone at the pump; guess at the fuel.**
- Your friend: full tank (grateful, +3).
- You: relief at having tried (+1).
- Risk: if you guess wrong, you cause expensive engine damage (say, −50 happiness units if this occurs, times 20% probability of guessing wrong = −10 expected happiness).
- **Total: +3 + 1 − 10 = −6 happiness units (worse than A).**

**Option C: Wait to reach your friend before returning the car.**
- Your friend: inconvenience from waiting (−2).
- You: inconvenience from waiting (−1).
- Your friend: reassurance that the car is returned safely with full tank (+2).
- You: peace of mind from doing it right (+1).
- **Total: 0 happiness units (better than A or B).**

Under utilitarian calculation, Option C is the right action because it produces the most net happiness (or the least net unhappiness).

This works when the variables are tractable. Bentham believed that as societies developed better tools for measuring well-being, the calculation would become more precise, not less. We would get better at estimating consequences. The principle would hold.

### The Trade-Off: What Utilitarianism Gains and Loses

**What it optimizes for:** Utilitarianism places one value above all — maximizing well-being. It is maximally impartial. Your happiness counts no more and no less than anyone else's. It demands that you step outside your own preferences and treat everyone equally. This produces a framework that is, in theory, fair: no one is worth less than anyone else.

**What it sacrifices:** The theory demands you ignore your own projects, your commitments, your relationships, and your agent-centered permissions whenever they do not produce the maximum good. Utilitarianism has been criticized for being too *demanding*. It seems to require that you spend every penny on effective charity, that you never relax or pursue your own happiness except as an instrument for maximizing overall happiness, that you would be morally obliged to harm an innocent person if that harm prevented greater harm elsewhere.

John Stuart Mill, Bentham's successor in the utilitarian tradition, tried to refine the calculation. In *Utilitarianism* (1861), Mill argued that not all pleasures are equal. Some pleasures are *higher* — pleasures of the mind, participation in culture, intellectual engagement — and these are intrinsically more valuable than *lower* pleasures, which are merely sensory.

Mill's famous claim: "It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied." The meaning is that a dissatisfied human who knows higher pleasures has access to something more valuable than a pig who knows only sensory satisfaction. Dissatisfaction itself — the recognition that you are capable of more — is a form of higher pleasure.

Mill also introduced the distinction between act utilitarianism and rule utilitarianism. An act utilitarian says: apply the greatest happiness principle to each action individually. Choose the action that produces the most happiness *right now*. A rule utilitarian says: apply the greatest happiness principle to *rules*. Choose the set of rules that, if everyone followed them, would produce the most happiness overall.

The difference is crucial. An act utilitarian might argue that in one specific case, lying would produce more happiness than truth-telling, so you ought to lie. A rule utilitarian says: the rule "everyone lies when they think it will maximize happiness" produces total catastrophe (no one trusts anyone; all cooperation collapses). So the rule "tell the truth" is better, and you follow it even in cases where breaking it would produce more happiness *for that act*.

### Common Misconception

**The misconception:** Utilitarians don't care about morality; they only care about happiness.

**What's actually true:** Utilitarians care about morality because they believe happiness *is* the moral value. They are not saying "forget about right and wrong; just be happy." They are saying "happiness and unhappiness are the only moral facts, and right action is whatever produces the most happiness." This is a claim about what morality actually is, not a dismissal of morality. The disagreement with other frameworks is real, but it is not about whether morality matters.

### Worked Example: The Drowning Child

Peter Singer, a contemporary utilitarian, asks us to imagine a child drowning in a shallow pond. You are fully clothed. Wading in will ruin your expensive shoes. No one else is nearby. Singer's question: are you obliged to wade in and save the child?

Almost everyone says yes, and the reason is straightforward: the child's life is more important than your shoes.

Singer then asks: if you are obliged to save a child you can see drowning, are you not equally obliged to donate your money to save a child you cannot see drowning but know exists — a child in extreme poverty, at risk of death from preventable disease?

The logic is identical. The child is equally real. The child's death is equally preventable. From the utilitarian perspective, the distance between you and the child is morally irrelevant. What matters is the consequence: a life saved or lost. If you can save a life by donating money instead of buying new clothes, the utilitarian calculation is the same. You ought to donate.

This is why utilitarianism has been criticized as too demanding. It seems to require that we sacrifice nearly all of our personal projects and comforts in service of maximizing overall happiness. Few people live that way. Most of us think we are permitted to pursue our own projects and relationships, not just maximize the good. But utilitarianism, taken seriously, seems to forbid this permission.

## Concept Section 2: Deontology and the Categorical Imperative

### The Cold Open

Immanuel Kant began with a different premise: happiness is not the measure of morality. In fact, focusing on happiness can corrupt morality.

When you act because you want to be happy, or because you want a reward, you are acting from an ulterior motive. You are using the action as a means to get something else. But genuine morality, Kant insisted, cannot be for the sake of happiness. Genuine morality must be *unconditional*. It must have what Kant called "unconditional value" — value in itself, regardless of consequences.

This is where the concept of good will comes in. A good will is the decision to act rightly *because it is right*, not because of what you will gain. Kant wrote: "A good will is good not because of what it effects... but good just by its willing, i.e. in itself."

The question then becomes: what does a good will do? How do you act right if consequences don't matter?

### The Mechanism: Universal Laws and Humanity as an End

Kant proposed that morality consists of laws — rules that bind you unconditionally. These are categorical imperatives. A categorical imperative is a command that applies regardless of what you want or what happens next.

Kant gave four formulations of the categorical imperative. The two most important are:

**First formulation — the Universal Law:** "Act only according to that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law."

A maxim is your rule for acting. Before you act, you should ask: could I will that everyone in the world follows this rule? If the answer is no — if the rule cancels itself when universalized — then the rule is immoral, and the action is wrong.

Example: You want to break a promise because keeping the promise is inconvenient.

Your maxim might be: "I will break promises when it suits me."

Can you will this as a universal law? No. If everyone broke promises whenever it suited them, promises would cease to exist. There would be no institution of promise-keeping to break. So the rule defeats itself under universalization. Therefore, breaking the promise is wrong.

**Second formulation — Humanity as an End:** "So act that you use humanity, in your own person as well as in the person of any other, always at the same time as an end, never merely as a means."

This says: every person possesses inherent dignity by virtue of being rational. You ought never to treat a person as mere means to your goals. You can cooperate with others and benefit from their actions, but you must treat them as ends in themselves — as rational beings who deserve respect and the freedom to make their own decisions based on their own reasoning.

Example: You want to lie to someone to get a discount.

You are treating the person you lie to as a mere means — their ability to make a rational decision based on truthful information has been undermined. You have used them to get what you want. This is wrong, not because the lie will produce bad consequences, but because it violates their dignity as a rational being.

### The Trade-Off: What Deontology Gains and Loses

**What it optimizes for:** Deontology makes morality unconditional. You have duties that bind you regardless of outcomes. This produces strong protections: you must not kill the innocent, even if killing one innocent person would save ten others. You must not lie, even if a lie would prevent harm. These protections treat fundamental moral rules as inviolable.

**What it sacrifices:** The theory can produce counterintuitive results in cases where following the rule causes great harm. The classical problem is the murderer-at-the-door: a killer comes to your house and asks, "Is your friend inside?" Your friend is inside. A strict Kantian reading says you must not lie — you must tell the truth and let your friend be murdered. (Kant actually did say this, though later Kantians have disputed the reading.)

Deontology also struggles with conflicting duties. If you have a duty to tell the truth and a duty to save innocent lives, what do you do when these duties conflict? Kant's framework does not easily accommodate the idea that duties can be weighed against each other.

### Common Misconception

**The misconception:** Kant believed in absolute rules that never admit exceptions, no matter what.

**What's actually true:** Kant believed in *categorical* imperatives — rules that apply unconditionally, not dependent on what you want. But he also believed that the right action often involves finding the *application* of the rule that fits the situation. The rule "do not lie" is categorical. But what counts as a lie? How does the rule apply when multiple duties seem to conflict? These require judgment. Later deontologists, like William David Ross, developed the concept of prima facie duties — duties that are binding other things being equal, but can be outweighed by competing duties in specific circumstances.

### Worked Example: The Gun-Owner's Dilemma

You know that a neighbor owns a gun. You are concerned about his mental state — he has recently expressed suicidal thoughts. He calls and asks you to return a gun he left at your house months ago.

**Utilitarian reasoning:** Calculate the consequences. If he uses the gun to harm himself or others, the harm is catastrophic. The probability of him obtaining a gun elsewhere is high, but not certain. You estimate that returning the gun increases the expected harm slightly above returning it. You refuse to return it.

**Deontological reasoning:** You made a promise to return his property. You have a duty of fidelity to your word. But you also have a duty of non-maleficence — not to harm others. When duties conflict, you must exercise judgment to determine which is more pressing in this case. The duty of non-maleficence seems more weighty and immediate than the duty of fidelity (you can compensate him later). You refuse to return it. But notice: your reason is not that the consequences are better. Your reason is that saving a life takes precedence over respecting property rights.

Both frameworks refuse to return the gun, but for different reasons. The utilitarian says consequences are better. The deontologist says duties to preserve life are weightier than duties to keep promises. These are not the same claim.

## Concept Section 3: Virtue Ethics, Care Ethics, and Non-Western Frameworks

### The Cold Open

In ancient Greece, around 335 BCE, Aristotle stood in the Lyceum — his school of learning in Athens — and opened his lectures on ethics with an observation that had shaped Greek philosophy for centuries: everything that lives is aimed at something. A plant aims at growth. An animal aims at food and reproduction. A human being aims at something more.

What is the ultimate aim of human life? The ancient Greeks had a word for it: *eudaimonia*. It is often translated as happiness, but the meaning is closer to *flourishing* — living well, realizing your potential, achieving excellence.

The question then becomes: what is human excellence? And the answer, for Aristotle, was not "produce the most happiness" or "follow the universal moral law." The answer was "develop virtuous character and cultivate practical wisdom."

### The Mechanism: Character, Habit, and the Mean

Virtue (aretê in Greek) means excellence. To understand what virtue is, Aristotle said, look at the function of a thing. A knife's function is to cut. A sharp knife that cuts extremely well is a virtuous knife. It realizes its function.

What is the human function? It is the exercise of reason. Everything else — hunger, reproduction, movement — we share with other animals. But reason belongs to us alone. Therefore, human excellence is the perfection of reason. And you perfect your reason through the development of virtuous character.

But virtue is not an intellectual achievement. You don't learn virtue from lectures. You learn it through habit. Aristotle's claim: "We are made perfect by habit."

Here is how it works. Every virtue lies at a mean — a middle ground between two extremes of excess and deficiency. Courage, for example, is the mean between cowardice (deficiency of boldness) and recklessness (excess of boldness). Generosity is the mean between stinginess and profligacy. Proper pride is the mean between humility and arrogance.

When you repeatedly *do* the courageous thing — when you repeatedly face fear and act anyway — you develop the disposition to be courageous. You become someone for whom courageous action is easy and natural, not because you have memorized a rule, but because you have trained your character through repetition.

This takes time. A good upbringing matters because it sets you on the path toward the mean. Poor habits, formed early, make the virtuous path harder. But virtue is always achievable through deliberate practice and right intention.

Once you have developed virtuous character, Aristotle says, you exercise practical wisdom (*phronesis*) — the ability to see what the virtuous action is in this particular situation. Not by applying a formula. By developed judgment.

### Confucian Ethics: Virtue Through Social Roles

In ancient China, during the Warring States period (ca. 475–221 BCE), a time of warfare and social collapse, Confucius arrived at a similar conclusion but grounded it in something different: not the human function in general, but the human function *in relationship*.

Confucius did not ask "what is human excellence in the abstract?" He asked "what duties do I have *in my role*?" He was concerned with social relationships. A son has duties to his father. A father has duties to his son. A ruler has duties to his people. A minister has duties to his ruler.

The concept at the heart of Confucian virtue is *ren* — often translated as benevolence or humaneness, though the meaning is closer to excellence in human relationships. You cultivate *ren* through practice of rituals (*li*) — the customary behaviors, ceremonies, and practices that bind society together.

To understand how this works, consider the ritual of filial piety. A son shows respect to his father not because a rule commands it, but because the ritual practice — the bowing, the obedience, the careful attention to his father's wishes — trains the son to be someone who genuinely cares for his father and understands his responsibilities in the family.

Confucius taught that if every person cultivates virtue within their social role, society becomes harmonious. A *junzi* — an exemplary person — is someone who embodies virtue in their relationships and serves as a model for others to emulate. The *junzi* knows what is right and chooses it, taking into account social roles and norms.

A Confucian ruler does not rule by force or punishment. A ruler who models virtue and integrity inspires his people to be virtuous. Confucius said: "The relation between superiors and inferiors is like that between wind and grass. When the wind blows, the grass bends." The grass does not bend because it is forced; it bends because that is its nature in response to wind.

### Care Ethics: Morality Grounded in Relationships

In the 1980s, psychologist Carol Gilligan published research that challenged traditional normative ethics on different grounds. Gilligan observed that men and women often approach moral dilemmas from different moral perspectives. Men tend to focus on justice, rights, and the application of abstract principles. Women tend to focus on caring, relationships, and the concrete needs of specific individuals.

This observation became the foundation for care ethics — an approach that makes relationships, not rules or consequences, the foundation of morality.

Care ethics asks: what if the fundamental unit of moral reasoning is not the individual, standing alone, but the *relationship*? What if you are always already embedded in networks of dependency and relationship, and morality is about responding to the needs and vulnerabilities of those in your care?

A caregiver — parent, nurse, teacher — is not reasoning about abstract rights or maximizing happiness. They are attending to the specific needs of the person in their care. They are responsive to context. They are aware of the power dynamics in the relationship. They are focused on maintaining the relationship itself.

Nel Noddings, who developed care ethics philosophically, argues that traditional normative theories — both utilitarian and Kantian — treat morality as abstract and universal. They demand impartiality. But impartiality is a false ideal when applied to caregiving. A parent should *not* be impartial between their child and a stranger's child. That partiality — that special commitment to those in your care — is ethically fundamental, not a moral failing.

Care ethics also emphasizes attentiveness to concrete situations. You cannot apply a general rule to determine the right action. You must attend to the specific person, their history, their needs, their vulnerabilities. You must understand context.

### Buddhist Ethics: The Eightfold Path

In Buddhist philosophy, the path to moral living is called the Eightfold Path. It includes: right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration, right understanding, and right intention. These are not rules enforced by an external authority. They are practices that cultivate a mind free from suffering and greed.

The foundation of Buddhist ethics is the principle of non-harm (*ahimsa*) and the understanding that all living beings wish to avoid suffering, just as you do. Therefore, you should not cause suffering to others. This is not because a rule commands it or because causing suffering produces bad consequences, but because non-harm flows from wisdom and compassion.

Buddhist ethics is fundamentally about transforming your character through spiritual practice — meditation, mindfulness, the cultivation of compassion. Right action emerges from a mind that has been purified of greed, hatred, and delusion. The goal is not to maximize happiness or follow rules, but to achieve enlightenment and free yourself and others from suffering.

### Daoist Ethics: Harmony with the Natural Way

Daoism, emerging in China around the same time as Confucianism, offers a radically different approach. Daoists believed that the root of suffering is not moral failure but the clash between human artificiality and the natural way of things — the *dao*.

Instead of cultivating virtues, Daoists practice *wu wei* — often translated as nonaction, though it means something closer to action-without-forcing. When you try too hard to achieve your aims, when you resist the flow of circumstance, you create conflict and suffering. When you act in harmony with the natural flow of things, you are effortless and effective.

Zhuangzi, who developed Daoist thought, offers an image: a skilled swimmer does not think about swimming. A master carpenter does not calculate each cut. Their actions flow from deep alignment with what the situation requires. *Wu wei* is action of this kind — natural, spontaneous, effortless because it accords with circumstance rather than resisting it.

Daoist ethics is not about duty, happiness, or virtue cultivation in the Confucian sense. It is about aligning your being with the natural order.

### Trade-Off: Character, Care, and Context

**What these frameworks optimize for:** Virtue ethics, care ethics, and the non-Western frameworks all emphasize character, relationships, and the concrete situation. They treat morality not as a calculation or the application of a rule, but as a matter of wisdom and good judgment. They take seriously the role of emotion, relationship, and particular contexts.

**What they sacrifice:** These frameworks are less codifiable. It is harder to teach someone what the virtuous action is if the answer depends on their judgment and their training. What counts as right action in a Confucian context depends on social roles and expectations that vary across cultures and time periods. Care ethics makes partiality — favoring those in your care — ethically fundamental, but this can seem unfair. How do you distribute care fairly? If you are partial to those closest to you, what about the distant others?

## Integration: All Five Frameworks Applied to One Case

Let me walk through a real moral dilemma that all five frameworks handle differently, so you can see how the machinery actually works.

**The Case:** You are a doctor. A patient with a terminal illness is in severe pain and has asked you to help him die. He is mentally competent, he has thought carefully about his decision, and he is certain. If you help him, you violate the law in your jurisdiction. Other physicians have gone to prison for this. But the patient is suffering unbearably, and continuing to prolong his life serves no purpose he values.

**Utilitarian Analysis:**
The utilitarian looks at consequences. What produces the most happiness (or the least unhappiness)?

- Continuing the patient's life: continued severe suffering (large negative value), relief to his family that "we tried everything" (small positive value), risk to you of legal consequences (negative value), but also: relief to others with terminal illnesses knowing that such help is available (positive value).
- Helping the patient die: immediate end to unbearable suffering (large positive value), respect for his autonomy and his expressed wishes (positive value), but also: grief to his family (negative value), legal and professional consequences for you (large negative value), possible deterrent effect on other physicians providing end-of-life care (negative value).

The calculation is complex, but a utilitarian might argue that on balance, helping the patient die produces more happiness than forcing him to live. The suffering prevented outweighs the harm caused. But a utilitarian might also argue the opposite: the legal consequences and social upheaval might produce more unhappiness overall. The calculation is empirical; the answer depends on the data.

**Deontological Analysis:**
The deontologist identifies competing duties. You have a duty to preserve life (or at least not to harm). You have a duty to respect the autonomy of a rational agent. You have a duty to relieve suffering.

In this case, the duties conflict. A strict Kantian might say: the duty not to harm an innocent person is absolute. You must not kill, even to relieve suffering. But a pluralist deontologist (like Ross) would say: these are prima facie duties, and when they conflict, you must exercise judgment to determine which is weightier in this context. The duty to relieve unbearable suffering and respect the autonomy of a competent person might outweigh the duty not to end a life when that life contains only agony and when ending it is what the person himself requests. The answer depends on which duty you judge to be most pressing.

**Virtue Ethics (Aristotelian) Analysis:**
The virtue ethicist asks: what would a person of excellent character do? What would courage, compassion, practical wisdom, and justice together suggest?

A virtuous doctor is compassionate — sensitive to suffering. A virtuous doctor is also courageous — willing to act rightly even when it is risky. A virtuous doctor has practical wisdom — the ability to see what the situation truly calls for. A virtuous doctor respects the autonomy and dignity of the patient.

A virtue ethicist might argue that a doctor of good character would help the patient die. The virtuous response to this situation is to acknowledge the patient's dignity and autonomy, to relieve suffering when prolonging it serves no good purpose, and to accept the risk in doing what is right.

But another virtue ethicist might argue differently: virtue includes humility about the limits of human judgment. Who are you to decide when a life is no longer worth living? The virtuous response might be to provide the best palliative care possible and to sit with the patient in his suffering, rather than to make the final decision to end his life.

**Care Ethics Analysis:**
A care ethicist focuses on relationships, vulnerabilities, and the concrete needs of this person. The moral priority is the relationship between doctor and patient, and the patient's expressed needs and wishes.

A care ethicist would emphasize: this is a particular person you know, who has thought carefully about what he needs, who is asking for your help. You are in a position of power and trust. The response should be attentive to his vulnerability, responsive to his wishes, and focused on maintaining the dignity of your relationship. Helping him die, when that is what he is asking for and when continuing to live means only agony, seems consistent with care. But so does providing palliative care combined with deep emotional presence.

The care ethicist would resist the idea that there is a single right answer that applies to all patients in this situation. What matters is the relationship, the particular person, their particular wishes and values.

**Confucian and Buddhist Analysis:**
A Confucian ethicist would focus on your role as a physician. What duties does that role carry? Confucianism emphasizes respect for life and the alleviation of suffering. But it also emphasizes proper roles and relationships. A physician has a duty to heal and to relieve suffering — but the ultimate judgment about when a life has reached its end might belong to the family or to larger social institutions, not to the individual doctor in isolation.

A Buddhist ethicist would apply the principle of non-harm and ask about the sources of suffering in this situation. The patient is suffering because of disease and pain. Helping him die alleviates that suffering. But what about your own actions? Does helping him die create karma that will lead to future suffering for you? Does it reflect a mind free from attachment? A Buddhist might argue that helping him die with compassion and without hatred or attachment is ethically sound. Or a Buddhist might argue that accepting death without hastening it reflects a deeper wisdom.

### The Point

None of these frameworks produces an obviously correct answer. In fact, they diverge. An honest utilitarian might reach one conclusion. An honest Kantian might reach another. An honest virtue ethicist might reach a third. A care ethicist might focus on factors the utilitarian ignored entirely.

This is not a failure of ethics. It is the reality of moral life. You face situations where legitimate ethical frameworks counsel different responses. Understanding *how* they work — what each framework optimizes for, what it sacrifices, where it succeeds and where it struggles — is how you develop judgment.

You learn to ask: in this situation, is the utilitarian's focus on consequences really the right place to look? Or is there something about duties or character or relationships that the consequentialist framework misses?

## Graduated Exercises

### Warm-Up (Conceptual Understanding)

**Exercise 1:** A colleague at work has a serious drinking problem. You have noticed him making mistakes on projects. You are considering whether to report him to your manager or talk to him privately first. For each of the five frameworks, write one sentence describing what that framework would prioritize.

**Exercise 2:** Write down a moral rule you believe in (e.g., "always tell the truth"). Now apply Kant's Universal Law formulation: could you will that everyone follows this rule? What would have to be true for the rule to defeat itself under universalization?

### Application (Using the Frameworks)

**Exercise 3:** You are offered a highly paid job that requires you to move to a city far from your aging parent, who needs increasing care and attention. Using utilitarian reasoning, list all the people affected, estimate the consequences for each, and calculate which option produces more happiness. Then explain what a virtue ethicist would be asking instead.

**Exercise 4:** A student plagiarizes a paper in your class. The student is at risk of expulsion, which would end their financial aid and college career. But allowing plagiarism sets a precedent and disrespects the work of other students. Write out what a deontologist would say (focusing on duties), what a consequentialist would say (focusing on outcomes), and what a care ethicist would ask (focusing on relationships and contexts).

### Synthesis (Integration and Judgment)

**Exercise 5:** The trolley problem — a thought experiment that tests ethical frameworks. A runaway trolley is headed toward five workers on the track. You are standing next to a large stranger on a bridge above the track. The only way to stop the trolley is to push the stranger off the bridge. He will die, but the five workers will be saved.

Is it morally permissible to push him?

First, answer this question from a utilitarian perspective (consequences). Then answer it from a Kantian perspective (duties and the humanity formulation). Then answer it from a virtue ethics perspective. Then explain how a care ethicist might refuse the premise of the question itself.

**Exercise 6:** You discover that a corporation you have invested in is engaging in environmental damage that is legal but harmful to a local community. You can sell your stock (losing money), stay invested (profiting from harm), or use your share of the profits to fund community remediation. Using all five frameworks, what considerations does each raise? Which framework seems to best capture what you think morality actually demands of you in this case? Why?

## Chapter Summary

Normative ethics asks: what should I do? What makes an action right?

Five major frameworks have emerged, each offering a different answer:

1. **Consequentialism and Utilitarianism** focus on outcomes. An action is right if it produces the most happiness (or the least unhappiness) for the most people. This framework is maximally impartial and focuses moral reasoning on measurable results. It struggles with the fact that it seems to require enormous sacrifices of personal projects and relationships.

2. **Deontology** focuses on duties and rules. An action is right if it conforms to a rule that could be universally followed (Kant) and treats persons as ends in themselves, not merely as means. This framework protects fundamental rights and makes morality unconditional. It struggles when duties conflict and when following a rule produces terrible consequences.

3. **Virtue Ethics** focuses on character. An action is right if it flows from the character of a virtuous person — someone who has cultivated excellent traits through habit and who exercises practical wisdom in applying them. This framework emphasizes the development of the whole person and the importance of relationships. It is harder to codify and harder to teach someone who lacks good initial upbringing.

4. **Care Ethics** focuses on relationships and responsiveness to concrete situations. An action is right if it attends to the vulnerabilities and needs of those in your care, respects their particularity, and maintains the quality of the relationship. This framework is sensitive to power dynamics and the specific needs of real people. It struggles with how to extend care fairly beyond those closest to you.

5. **Non-Western and Relational Frameworks** — Confucian ethics, Buddhist ethics, and Daoism — emphasize harmony within social roles (Confucianism), the reduction of suffering through wisdom and compassion (Buddhism), or alignment with the natural flow of things (*wu wei* in Daoism). These frameworks take seriously the context of human relationships and larger natural and social orders. They can struggle with cross-cultural application and with the question of how to act when cultural contexts conflict.

No single framework handles all cases perfectly. Understanding how they work, where they diverge, and why they diverge is how you develop moral judgment.

## Connections Forward

Chapter 10 (Applied Ethics) takes these five frameworks and asks how they work when you apply them to specific, contested issues: euthanasia, abortion, artificial intelligence, environmental ethics. You will see how utilitarians and Kantians reach opposite conclusions on the same case. You will see how virtue ethics and care ethics ask different questions altogether. You will develop the ability to recognize which framework is doing the work in moral arguments you hear and read.

The goal is not to find the one true framework. The goal is to develop fluency across all of them, so that when you face a moral decision, you can ask: what does each framework illuminate about this situation? What does each one miss? And then you can exercise judgment — the distinctly human capacity to weigh evidence, acknowledge uncertainty, and make a decision you can defend.

## What Would Change My Mind

I assume that each framework captures something real about morality — that consequences matter, that duties matter, that character matters, that relationships matter. But I do not assume that they all matter equally in all cases. What would change my thinking would be a decisive argument that one of these frameworks is correct and the others are systematically mistaken — not just less useful in some situations, but actually wrong about what morality is. I have not yet encountered such an argument, which is why I present them as complementary, each with real strength and real weakness.

Or is moral disagreement genuine — are we sometimes simply stuck between legitimate but incompatible ethical demands?
