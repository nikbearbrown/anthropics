## Candidate 01 — Why Taxes Don't Land Where You Think: The Incidence Illusion
- Source: `mba-economics/chapters/05-elasticity.md`
- Topic: ECONOMICS
- Hook: The U.S. payroll tax is split 50/50 by law between workers and employers — but economists estimate workers absorb roughly 80% of it. The legal split and the economic reality are different things.
- Key case: Social Security payroll tax — legally 7.65% each side; empirically most falls on workers because labor supply is more inelastic than labor demand.
- The Question: When the government taxes a seller, who actually ends up poorer — and what determines the answer?
- Core idea: Tax incidence is determined entirely by relative elasticities, not by legal assignment. The burden falls more heavily on whichever side of the market can escape less easily — meaning whoever has fewer substitutes or less time to adjust.
- Visual object: Two-panel tax-wedge diagram — steep inelastic demand vs. steep inelastic supply — showing the consumer price barely moves in one case and the producer price barely moves in the other.
- Manim move: morph (tax wedge inserted, price lines split apart; the consumer-price and producer-price lines diverge as the wedge grows, showing burden allocation)
- Example seed: A $1-per-pack cigarette tax levied on sellers. Cigarette demand is inelastic (addicted buyers, few substitutes), supply is elastic (many tobacco producers). Price to smokers rises nearly the full dollar. The seller absorbs almost nothing. Switch to a luxury yacht tax (elastic demand, inelastic supply): the opposite — prices barely move, sellers absorb the cost.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: c2v (tax wedge diagrams), raster (payroll tax headline, cigarette tax signage)
- Prerequisites: basic supply-and-demand, what "elasticity" means
- Exclusions: omit income elasticity, cross-price elasticity, detailed math derivations
- Score: 10/10

## Candidate 02 — The Lemons Problem: Why Good Used Cars Disappear
- Source: `mba-economics/chapters/16-information-risk-and-insurance.md`
- Topic: ECONOMICS
- Hook: Akerlof's 1970 paper was rejected by three top journals. It won the Nobel Prize. Its insight: when sellers know quality and buyers don't, the market for good cars collapses — and only lemons are left.
- Key case: Used car market. Good-car owners won't sell at the average price (they'd lose money). Bad-car owners will. So only bad cars show up. The buyer figures this out, lowers the offer. More good cars exit. The market unravels.
- The Question: Why do information gaps cause entire markets to fail — not just to price things wrong, but to stop existing?
- Core idea: Asymmetric information creates adverse selection — the informed party's private knowledge skews who shows up to trade. The pool of sellers fills with bad types; the good types exit; eventually the market can't sustain itself.
- Visual object: The adverse-selection death spiral flowchart — a circular arrow diagram showing premiums rising → low-risk exits → pool worsens → premiums rise again.
- Manim move: decay (good cars fade out of the market one by one, leaving only lemons in view; then the spiral animates as a feedback loop)
- Example seed: Health insurance before the ACA. Insurers could price by risk. Remove that ability (guarantee issue) but don't require participation: healthy people drop coverage because it's overpriced for them. Pool gets sicker. Premiums spike. More healthy people leave. By 2016, many markets had one insurer left. The ACA mandate was an attempt to break the spiral by forcing the healthy back in.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: c2v (death spiral flowchart, used car lot), raster (Akerlof Nobel portrait, ACA enrollment chart)
- Prerequisites: basic supply and demand, concept of market equilibrium
- Exclusions: omit moral hazard (its own video), signaling theory details, full ACA policy history
- Score: 10/10

## Candidate 03 — The Deadweight Loss Triangle: Value That Vanishes Into Nothing
- Source: `mba-economics/chapters/09-monopoly.md`
- Topic: ECONOMICS
- Hook: When a monopoly raises its price, some of the money goes to the company as profit. But some value just disappears — it doesn't go to anyone. It evaporates. Economists have a name for it and can draw exactly where it went.
- Key case: Pharmaceutical monopoly. A patented drug priced at $400/pill costs $2 to make. The triangle between the monopoly quantity and the competitive quantity — all the patients who would have paid between $2 and $400 but can't afford $400 — represents treatments that never happened. Neither the company nor the patient captured that value. It ceased to exist.
- The Question: When a company charges more than competitive prices, what exactly is the "harm to society" — and where does the missing value actually go?
- Core idea: Monopoly creates two effects: a transfer (some consumer surplus moves to the firm as profit) and a deadweight loss (a triangle of value that existed at competitive prices but evaporates because those transactions never happen). Only the deadweight loss is a true efficiency cost — the transfer is just redistribution.
- Visual object: The two-shaded monopoly welfare diagram — a rectangle of monopoly profit plus a triangle of deadweight loss, each color-coded differently.
- Manim move: split (the total surplus area splits into three parts: remaining consumer surplus, the profit rectangle, and the dead-weight triangle; the triangle then fades, distinguishing redistribution from erasure)
- Example seed: The Boston Tea Party. The East India Company's tea monopoly created exactly this structure. Colonial merchants and consumers paid above-competitive prices (the rectangle). But the transactions that simply didn't happen at all — trade that would have benefited both parties — were the deadweight loss. The monopoly lit a revolution.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: c2v (monopoly welfare diagram), geo (Boston Harbor), raster (pharma pricing headlines)
- Prerequisites: supply and demand, consumer surplus concept
- Exclusions: omit natural monopoly regulation, antitrust law details, price discrimination
- Score: 10/10

## Candidate 04 — Comparative Advantage: Why You Should Trade Even When You're Better at Everything
- Source: `mba-economics/chapters/02-choice-in-a-world-of-scarcity.md`
- Topic: ECONOMICS
- Hook: Suppose you are better than your assistant at both typing and managing your schedule. Should you do both yourself? Economics says no — and the reason explains why nations trade even when one country is more efficient at producing literally everything.
- Key case: Two countries, Country A and Country B. Country A can produce 10 computers OR 100 bushels of wheat per worker; Country B can produce 5 computers OR 80 bushels. Country A is more productive at both. But Country A gives up 10 computers to grow 100 bushels; Country B gives up only 5 computers to grow 80 bushels. Country B has a comparative advantage in wheat. Both gain by specializing and trading.
- The Question: If one country is better at producing everything, why would it benefit from trading with a country that's worse at everything?
- Core idea: Comparative advantage is about opportunity cost, not absolute productivity. Even if one party is more efficient at everything, they should specialize in whatever has the lowest opportunity cost for them — and trade for the rest. The slope of each country's PPF tells you what they should produce.
- Visual object: Two PPF diagrams side by side, with different slopes at each axis, showing the slope difference as the source of gains from trade.
- Manim move: transform (a single PPF morphs into two country-specific PPFs with different slopes; then the trade arrows appear, showing the joint consumption possibility exceeding either country's individual frontier)
- Example seed: England and Portugal in Ricardo's original example. Portugal was actually more efficient at producing both wine and cloth in absolute terms. But Portugal's opportunity cost of wine was lower than England's. England specialized in cloth; Portugal in wine. Both consumed more of both goods than they could have produced alone.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: c2v (PPF diagrams, trade arrow diagrams), geo (England/Portugal map)
- Prerequisites: what a production possibilities frontier is, opportunity cost concept
- Exclusions: omit terms-of-trade arithmetic, Heckscher-Ohlin factor endowments, trade policy
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 05 — The Prisoner's Dilemma in Every Aisle: Why Oligopolies Can't Stop Competing
- Source: `mba-economics/chapters/10-monopolistic-competition-and-oligopoly.md`
- Topic: ECONOMICS
- Hook: Four executives from the world's largest laundry detergent companies met in Paris cafés for years, agreeing on prices. They were caught. Fined €361 million. And their cartel was unstable the whole time — because each member had an irresistible incentive to secretly cheat.
- Key case: Detergent cartel (Procter & Gamble, Henkel, Unilever, Colgate-Palmolive). Cooperative equilibrium: high prices, €50M each. Dominant strategy: cheat quietly, undercut rivals, grab €80M. If everyone cheats: €20M each. The same payoff structure as the prisoner's dilemma.
- The Question: When firms in an industry would all make more money by cooperating, why is cooperation so hard to maintain?
- Core idea: In oligopoly, the cooperative high-price outcome is visible and better for all firms collectively. But each firm's rational dominant strategy is to defect — charge lower, steal market share. The individually rational choice produces the collectively worst outcome. This is the prisoner's dilemma applied to markets.
- Visual object: 2×2 payoff matrix — rows/columns are Firm A and Firm B's price choices (High/Low); cells show the four profit outcomes; the Nash equilibrium cell highlighted.
- Manim move: accumulate (payoff matrix appears cell by cell; dominant strategy arrows drawn in; Nash equilibrium circled; then the "better world" cooperative cell highlighted with the gap between the two cells quantified)
- Example seed: OPEC. Oil-producing nations would all prefer the high-price cooperative outcome. But each individual member's dominant strategy is to produce more than its quota — capturing the high price while other members restrict output. OPEC has broken down repeatedly for exactly this reason. It only works through repeated interaction and the threat of future punishment.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: raster (Paris café image, OPEC meeting photo, detergent aisle), c2v (payoff matrix)
- Prerequisites: basic idea of monopoly profit, what a market is
- Exclusions: omit Cournot/Bertrand/Stackelberg model details, antitrust law
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 06 — The Pigouvian Tax: How to Make the Market Price the Damage It Causes
- Source: `mba-economics/chapters/12-environmental-protection-and-negative-externalities.md`
- Topic: ECONOMICS
- Hook: In 1952, a five-day London fog killed up to 12,000 people. Every coal fire that contributed to it was a perfectly normal market transaction — privately priced, with a buyer and seller. But the price of the coal never included the cost of the smoke filling those neighbors' lungs.
- Key case: The U.S. acid-rain cap-and-trade program, launched in 1990. The EPA set a cap on total SO₂ emissions, issued tradable permits, and let firms sort themselves. A 65% reduction in sulfur dioxide was achieved at 25–50% lower cost than command-and-control regulation would have required. The market found the cheapest abatement without the regulator needing to know any firm's cost structure.
- The Question: When market prices are missing a real cost — like pollution — how do you fix the market without replacing it?
- Core idea: Negative externalities cause overproduction because the private cost is below the social cost. A Pigouvian tax set at exactly the marginal damage restores efficiency: each firm faces the full social cost and reduces until its marginal abatement cost equals the tax. All firms equalize at the same rate — the cheapest abatement happens automatically, without central planning.
- Visual object: The negative externality diagram — private supply, social supply (shifted up by the damage cost), equilibrium quantity vs. efficient quantity, deadweight loss triangle of overproduction.
- Manim move: morph (private supply curve lifts to become social supply curve as the external damage "d" is added; new equilibrium appears at lower quantity; deadweight loss triangle fills in red, then the Pigouvian tax arrow closes that gap)
- Example seed: Sweden's carbon tax, introduced in 1991 at $30/ton, now over $130/ton. Sweden has cut per-capita emissions roughly 27% since 1990 while growing its economy substantially. The firms that cheapest-to-abate reduced most; expensive-to-abate firms paid the tax. No regulator had to know which was which — the price signal sorted it.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: geo (London 1952 photo, Swedish forest/clean industry), c2v (externality diagrams), raster (SO₂ emissions chart)
- Prerequisites: supply and demand equilibrium, basic externality concept
- Exclusions: omit Coase theorem, international coordination problem, political economy of carbon taxes
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 07 — The Sunk Cost Trap: Why the Past Should Never Vote on the Future
- Source: `mba-economics/chapters/02-choice-in-a-world-of-scarcity.md`
- Topic: ECONOMICS
- Hook: You're watching a terrible movie. You paid $18 for the ticket. The rational move is to walk out and spend the next two hours on anything more valuable. Almost nobody does this. And staying doesn't get your $18 back.
- Key case: The Concorde supersonic jet. By 1974, both Britain and France knew the plane would never be commercially profitable. They kept funding it for another three decades. The program eventually cost over £1 billion. The term "Concorde fallacy" is now synonymous with sunk-cost thinking at the institutional level.
- The Question: Why do past expenditures — money you can never get back — so powerfully influence future decisions, even when you know they shouldn't?
- Core idea: Sunk costs are irreversible. A rational decision only involves future costs and benefits. But loss aversion makes acknowledging a sunk cost feel like taking a loss — so people keep investing to avoid admitting the past spending was wasted. They're not avoiding the loss; they're performing a ritual that delays accepting it.
- Visual object: Three-scale infographic: movie ticket ($18 sunk, 90 minutes at stake), corporate product ($300M sunk, next decision $50M), Concorde (£1B sunk, ongoing losses). Each: grayed sunk-cost bar, highlighted future-decision bar.
- Manim move: collapse (a large "total invested" bar collapses; only the future-decisions portion remains highlighted; the past portion grays out with a "sunk — irrelevant" label)
- Example seed: Companies that throw good money after bad acquisitions. Quaker Oats bought Snapple for $1.7 billion in 1994. Within three years it was clearly a disaster. They held on, tried to fix it, and finally sold Snapple for $300 million — but not until they'd spent additional millions trying to salvage the sunk acquisition cost.
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: raster (Concorde photo, movie theater), c2v (sunk cost infographic)
- Prerequisites: opportunity cost concept
- Exclusions: omit PPF, budget constraint algebra, formal behavioral economics
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 08 — Price Floors and Ceilings: Why "Controlling" a Price Just Moves the Problem
- Source: `mba-economics/chapters/03-demand-and-supply.md`
- Topic: ECONOMICS
- Hook: A city caps rent at $1,500/month to make housing affordable. Within a decade, there are fewer apartments, longer waitlists, and landlords who screen applicants more aggressively than ever. The price was controlled. The scarcity wasn't.
- Key case: Rent control in New York City. The nominal goal — keep housing affordable — was achieved for whoever currently holds a controlled apartment. The unintended results: units converted to condos, deferred maintenance, landlords screening for stable-income tenants, black-market key payments, long wait times. The shortage didn't disappear; it was reallocated to queues and favoritism.
- The Question: When a government fixes a price below or above market equilibrium, where does the unmet demand or excess supply actually go?
- Core idea: Price controls don't eliminate the market pressure — they transform it. A binding price ceiling creates a shortage; the shortage gets allocated by non-price mechanisms (queues, connections, screening). A price floor creates a surplus; the surplus represents willing transactions that can't happen. The deadweight loss from the foregone trades is real and measurable.
- Visual object: The binding price ceiling diagram — demand, supply, equilibrium P*, ceiling set below it, horizontal bracket between Qs and Qd labeled "Shortage."
- Manim move: split (the gap between Qs and Qd animates to show the shortage; then the "non-price rationing" mechanisms appear as icons filling that gap — queue, connections, screening — each eating part of the deadweight loss triangle)
- Example seed: The 1973 U.S. oil price ceiling. The Nixon administration capped gasoline prices below market. The result wasn't affordable gasoline — it was gas lines stretching around blocks. The price was held down; the allocation shifted to time spent waiting. Americans spent billions of hours in queues. The cost of gasoline fell in dollars; the full cost (price + wait time) stayed high.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: geo (1973 gas line photo, NYC apartment building), c2v (price ceiling/floor diagrams)
- Prerequisites: supply and demand equilibrium, what a market price is
- Exclusions: omit price floor (minimum wage) in depth — different video; omit deadweight loss algebra
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 09 — The Free-Rider Problem: Why GPS Had to Be Built by the Government
- Source: `mba-economics/chapters/13-positive-externalities-and-public-goods.md`
- Topic: ECONOMICS
- Hook: The GPS satellite constellation cost tens of billions of dollars over decades. You use it every day, for free. No private company would have built it — because no private company could have made you pay for it. This isn't a failure of capitalism. It's a structural feature of a specific class of goods.
- Key case: GPS — non-rival (one more phone doesn't reduce the signal for others), non-excludable (once broadcast, anyone with a receiver can use it). Private provision is impossible because the free-rider logic dominates: why pay for what you can get without paying? The Defense Department built it anyway; the entire modern app economy is a byproduct.
- The Question: Some valuable things can only be built by governments — not because governments are better at building things, but because markets structurally can't provide them. What makes a good fall into this category?
- Core idea: Public goods are non-rival (one person's consumption doesn't diminish others') and non-excludable (can't prevent non-payers from benefiting). The free-rider problem means every rational individual waits for someone else to pay — so no one does, and the good isn't produced. Coercive collective provision (taxation) solves the problem the market cannot.
- Visual object: The four-quadrant goods typology table (Rival/Non-rival × Excludable/Non-excludable) with real examples in each cell; GPS, national defense, radio signals in the public-goods quadrant.
- Manim move: spread (the four-quadrant grid populates with goods, each appearing in its cell; then the public-goods cell zooms in, showing the GPS constellation orbiting above Earth; then the free-rider decision tree appears)
- Example seed: Basic scientific research. The returns to R&D are 30–50% socially but only 7–15% privately — the gap is value the inventor can't capture. NIH spends $50B/year funding research no private company would fund. The PCR technique (invented with public funding) enabled forensic DNA, genetic diagnosis, and COVID vaccine development. The private return was small; the social return was incalculable.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: geo (satellite orbit graphic, NIH campus), raster (GPS phone screenshot, DNA gel), c2v (four-quadrant table)
- Prerequisites: basic supply and demand, externality concept
- Exclusions: omit common-pool resources, Ostrom's work, detailed patent trade-off
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 10 — The Poverty Trap: How Helping Can Lock People In
- Source: `mba-economics/chapters/15-poverty-and-economic-inequality.md`
- Topic: ECONOMICS
- Hook: A government gives $20,000 in annual benefits to households below the poverty line, phased out at $1 for every $1 earned. A person who gets a full-time job at $20,000 a year ends up with exactly the same income as someone who doesn't work at all. The program meant to help has made work pointless.
- Key case: The EITC vs. traditional benefit phase-outs. The EITC pays more as you earn more (in the phase-in range), explicitly rewarding work. Its phase-out is gradual. The result: single-mother labor-force participation rose measurably after EITC expansions. The structural difference — a slope vs. a cliff — is the entire work incentive.
- The Question: When a safety-net program phases out as recipients earn more, it effectively taxes their income. At what rate? And what happens when you stack several programs together?
- Core idea: Safety-net programs that reduce benefits dollar-for-dollar with earnings impose an implicit marginal tax rate of 100%. When multiple programs stack, combined effective marginal rates can exceed 70–80% in some income ranges. The poverty trap isn't a character flaw — it's a program-design flaw. The EITC avoids it by making the benefit slope upward before phasing out.
- Visual object: Two line charts on the same axes — "100% phase-out" (flat line, no work reward) vs. "EITC design" (upward slope in phase-in zone, then gradual decline). The gap between the two curves is the work incentive.
- Manim move: trace (a household earns more along the x-axis; the "total income" line traces out both paths simultaneously; the gap between the flat trap line and the EITC slope fills in as the incentive difference)
- Example seed: A single mother working in retail. With traditional SNAP + housing + Medicaid stacking: earning $15,000 leaves her barely better off than earning $0 after factoring phase-outs. Under EITC: earning $15,000 is substantially better than $0 — the credit adds $3,600 on top of her wages. The structural difference explains decades of empirical research on why the EITC moves people into work when cash transfers don't.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: c2v (EITC vs. trap diagram, benefit stack chart), raster (EITC form, grocery store)
- Prerequisites: marginal tax concept, basic supply and demand in labor markets
- Exclusions: omit Gini coefficient, Lorenz curve, full inequality discussion — separate topic
- Score: 8/10
