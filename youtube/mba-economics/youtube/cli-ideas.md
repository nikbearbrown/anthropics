## Candidate 01 — Build an Elasticity Revenue Predictor with Claude Code
- Source: mba-economics/chapters/05-elasticity.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: Netflix raised prices 60% and came out ahead. Every economics textbook says higher prices reduce quantity — but whether that helps or hurts revenue depends entirely on one number: price elasticity of demand. This card computes it live and predicts the revenue outcome before you open the news.
- The artifact: A Python elasticity revenue predictor: inputs are current price, new price, current quantity, and estimated price elasticity (or the user provides two price-quantity observations for Claude to calculate elasticity empirically); outputs: (1) estimated new quantity demanded, (2) new total revenue, (3) revenue change in dollars and percentage, (4) verdict (elastic = price hike hurts revenue, inelastic = price hike helps revenue); plotted as a Manim animated demand curve with the old and new price-quantity points marked.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Write a Python elasticity revenue predictor. Inputs: P1 (current price), Q1 (current quantity), P2 (new price), elasticity (float, absolute value). Compute: pct_change_P = (P2-P1)/P1; pct_change_Q = -elasticity × pct_change_P; Q2 = Q1 × (1 + pct_change_Q); revenue_old = P1 × Q1; revenue_new = P2 × Q2; revenue_change = revenue_new - revenue_old. Verdict: if elasticity > 1, revenue falls on price increase; if < 1, revenue rises. Also: compute the elasticity from two observations using midpoint formula: e = ((Q2-Q1)/((Q2+Q1)/2)) / ((P2-P1)/((P2+P1)/2)). Test: Netflix case — P1=$9.99, Q1=25M, P2=$15.98, elasticity=0.03 (reflecting low churn observed)."`
- Read / check: Verify Netflix case: pct_change_P = 60%; pct_change_Q = -0.03 × 60% = -1.8%; Q2 = 25M × 0.982 = 24.55M; revenue_old = $249.75M; revenue_new = $15.98 × 24.55M = $392.3M; revenue increases by $142.5M — confirming the inelastic result. Verify the midpoint elasticity formula is correctly implemented (it produces a symmetric result regardless of which price is called P1 or P2).
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Real price and quantity data (the Netflix case uses public subscriber counts from earnings reports; the elasticity of 0.03 is an illustrative estimate consistent with the chapter's observation that most subscribers stayed). For a real company, viewers would need actual subscription data.
- Output medium: Manim (linear demand curve animating; old price-quantity point in blue; new price-quantity point in orange after the price hike; a revenue rectangle drawn under each point; the change in rectangle area labeled as revenue gain/loss)
- The change: Sweep the elasticity from 0 to 2 and show how the revenue-change sign flips at elasticity=1 (the unitary elastic threshold). Animate the elasticity value on the demand curve changing shape as it sweeps, with the revenue change updating in real time.
- Teardown angle: Elasticity is not a property of the product — it is a property of the product-in-context: this market, at this time, with these available substitutes, for these buyers. Netflix had inelastic demand because the switching cost of learning a new streaming interface was real, the content was unique, and the absolute price was still small relative to household entertainment budgets. The number changes; the framework for finding it stays the same.
- Exclusions: Cross-price elasticity of demand (interesting but a separate card); income elasticity and Giffen goods; supply elasticity and the tax incidence calculation; long-run vs. short-run elasticity distinction.
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 02 — Build a Monopoly Deadweight Loss Calculator with Claude Code
- Source: mba-economics/chapters/09-monopoly.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: The monopolist produces less and charges more. Every economics student knows this. But how much less? And what does the gap cost society in dollars? Deadweight loss is the wealth that doesn't transfer — it just disappears. This card computes it for any monopoly with a linear demand curve.
- The artifact: A Python monopoly deadweight loss calculator: inputs are demand curve parameters (intercept a, slope b: Q = a - b×P) and marginal cost (MC = constant); computes competitive equilibrium (P=MC), monopoly equilibrium (MR=MC), consumer surplus at each equilibrium, producer surplus, and deadweight loss triangle; plotted as a Manim animated demand-MR-MC diagram with consumer surplus, producer surplus, and DWL regions shaded and labeled.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Write a Python monopoly deadweight loss calculator. Demand: Q = a - b×P, so P = (a-Q)/b (inverse demand). MR = (a-2Q)/b. Inputs: a=100, b=1 (demand: Q=100-P), MC=20. Compute: competitive Q* where P=MC → Q_c=80, P_c=20; monopoly Q_m where MR=MC → Q_m=40, P_m=60. Consumer surplus at competitive = 0.5×(100-20)×80=$3200. Consumer surplus at monopoly = 0.5×(100-60)×40=$800. Producer surplus at monopoly = (60-20)×40=$1600. DWL = 0.5×(60-20)×(80-40)=$800. Output all values in a table and plot the diagram with labeled regions."`
- Read / check: Verify competitive equilibrium: Q=100-P=100-20=80 units; verify monopoly MR=MC: MR=100-2Q=20 → Q_m=40, P_m=100-40=60; verify consumer surplus at monopoly = 0.5×(100-60)×40 = 0.5×40×40 = $800; verify DWL = 0.5×(60-20)×(80-40) = 0.5×40×40 = $800 (the DWL equals the reduction in consumer surplus not captured as producer surplus: CS_competitive=$3200, CS_monopoly=$800, PS_monopoly=$1600; DWL = $3200 - $800 - $1600 = $800, verified); confirm the diagram has demand curve, MR curve (twice as steep), MC line, and three shaded regions.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Nothing — fully synthetic. All demand curve parameters are illustrative teaching values. Real monopoly DWL calculations require empirical demand estimation.
- Output medium: Manim (demand and MR curves drawing; MC horizontal line appearing; competitive equilibrium point appearing in blue; monopoly equilibrium point appearing in red; DWL triangle filling in yellow; consumer surplus in green; producer surplus in blue; labels appearing one by one)
- The change: Add price discrimination — show what happens to DWL if the monopolist can perfectly price-discriminate (first-degree price discrimination). In this case, DWL = 0 (the monopolist captures all consumer surplus), and output is the same as the competitive level. Ask Claude to compute the redistribution: who wins, who loses, and why is total surplus maximized but distribution dramatically worse.
- Teardown angle: Deadweight loss is the economic term for "wealth that nobody got." In the competitive case, that value goes to consumers. In the monopoly case, part of it goes to the monopolist as producer surplus and part just disappears — neither producer nor consumer captures it. Antitrust enforcement exists precisely to recover that vanishing triangle.
- Exclusions: Natural monopoly regulation (rate-of-return vs. price-cap regulation); network effects as a source of monopoly (covered in antitrust); Ramsey pricing for regulated monopolies; dynamic monopoly and innovation incentives.
- Score: 9/10

- Prompt seed: `claude "Write a Python supply-and-demand simulator. Demand: Q_d = D_intercept - D_slope × P. Supply: Q_s = S_intercept + S_slope × P. Equilibrium: solve Q_d = Q_s for P*. Plot demand and supply curves from P=0 to P=max_price. Mark equilibrium point. Then: optional price_ceiling (if ceiling < P*, show shortage = Q_d(ceiling) - Q_s(ceiling) as a horizontal gap on the graph). Also: simulate a demand shift (D_intercept increases by delta) and animate the old equilibrium and new equilibrium points. Test: D_intercept=100, D_slope=1, S_intercept=20, S_slope=2. Equilibrium: 100-P=20+2P → 3P=80 → P=26.67, Q=73.33. Price ceiling at P=20."`
- Read / check: Verify equilibrium P* = 80/3 ≈ 26.67 and Q* ≈ 73.33; verify at ceiling P=20: Q_d = 100-20 = 80, Q_s = 20+40 = 60, shortage = 20 units; verify the demand-shift result: if D_intercept rises to 120, new equilibrium: 120-P=20+2P → 100=3P → P=33.33, Q=86.67; confirm the diagram shows curves as straight lines with equilibrium clearly marked.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Nothing — fully synthetic. For the NEXT STEPS beat, apply to real examples: NYC rent stabilization as a price ceiling, agricultural price floors.
- Output medium: Manim (supply and demand curves drawing; equilibrium point appearing; price ceiling line appearing as a dashed horizontal at P=20; shortage gap appearing as a horizontal red arrow between Q_s and Q_d at the ceiling price; then a demand shift animation moving the demand curve right and the equilibrium point moving up and right)
- The change: Replace the price ceiling with a tax (shift supply curve up by tax amount) and show: (1) the new equilibrium price buyers pay, (2) the price sellers receive, (3) the government tax revenue rectangle, (4) the deadweight loss triangle. Demonstrate tax incidence — who actually pays the tax depends on the relative slopes of supply and demand, not on who the tax is legally assessed on.
- Teardown angle: The price mechanism is the least romantic feature of market economics and the most consequential. When it works, it coordinates millions of decisions simultaneously without any central planner. When it is overridden — by a ceiling, a floor, or a tax — the coordination stops, and something else (queues, rationing, black markets) has to fill the gap. Understanding supply and demand is understanding the cost of overriding it.
- Exclusions: Non-linear demand curves; multiple equilibria and cobweb dynamics; factor markets (labor and capital supply/demand); general equilibrium and Walrasian adjustment.
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 04 — Build a CPI Basket Inflation Calculator with Claude Code
- Source: mba-economics/chapters/22-inflation.md

- Prompt seed: `claude "Write a Python personal CPI calculator. Inputs: a dictionary of spending categories and monthly amounts (user basket), and a dictionary of price-change percentages by category since base period. Compute: weighted_inflation = sum(spending_amount_i / total_spending × price_change_i). Compare to official CPI (user inputs official CPI). Output: personal CPI, official CPI, divergence, and a per-category breakdown of which categories are contributing most to the divergence. Test with: basket = {housing: 2000, food: 600, transport: 400, healthcare: 300, entertainment: 200, other: 500}, price_changes = {housing: 0.12, food: 0.08, transport: 0.04, healthcare: 0.06, entertainment: 0.02, other: 0.05}, official_CPI = 0.065."`
- Read / check: Verify total spending = $4,000; weighted_inflation = (2000/4000 × 0.12) + (600/4000 × 0.08) + ... = 0.50×0.12 + 0.15×0.08 + 0.10×0.04 + 0.075×0.06 + 0.05×0.02 + 0.125×0.05 = 0.060 + 0.012 + 0.004 + 0.0045 + 0.001 + 0.00625 = 0.0878 = 8.78%; divergence from official 6.5% = +2.28 percentage points (driven by high housing weight); verify housing is flagged as the largest contributor.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Real household spending data (the synthetic basket is illustrative; real data requires the Bureau of Labor Statistics Consumer Expenditure Survey or the user's own bank/credit card records). For the NEXT STEPS beat, direct viewers to the BLS CPI category weights (available for download at bls.gov).

- The change: Add the three CPI biases (substitution bias, quality-change bias, new-goods bias) and estimate how much the official CPI overstates true inflation in the user's basket. Ask Claude to compute the "true inflation" lower bound using a chained-CPI adjustment (subtract 0.5 percentage points as the substitution-bias correction).
- Teardown angle: The CPI is the price of a fixed basket re-expressed as an index. When prices for different categories move at different rates, the CPI tells you the weighted average — but your weighted average depends on your basket, not the BLS average basket. The divergence is not an error; it is the reality that inflation is not one number but a distribution of numbers, and the CPI reports the median while many real households experience the tail.
- Exclusions: PCE vs. CPI methodological differences; hedonic regression for quality adjustment; core CPI vs. headline CPI; hyperinflation mechanics (Zimbabwe case).

- Prompt seed: `claude "Write a Python AD-AS model simulator. Parameterize: AD curve: Y_d = 100 - 2P (inverse: P = 50 - Y/2). SRAS curve: Y_s = 20 + 3P (upward-sloping short run). LRAS: Y* = 80 (vertical at full employment). Equilibrium: solve 100-2P = 20+3P → 5P=80 → P=16, Y=68. Note recessionary gap: Y=68 < Y*=80, gap=12. Apply demand shock: AD shifts left by 20 (new AD: Y_d = 80-2P). New equilibrium: 80-2P=20+3P → 5P=60 → P=12, Y=56. New recessionary gap=24. Plot both AD curves, SRAS, and LRAS vertical line. Mark both equilibria. Animate the shift."`
- Read / check: Verify initial equilibrium: 100-2P=20+3P → P=16, Y=100-32=68; verify recessionary gap = 80-68=12; verify post-shock equilibrium: 80-2P=20+3P → P=12, Y=80-24=56; verify new gap=24; confirm the LRAS line is vertical at Y=80 and the diagram correctly shows all three curves.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Nothing — fully synthetic. The AD-AS model is entirely theoretical; parameters are teaching values. For the NEXT STEPS beat, direct viewers to FRED economic data to calibrate approximate AD-AS relationships from real GDP and CPI data.
- Output medium: Manim (AD and SRAS curves drawing; LRAS vertical line appearing; initial equilibrium point labeled; then the AD curve shifting left as the "shock"; new equilibrium point appearing lower and to the left; the recessionary gap labeled as a horizontal arrow between new Y and Y*)
- The change: Apply a stagflation scenario — supply shock (SRAS shifts left, costs rise). Show how the equilibrium moves to higher prices AND lower output simultaneously — the worst possible macroeconomic outcome, where the standard policy tools (stimulus = more inflation, tightening = more unemployment) both make something worse.
- Teardown angle: The AD-AS model holds two competing macroeconomic visions simultaneously: Say's Law (in the long run, supply determines output) and Keynes's Law (in the short run, demand can collapse below potential). The neoclassical zone (steep SRAS near full employment) is where stimulus mainly produces inflation.

## Candidate 06 — Build a Tax Incidence Calculator with Claude Code
- Source: mba-economics/chapters/05-elasticity.md (tax incidence section) + chapters/18-public-economy.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: Politicians say a gas tax is paid by oil companies. Economists say who actually pays the tax is determined entirely by the relative elasticities of supply and demand — not by where the law says the obligation falls. This card proves it.
- The artifact: A Python tax incidence calculator: inputs are supply elasticity, demand elasticity, tax amount, and initial price; computes: (1) fraction of tax borne by buyers (demand-side incidence = supply_elasticity / (supply_elasticity + |demand_elasticity|)), (2) fraction borne by sellers, (3) new buyer price, new seller price, quantity reduction, and government tax revenue; plotted as a Manim supply-demand diagram with the tax wedge shown between the buyer price and seller price.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Write a Python tax incidence calculator. Inputs: epsilon_d (demand elasticity, negative float), epsilon_s (supply elasticity, positive float), tax (amount per unit), P0 (initial equilibrium price), Q0 (initial equilibrium quantity). Tax incidence on buyers: share_buyer = epsilon_s / (epsilon_s + abs(epsilon_d)). Share_seller = abs(epsilon_d) / (epsilon_s + abs(epsilon_d)). New buyer price: P_buyer = P0 + share_buyer × tax. New seller price: P_seller = P0 - share_seller × tax. Quantity change: dQ = Q0 × (-abs(epsilon_d) × share_buyer × tax / P0). Tax revenue = tax × (Q0 + dQ). DWL = 0.5 × tax × abs(dQ). Test 1: elastic demand (epsilon_d=-2, epsilon_s=1), tax=$1, P0=$10, Q0=1000. Test 2: inelastic demand (epsilon_d=-0.3, epsilon_s=1), same tax."`
- Read / check: Test 1 (elastic demand): share_buyer = 1/(1+2) = 33%; share_seller = 67%; P_buyer = $10.33; P_seller = $9.67. Test 2 (inelastic demand): share_buyer = 1/(1+0.3) = 77%; share_seller = 23%; P_buyer = $10.77; P_seller = $9.77. Verify that in test 2, buyers bear most of the tax despite the tax being legally assessed on sellers — this is the key tax incidence result. Verify DWL is larger in test 1 (elastic demand → more quantity reduction).
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Nothing — fully synthetic. Tax incidence is analytically derived from elasticity values. For the NEXT STEPS beat, cite empirical elasticity estimates for specific goods (gasoline demand elasticity ≈ -0.3 to -0.5 short run; cigarette demand elasticity ≈ -0.4).
- Output medium: Manim (supply-demand diagram with a vertical tax wedge appearing between the two equilibrium prices; buyer price labeled above old equilibrium, seller price labeled below; government revenue rectangle filled in gold; DWL triangle filled in red; the two test cases animated side by side)
- The change: Apply the tax incidence framework to the payroll tax (Social Security and Medicare) — legally split 50/50 between employer and employee, but economically, the incidence depends on labor supply and demand elasticities. Ask Claude to cite the empirical evidence on who actually bears the payroll tax and show the incidence calculation.
- Teardown angle: Tax incidence is one of the most politically misunderstood results in economics. Who writes the check to the government and who bears the economic burden are completely different questions, and the law cannot change the answer to the second question — only relative elasticities can. The more inelastic the buyer (nowhere else to go), the more of the tax they bear, regardless of what the statute says.
- Exclusions: Optimal tax theory (Ramsey taxation); progressive vs. regressive incidence at the income distribution level; tax avoidance vs. tax incidence; international tax competition.
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 07 — Build an Oligopoly Game Theory Solver with Claude Code
- Source: mba-economics/chapters/10-monopolistic-competition-and-oligopoly.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: Two airlines both know that if they both discount, they both lose money. If one discounts and the other doesn't, the discounter wins big. If neither discounts, they both earn comfortable profits. This is a prisoner's dilemma — and the Nash equilibrium is not the best outcome for either player.
- The artifact: A Python two-player game theory solver: inputs are a 2×2 payoff matrix (Player 1 rows, Player 2 columns, each cell a tuple of payoffs); outputs: (1) dominant strategies (if any) for each player, (2) Nash equilibrium (the strategy combination where neither player wants to deviate), (3) Pareto-optimal outcome (the combination that maximizes total welfare), (4) whether the game is a prisoner's dilemma (Nash equilibrium is worse than Pareto optimum for both); plotted as a Manim animated payoff matrix with Nash equilibrium and Pareto outcome highlighted.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Write a Python 2×2 game theory solver. Input: payoff matrix as a 2×2 list of tuples [(row1_col1_payoffs), (row1_col2_payoffs), (row2_col1_payoffs), (row2_col2_payoffs)] where each tuple is (player1_payoff, player2_payoff). Find: dominant strategy for P1 (compare rows for P1's payoffs holding P2's column fixed); dominant strategy for P2; Nash equilibrium (cell where both are best-responding); Pareto optimum (cell maximizing P1+P2). Classify: if Nash ≠ Pareto and both players are worse off at Nash, flag as Prisoner's Dilemma. Test: airline price game: (Compete, Compete)=(2,2), (Compete, Discount)=(0,5), (Discount, Compete)=(5,0), (Discount, Discount)=(1,1)."`
- Read / check: Verify airline game: P1 dominant strategy: if P2 competes, P1 prefers Discount (5>2); if P2 discounts, P1 prefers Discount (1>0) → Discount is dominant for P1. Same for P2. Nash equilibrium = (Discount, Discount) = (1,1). Pareto optimum = (Compete, Compete) = (2,2) (both players are better off). This is a prisoner's dilemma (Nash is (1,1), Pareto is (2,2), Nash is worse for both). Confirm the game correctly identifies and flags this.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Nothing — fully synthetic. For the NEXT STEPS beat, apply the same solver to real-world oligopoly situations (OPEC production decisions, tech platform interoperability decisions).
- Output medium: Manim (2×2 payoff matrix animating; Nash equilibrium cell highlighted with a red border; Pareto optimal cell highlighted with a green border; arrows showing each player's best-response direction; Prisoner's Dilemma flag appearing as a text annotation)
- The change: Add a repeated-game extension — if the two airlines play this game 10 times and know it, show how cooperation can be sustained through "tit-for-tat" strategy (cooperate in period 1, copy the other player's last move in each subsequent period). Ask Claude to simulate 10 rounds of tit-for-tat vs. 10 rounds of always-defect and show the payoff comparison.
- Teardown angle: The prisoner's dilemma is not a tragedy of individual irrationality — it is a structural result that emerges from rational individual optimization in certain payoff environments. Both players know cooperation is Pareto-superior. Both choose the Nash equilibrium anyway, because defection is the individually rational choice regardless of what the other player does. The structural fix (binding agreements, repeated interactions, enforcement mechanisms) is what antitrust law and collusion law are designed to prevent in one context and enable in another.
- Exclusions: Mixed-strategy Nash equilibria (requires calculus of mixed strategies); evolutionary game theory; multi-player games; Stackelberg leadership (first-mover advantage).
- Score: 8/10
