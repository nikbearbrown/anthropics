# Chapter 22 — Inflation

*The slow erosion of money's value — what it actually is, why it matters more than you'd think, and what happens when it gets out of control.*

In July 2008, in Zimbabwe, a loaf of bread cost about 1.6 trillion Zimbabwean dollars.

The country was running at an estimated 89.7 sextillion percent annual inflation. Written out: 89,700,000,000,000,000,000,000 percent. Prices doubled roughly every 24 hours. The Reserve Bank issued a 100-trillion-dollar note. People carried groceries home in cash-filled wheelbarrows — not because they bought a lot of groceries, but because the cash took more room than the food. Eventually Zimbabwe abandoned its own currency and started using the U.S. dollar instead.

That is one end of the inflation spectrum. The other end is what the U.S. experienced for most of the 2000s and 2010s: annual inflation hovering around 1.5 to 2 percent. Prices rising slowly enough that most people didn't notice them rising at all.

Most of the time, in most wealthy countries, inflation is somewhere in that lower range. The U.S. Consumer Price Index has averaged about 3 percent per year over the past four decades — with a notable spike to 9 percent in 2022. Not Zimbabwe. Not even close. But not irrelevant either.

Even modest inflation matters. It changes who wins and who loses across the economy. It affects how people invest, save, contract, and plan. It is the central obsession of monetary policy — the subject of Chapter 28. And the transition from the very low inflation of the 2010s to the higher inflation of 2021–2024 reminded everyone that "low inflation" is a managed outcome, not a natural state.

This chapter is about what inflation actually is, how we measure it, what it does to different groups of people, and what happens when it escapes control entirely.

## Measuring Inflation: The Basket and the Index

Inflation is the rate at which the general price level rises. To measure it, you need a single number representing the average price level — averaged over a representative sample of what people actually buy.

The standard approach uses a **basket** of goods and services typical of household spending. The Bureau of Labor Statistics constructs the **Consumer Price Index (CPI)** by defining that basket, pricing it each month, and expressing each month's price as an index relative to a base period.

Here's the calculation in a simplified case. Suppose the basket is 12 burgers, 5 movie tickets, and one month's rent.

| Item | Quantity | Price (2020) | Cost (2020) | Price (2024) | Cost (2024) |
|------|----------|--------------|-------------|--------------|-------------|
| Burgers | 12 | $5.00 | $60 | $7.00 | $84 |
| Movie tickets | 5 | $10.00 | $50 | $13.00 | $65 |
| Rent | 1 month | $1,200 | $1,200 | $1,500 | $1,500 |
| **Total** | | | **$1,310** | | **$1,649** |

The CPI in 2024, with 2020 as the base period, is $(1{,}649 / 1{,}310) \times 100 = 125.9$. Inflation from 2020 to 2024 was 25.9 percent over four years — roughly 5.9 percent annually if it compounded evenly. (Actual U.S. inflation 2020–2024 averaged about 4 percent annually — the example is in the right neighborhood.)

The actual U.S. CPI basket covers roughly 80,000 prices across about 200 categories, collected monthly. Housing costs alone make up about 33 percent of the total weight, which is why housing-price surges from 2021 through 2023 drove the headline number so high.

## Biases in the CPI

The CPI is the most important inflation measure, but it has three documented biases, each operating in the same direction — all slightly overstating true inflation.

**Substitution bias.** When the price of beef rises, consumers buy more chicken. The CPI, using a fixed basket, assumes they keep buying the same amount of beef — and therefore overstates the cost of living for a consumer who actually substituted. A "chained" CPI updates the basket regularly to reflect actual behavior; the standard CPI doesn't.

**Quality-change bias.** A 2024 smartphone is enormously more capable than a 2004 phone, but both appear in the "telephone" category. If the index doesn't adjust for the quality improvement — treating a $800 better phone the same as $800 the same phone — the price level appears to rise faster than the true cost of achieving a given standard of living. The BLS uses **hedonic regression** to adjust for quality in some categories (cars, computers), but not all.

**New-goods bias.** When genuinely new products enter the market — streaming video, smartphones, AI assistants — the welfare gain from their introduction doesn't appear in the index until they're included in the basket. By the time they're added, early adopters have already captured years of consumer surplus.

The combined effect: the standard U.S. CPI probably overstates true inflation by roughly half to a full percentage point per year. The Federal Reserve's preferred measure, the **PCE Price Index** (Personal Consumption Expenditure), uses chain-weighting and tends to read about 0.3 percentage points lower than the CPI. The BLS also publishes the **chained CPI**, which is closer to the PCE and which Congressional Budget Office analyses increasingly favor.

This matters beyond academic precision. Social Security cost-of-living adjustments are tied to CPI. Federal income-tax brackets are CPI-indexed. If CPI overstates inflation by 0.5 to 1 percentage point per year, COLA payments are larger than the true cost-of-living change requires — and compounded over decades, the fiscal implications are substantial.

## The Historical Pattern

U.S. inflation since 1950 has moved through distinct episodes, each with its own cause.

**1950s–1960s.** Low and stable, generally 1 to 3 percent. The postwar economy was expanding; monetary policy was accommodating but inflation remained contained.

**1970s.** The decisive break. Two oil price shocks — the 1973 OPEC embargo and the 1979 Iranian Revolution — hit an economy that was already running warm from Great Society spending and Vietnam War financing. The Federal Reserve, under political pressure to avoid recession, accommodated rather than tightened. Inflation peaked at about 14 percent in 1980.

**1980s.** Federal Reserve Chairman Paul Volcker raised the federal funds rate to 20 percent. The medicine was brutal — a deep recession in 1981–1982 — but it worked. Inflation fell from 14 percent to 3 percent by mid-decade. The lesson was noted by every central banker who came after.

**1990s–2010s.** A long period of low and stable inflation — under 3 percent for most of two decades. Sometimes called the "Great Moderation." The 2009 Great Recession briefly pushed the U.S. toward deflation.

**2021–2024.** COVID supply disruptions, combined with substantial pandemic fiscal stimulus, produced the sharpest inflation in four decades. The CPI peaked at about 9.1 percent in June 2022. The Federal Reserve raised rates aggressively beginning in 2022; inflation returned to the 3–4 percent range by late 2023 and continued falling.

## What Inflation Does: Winners and Losers

Inflation doesn't affect everyone equally. Some benefit; some lose. The pattern depends on the structure of each party's obligations — fixed versus flexible, nominal versus real.

**Debtors benefit from unexpected inflation.** If you borrow $200,000 at 4 percent, expecting 2 percent inflation, your expected real interest rate is 2 percent. If actual inflation turns out to be 6 percent, your real rate is −2 percent — you're paying back dollars worth less than the ones you borrowed. The bank lent you 2020 purchasing power and you're repaying it in cheaper 2026 dollars.

**Creditors lose.** The bank expected to be paid back in dollars with the purchasing power it originally lent. Unexpected inflation transfers wealth from creditor to debtor.

**Fixed-income recipients lose.** A retiree receiving a pension of $3,000 per month finds that $3,000 buys progressively less as prices rise. Unless the pension is indexed, each year of inflation represents a permanent real pay cut.

**Workers with bargaining power gain.** Those who can renegotiate wages upward, or whose contracts include explicit cost-of-living adjustment clauses, can keep pace or get ahead. Those who can't fall behind.

These distributional effects explain why high inflation generates such strong political pressure. The losers — retirees, savers, fixed-income workers — are vocal and organized. The gainers — mortgaged homeowners, indebted businesses — are quieter about their windfall. Politicians get blamed for inflation even when they have limited short-term ability to stop it.

## Why High Inflation Is Bad Even Beyond the Redistribution

Setting aside who wins and loses, high inflation imposes costs on the economy that are losses to everyone.

**Menu costs.** Firms must continuously update prices — reprinting menus, retagging products, updating catalogs, reprogramming systems. Individually small; in aggregate, substantial.

**Shoe-leather costs.** With money rapidly losing value, people minimize their cash holdings and make more trips to the bank to move money into assets that hold value. Time and effort spent managing money is time and effort not spent on anything productive.

**Distorted price signals.** Prices contain information — a rising price tells producers more of something is wanted; a falling price tells them less. When all prices are rising at different unpredictable rates, it's hard to distinguish the signal from the noise. Buyers and sellers make worse decisions.

**Tax distortions.** The tax code operates on nominal values. Capital gains taxes apply to nominal gains, including the portion that's just inflation. Bracket creep pushes workers into higher nominal tax brackets through raises that don't represent real income increases.

**Investment uncertainty.** Long-run investment decisions require comparing future returns to current costs. High and variable inflation makes these comparisons unreliable. The risk premium rises; less investment happens.

These costs are why modern central banks target low and stable inflation — typically around 2 percent annually. Lower carries deflation risk (negative effects from the opposite direction). Higher imposes the costs above.

## Indexing: A Partial Fix

One institutional response to inflation is to remove the surprise from nominal contracts — **indexing** them to automatically adjust with prices.

Major U.S. indexed programs and instruments:

- **Social Security.** Benefits adjusted annually by CPI.
- **Federal income tax brackets.** Adjusted for CPI, preventing bracket creep.
- **Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS).** Bonds whose principal rises with CPI, so the real return is protected.
- **Some union wage contracts.** Explicit cost-of-living adjustment provisions.
- **Adjustable-rate mortgages.** Tied to nominal rates that incorporate expected inflation.

Indexing softens the distributional harm from inflation — it protects the indexed party from the transfer. But it doesn't eliminate the real costs. The distorted price signals, the uncertainty, the menu and shoe-leather costs — none of these are eliminated by indexing. And indexing transfers the burden rather than removing it: a Social Security COLA that protects retirees has to be funded by someone.

## Hyperinflation: The Limit Case

Most high-inflation episodes are painful but ultimately reversible. **Hyperinflation** — defined by economist Phillip Cagan as inflation exceeding 50 percent per month — is a different phenomenon.

At 50 percent monthly inflation, prices double every couple of months. Over a year, they rise about 130-fold. Major episodes:

- **Weimar Germany, 1922–1923.** Prices doubled every few days at the peak. Workers were paid twice daily so they could spend wages before they became worthless. Wheelbarrows of cash for a loaf of bread was literal, not metaphorical.
- **Hungary, 1945–1946.** The worst on record: prices doubled every 15 hours at the peak.
- **Zimbabwe, 2007–2008.** The episode from the chapter's opening. Daily price doubling at the peak.
- **Venezuela, 2018.** Inflation exceeded 1 million percent annually.

The mechanism is consistent across all cases. A government runs large fiscal deficits. It cannot fund them through taxes or normal borrowing — either because the tax base has collapsed, or because creditors won't lend at manageable rates. The central bank prints money to fund the government directly. The money supply grows far faster than real output. Prices rise to absorb the new money. People anticipate more inflation and adjust wages and prices accordingly — which causes more actual inflation. The expectations become self-fulfilling. Velocity increases: people spend money faster because holding it costs you daily.

Breaking the cycle requires two things: genuine fiscal consolidation (the deficit must stop requiring monetary financing) and a credible commitment to monetary stability (people must believe the printing press has stopped). Both are politically costly and take time to establish. Some hyperinflations — Germany 1923, Brazil 1994, Argentina at various points — were ultimately resolved. Others (Zimbabwe, Venezuela) lingered for years after the formal end of the worst inflation because the underlying institutional problems persisted.

The U.S. has not experienced hyperinflation since the Confederate currency collapse during the Civil War. The institutional design that prevents it — Federal Reserve independence, fiscal constraints, inflation targeting, a credible 2-percent anchor — is valuable precisely because the alternative is catastrophic.

## Back to the Wheelbarrows

The Zimbabwe image is extreme. But it illustrates, in high relief, what inflation does in its ordinary forms.

Money is a technology for coordinating economic activity. It works because people trust it to have roughly stable value. The moment that trust erodes — because prices are doubling every week, or every day, or every 15 hours — the technology breaks. People revert to barter, or to foreign currency, or to holding physical goods instead of money. The efficiency gains that money enables evaporate. The economy shrinks.

Modest inflation at 2–3 percent per year doesn't do this. But it does, quietly and continuously, shift wealth from creditors to debtors, from savers to borrowers, from fixed incomes to flexible ones. And the 2021–2024 U.S. episode was a reminder that "price stability" is not a natural condition. It's an institutional achievement that requires deliberate maintenance.

Chapter 28 covers monetary policy — the Fed's tools for maintaining price stability and the logic behind the 2 percent target. The two will come together in the question of how an open economy manages inflation in a world where supply chains, capital flows, and commodity prices all originate abroad.
