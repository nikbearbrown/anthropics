# Chapter 30 — Government Budgets and Fiscal Policy

*How the U.S. government raises and spends money — and how those decisions affect the macroeconomy.*

In October 2013, the U.S. federal government partially shut down for 16 days. National parks closed. Some federal employees were furloughed. Government services slowed or stopped. The cause: Congress could not agree on a continuing resolution to keep the government funded.

This was not a freak event. The U.S. government has shut down or partially shut down at least four times in the past three decades. Each time the immediate trigger was political. The deeper cause was structural: a chronic mismatch between what the government spends and what it collects — a budget deficit that requires either continual borrowing, a change in spending, a change in taxes, or some political compromise between all three.

In 2024, the federal government spent about $7 trillion. It collected about $4.4 trillion. The $2.6 trillion difference — funded by borrowing — is not an emergency. It's the current normal. The accumulated result of decades of similar gaps is a national debt of about $34 trillion.

Whether this is a crisis, a manageable feature, or a ticking bomb is one of the most contested questions in macroeconomics. This chapter provides the vocabulary and the structure to participate in that debate — how the government spends, how it raises money, how fiscal policy moves the macroeconomy, and what the lags and limits actually are.

## How the U.S. Government Spends

Federal spending in 2024 was roughly $7 trillion. Three categories.

**Mandatory spending** (~60 percent of the budget). Programs whose spending is set by formulas in law, not by annual appropriations. Spending rises automatically as eligible beneficiaries grow or as the economy weakens. The major items:

- *Social Security* (~$1.5 trillion): retirement, disability, and survivor benefits. The largest single federal program.
- *Medicare* (~$0.9 trillion): health insurance for those 65 and older.
- *Medicaid* (~$0.6 trillion): health insurance for low-income individuals, jointly funded by federal and state governments.
- *Other mandatory* (~$0.6 trillion): SNAP (food stamps), federal employee retirement, veterans' benefits, the Earned Income Tax Credit.

**Discretionary spending** (~30 percent of the budget). Appropriated annually by Congress — requiring active decisions. Two broad categories:
- *Defense* (~$0.9 trillion): military, foreign aid with security purposes, Veterans Affairs operations.
- *Non-defense discretionary* (~$0.9 trillion): education, transportation, housing, science, environment, federal courts, the executive branch.

**Net interest** (~10 percent of the budget, and growing). Interest payments on the outstanding national debt. About $0.7 trillion in 2024. This category grows automatically as the debt grows and as interest rates rise — neither of which requires a Congressional vote.

State and local governments spend about $4 trillion more. Their biggest categories: education (~30 percent), Medicaid matching funds (~15 percent), public safety, transportation, and public employee compensation and benefits. States and localities are, unlike the federal government, generally required to balance their operating budgets.

## How the U.S. Government Raises Money

Federal revenue in 2024 was about $4.4 trillion.

- **Individual income tax** (~$2 trillion, roughly 50 percent of revenue).
- **Payroll taxes** (~$1.6 trillion, ~37 percent): employees and employers each pay 6.2 percent of wages into Social Security (up to an earnings cap) and 1.45 percent each into Medicare.
- **Corporate income tax** (~$0.4 trillion, ~10 percent).
- **Excise taxes, customs, estate taxes, miscellaneous** (~$0.4 trillion, ~3 percent).

The U.S. federal tax structure is progressive — higher earners pay a higher fraction of their income in tax. The top marginal rate on ordinary income is currently 37 percent. Deductions, credits, and the treatment of capital gains complicate the actual progressivity at the high end; the Earned Income Tax Credit makes the structure more generous at the low end.

State income taxes range from zero (Texas, Florida, Nevada, Washington) to 13.3 percent (California). State sales taxes range from 4 to 9 percent. Property taxes vary enormously by locality.

## How Taxes Are Classified

Three categories based on how the burden falls relative to income.

**Progressive tax.** Higher earners pay a higher fraction of their income. The federal income tax is progressive by design. The estate tax is steeply progressive.

**Proportional (flat) tax.** Everyone pays the same fraction. Some state income taxes are flat. The Medicare payroll tax is proportional on most wages (1.45 percent, plus a 0.9 percent surcharge above a threshold).

**Regressive tax.** Lower earners pay a higher fraction of their income. Sales taxes are regressive in operation — lower-income households spend a higher fraction of their income, so the same tax rate takes a larger share of their budget. The Social Security payroll tax has a regressive feature: it's collected only up to a cap (~$168,000 in 2024), so earnings above that threshold pay no Social Security tax, making the effective rate lower for high earners.

Taking everything together — income taxes, payroll taxes, excises, refundable credits like the EITC — the U.S. federal tax system is moderately progressive. State and local taxes, in aggregate, are typically regressive. The combined system is less progressive than in many other developed countries.

## Annual Deficit vs. Accumulated Debt

Two concepts that are constantly confused — and shouldn't be.

**Annual budget deficit** is the gap between revenue and spending in a single fiscal year. In 2024: spending $7 trillion, revenue $4.4 trillion, deficit roughly $2.6 trillion. A surplus would mean revenue exceeded spending in that year.

**National debt** is the accumulated total of all past deficits, minus any past surpluses. As of 2024, the total federal debt is about $34 trillion; "debt held by the public" (excluding intra-government holdings) is about $28 trillion. This represents the face value of all outstanding Treasury securities.

The deficit adds to the debt each year. If deficits are large and persistent, the debt grows. The **debt-to-GDP ratio** is the standard international measure — about 100 percent for the U.S. in 2024.

The U.S. has run budget deficits in nearly every year for decades. The brief exceptions — the late 1990s, when rapid economic growth, the tech boom, and 1990s tax increases produced surpluses — were followed by a return to deficits after the 2001 recession and the Bush-era tax cuts and spending increases.

The deficit is not automatically unsustainable. If the economy grows faster than the interest rate on the debt, debt-to-GDP can fall even with moderate deficits. The question is whether the U.S. is on that trajectory — and the honest answer is: not clearly, at current debt levels and interest rates.

## Expansionary and Contractionary Fiscal Policy

Fiscal policy is the government's use of spending and tax decisions to influence the macroeconomy.

**Expansionary fiscal policy** — increase spending, cut taxes, or both. This shifts aggregate demand right. In a recession (Keynesian zone of the SRAS curve), this raises real GDP and employment. The major U.S. examples: the 2009 American Recovery and Reinvestment Act (~$800 billion) enacted in response to the Great Recession, and the 2020 CARES Act (~$2 trillion) and 2021 American Rescue Plan (~$1.9 trillion) in response to COVID.

**Contractionary fiscal policy** — decrease spending, raise taxes, or both. This shifts AD left. In an overheating economy, this cools inflation. U.S. examples of deliberate contractionary discretionary policy are rare; cutting popular programs and raising taxes faces intense political resistance. Contractionary policy has mostly happened through the automatic stabilizer channel (revenue rising in booms, expenditure falling) rather than through explicit decisions.

The effect of any fiscal change is multiplied. The fiscal multiplier describes how much GDP changes for each dollar of fiscal stimulus. A multiplier of 1.5 means $100 billion in spending raises GDP by $150 billion. Multipliers vary with the type of spending, the state of the economy, and the monetary policy stance — they're larger in recessions, larger at the zero lower bound, and larger for direct government spending than for tax cuts (which may be saved rather than spent).

**Automatic stabilizers** are programs that expand in recessions without any new legislation — unemployment insurance, SNAP, Medicaid, and the income-tax system itself (lower incomes → lower taxes). In a recession, these automatically pump money into the economy. In a boom, they automatically drain some spending. They are counter-cyclical by design and require no political decision to operate.

## The Three Lags

Discretionary fiscal policy faces three timing problems that make it a blunt instrument for stabilization.

**Recognition lag.** It takes months to know a recession has started. The National Bureau of Economic Research — the official U.S. body that dates recessions — typically doesn't declare one until several months after it began. By the time policymakers are confident they're in a recession, a quarter or two may already have passed.

**Decision lag.** Congress must enact legislation. A bill must be drafted, debated, amended, passed by both chambers, and signed. In divided government, this can take a year or more. The 2009 ARRA was enacted within weeks of the Obama inauguration — fast by congressional standards — but the recession had begun in December 2007, 13 months earlier.

**Implementation lag.** Once enacted, spending takes time to flow. Infrastructure projects require environmental review, design, contracting, and construction — often years. Tax changes take effect more quickly but their economic effect comes through subsequent decisions over months.

The combined lags can mean fiscal stimulus enacted in response to a recession arrives during the recovery, when its effect is mostly inflationary rather than stimulative. This is one reason most macroeconomists favor monetary policy as the primary stabilization tool — the Fed can move at eight scheduled meetings per year, much faster than any legislative process.

Automatic stabilizers don't have these lags. They activate immediately, without legislation, which is one reason they're valued above discretionary fiscal action in normal recessions.

## The Balanced-Budget Debate

Should the federal government be required by law to balance its budget every year?

**The case for.** A balanced-budget requirement would discipline politicians from running chronic deficits, prevent intergenerational transfer (current benefits paid by future taxpayers), and limit debt accumulation that restricts future fiscal options. It would force explicit trade-offs between programs and taxes rather than deferring them through borrowing.

**The case against.** A strict balanced-budget rule eliminates the government's ability to respond to recessions — Keynesian fiscal stabilization becomes impossible. It forces pro-cyclical policy: in recessions, when revenue automatically falls, the government would have to cut spending or raise taxes, making the recession worse. It treats the government budget like a household budget, which economists broadly consider incorrect — the federal government has unique borrowing capacity, is the issuer of its own currency, and operates on a scale where its own spending affects the tax base it's trying to balance against.

Most economists oppose a strict balanced-budget rule. The mainstream view is that fiscal flexibility is necessary for stabilization, that automatic stabilizers should be allowed to operate freely, and that long-run sustainability is a separate question from year-to-year balance. A modified "cyclically adjusted" balanced budget — balance the budget over the full business cycle, not every year — might combine some fiscal discipline with the flexibility needed for recessions.

The political argument for balanced budgets often masks a different set of disputes: which programs should be reduced, which taxes should be raised, who should bear the costs of fiscal restraint. Without resolving those underlying questions, "balance the budget" functions as a slogan that commands broad support precisely because it's noncommittal about the hard choices.

## Back to the Shutdown

The October 2013 shutdown was about a continuing resolution — a temporary measure to keep funding at existing levels while the long-term budget was negotiated. It had nothing to do with a recession or a macro crisis. It had everything to do with the structural reality this chapter describes: persistent deficits, a spending structure dominated by programs that are politically untouchable, a revenue structure that's politically difficult to raise, and a political system that must reach agreements on all of it to keep the lights on.

The economics of this isn't mysterious. The politics is hard. The chapter's contribution is to give you the vocabulary to see clearly which parts are economic claims and which are political choices. When someone says "we need to cut spending," the economics can tell you what that means for the macro stabilizers and for specific programs. When someone says "we can't raise taxes," the economics can tell you which taxes are most and least distortive. Whether to cut programs, raise taxes, run deficits, or some combination — those are values questions, not economics questions. But you can't even begin to have that argument honestly without the vocabulary this chapter provides.

## LLM Exercises

**Exercise 1 — Build a federal budget breakdown.** Ask an LLM to construct a simplified U.S. federal budget breakdown using rough current numbers. Spending categories (Social Security, Medicare, defense, etc.) and revenue sources (income tax, payroll tax, etc.). Compute the deficit. Verify that it matches recent CBO estimates.

**Exercise 2 — Classify a specific tax.** Tell an LLM about a specific U.S. tax (federal income tax, sales tax, gasoline tax, payroll tax, property tax). Ask it to classify the tax as regressive, proportional, or progressive, and to explain the reasoning. Push for specifics about which features make the tax fall in that category.

**Exercise 3 — Apply expansionary fiscal policy.** Tell an LLM the U.S. is in a recession with high unemployment. Ask: design a $500B fiscal stimulus package. What spending categories would have the highest multiplier? What tax changes would? Push for evidence-based reasoning.

**Exercise 4 — Trace the three lags.** Walk an LLM through a hypothetical: a recession begins in March 2026. Ask it to estimate when (a) the recession is officially recognized, (b) Congress passes legislation, (c) the spending actually starts. Compare to typical historical lags.

**Exercise 5 — Balanced-budget thought experiment.** Tell an LLM that a balanced-budget amendment has been ratified, requiring federal spending to equal revenue every year. Ask it to predict the consequences for: (a) recession response, (b) discretionary spending stability, (c) tax policy, (d) intergenerational equity. Push the LLM to be specific.
