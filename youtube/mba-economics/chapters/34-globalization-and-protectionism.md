# Chapter 34 — Globalization and Protectionism

*The political backlash against trade — and the policy debates that close this book.*

In June 2016, voters in the United Kingdom voted to leave the European Union. Five months later, voters in the United States elected a president who had campaigned on tariffs and renegotiating trade agreements. By 2018, the U.S. had imposed tariffs on $360 billion of Chinese goods, with China retaliating in kind. By 2020, the political consensus that had supported expanding trade for the previous several decades had visibly cracked.

The trade-skeptical politics did not come from nowhere.

The aggregate gains from trade documented in Chapter 33 were real. But they had been distributed unevenly. Manufacturing communities in the American Rust Belt had absorbed substantial losses from import competition, especially after China's accession to the World Trade Organization in 2001. Wages stagnated for non-college-educated workers. Geographic inequality widened. The Economist Jason Furman estimated that increased imports from China roughly doubled after China's WTO entry; David Autor, David Dorn, and Gordon Hanson estimated that Chinese import competition cost between 800,000 and 1 million U.S. manufacturing jobs by 2011.

The losers were concentrated, visible, and politically activated. The winners — consumers paying a few dollars less for a T-shirt, workers in export industries, capital holders diversifying into global markets — were dispersed and mostly unaware of their gains.

The framework from Chapter 18 — concentrated benefits, dispersed costs — runs in reverse here. The *losses* from trade are concentrated; the *gains* are dispersed. This produces political pressure for protection that is, in the framework of standard trade theory, economically inefficient but politically rational from the standpoint of those most affected.

This chapter is about that political reality — the tools of protection, the standard arguments for and against, the institutional framework that has governed trade, and the policy failures that turned aggregate winners into political losers.

## How Protectionism Works

A **tariff** is a tax on imports. A 25 percent tariff means a $1,000 imported good costs $1,250 to bring across the border. The tariff raises the domestic price, making imports less competitive with domestic production.

A **quota** is a quantitative limit on imports. The U.S. might say: no more than 1 million tons of imported steel per year. Above the quota, imports are blocked. The constraint restricts supply, raising the domestic price.

Both have the same structural effect on the supply-demand diagram.

Domestic producers gain — they sell at higher prices. The government collects tariff revenue. Domestic consumers lose — they pay more. Foreign exporters lose — they sell less. Some transactions that would have happened at the free-trade price don't happen at the tariff price — that's deadweight loss, pure social waste.

Quotas produce the same price and quantity effects as tariffs, but the equivalent "revenue" goes to whoever holds the import licenses rather than to the government. Non-tariff barriers — technical standards, regulatory requirements, labeling rules — impose similar effective restrictions without the explicit tax.

The math is unambiguous: for the imposing country as a whole, tariffs are costly. The question is not whether they're economically efficient (they're not) but whether specific circumstances justify their use anyway.

## The Standard Arguments for Protection

Protection has been justified on multiple grounds for centuries. Here's an honest assessment of each.

**National security.** Some industries are essential in a military crisis, and market forces won't guarantee their availability. Domestic capacity in weapons, military electronics, energy, food, and critical minerals (rare earths, semiconductors) has genuine strategic value that isn't captured by price signals.

The argument has merit. Its limit is that every industry eventually claims strategic importance. The useful test is narrow: would this specific input be unavailable in a crisis that market substitution couldn't address? Broad invocations of national security protection — applied to washing machines, solar panels — often disguise rent-seeking.

**Infant industry.** A new industry needs time to develop scale, learning curves, and competitive position before it can survive against established foreign rivals. Temporary protection buys that time.

The argument has theoretical support and historical examples — Korean steel, Japanese automobiles in their early years, Taiwanese electronics. It also fails frequently: temporary protection becomes permanent when the protected industry builds political constituencies that prevent removal. Picking which industries merit infant protection is politically corrupted; the industries that get it are often selected for political, not economic, reasons.

**Anti-dumping.** Foreign producers selling below cost could drive domestic competitors out of business, then raise prices to monopoly levels after the competition is eliminated. Anti-dumping duties prevent this predatory pricing.

The predatory logic is coherent in theory. In practice, most anti-dumping cases involve foreign goods priced low because of genuine comparative advantage or government subsidies, not predatory pricing. The legal standard is gameable. Anti-dumping duties have become a protectionist tool more often than they identify actual predatory behavior.

**Labor and environmental standards.** Free trade with countries that have weaker labor or environmental standards creates pressure to weaken domestic standards — a "race to the bottom." Protection (or trade conditions tied to standards) prevents this.

The race-to-the-bottom concern is real in some sectors. But weaker standards in poor countries also partly reflect poverty rather than policy choice. Rising standards correlate with rising income — trade itself generates the income that eventually supports better standards. And trade conditions tied to labor rights can be used as disguised protectionism.

**Strategic trade policy.** Government support for specific high-scale industries can produce internationally competitive winners that wouldn't emerge under free trade. The theory is sound in narrow cases. The empirical record is mixed; governments are not reliably good at picking winners, and the subsidies often get captured by incumbents rather than directing investment to the best opportunities.

## The Case Against Protection

The arguments against protection are straightforward. The tariff analysis in the previous section captures most of it, but the political economy makes it worth stating explicitly.

**Consumers pay more.** This is the most direct cost. The 2018–2019 U.S. tariffs on Chinese goods raised prices for American consumers of electronics, appliances, clothing, and hundreds of other categories. Some economic studies estimated total consumer cost in the range of $40–68 billion annually.

**Export industries suffer.** Tariffs cause currency appreciation (foreign demand for dollars falls as exports to the U.S. become more expensive) that hurts U.S. exporters. Tariffs raise input costs for industries that use imported intermediates — U.S. steel tariffs raised costs for U.S. automobile manufacturers, appliance makers, and construction. China retaliated against American agricultural exports; U.S. farmers lost an estimated $12–15 billion in Chinese sales in 2018–2019.

**Retaliation compounds the damage.** Trade is not a one-player game. Protection invites retaliation. By 2019, the U.S. and China were in a trade war that had imposed bilateral tariffs on hundreds of billions of dollars of goods, with economic damage flowing in both directions.

**Dynamic losses accumulate.** Less foreign competition means less pressure on domestic industries to innovate, cut costs, and improve quality. Less exposure to foreign technologies and production methods means slower diffusion of best practices. The long-run productivity cost of sustained protection probably exceeds the short-run distributional benefit.

## Trade Adjustment and the Failure of Compensation

The political problem with free trade is not that the economics is wrong. It's that the economics describes aggregate gains that were never redistributed to the workers who absorbed the losses.

The textbook answer to trade's distributional consequences is **trade adjustment assistance** — programs to compensate workers displaced by imports. The U.S. has had a Trade Adjustment Assistance program since 1962. It provides extended unemployment benefits, retraining funds, and relocation assistance.

The empirical record is not encouraging.

Research consistently shows that workers displaced by import competition suffer large, persistent earnings losses — often 15 to 20 percent lower wages in new jobs. TAA reaches only a fraction of those eligible and even among recipients, retraining outcomes are weak. The retraining programs often aim workers at credentials for jobs that don't exist in their geographic areas. The communities that lose manufacturing plants often don't recover for a generation. Geographic mobility is harder when your home has lost value, your family is rooted, and the skills you have aren't portable.

The aggregate gains from trade were real. They were also captured by consumers (dispersed, individually small), shareholders (concentrated, individually large), and workers in export industries (less concentrated than import-competing workers, but still diffuse). The workers who lost manufacturing jobs to import competition did not receive the gains that accrued elsewhere. That mismatch — gains here, losses there, no mechanism to connect them — is the political-economy failure that produced Brexit and the trade-war politics of the late 2010s.

It is worth saying this clearly: the standard free-trade framework predicts that the gains exceed the losses, so compensation is feasible. But feasibility is not the same as implementation. The political mechanism for redistributing the gains to the losers — progressive taxation, adequately funded worker support, geographic reinvestment — was not built at the scale the trade-expansion era required.

## Trade Institutions and Their Erosion

The post-WWII international trading system was built on several institutions:

**GATT** (General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, 1947) established the principle of reciprocal tariff reduction and most-favored-nation treatment. Over eight rounds of negotiations, global tariffs fell from roughly 30–40 percent to under 5 percent on most goods.

**WTO** (World Trade Organization, 1995) replaced GATT with stronger dispute-resolution mechanisms, expanded coverage to services and intellectual property, and admitted China in 2001. The WTO's dispute system was, for two decades, the closest thing the world had to a functioning international trade court.

**Regional agreements** like NAFTA (1994, renegotiated as USMCA in 2020), the EU single market, and ASEAN agreements extended free-trade principles within specific geographic areas.

The system faced sustained erosion after 2016. The U.S. paralyzed WTO dispute resolution by blocking appointments to its appellate body. The 2018–2019 tariff escalations violated WTO commitments. Brexit fragmented European trade relationships. China's industrial subsidies and market restrictions generated tensions the WTO's rules weren't designed to address.

Whether this represents a permanent restructuring of the international trade order or a temporary disruption is one of the major open questions in international economics. Some economists argue that globalization has run its political limits and a partial retreat is the new normal. Others argue the institutional framework can be repaired if there is political will. The outcome is genuinely uncertain.

## Aggregate Gains, Concentrated Losses, and the Path Forward

Pull back to where this chapter and this book began.

Markets produce aggregate gains and distributional consequences. The gains from trade are real and substantial — the post-1945 expansion of global trade accompanied the largest sustained improvement in material living standards in human history. The distributional consequences are also real — concentrated losses in import-competing communities, wage stagnation for non-college workers, geographic inequality.

The political response to those consequences — protection, retrenchment, managed trade — is economically costly. The alternative — free trade without adequate adjustment — is politically unsustainable.

The economist's uncomfortable position is that the right answer requires both: maintain the aggregate gains from trade while building better mechanisms to share them. That requires policy outside the trade channel — labor-market policy, geographic investment, education and retraining, tax and transfer systems adequate to redistribute gains to losers. None of that is technically complicated. All of it is politically hard.

The debate over trade is not really about whether trade is good. It is about whether societies can build the institutions to make trade's gains accessible to everyone, not just to those who were already positioned to benefit.

That question doesn't have an economics answer. It has a political answer. The economist's task is to show clearly what's at stake on each side. That's what this chapter — and this book — has tried to do.

## LLM Exercises

**Exercise 1 — Calculate a tariff's effect.** Tell an LLM about a hypothetical good: domestic price $100, world price $80, domestic supply at $80 = 100 units, domestic demand at $80 = 200 units. A 25% tariff is imposed. What's the new price? New domestic production? New imports? Tariff revenue? Deadweight loss?

**Exercise 2 — Argue for and against a specific protection.** Pick a real protective measure (U.S. steel tariffs, EU agricultural subsidies, Japan's rice protections, U.S. lumber tariffs on Canada). Ask an LLM to argue both sides. Push for specifics rather than generic arguments.

**Exercise 3 — Diagnose the China shock.** Tell an LLM about the U.S. labor-market effects of China's WTO entry in 2001. Ask it to summarize the empirical evidence (Autor-Dorn-Hanson "China shock" research). What share of U.S. manufacturing job loss was attributable to Chinese import competition? What did trade adjustment do or fail to do?

**Exercise 4 — Design a trade adjustment program.** Ask an LLM to design a U.S. trade adjustment program adequate to compensate workers losing jobs to imports. Consider scale, eligibility, retraining, relocation, geographic targeting. Push for specifics that engage with the empirical limitations of past programs.

**Exercise 5 — Evaluate the Brexit decision.** Ask an LLM to evaluate, eight years after the 2016 vote, whether Brexit produced the economic outcomes that pro- and anti-Brexit economists predicted. Push for specific evidence on trade, growth, regulatory changes, immigration, financial sector. The exercise teaches the difficulty of separating policy effects from other forces.
