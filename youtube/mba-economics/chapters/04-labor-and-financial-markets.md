# Chapter 4 — Labor and Financial Markets

*The same diagram applied to two markets that don't look like markets — the one for hours, and the one for dollars across time.*

*When the thing being sold is a person's time, or a promise about the future, the price still clears the market — and price controls still break it the same way.*

In 2020, the U.S. Census Bureau reported that 22 percent of the country's population was at least 60 years old. More than 74 million people. The baby boomers — born between 1946 and 1964 — were hitting retirement and the medical conditions that come with it: heart disease, arthritis, Alzheimer's, the everyday wear of long lives. The Affordable Care Act, in the years just before, had pulled millions more people into the formal healthcare system.

The result, in the labor market for nurses, was not a slow drift. It was a surge. Hospitals couldn't hire fast enough. Wages for registered nurses rose. Nursing schools couldn't expand fast enough to meet enrollment. Travel-nurse agencies, which existed before the surge but at modest scale, became enormous — paying nurses two or three times the staff salary to cover short-term contracts.

I want you to look at that surge carefully, because it is not a special story about nurses. It is the Chapter 3 demand-and-supply model — the same two curves, the same equilibrium, the same shift logic — running on a market that most people don't think of as a market at all: the market for hours of skilled human work.

That move is what this chapter is about. We take the machinery we built in Chapter 3 and apply it to two markets where it does unusual and revealing work. The first is the **labor market**, where workers sell their time and firms buy it. The second is the **financial-capital market**, where savers sell money-now and borrowers buy it, and the price is the **interest rate**. By the end you should see why the same diagram explains the nurse wage surge, why your student loan rate is what it is, and why a law capping interest rates works exactly the same way as a law capping rent.

## Labor as a Market

Start by being precise about what's actually traded. In a labor market, what's bought and sold is **hours of work** — specifically, hours of a particular kind of work, by people with a particular skill set, in a particular geographic area. The "market for nurses in the Minneapolis–St. Paul metro area in 2020" is a labor market. The "market for software engineers in San Francisco in 2025" is another. They are not the same market, because the workers in one cannot easily substitute into the other, and the buyers are different firms with different needs.

The price in this market is the **wage** — dollars per hour, or its annual equivalent.

The supply curve slopes upward for the same reason it always does: higher price, more sellers willing to show up. A nurse who wouldn't work for $40 an hour might work for $60. A software engineer who wouldn't move across the country for $120,000 might move for $250,000. As the wage rises, the pool of workers willing to take the job expands.

The demand curve slopes downward for the same reason it always does: higher price, fewer buyers. A hospital that would hire 200 registered nurses at $40 an hour might hire only 150 at $60. The mechanism on the firm's side is this: every hiring decision is really a comparison between what a worker *produces* for the firm and what the worker *costs*. As wages rise, some positions that were marginally worth filling are no longer worth filling.

The two curves cross at an equilibrium wage and an equilibrium employment level. At that crossing, every employer who wants a worker at the going wage can find one, and every worker who wants a job at the going wage can find one.

W* is the wage that clears the market; L* is the number of people working. Every subsequent shift in this chapter moves one of these two curves."] -->

Now: what shifts the curves?

Demand for labor — the firm's side — shifts with the demand for the firm's *output*. Hospitals want more nurses when more patients arrive. Tech firms want more engineers during a software boom. This is the mechanism behind the 2020 nurse surge: aging demographics plus ACA expansion shifted the demand for healthcare, which shifted the demand for nurses to the right. The equilibrium wage rose. Employment rose.

<!-- → [CHART: Labor market demand shift for nurses — two panels showing the same supply-demand diagram. Left panel: pre-2020 equilibrium (D1, S1, wage W1, employment L1). Right panel: demand curve shifts right to D2, new equilibrium at higher wage W2 and higher employment L2. Caption: "The demand for nurses is derived demand — it rises when demand for healthcare rises. The 2020 surge moved the demand curve right; wages and employment both increased."] -->

Demand for labor also shifts with technology — and this is where I want you to slow down, because the story is almost always messier than the headline version. New technologies that make a worker *more productive* shift demand for that worker right. New technologies that *replace* the worker shift demand left. But most technologies do both at once — they automate some tasks and amplify others. ATMs were predicted to wipe out bank tellers in the 1980s. The number of bank tellers actually rose for a while, because ATMs made each branch cheaper to run, so banks opened more branches. Eventually, online banking did what ATMs were predicted to do. The lag was decades. When someone tells you a technology will kill an occupation, the question worth asking is not "will it reduce demand?" but "when, at what rate, and will it also create tasks the workers can absorb?"

<!-- → [INFOGRAPHIC: Technology and labor demand — two-column layout. Left column: "Technologies that shift demand RIGHT" with examples (spreadsheet software → accountants, MRI machines → radiologists who read them, ATMs short-run → bank tellers). Right column: "Technologies that shift demand LEFT" with examples (ATMs long-run + online banking → tellers, automated checkout → cashiers, word processing → typists). Caption: "Most technologies appear in both columns — at different points in time. The lag between the first column and the second is where policy and retraining decisions matter most."] -->

Supply shifts with population, with the cost and duration of training, and with cultural and policy shifts in who participates in the workforce. The number of women in the U.S. paid labor force roughly doubled between 1950 and 2000. For many occupations, that shifted the supply curve dramatically rightward over that span — holding wages lower than they otherwise would have been, even as demand for those workers grew. Immigration shifts supply right. A generation aging out of the workforce shifts supply left.

## The Minimum Wage as a Price Floor

The model makes a sharp prediction about what happens when you impose a minimum wage. If the minimum sits *above* the equilibrium wage in a competitive labor market, it creates a surplus of labor — more workers want to work at that wage than firms want to hire. That surplus is what we call **unemployment** generated by the price floor.

<!-- → [CHART: Minimum wage as price floor — standard labor market diagram with equilibrium wage W* below the minimum wage W_min. At W_min: quantity supplied (workers wanting jobs) exceeds quantity demanded (jobs available). Show the gap as the unemployment created. Label: "Price floor above equilibrium → surplus of labor."] -->

The empirical record, though, is messier than this prediction. Modest minimum-wage increases — a dollar or two in a large metro area — often produce no measurable effect on employment, or a very small one. Larger or more sudden increases sometimes produce visible effects. Why the gap?

A few reasons, and I want to be honest about all of them.

First, many local labor markets are not competitive in the textbook sense. A small town with one large employer — a hospital network, a meat-packing plant — means that employer has real power to set wages below what a competitive market would produce. We call this *monopsony* — the buyer's analog of monopoly. A minimum wage in a monopsony market can *increase* employment, by pushing the wage up toward where competition would have set it anyway. The simple competitive model predicts a price floor creates unemployment; the monopsony model predicts the opposite. The real world contains both, and the researcher's job is to figure out which one describes the market she's looking at.

<!-- → [CHART: Competitive market vs. monopsony — two side-by-side diagrams. Left: competitive labor market, equilibrium at W*, minimum wage W_min above W*, showing unemployment gap (surplus of workers). Right: monopsony market, employer wage W_mono below competitive W*, minimum wage W_min between W_mono and W*, showing employment *increases* from L_mono to L_min. Caption: "The same minimum wage, two different markets, two different predictions. Which diagram describes your local labor market is an empirical question, not a theoretical one."] -->

Second, in the short run, firms can absorb modest wage increases by adjusting things other than headcount: raising prices a little, accepting slightly thinner margins, rerouting tasks. The demand curve for labor is not perfectly elastic. Some adjustment does happen — just not always through layoffs.

Third, real labor markets have search costs and frictions. Workers and firms don't instantly find each other; adjustment takes time. The model assumes frictionless adjustment.

The model is right about the *direction* of the employment effect in a competitive market. It is right *less robustly* than the rent-control case from Chapter 3, because the competitive assumption is on shakier ground in most labor markets than it is in most goods markets. We will return to this in Chapter 14, where we look at labor markets more carefully, with better tools.

## Money Across Time

Now the second market, and this one is harder to see as a market at all.

Here is what's traded: a present amount of money, exchanged for a promise of a future amount. A saver deposits $1,000 today; the bank returns $1,030 a year from now. A borrower takes $1,000 today; repays $1,080 a year from now. The thing being exchanged is *purchasing power through time* — the ability to spend now versus the ability to spend later. The price of that exchange is the **interest rate**.

Put the interest rate on the vertical axis. Put the quantity of financial capital — dollars moving from savers to borrowers — on the horizontal axis. Now draw the two curves.

The supply curve slopes upward: higher interest rate, more saving. At 1 percent, you might leave your money in a checking account. At 5 percent, you'd put it in a certificate of deposit. At 10 percent, you might forgo a vacation to save more. The mechanism is simple: a higher rate raises the reward for waiting, so more people are willing to wait.

The demand curve slopes downward: higher interest rate, fewer borrowers. A business that would borrow $100,000 to expand at 4 percent might decline at 9 percent. Mortgage applications fall when rates rise; car loan demand falls when rates rise. The mechanism is equally simple: a higher rate raises the cost of getting money now, so fewer projects look worth the cost.

<!-- → [CHART: Financial capital market equilibrium — standard supply-demand diagram with interest rate on the vertical axis and quantity of financial capital on the horizontal axis. Upward-sloping supply (savers), downward-sloping demand (borrowers), equilibrium at interest rate r* and quantity Q*. Caption: "The interest rate is the price that clears this market — it rises until the quantity savers want to supply equals the quantity borrowers want to demand."] -->

The two curves cross at an equilibrium interest rate and an equilibrium quantity of capital flowing from savers to borrowers.

What shifts the curves?

Supply — savings — shifts with income, expectations, demographics, tax policy, and foreign capital flows. When households earn more, they typically save more. When older populations are building retirement funds, they save heavily. When the U.S. offers better returns than other countries, foreign capital flows in — shifting the U.S. supply curve right and pushing domestic rates down.

Demand — borrowing — shifts with the perceived profitability of investment, with consumer confidence, with government deficit spending, and with foreign borrowing. When the federal government runs a large deficit, it must borrow — competing with private businesses for the available pool of capital. This pushes the demand curve right and, all else equal, raises interest rates. The effect has a name: *crowding out*. Whether it's large or small in practice depends on how elastic the supply curve is — if foreign capital flows in to compensate, crowding out can be modest.

Column 1: Supply of capital (savers) — rows: household income rises (shift right); older population saving for retirement (shift right); foreign capital inflows seeking U.S. returns (shift right); tax incentives for retirement savings (shift right); recession expectations, precautionary saving (shift right). Column 2: Demand for capital (borrowers) — rows: profitable investment opportunities (shift right); consumer confidence in housing/cars (shift right); federal government deficit spending (shift right); business pessimism or recession (shift left); foreign borrowers shift demand to other markets (shift left). Caption: "Every item in this table corresponds to a news headline you've read. The table is a translation key."] -->

Let me walk through a concrete case. In the early 2020s, the Federal Reserve raised short-term interest rates aggressively to fight inflation. Mortgage rates, closely tied to longer-term Treasury yields, rose from roughly 3 percent in 2021 to roughly 7 percent in 2023. What does the model predict?

Original equilibrium: low rate, large quantity of mortgage credit extended. The change: short-term rates rose, pulling longer-term rates with them; mortgage rates climbed. From the borrower's side, the cost of borrowing rose. This is a movement *along* the demand curve — fewer borrowers want mortgages at 7 percent than wanted them at 3 percent. Quantity of new mortgages drops. New home sales fell sharply through 2022 and 2023.

The model handled this case cleanly. It also missed something the simple diagram doesn't contain: existing homeowners, locked into 3 percent mortgages, didn't want to sell, because selling would mean financing their next purchase at 7 percent. The supply of existing homes for sale collapsed. The model predicted correctly what happened to *new mortgage demand*. The fuller picture — that rising rates also suppress housing supply through this lock-in mechanism — required more than the two-curve diagram contains.

<!-- → [CHART: Mortgage market 2021–2023 — financial capital market diagram showing movement along the demand curve. Mark two points on the downward-sloping demand curve: point A at r=3% with high quantity of mortgages (2021), and point B at r=7% with lower quantity (2023). Arrow along the curve showing the movement. Annotate: "Fed rate hikes raised mortgage rates; borrowers moved along the demand curve — not a shift, a slide." Caption: "This is a movement along the demand curve, not a shift of it. The curve didn't move; the price changed and borrowers responded."] -->

That is a general lesson about the model, not a criticism of it. Every model leaves things out. The question is whether what it leaves out matters for the question you're asking.

## Usury Laws as Price Ceilings

Usury laws are old. Medieval canon law prohibited charging *any* interest on loans. Modern usury laws are less absolute: they cap the rate a lender can legally charge, typically to protect borrowers from predatory lending. Some states cap consumer interest at 10, 18, or 36 percent annual rates. Various federal rules cap rates in specific credit categories.

A usury cap that sits below the market interest rate is a **price ceiling**. The diagram is exactly the one we drew in Chapter 3 for rent control — just with different labels.

<!-- → [CHART: Usury cap as price ceiling — financial capital market diagram with equilibrium rate r* above the usury cap r_cap. At r_cap: quantity demanded (borrowers who want loans) exceeds quantity supplied (loans lenders are willing to make). Show the gap as the shortage of credit. Label: "Price ceiling below equilibrium → shortage of credit."] -->

The model predicts: at the capped rate, more borrowers want loans than lenders are willing to make. Some borrowers get rationed out. Which ones? Usually the riskiest — the borrowers most likely to default, the ones whose expected default rate makes them unprofitable to lend to at the capped rate. They either get no loan, or they get pushed into the gray market: payday lenders who charge effective triple-digit annual rates, structured through fees to route around the cap.

The same trade-off the rent-control diagram showed us appears here. Redistribution toward the borrowers who still get loans, away from lenders, with deadweight loss in the form of credit not extended and projects not financed. Whether that trade-off is worth making — whether the harms of predatory lending justify the rationing — is a question the model cannot answer. What the model does is make the structural consequences visible, before the policy debate begins.

## The Same Diagram, Different Labels

Pull back. What have we actually done in this chapter?

We took the demand-and-supply model from Chapter 3 — two curves, equilibrium, shifts, surplus, shortage, price controls — and applied it to two markets where the thing traded is not a physical object. In both cases, the same structure holds: a price, a supply curve, a demand curve, an equilibrium, and a set of forces that shift the curves.

In the labor market: the price is the wage, the seller is the worker, the buyer is the firm, and the curves shift with output demand on the firm's side and with population, training costs, and labor-force participation on the worker's side.

In the financial-capital market: the price is the interest rate, the seller is the saver, the buyer is the borrower, and the curves shift with income, demographics, expectations, government deficits, and foreign capital flows on both sides.

Rows: What is traded / Who is the seller / Who is the buyer / What is the price / What shifts demand / What shifts supply / Price floor example / Price ceiling example. Columns: Labor Market | Financial Capital Market. Caption: "The same diagram, twice. The labels change; the logic doesn't."] -->

I want to make one more observation, and it is the one that should stay with you when everything else fades.

Every price you see in the world — a salary, a mortgage rate, a sticker price on a tomato, an Uber surge multiplier — is the visible output of two curves you cannot see directly. The price contains information about both the buyer's side and the seller's side. When a price rises, something on one or both sides changed. The economist's reflex is to ask: which curve moved, and why? That reflex — applied to wages, to interest rates, to housing prices, to the cost of a nurse in a short-staffed hospital — is most of what this course is teaching you to do.

A more sober observation: the model is most reliable when the market resembles the assumptions behind it — many buyers, many sellers, no one with much pricing power, low information asymmetry, low friction, easy entry and exit. Some markets fit this description reasonably well. Many do not. Healthcare markets with insurance intermediaries, labor markets with monopsony employers, financial markets in a crisis — none of these look quite like the textbook diagram. In those cases, the model still often gets the *direction* right, but the magnitude becomes unreliable, and occasionally so does the direction itself. Knowing when to trust the model and when to hold it more lightly is the judgment that takes years to develop, and that no single chapter can install. What the chapter can do is give you the tool and be honest about its limits.

## LLM Exercises

**Exercise 1 — Predict the labor market.** Pick an occupation likely to face significant change in the next ten years (radiologists, truck drivers, registered nurses, software engineers, K–12 teachers). Ask an LLM to predict whether equilibrium wages and employment will rise or fall, and to identify which curve shifts and why. Then push: ask it to identify the *strongest counterargument* to its own prediction. Evaluate whether the LLM is doing genuine reasoning or restating standard talking points.

**Exercise 2 — Minimum wage with two views.** Tell an LLM you want to think carefully about minimum-wage effects. Ask it to write two short analyses: one assuming a perfectly competitive labor market, and one assuming the local market has significant monopsony power. Compare the predictions. Use this exercise to internalize that the model's predictions depend on which assumptions are doing the work.

**Exercise 3 — Interest rate as a price.** Give an LLM this task: explain to a high-school student what an interest rate *is*, in plain English, by analogy to the price of any other thing. Evaluate the explanation. Does the LLM treat the interest rate as the price of money-across-time? Does it explain why both savers and borrowers respond to it? If the analogy is weak or the explanation circular, push it to revise.

**Exercise 4 — Crowding out.** Tell an LLM: "When the federal government runs a large deficit, it borrows from the same pool of capital that private businesses borrow from." Ask it to predict what happens to the interest rate and to private investment using the demand-supply-of-capital diagram, and to use the four-step method. Then ask: under what conditions does this crowding-out effect *not* happen? (Hint: foreign capital inflows, slack in the economy.) Evaluate whether the LLM names the conditions clearly.

**Exercise 5 — Usury cap stress test.** Describe to an LLM a hypothetical state that has just imposed a 15 percent annual interest rate cap on payday loans. Ask it to predict three consequences using the demand-supply diagram. Then ask: which borrowers benefit, and which borrowers are *worse off* than before the cap? A good answer notices that borrowers who used to get loans at 200 percent annual rates — a real number for some payday products — and now get no loans at all are not obviously better off, even though the cap was designed to help them. Evaluate whether the LLM reaches that uncomfortable conclusion.

