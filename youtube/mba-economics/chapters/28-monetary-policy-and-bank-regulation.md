# Chapter 28 — Monetary Policy and Bank Regulation

*The Federal Reserve — what it does, how it does it, and what it can't do.*

In December 2008, the Federal Reserve cut its benchmark interest rate to a target range of 0 to 0.25 percent — effectively zero. The U.S. economy was in the deepest recession since the Great Depression. Standard monetary policy says: in a recession, cut rates to encourage borrowing, investment, and spending. But the rate was already at zero. The Fed had run out of conventional ammunition.

What it did next was unprecedented. The Fed launched **quantitative easing** — buying massive quantities of long-term Treasury bonds and mortgage-backed securities directly. Over the following years, the Fed's balance sheet expanded from roughly $900 billion to about $4.5 trillion. The goal was to push down long-term interest rates, support asset prices, and signal a sustained commitment to recovery.

Whether it worked is contested. That it changed how monetary policy operates is not.

This chapter is about the Federal Reserve: what it is, how it moves the economy, what happens when conventional tools run out, and what bank regulation has to do with any of it. By the end you should be able to trace a monetary policy decision from the FOMC meeting room through interest rates, investment decisions, aggregate demand, and ultimately to output and inflation.

## How the Fed Is Structured

The Federal Reserve System was created by Congress in 1913, in response to a series of banking panics in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Its purpose: provide a stable currency, supervise banks, serve as lender of last resort in financial crises, and conduct monetary policy.

Three interlocking parts:

**The Board of Governors.** Seven members, appointed by the President, confirmed by the Senate, to staggered 14-year terms. The Chair — appointed from the Board to a 4-year renewable term — is the public face of the institution and by far the most visible central bank official in the world. Located in Washington.

**Twelve regional Federal Reserve Banks.** Each serves a geographic district. The New York Fed is the most operationally important — it's where open market operations are actually conducted and where the financial system's plumbing runs. The regional banks do research, handle banking-sector services, and supervise banks in their districts.

**The Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC).** The body that sets interest rate policy. Twelve voting members: the seven Board governors plus five rotating regional bank presidents (the New York Fed president always votes). Meets eight times a year. Each meeting ends with a policy decision and a statement; each December includes updated forecasts of GDP, unemployment, and inflation.

The Fed's independence from the executive branch is by design. Fourteen-year terms, staggered so no single president controls the Board, and a structure that makes day-to-day monetary decisions insulated from political pressure. This independence is not absolute — Congress can change the mandate, the President appoints governors — but in practice, rate decisions are made on technical grounds with limited direct political interference.

The Fed operates under a **dual mandate** from Congress: maximum employment and stable prices. Most other major central banks focus primarily on price stability; the U.S. mandate is unusual in formally requiring balance between both.

## The Three Traditional Tools

Before 2008, the Fed used three main tools to influence the money supply and interest rates.

**Open Market Operations (OMO).** The Fed buys or sells U.S. Treasury securities in the secondary market. Buying injects reserves into the banking system — banks have more reserves, the federal funds rate falls, borrowing costs drop, lending expands. Selling drains reserves — tighter banking conditions, higher rates, slower lending. OMO has been the primary instrument since the Fed started explicitly targeting the federal funds rate in the 1990s. The New York Fed's trading desk executes OMO daily.

**The Discount Rate.** The rate at which banks can borrow directly from the Fed's discount window. Lowering the rate makes it cheaper for banks to borrow from the Fed as a backstop, encouraging more lending at the margin. In practice, banks prefer to borrow from each other in the federal funds market, so the discount rate is a secondary tool — more important as a safety valve in crises than as a day-to-day lever.

**Reserve Requirements.** The fraction of deposits banks must hold as reserves rather than lend. The Fed eliminated formal reserve requirements in March 2020, moving to a system that sets the policy rate directly through interest paid on reserve balances.

## Expansionary and Contractionary: Tracing the Mechanism

The two directions of monetary policy each follow a chain from the FOMC decision to the macroeconomic outcome.

**Expansionary monetary policy** is used during recessions or when inflation is below target. The chain:

The FOMC directs the New York Fed to buy Treasuries. Banks receive reserves. The federal funds rate falls — banks have excess reserves and compete to lend them at the margin. Short-term rates fall. Long-term rates follow with a lag. Households and firms see lower mortgage rates, lower auto-loan rates, lower corporate borrowing costs. Borrowing increases. Investment rises. Consumer spending on durable goods rises. Aggregate demand shifts right. Real GDP rises, unemployment falls, inflation pressure builds.

**Contractionary monetary policy** runs the chain in reverse. The FOMC directs securities sales. Reserves drain. The federal funds rate rises. Borrowing becomes more expensive. Investment slows. Consumer spending tightens. Aggregate demand shifts left. Inflation declines; real GDP slows; unemployment rises.

The key lag to appreciate: monetary policy is not immediate. A rate cut today affects investment decisions over the next quarter, construction activity over the next year, and the eventual GDP and inflation readings over 12 to 18 months. This is why monetary policy is often described as driving by looking in the rearview mirror — by the time a problem appears in the data, the right policy intervention is already months past due.

## The Zero Lower Bound Problem

Standard monetary policy assumed that interest rates are positive and that the Fed can always cut them further to fight a recession. In December 2008, the federal funds rate reached 0 to 0.25 percent, and the recession continued. The Fed had hit the **zero lower bound (ZLB)**.

Why is zero a practical floor? In theory, rates could go negative — the central bank charges banks for holding reserves, forcing them to lend or spend. In practice, depositors can convert to physical cash to avoid negative rates, which limits how negative rates can go before the system breaks down. The European Central Bank, the Bank of Japan, and a few others have experimented with mildly negative rates; the U.S. has not gone there.

When the policy rate hits zero, the Fed must use unconventional tools.

**Quantitative easing (QE).** Buy long-term assets directly — Treasuries and mortgage-backed securities — to push down long-term rates (which the federal funds rate doesn't directly control when it's at zero). QE works through the "portfolio balance" channel: removing long-term safe assets from circulation forces investors into riskier assets, pushing up prices and pushing down yields across the board.

The Fed ran three major QE programs from 2008 to 2014, buying roughly $3.5 trillion of long-term assets. The 2020–2022 program was even larger — the balance sheet peaked near $9 trillion. The eventual unwinding of those balance sheets ("quantitative tightening," or QT) is a process that itself takes years.

**Forward guidance.** Announce explicitly how long the policy rate will remain low. If markets believe the announcement, long-term rates fall (because they're determined partly by expected future short rates). "We expect to maintain the federal funds rate near zero until the labor market has reached maximum employment" — if credible, this shifts the entire yield curve. Forward guidance is cheap to deploy but depends entirely on credibility.

**Operation Twist.** Sell short-term securities, buy long-term ones — extending the duration of the Fed's portfolio without expanding its size. Used in 2011–2012 to push down long-term rates without additional balance sheet expansion.

## The Equation of Exchange

A useful conceptual anchor for all of this is the **equation of exchange**:

$$M \times V = P \times Q$$

$M$ is the money supply. $V$ is velocity — the average number of times each dollar is spent per year. $P$ is the price level. $Q$ is real GDP. The product $P \times Q$ is nominal GDP.

Rearranging: any increase in the money supply must show up as some combination of more real output, a higher price level, or slower velocity. If velocity is constant, more $M$ directly means more $P \times Q$.

The equation helps explain two apparent contradictions in recent history.

Why didn't the 2008–2014 QE produce massive inflation? Because velocity fell sharply at the same time. Banks accumulated excess reserves rather than lending. Households saved rather than spent. The money supply grew, but each dollar circulated more slowly. $M$ rose, $V$ fell, and the product didn't change as dramatically as $M$ alone would suggest.

Why did the 2020–2022 monetary expansion produce substantial inflation? The supply side was disrupted simultaneously — COVID reduced the productive capacity of the economy. With $Q$ constrained by supply, a large rise in $M \times V$ had to show up in $P$. The stimulus also reached households with high marginal propensities to spend, which maintained velocity. The money supply grew *and* kept circulating.

## Bank Supervision and Regulation

Beyond monetary policy, the Fed is one of several agencies that supervises banks. The two roles are interconnected because monetary policy transmits through banks — if banks are undercapitalized or unwilling to lend, rate cuts don't stimulate the economy.

**Capital requirements.** Banks must hold a minimum amount of equity capital relative to their risk-weighted assets. Capital is the buffer that absorbs losses before depositors or the government are hit. Post-2008 reforms (Basel III, Dodd-Frank) substantially raised capital requirements. U.S. large banks now hold more capital than they did pre-crisis, which is why the banking system was broadly resilient during 2020.

**Liquidity requirements.** Banks must hold enough liquid assets (mostly short-term government securities) to cover potential outflows during a stress scenario. The Liquidity Coverage Ratio is a post-crisis requirement.

**Stress tests.** Annual exercises in which large banks model their losses under hypothetical economic shocks — a severe recession, a spike in unemployment, a collapse in housing prices. Failure triggers restrictions on dividends and share buybacks.

**Resolution authority.** Mechanisms for winding down a failing bank without the disorderly collapse that made Lehman Brothers' failure so catastrophic in 2008. The Fed (and FDIC) gained expanded resolution authority under Dodd-Frank.

**Lender of last resort.** In a panic, the Fed lends to solvent but illiquid banks. The principle, articulated by Walter Bagehot in 1873: lend freely, at high rates, against good collateral. In 2008 and 2020, the Fed extended this to non-bank financial institutions as well, reflecting how much of the financial system now operates outside traditional banks.

## Fed Independence: Why It Matters

Return to a structural point. The Fed's independence from short-run political pressure is not incidental; it's load-bearing.

The problem without independence: fighting inflation is politically costly — it requires raising rates, which slows the economy, which raises unemployment, which angers voters. A politically controlled central bank would face constant pressure to keep rates low and the economy running hot, regardless of inflation. The 1970s Fed, more sensitive to political pressure than it should have been, produced exactly this: rates stayed too low for too long, inflation expectations became embedded, and dismantling them required the Volcker recession.

The problem of too much independence: an unaccountable technocracy making trillion-dollar decisions with enormous distributional consequences. QE programs enriched holders of assets. Rate hikes benefit holders of bonds and savings while hurting borrowers and workers. These are distributional choices that arguably deserve more democratic oversight than they currently receive.

The current balance — independent in operational decisions, but with a congressionally-set mandate, presidentially-appointed governors, and regular public testimony — is a reasonable but imperfect institutional design. Most economists favor the current level of independence. Most also acknowledge it's under more political pressure than it was a decade ago, which is a risk worth tracking.

## From December 2008 Forward

The chapter opened with the Fed hitting zero in December 2008. What happened next is the story of central banking in the modern era: a decade of unconventional policy, a new operating framework, a massive COVID response, and then the 2022 inflation — the worst since the early 1980s — that required the fastest rate-hiking cycle in decades.

The Fed raised rates from near zero to over 5 percent in 2022–2023. Inflation came down from 9 percent to around 3 percent. The soft landing — inflation reducing without a recession — was not fully predicted by standard models. Whether it was the policy, luck, supply normalization, or some combination is still being debated.

What it illustrates: monetary policy is the primary stabilization tool in modern macroeconomics, and it works, but not precisely and not immediately. The Fed operates with imperfect models, incomplete data, and a 12–18 month lag between its decisions and their effects. Every interest-rate decision is a bet on where the economy will be in a year and a half. Most of the time, the Fed gets it roughly right. When it doesn't, the consequences — the 1970s inflation, the 2008 crisis, the 2021–2022 inflation surge — are large enough to feel for decades.

**Exercise 1 — Trace expansionary monetary policy.** Tell an LLM the Fed lowers the federal funds rate by 0.5 percentage points. Walk through the chain: how does this change short-term rates → long-term rates → borrowing decisions → investment → AD → real GDP and inflation? Push for specifics at each step.

**Exercise 2 — Diagnose a ZLB situation.** Tell an LLM the U.S. is in a recession with the federal funds rate already at zero and inflation below target. Ask: what tools does the Fed have? What are the costs of each? What can fiscal policy do that monetary cannot?

**Exercise 3 — Read a balance sheet expansion.** Show an LLM the Fed's balance sheet history: ~$900B pre-2008 → ~$4.5T by 2014 → ~$9T at 2022 peak → declining. Ask it to explain what each phase represents. The exercise tests whether the LLM understands the relationship between QE, financial conditions, and the underlying economic situation.

**Exercise 4 — Argue for and against Fed independence.** Ask an LLM to argue for greater democratic oversight of monetary policy and for stronger Fed independence. Evaluate which case is stronger, and what specific mechanisms would shift the balance.

**Exercise 5 — Apply the equation of exchange.** Tell an LLM: "The U.S. money supply M2 grew 25% from 2020 to 2022. Velocity of M2 fell over the same period. Real GDP grew about 5%. What does the equation of exchange predict about inflation?" Push for the calculation: % change in P ≈ % change in M + % change in V − % change in Q.
