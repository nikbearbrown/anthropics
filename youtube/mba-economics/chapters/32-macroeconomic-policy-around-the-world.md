# Chapter 32 — Macroeconomic Policy Around the World

*The same problems, different institutional answers — what we can learn from how other countries do macro.*

In 1960, South Korea had a GDP per capita of about $158 — comparable to Ghana, Burma, and the Philippines. By 2024, South Korean GDP per capita exceeded $35,000. Ghana's was about $2,300. The Philippines' was about $4,000. Burma (Myanmar) was below $1,500.

Sixty years. One country transformed. Three did not.

What changed for Korea was not luck, natural resources, or geography. Korea in 1960 was poor, war-destroyed, resource-scarce, and politically unstable. What changed was a sustained policy framework over four decades: investment in education, investment in physical capital, integration with world markets, market-oriented reforms sequenced with industrial policy. The pattern is also visible in Taiwan, Singapore, Hong Kong — the "East Asian Tigers" — in Japan's earlier postwar transformation, and in China from 1980 onward. Each chose policies that otherwise similar countries did not. The differences in choices produced differences in outcomes measured in tens of thousands of dollars per person.

This chapter looks at macroeconomic policy across countries. The questions that occupied the previous dozen chapters — how to manage inflation, how to respond to recessions, how to raise long-run productivity — have different answers depending on where a country sits on the income spectrum, what its institutions look like, and what its constraints are. "The right macroeconomic policy" is not universal. It is contextual.

## Income Classifications

The World Bank classifies countries into four groups by Gross National Income per capita (GNI — similar to GDP per capita with some adjustments for income earned abroad):

**High-income** (above roughly $13,845): United States, Germany, Japan, United Kingdom, South Korea, Singapore, Australia, most of Western Europe.

**Upper-middle-income** (~$4,466 to $13,845): China, Brazil, Mexico, Russia, Turkey, Argentina.

**Lower-middle-income** (~$1,136 to $4,466): India, Egypt, Indonesia, Vietnam, Nigeria.

**Low-income** (below ~$1,136): roughly 30 countries, mostly in Sub-Saharan Africa plus parts of South Asia.

These categories are rough. Within each, enormous variation exists. The high-income group includes the U.S. at around $80,000 per capita and Greece at $25,000 — both high-income, but the macroeconomic challenges are very different. The low-income group includes countries with fundamentally different situations.

What the classifications do is sort countries into different regimes of macroeconomic challenge. High-income countries spend their policy energy managing the business cycle, controlling inflation, and maintaining productive capacity. Low-income countries spend theirs trying to create the conditions for sustained growth in the first place. The tools that work for one situation often don't transfer directly to the other.

## What Makes Countries' Situations Different

Several factors shape what macroeconomic policy is feasible and likely to work in each country.

**Geography.** Landlocked countries face higher trade costs and trade less. Tropical climates carry disease burdens — malaria, schistosomiasis, soil degradation — that extract productivity. Coastal access matters for manufacturing export development. Resource abundance is ambiguous: oil and mineral wealth creates Dutch Disease pressures (appreciation of the exchange rate that crowds out tradable manufacturing) and corruption incentives. The "resource curse" is not universal but is well-documented.

**Demographics.** Rapidly growing young populations need to generate massive employment; aging populations face fiscal pressure on pensions and healthcare. The demographic dividend — when a country transitions from high to lower fertility, temporarily creating a large working-age cohort relative to dependents — was part of the East Asian growth story. Countries that haven't yet completed that transition face different challenges than those that have.

**Institutions.** Property rights, rule of law, contract enforcement, financial-system depth, anti-corruption norms, regulatory quality — these determine whether physical and human capital can produce growth or get extracted before it compounds. The World Bank's Governance Indicators consistently correlate with long-run growth; causality runs both ways (better governance enables growth, and growth builds capacity for governance), but the correlation is strong.

**Industry structure.** Countries with diversified industrial bases absorb external shocks better than those dependent on a single commodity. When oil prices fell in 2014–2016, Saudi Arabia — with a narrow commodity-dependent base — was hit far harder than Germany, whose industrial structure spans many sectors.

**Openness.** Open economies access global markets and capital but face external shocks. Closed economies lose the gains from trade and access to technology diffusion through foreign investment and import competition. The evidence overwhelmingly favors openness in the long run; the debate is about sequencing and management of the transition.

## The Growth Policy Menu by Income Level

There is no universal development recipe. But there are patterns in what has worked at different income levels.

**Low-income countries** typically have the most to gain from:

- Basic infrastructure (roads, electricity, clean water) that raises productivity for the whole economy.
- Expanding primary and secondary education.
- Basic public health (reducing child mortality, managing infectious disease).
- Strengthening property rights and rule of law enough to allow productive investment.
- Reducing corruption that diverts public investment into private extraction.

The challenge is deep: these policies require functioning institutions to implement, and weak institutions are often the core problem. Countries that have managed to build basic institutional capacity — Botswana from the 1960s, Rwanda from the 1990s, Ethiopia in the 2000s and 2010s — have made dramatic progress. Many others have tried similar policies with weaker results because the institutional capacity to implement and sustain them was insufficient.

**Middle-income countries** face a different challenge: the growth that worked to get them out of poverty stops working at higher incomes. Cheap-labor manufacturing advantages exhaust themselves as wages rise. Moving up the value chain into more sophisticated production requires capabilities that low-income-stage policies don't build. This is the **middle-income trap** — the tendency for countries to stall around $5,000–$15,000 per capita.

The successful middle-income transitions combined:

- Sustained high savings and investment rates (Korea reached savings rates above 30 percent of GDP in the 1970s).
- Investment in higher education and technical skills, not just primary schooling.
- Export-oriented industrial policy that forced domestic producers to compete internationally.
- Selective integration with global supply chains.
- Public investment in research, applied technology, and enabling infrastructure.
- Gradual market-oriented reform while maintaining state capacity.

**High-income countries** have a fundamentally different policy agenda. Growth rates are lower (1–3 percent rather than 5–10 percent). The constraint is no longer building basic capacity but maintaining and improving productivity at the frontier — where new knowledge must be created rather than borrowed. The challenges include:

- Managing the business cycle without amplifying it.
- Maintaining investment in R&D, education, and infrastructure.
- Adapting to demographic aging (immigration policy becomes a growth tool).
- Managing financial-system stability (high-income financial markets are more complex and fragile).
- Supporting workers displaced by trade competition and automation.
- Distributional questions — inequality within high-income countries has risen, creating political pressures that affect macroeconomic policy.

## Unemployment, Inflation, and Trade Across Countries

Each major macroeconomic indicator looks different in different country contexts, and comparing across contexts without accounting for these differences misleads.

**Unemployment.** Official unemployment rates in high-income countries typically range from 3 to 10 percent, representing people who are actively looking for work in formal labor markets. Many low-income countries have official unemployment below 5 percent — but this is partly because subsistence agriculture and informal activity absorb workers who, in a high-income country, would be counted as unemployed or underemployed. The relevant measure in a low-income country is often not unemployment but *underemployment*: people doing some work, but at very low productivity. Sub-Saharan African official unemployment can look surprisingly low while GDP per capita remains extremely low, because the workers are employed — just not productively.

**Inflation.** High-income countries with credible central banks and stable institutions typically see inflation in the 0–5 percent range. The Fed, the ECB, and the Bank of Japan have maintained that range for decades (with the notable post-COVID spike). Many emerging markets have struggled with chronic higher inflation — Argentina has experienced inflation above 50 percent in multiple years, Turkey above 80 percent in 2022. The institutional capacity to maintain monetary stability is one of the key dividers between high-income and lower-income macroeconomic experiences. Hyperinflation (Zimbabwe, Venezuela, Yemen) is almost always a story about institutional failure, not just policy error.

**Trade balances.** Some high-income economies run persistent surpluses — Germany and Japan have run current account surpluses for decades, reflecting structural high savings and export-oriented industrial sectors. Others run persistent deficits — the U.S. and U.K. have run chronic deficits reflecting high consumption relative to saving. Neither is automatically good or bad. The trade balance is the mirror image of the savings-investment gap; a country that invests more than it saves must borrow from abroad, which shows up as a trade deficit. Some deficits reflect productive capital investment; others reflect consumption in excess of income. The direction alone doesn't tell you the story.

## Market-Oriented Reforms: The Mixed Record

The past four decades have seen many countries implement market-oriented reforms — privatization, deregulation, trade liberalization, fiscal discipline. The results have been mixed enough that a serious analysis can't just say "markets good" or "markets bad."

**Successes.** China since 1980 is the largest economic transformation in human history — 800 million people lifted out of extreme poverty through a combination of market liberalization and state-led development. India's 1991 reform package, which ended the License Raj, produced a sustained increase in growth. Eastern European countries that implemented market transitions in the 1990s are now mostly high-income. Many Latin American countries successfully conquered chronic inflation in the 1990s through reform packages.

**Failures and partial failures.** Russia's rapid transition in the 1990s produced enormous corruption, inequality, and economic collapse before eventual stabilization. Some African reform packages didn't produce the expected growth because the institutional preconditions weren't in place. Some Latin American reforms produced growth while simultaneously increasing inequality and instability — Argentina's 1990s convertibility regime ended in crisis.

The pattern in the successes: reforms worked when they built on existing institutional capacity, sequenced appropriately (institutions before deregulation, not simultaneously), included safety nets to manage the transition costs, and avoided creating new corruption opportunities. The pattern in the failures: ignored institutional preconditions, sequenced poorly, failed to protect the losers, or were implemented in ways that allowed elite capture of the reform dividends.

There is no universal market-reform template. The right approach depends heavily on the country's specific institutional situation, political economy, and starting conditions.

## What Doesn't Transfer Across Borders

One of the most useful things you can take from a comparative macroeconomics chapter is knowing what doesn't transfer.

The Fed's interest rate decisions and the Bank of Korea's are made in the same theoretical framework but face different transmission mechanisms. The Fed operates in a deep, liquid financial market; its rate changes propagate broadly. The Bank of Korea operates in a market that's sophisticated but smaller and more exposed to capital flows — a rate change that works in the U.S. can trigger capital flight or currency volatility in Korea.

The German fiscal framework — a constitutionally mandated debt brake, strict rules on deficits — made sense for Germany in the 2010s: a high-income country with strong institutions, low borrowing costs, and no ZLB constraint. The same rule applied to Italy in 2012 was an economic disaster, forcing austerity in the depths of a recession. Same policy, different context, opposite result.

The lesson isn't that economics is different everywhere, or that we can't learn across borders. It's that the institutional context, the financial system depth, the political capacity to sustain policies through economic difficulty — all of these mediate the relationship between policy and outcome. Policy travels better as principles than as templates.

## Korea, Sixty Years On

Return to where the chapter started.

Korea in 1960 was not predestined to succeed. Many economists at the time were more optimistic about the Philippines. Korea had several advantages — social cohesion, land reform that had already reduced inequality, a newly educated population — and several disadvantages — no natural resources, a devastated economy, political instability. What changed things was a set of decisions about how to organize the economy, made and sustained over four decades.

The deepest lesson from comparing countries is not "do what Korea did." Korea's specific context — the Cold War, U.S. support, its particular industrial structure — can't be exactly replicated. The lesson is more general: outcomes are the product of choices, and choices are shaped by context. A country that understands its own context — its institutions, its constraints, its comparative advantages, its political capacity to sustain policies through short-run difficulty — is better positioned to make those choices well.

That's what macroeconomic policy around the world looks like in practice: the same concepts applied to very different contexts, with very different results. Understanding the variation is as important as understanding the concepts.

## LLM Exercises

**Exercise 1 — Classify and compare countries.** Pick three countries from different income tiers and ask an LLM to compare them on (a) GDP per capita, (b) unemployment rate, (c) inflation rate, (d) trade balance. Explain why each indicator looks the way it does given the country's situation.

**Exercise 2 — Diagnose a development success.** Pick a country that has successfully transitioned from low-income to middle- or high-income status (Korea, Singapore, Taiwan, Vietnam, Botswana). Ask an LLM to identify the policies and conditions that enabled the transition. Push for specifics rather than generic claims.

**Exercise 3 — Explain a development failure.** Pick a country that has stagnated or regressed economically over decades (Argentina, Venezuela, Zimbabwe, parts of Sub-Saharan Africa). Ask an LLM to diagnose the structural, institutional, and policy factors involved. Where do reasonable economists disagree about the causes?

**Exercise 4 — Apply the middle-income trap.** Tell an LLM about a hypothetical country at $8,000 GDP per capita with growth slowing from 7% to 3% per year. Ask: what policies could move it back to high growth? What's the empirical evidence on which approaches have worked?

**Exercise 5 — Evaluate a market-reform package.** Tell an LLM about a hypothetical country considering reform: privatize state-owned enterprises, liberalize trade, deregulate financial services, reduce fiscal deficit. Ask: in what order should these be done? What sequence produces the best outcome? Push the LLM to engage with the empirical record on reform sequencing.
