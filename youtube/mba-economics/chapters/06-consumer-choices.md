# Chapter 6 — Consumer Choices

*Where the demand curve actually comes from — and where the rational consumer model breaks.*

In the middle of the 2008–2009 financial crisis, when consumer spending across the United States cratered and family budgets tightened in ways economists were still trying to track in real time, one category of spending went *up*.

Not necessities. Not health care. Not housing.

Lipstick.

The "lipstick effect" had been observed in earlier downturns, but the 2008 data made it specific: an 18 percent uptick in U.S. cosmetics spending during a year when overall discretionary spending fell sharply. Estée Lauder's Leonard Lauder had been talking about the pattern since the 2001 recession. Why did people whose disposable income was shrinking buy *more* of a small luxury?

The standard demand curve from Chapter 3 would not have predicted this. The standard curve treats consumers as a black box that responds to prices in a downward-sloping way. To explain the lipstick effect, you have to open the box. You have to ask *how* people decide what to buy.

This chapter opens the box. We start with the textbook model — utility, marginal utility, the rule a rational consumer is supposed to follow — and use it to derive the demand curve from first principles. We then apply the same machinery to choices over time: saving, borrowing, investing in your own skills. Then, because the model is too clean to be the whole truth, we turn to behavioral economics — the systematic ways real consumers depart from the textbook prediction, and what those departures reveal.

By the end you should see why the demand curve slopes the way it does, why people save less than the model predicts, and why a 5-percent-off coupon often outperforms a 5-percent price drop.

## The Textbook Consumer

Start with the rule.

A consumer has a fixed income, faces fixed prices, and gets some satisfaction — call it **utility** — from each good she consumes. Economists use "utility" as a placeholder for whatever people actually care about; it is not a quantity we can measure directly, but the assumption that people prefer more of it to less is doing real work in the model.

The first slice of pizza brings a lot of utility. The second, somewhat less. The fifth, much less. This is the diminishing marginal utility from Chapter 2, but now it has a precise job: it tells us how the consumer's willingness to pay changes as she accumulates more of any one thing.

The consumer's job is to allocate her budget to maximize total utility, given prices. The condition that solves this problem is called the **equimarginal rule**: at the optimum, the consumer buys so that the marginal utility *per dollar* is equal across everything she's buying.

In symbols: $\frac{MU_1}{P_1} = \frac{MU_2}{P_2} = \ldots$ for every pair of goods consumed.

Why does this rule produce the optimum? Suppose it doesn't hold — say the marginal utility per dollar of pizza is higher than that of beer. Then taking a dollar away from beer and spending it on pizza produces a gain: that dollar, applied to pizza, delivers more utility than it did applied to beer. The consumer keeps reallocating until the per-dollar marginal utilities are equal. At that point, no further rearrangement helps.

This is also where the demand curve comes from. When the price of a good rises, $MU/P$ for that good falls — same marginal utility, divided by a larger price. To restore the equimarginal balance, the consumer buys less of the good. As she does, its marginal utility climbs back up the diminishing-returns curve, restoring the ratio. Higher price, lower quantity demanded. The downward-sloping demand curve, derived from a rule about how rational consumers allocate.

I want to be clear about what this model is and isn't. It is not a description of how people consciously calculate. No one runs these ratios in their head at the grocery store. The model's claim is more modest: that the *pattern* of choices people make — across goods, across prices, across budgets — looks like what you'd predict if they were running the calculation. It's the same kind of claim we made about marginal analysis in Chapter 2. Useful precisely because it produces testable predictions. Limited precisely because real behavior is messier.

## Two Things a Price Change Does

When a price changes, it does two distinct things to a consumer's choice, and economists have found it productive to keep them separate.

The first is the **substitution effect**. When pizza gets more expensive relative to beer, consumers switch toward beer — not because they're richer or poorer, but because the relative prices have shifted. This effect is purely about the ratio of prices. It always points away from the good that got more expensive, toward its substitutes.

The second is the **income effect**. When pizza gets more expensive, a fixed budget no longer goes as far. The consumer is effectively poorer — her real income has fallen — even if nothing happened to her paycheck. For most goods, being effectively poorer means buying less. These are called **normal goods**: higher effective income, more consumption; lower effective income, less.

But there is a category of goods — **inferior goods** — where the income effect runs the other way. Ramen noodles. Generic-brand cereal. Long bus rides instead of Uber. When people's real incomes fall, they sometimes buy *more* of these because they can no longer afford what they'd eat or do instead.

<!-- → [CHART: Substitution and income effects — budget constraint diagram with original budget line, new budget line after price increase, and a compensated budget line (Hicksian decomposition) showing the substitution effect as the movement along the original indifference curve, and the income effect as the shift to the new budget line. Label the two movements separately. Caption: "A price increase does two things: it changes relative prices (substitution effect, always away from the now-costlier good) and it reduces real income (income effect, direction depends on whether the good is normal or inferior)."] -->

For most goods, both effects work in the same direction: price up, quantity down. The demand curve slopes downward. For inferior goods, the effects work in opposite directions: the substitution effect pushes down, the income effect pushes up. In principle, if the good is *extreme* enough — if it takes up a huge share of a poor household's budget, so the income effect overwhelms the substitution effect — you could get an upward-sloping demand curve. These are called **Giffen goods**. The empirical evidence for them in modern economies is thin. The point is not that Giffen goods are common; it is that the downward slope of demand is a very strong empirical regularity, not a logical inevitability.

Giffen goods are theoretically possible but empirically rare."] -->

## Choices Across Time

The same utility-maximization machinery works when the two "goods" on the axes are not pizza and beer but *consumption now* and *consumption later*.

This is **intertemporal choice**, and it covers more ground than it first appears: saving for retirement, taking out student loans, deciding whether to get additional education, deciding whether to replace an aging appliance now or wait. In every case, the question is whether to give up something present in exchange for something future, or vice versa.

The budget constraint here has a slope determined by the interest rate. If you save $1 today at 5 percent, you can consume $1.05 next year. The opportunity cost of consuming $1 today is $1.05 next year. The slope of the intertemporal budget constraint is $-(1 + r)$ — meaning each dollar of present consumption given up buys $(1 + r)$ dollars of future consumption.

<!-- → [CHART: Intertemporal budget constraint — two axes: consumption now (horizontal) and consumption later (vertical). Budget line with slope −(1+r). Show effect of a rise in r: the line pivots, making the vertical intercept higher (more future consumption available per dollar of current saving). Caption: "A higher interest rate steepens the intertemporal budget constraint — saving becomes more valuable, and the rational consumer should save more. Whether real consumers actually respond this way is the question the next section raises."] -->

A higher interest rate makes the budget line steeper: each dollar saved today produces more consumption later. The model predicts that higher interest rates increase saving. 

The model also predicts something else, more uncomfortable: that Americans, on average, should save more than they do. The U.S. personal saving rate has hovered around 4 to 8 percent of disposable income for decades, with brief spikes during recessions and a notable surge during the early pandemic. Comparable figures in Germany, Japan, and France run meaningfully higher. The intertemporal model, presented with these numbers, would predict that Americans strongly prefer present consumption to future consumption — an unusually steep discount on the future. Maybe. Or maybe something else is going on.

That "something else" is what the rest of the chapter is about.

## Where the Model Breaks

Through the 1970s and 1980s, two psychologists — Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky — ran experiment after experiment showing that people's actual choices reliably violate the predictions of utility maximization. Not randomly. *Systematically*. Their work became the foundation of **behavioral economics**, eventually recognized with a Nobel for Kahneman in 2002. The core finding: real behavior departs from the standard model in predictable directions. Not by accident. By mechanism.

Three mechanisms matter most for understanding what the standard model misses.

**Loss aversion.** Losses hurt roughly twice as much as equivalent gains feel good. Losing $100 produces about twice the distress that gaining $100 produces satisfaction. The standard model says a $100 swing in either direction is worth the same magnitude; the data say it isn't.

Loss aversion explains a great deal. It explains why people hold losing investments too long — selling locks in the loss, and the pain of that is disproportionate to its actual significance. It explains why "$5 off if you order today" frequently outperforms "pay $5 more if you wait" even though the math is identical: the second framing invokes a loss, and loss aversion amplifies its weight.

Loss frames consistently outperform gain frames — not because consumers are irrational, but because their utility function is kinked at the reference point."] -->

And loss aversion offers a way into the lipstick puzzle. In a recession, large status purchases — new car, vacation, renovation — become impossible. The *loss* of the ability to make them is real and painful. Small remaining indulgences — an expensive lipstick, a quality coffee — become more important precisely because they are still within reach. Clinging harder to what's affordable partially offsets, psychologically, the loss of what isn't.

**Present bias.** People weight near-term costs and benefits far more heavily than future ones — and they do so in a time-inconsistent way. Asked today whether to exercise tomorrow, you say yes. Asked tomorrow whether to exercise today, you say no. Same question, different answer depending on when "now" is.

The standard intertemporal model assumes a constant discount rate: you value a dollar in one year versus now at the same ratio you value a dollar in 366 days versus 365 days. Real people don't. The discount on anything past "right now" is enormous; the difference between 365 days from now and 366 days from now is nearly negligible. This produces time-inconsistent preferences — the "future self" makes plans the "present self" doesn't honor.

<!-- → [CHART: Constant discounting vs. present bias (hyperbolic discounting) — line chart with x-axis: time from now (today, 1 week, 1 month, 6 months, 1 year, 5 years) and y-axis: perceived value of $100 received at that future time. Two curves: (1) exponential discounting (straight decay, constant slope on log scale) — the textbook model; (2) hyperbolic discounting (steep drop from today to next week, then much flatter). The two curves diverge sharply in the near term. Caption: "Present bias is the gap between the two curves. The textbook model says today vs. next week looks the same as year 4 vs. year 5. Real people put an enormous premium on 'right now' and discount everything past it at roughly the same rate."] -->

Present bias explains chronic undersaving, chronic overindulgence, and the gap between what people say they will do and what they actually do. It also explains why automatic enrollment in 401(k) retirement plans dramatically increases participation. When the default is "you're enrolled unless you opt out," the decision isn't in front of the present self. The same workers, asked to actively sign up, often defer indefinitely — present bias blocks action at every "later" moment they plan to act.

**Framing effects.** The same options, presented differently, produce different choices. A medical treatment described as having a "90 percent survival rate" is chosen more often than the identical treatment described as a "10 percent mortality rate." A price labeled "regular $100, today $80" produces more sales than a price simply reading "$80." The reference point — what the consumer is comparing against — shapes the choice as much as the options themselves.

<!-- → [TABLE: Framing effects in consumer and policy contexts — three columns: Context | Standard framing | Behavioral framing. Rows: (1) Retirement savings: "Enroll in 401(k)" → "You are enrolled; opt out to stop saving." (2) Energy efficiency: "This appliance uses X kWh per year" → "This appliance costs you $Y more than the efficient model over 5 years." (3) Insurance: "Add $10/month coverage" → "Without coverage, you risk paying $Z out of pocket." Caption: "In each case, the behavioral framing converts an abstract gain into a salient loss. Same information, different decision."] -->

Standard economics says choices depend on the options available. Behavioral economics says choices depend on the options *and* on how they are framed relative to a reference point. Marketers, lawyers, and doctors have known the second thing intuitively for a long time. The contribution of Kahneman and Tversky was to show that the departures are not random — they are systematic enough to model, predict, and correct.

## What the Two Models Are Actually Doing

I want to be careful here, because the standard reading of behavioral economics — "the rational model is wrong, behavioral economics is right" — misses the point.

The rational model is not wrong. It is incomplete. It is a useful first approximation that breaks in predictable ways. Those two things together are more valuable than either alone.

The standard model still gets the direction of price effects right in most markets. Higher price, lower quantity demanded — loss aversion and present bias don't reverse that. What behavioral economics adds is a precise account of *where* the magnitudes go wrong, and *why*. Loss aversion tells you that a price increase will feel worse than an equivalent price decrease feels good — so a firm that raises prices will lose customers faster than it gains them by cutting by the same amount. Present bias tells you that a delayed cost (a higher electricity bill three months from now) discounts heavily relative to an immediate benefit (comfort today) — so energy efficiency standards work better as mandates than as price signals. Framing effects tell you that the default choice is stickier than any other — so retirement enrollment works better as opt-out than opt-in, independent of what the optimal savings rate actually is.

The practical read: when you see a pricing decision, a savings program, or a behavior-change campaign, the textbook model tells you the baseline prediction. The behavioral patterns tell you where the baseline will be wrong, and in which direction. A policy designer who knows only the standard model will design an intervention that fails. A policy designer who knows only the behavioral departures will have no framework for predicting which interventions are likely to work. You need both.

## Back to the Lipstick

Now we can answer the question.

The standard demand curve says: income falls, quantity demanded of normal goods falls. Lipstick, by that measure, should have dropped in 2008. It didn't.

What the standard model missed is that utility in a recession is not just a function of what you buy. It is a function of what you buy *compared to what you've lost*. The reference point shifted when the recession hit — big purchases became impossible, and the comparison set collapsed. Against that backdrop, an affordable luxury signals something it didn't need to signal in good times: that some standard of living persists, that some small indulgence is still within reach.

Loss aversion amplifies the effect. The pain of losing the ability to take a vacation is real. A small gain — a purchase that still feels like a treat — offers partial relief. The substitution isn't just economic (buy something cheaper instead of something expensive). It's psychological: buy something that *feels like* the self you were before the crisis.

That's a story the demand curve from Chapter 3 can't tell. It requires knowing where the curve *comes from* — which is what this chapter was about.

## LLM Exercises

**Exercise 1 — Apply the equimarginal rule.** Tell an LLM your weekly entertainment budget and the prices of three things you spend it on (streaming, books, concert tickets, whatever). Ask it to walk through the equimarginal rule and identify the optimal allocation, given your subjective valuation of each. Then ask: where in this exercise does the LLM rely on numbers you supply, and where does it have to assume a utility function? Notice how much the answer depends on inputs you can't observe directly.

**Exercise 2 — Decompose a price change.** Tell an LLM about a recent price increase you noticed in something you regularly buy. Ask it to decompose your behavioral response into a substitution effect and an income effect. Push: which effect was larger in your case, and how can the LLM tell? The exercise teaches you to feel the two effects working separately.

**Exercise 3 — Find your present bias.** Describe to an LLM a decision you've put off (saving more, exercising, applying for a job). Ask it to explain the decision in terms of present bias — what's the "now self" doing that the "later self" disagrees with? Then ask the LLM to suggest one structural change (an automatic enrollment, a commitment device) that would help. Evaluate whether the suggestion is genuinely structural or just willpower advice.

**Exercise 4 — Loss aversion in pricing.** Tell an LLM you're a marketer choosing between two equivalent offers: "Save $20 if you order today" or "Pay $20 more if you wait." Ask which framing will produce more orders and why, using loss aversion. A good answer explains *why* the second framing — invoking a loss — is psychologically more powerful, even though the math is identical.

**Exercise 5 — Behavioral nudge design.** Tell an LLM you want to design a policy that helps people save more for retirement, using only the behavioral findings from this chapter. No mandates, no taxes, no education campaigns. The policy must be a structural change to defaults, framings, or timing of decisions. Evaluate the LLM's proposal: does it match the empirical evidence (e.g., automatic enrollment in 401(k) plans dramatically increases participation)? Or does it default to "tell people to save more"?
