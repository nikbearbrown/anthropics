# Chapter 18 — Public Economy

*How political decisions get made — and why the rules of politics produce predictable distortions even when everyone is acting reasonably.*

In September 2009, the U.S. government imposed a tariff on tires imported from China. It started at 35 percent, fell to 30 percent in year two, and 25 percent in year three. The stated goal: protect American tire manufacturing jobs.

The tariff worked, in a narrow sense. Approximately 1,200 U.S. tire manufacturing jobs were preserved.

The tariff also cost American consumers — through higher tire prices — about $900,000 per preserved job, per year.

Twelve hundred jobs. About a billion dollars in total consumer cost. A cost-per-job that, if those jobs paid $50,000 annually, exceeded the wage by a factor of eighteen. Every economist who ran the numbers agreed: this was a terrible trade. A transfer from millions of consumers, each paying a small amount more per tire, to a tiny number of workers and shareholders with an enormous stake in the outcome.

And it passed anyway. It was renewed. It produced no major political backlash.

The question this chapter asks is: why does this happen? Not just once, but repeatedly, predictably, across thousands of policies in every democracy? The market models from Chapters 3 through 17 assume policy outcomes at least approximate what's efficient — or that we can identify the optimal policy and pursue it. Real political systems don't reliably produce that. They produce *political* outcomes, and the structural features of how political decisions get made predict systematic departures from what any textbook welfare analysis would recommend.

This is **public choice economics**, founded primarily by James Buchanan and Gordon Tullock in the 1960s. Its core insight is simple: apply the same self-interest assumption economists use for consumers and firms to voters, politicians, and bureaucrats. If you do, you can predict not just that bad policies sometimes pass, but *which* bad policies and *why*.

## Rational Ignorance: Why Voters Don't Study

Start with a fact most people find uncomfortable. The average voter knows surprisingly little about candidates, issues, or policies on the ballot.

This is not a moral failing. It is the predicted outcome of a straightforward benefit-cost calculation.

Consider a consumer deciding which car to buy. She will use the car for years. The right choice makes her daily life better; the wrong one frustrates her constantly. The benefit of becoming well-informed is large and personal. The cost — research, test drives, reading reviews — is moderate. Benefit-to-cost ratio: high. Consumers are usually reasonably informed about major purchases.

Now consider a voter in a presidential election. Her single vote will, almost certainly, not change the outcome. The probability that any individual ballot decides an election is effectively zero in a large electorate. The personal benefit of being well-informed — of knowing every candidate's position, reading the actual budget documents, understanding the macroeconomic implications of competing tax plans — is therefore essentially zero. The cost of that information is substantial.

Rational benefit-cost analysis says: don't bother. Vote on rough impressions, party identification, what your friends say, how the candidate came across in a debate. **Rational ignorance** is the formal name for this outcome — voters are *rationally* uninformed because the cost of becoming informed exceeds the personal benefit of doing so.

This has consequences. Politicians who present simple, emotionally resonant messages often beat politicians with better but more complex policies. Media that entertains beats media that informs. Single-issue voters — who know one thing deeply — dominate over breadth-of-issue voters who know many things shallowly. And a small number of highly motivated, well-organized interest groups can shape policy outcomes that millions of inattentive citizens would oppose if they knew the details.

## Concentrated Benefits, Dispersed Costs

The tire tariff illustrates the core structural dynamic.

The tariff produces concentrated benefits: 1,200 workers keep their jobs; a small number of shareholders hold a more profitable company. Those beneficiaries know exactly what's at stake, organize accordingly, lobby, contribute to political campaigns, show up at hearings. They are a coherent, motivated political force.

The tariff also produces dispersed costs: two hundred million American tire-buying consumers each pay a few extra dollars per year. None of them organize. None of them lobby. None of them threaten politicians with electoral defeat over tire prices. The dollar amount per consumer is too small to motivate action — even though the aggregate cost across all consumers is nearly a billion dollars.

This is the arithmetic of **concentrated benefits, dispersed costs**. The organized few beat the unorganized many — even when the unorganized many would collectively benefit far more from opposing the policy.

The pattern recurs across thousands of policies. Sugar import quotas raise the price of every American food product containing sugar, to benefit a few thousand sugar producers. Occupational licensing requirements (for cosmetologists, interior designers, florists, in many states) restrict supply and raise prices for consumers, to benefit incumbent practitioners who lobby the licensing boards. Narrow tax loopholes produce large savings for the industries that lobbied them in while dispersing tiny costs across 160 million tax filers who don't know the loophole exists.

No individual policy is large enough to mobilize the dispersed losers. But the *aggregate* of all such policies — the total of every tariff, every narrow subsidy, every occupational licensing restriction — adds up to substantial deadweight loss distributed across the whole economy.

## Pork-Barrel Spending and Logrolling

Two related mechanisms deepen the pattern.

**Pork-barrel spending** is government spending designed to benefit a specific geographic area or constituency, paid for from general tax revenue. A bridge that benefits one congressional district while 330 million taxpayers share the cost. A research facility in one senator's state. A military base the Pentagon doesn't need but that provides jobs in a swing district. The legislator who secures the project gets concentrated political credit from the district. The cost is diffused into the federal budget, invisible to any individual taxpayer.

**Logrolling** is the legislative practice of trading votes: "I'll support your project if you support mine." Through logrolling, projects that would fail on their own merits assemble majority coalitions. A senator from Iowa votes for a Maine fisheries subsidy she'd otherwise oppose, in exchange for the Maine senator's vote on an Iowa ethanol mandate. Each project is individually wasteful. Together, they build a majority.

<!-- → [TABLE: Pork-barrel and logrolling mechanics — two-column comparison. Column 1: Pork-barrel. Column 2: Logrolling. These practices are not technically illegal. They are structural features of legislative politics. Institutional responses — line-item vetoes, earmark bans, balanced-budget amendments — have had limited and uneven success. The structural incentive is persistent: legislators are rewarded by their constituents for delivering benefits, and they bear essentially none of the aggregate cost.

## The Median Voter Theorem

Now the other side of the coin: when does democracy actually produce policies the majority wants?

The **median voter theorem** says that in a single-issue election between two candidates, both will converge on the position of the median voter — the voter exactly in the middle of the distribution.

The logic: suppose Candidate A is to the left of the median, Candidate B to the right. Voters to A's far left will vote for A regardless; voters to B's far right will vote for B regardless. The election is won or lost by the voters in the middle. Whoever is closer to the median wins them. Both candidates therefore have an incentive to move toward the median. In equilibrium, both candidates announce positions close to what the median voter wants.

<!-- → [CHART: Median voter convergence — horizontal number line representing the voter distribution from left to right. Bell curve showing density of voters. Median voter marked at center. Two candidate positions, A (left of median) and B (right of median), with arrows showing them moving toward the median. In equilibrium, both converge near median. Caption: "Both candidates chase the median because that's where the pivotal voters are. The voter at the extreme left will vote for A regardless; the candidate's energy is better spent converting voters in the middle."] -->

This is a useful baseline prediction. It explains why two-party democracies tend to converge on similar positions across many issues — both parties are chasing the same median voter. It explains why genuinely extreme parties usually lose national elections.

The theorem's limits are real and worth naming. It assumes a single issue on the ballot, two candidates, full voter participation, and complete information. Real elections violate every assumption simultaneously. With multiple issues, the median voter on healthcare may differ from the median voter on immigration — no candidate can optimize for both simultaneously. With low turnout, the operative "median" is the median of those who actually vote, not the full population.

The most interesting application is the primary election. Presidential primaries are dominated by party activists and highly motivated partisans who are systematically more ideologically extreme than the general-election median. The "median voter" in a Democratic primary sits substantially left of the median general-election voter; the "median voter" in a Republican primary sits substantially right. So primaries produce candidates who then must move back toward the center for the general election. The shift looks like strategic insincerity; it is in fact responding to two genuinely different populations at two sequential stages.

## Voting Paradoxes: When No Winner Exists

Sometimes no policy commands a stable majority. The simplest example was identified by the Marquis de Condorcet in 1785.

Three voters, three options (A, B, C):
- Voter 1 prefers A over B over C.
- Voter 2 prefers B over C over A.
- Voter 3 prefers C over A over B.

In pairwise votes: A beats B (voters 1 and 3). B beats C (voters 1 and 2). C beats A (voters 2 and 3). A beats B. B beats C. C beats A.

There is no winner. The outcome of any vote depends entirely on the *order in which comparisons are made*. Whoever controls the agenda — which alternative gets compared to which, and when — controls the outcome. The votes themselves are less decisive than the procedural rules.

Kenneth Arrow's impossibility theorem (1951, Nobel Prize) generalized this result. No voting system can simultaneously satisfy all of a small set of intuitive fairness criteria when there are three or more options. Every voting rule will violate at least one fairness condition somewhere.

<!-- → [TABLE: Arrow's impossibility conditions and their meaning — four-row table. Columns: Condition / Plain-language meaning / Why it seems fair / Which voting systems violate it. Rows: (1) Unanimity (Pareto) — if everyone prefers A to B, A should win, because ignoring unanimous agreement seems absurd, some approval-voting variants can violate in edge cases; (2) Non-dictatorship — no single voter's preferences should always determine the outcome, because one person overriding all others is not democratic, dictatorial rules trivially satisfy but are excluded by assumption; (3) Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives — the ranking of A vs. B should not depend on option C, because introducing a third option shouldn't flip a binary comparison, plurality voting and many ranked systems violate this; (4) Completeness and transitivity — the system should always produce a consistent ranking, because cycling outcomes (A>B>C>A) paralyze collective decision-making, Condorcet voting is vulnerable to this cycle. Caption: "Arrow showed no system satisfies all four. The choice of voting rule is not neutral — it systematically favors some outcomes over others. Procedural design is substantive design."] -->

The implication is not that democracy is impossible. Most decisions are made without hitting the cycling problem, because many electorates have single-peaked preferences (preferences that peak at one point on a spectrum) that produce clean majorities. But the impossibility result tells us something important: there is no *neutral* aggregation mechanism. The agenda-setter, the rule-maker, and the person who designs the procedure all have power that doesn't appear in any individual ballot.

## Why Politics Looks the Way It Does

Pull the threads together.

Public choice theory predicts that rational ignorance will make most voters poorly informed on policy details. Concentrated-benefit / dispersed-cost dynamics will repeatedly produce policies that benefit small organized groups at the expense of large unorganized ones. Pork-barrel spending and logrolling will produce coalitions for individually wasteful projects. The median voter theorem will pull two-party systems toward the center on most issues. Voting paradoxes and agenda control will shape outcomes in ways that voter preferences alone cannot fully explain.

These are structural predictions, not moral claims. They don't say democracy is bad. They say democracy has a structure, and the structure bends outputs in identifiable directions.

The corrective implications differ by case. Campaign-finance reform, independent regulatory bodies, central bank independence, sunset clauses on tax expenditures, ranked-choice voting — each addresses a different part of the distortion. None eliminates the structural pressures entirely; the incentives keep reproducing the behavior. The reformer's hardest lesson: the political market has failure modes just as the economic market does, and neither "government should always fix market failures" nor "markets should always replace government failures" is structurally complete. The empirical question, always, is which failures are larger in which context, with which available remedies. The honest answer is that it varies, and the trade-offs are real.

The Chinese tire tariff is the whole chapter in miniature. A small organized group with an enormous per-person stake lobbied for a policy that cost a large unorganized group a tiny amount each. The aggregate welfare calculation was clearly negative. The political calculation was clearly positive. The tariff was enacted, and the pattern has repeated thousands of times, in every democracy, ever since.

Rows: (1) Rational ignorance — voters poorly informed on most policy details, cost of info > personal benefit from one vote, voter education has low ROI; simpler messaging matters more; (2) Concentrated benefits / dispersed costs — organized small groups win over disorganized large ones, per-person stakes drive organizing decisions, coalitions of dispersed losers are hard to build proactively; (3) Pork-barrel + logrolling — individually wasteful spending passes in packages, concentrated credit + dispersed cost = rational for each legislator, earmark restrictions + line-item vetoes reduce but don't eliminate; (4) Median voter convergence — two-party systems converge at center on most issues, both candidates chase same pivotal voters, primary elections complicate by producing a different interim median; (5) Voting paradoxes + agenda control — procedural rules shape outcomes as much as voter preferences, no neutral aggregation mechanism exists, agenda-setter power deserves explicit institutional attention. Knowing the patterns is half of designing better institutions."] -->

## LLM Exercises

**Exercise 1 — Identify a concentrated-benefit / dispersed-cost policy.** Pick a current U.S. policy you suspect fits the pattern (a specific subsidy, tariff, occupational licensing requirement, tax loophole). Ask an LLM to identify (a) who benefits and how concentrated they are, (b) who pays and how dispersed they are, (c) the rough magnitudes if estimable. Push for specifics.

**Exercise 2 — Stress-test the median voter theorem.** Ask an LLM under what conditions the median voter theorem fails to predict observed political outcomes. The exercise tests the LLM's grasp of the theorem's assumptions: single-issue, two candidates, full participation, complete information. Ask it to explain why U.S. presidential primaries appear to produce more extreme candidates than the general electorate would prefer.

**Exercise 3 — Design a voting paradox.** Ask an LLM to construct a three-voter, three-option example demonstrating Condorcet's voting paradox. The LLM should produce preferences such that A beats B, B beats C, and C beats A in pairwise majority votes. Verify the math. Then ask: what real-world policy choice has this structure?

**Exercise 4 — Evaluate a campaign-finance reform.** Tell an LLM you're considering proposing a campaign-finance rule (e.g., a public-funding-only system, a strict $50 individual contribution cap, mandatory disclosure of all donations above $200). Ask it to use public-choice logic to predict the effects on (a) policy outcomes, (b) candidate selection, (c) voter information. Push for specific predictions, not generic arguments.

**Exercise 5 — Read a piece of legislation through the lens.** Find a recent piece of significant legislation (the Inflation Reduction Act, the CHIPS Act, an annual omnibus spending bill, your pick). Ask an LLM to identify which provisions look like concentrated-benefit / dispersed-cost transfers, which look like genuine public-goods provision, and which look like pork-barrel spending. Evaluate the LLM's classifications.

**Project:** Policy Brief.
**What you're building this chapter:** the political-economy analysis — why this policy is being proposed (or rejected), and which interest groups are organized for and against it.
**Tool:** **Claude Project** "Policy Brief" — appends a section.

**The Prompt:**

```
Chapter 18 of my Policy Brief project. Prior sections in this Claude
Project. Chapter 18 taught: rational ignorance (voters don't study
because the cost is high and the marginal vote effect is tiny);
concentrated benefits + dispersed costs (small group with $X
million stake organizes; large group with $1 each stake doesn't);
pork-barrel and logrolling; the median-voter theorem (under
specific conditions, two-party democracy converges on the median
voter's preference); voting paradoxes (Arrow's impossibility);
why politics produces the policies it does even when economists
think the policies are bad.

Write the brief's "Political Economy" section in 300–500 words.

1. **Who's organized for the policy?** Name specific groups —
   trade associations, advocacy NGOs, individual companies,
   labor unions, ideological foundations. Note their resources
   (rough budget if knowable) and their organizing strength
   (membership? legal standing? lobbying spending?).

2. **Who's organized against?** Same exercise. Often the
   opposition is more concentrated than the support, because the
   costs of the policy are concentrated on a small group with
   high per-capita stake.

3. **The concentrated-benefits-dispersed-costs read.** Which side
   of the policy fits the concentrated-benefits pattern (and is
   therefore likely to win in the legislative process even if the
   welfare case is on the other side)?

4. **The median-voter test.** Where does the median voter sit on
   this policy? If the policy's substantive case is strong but
   the median voter opposes it, the brief should name how
   advocates would build the political coalition. If the median
   voter supports it but the policy is stuck, the brief should
   name what's blocking it.

End with one sentence on the political-economy reason this
policy is harder (or easier) to enact than the welfare analysis
in earlier chapters would predict. The gap between welfare
analysis and political feasibility is itself a finding the brief
should name.
```

---

**What this produces:** A 300–500 word section on the political economy of the policy — interest groups, organized constituencies, median-voter placement. Most policy briefs duck this; the discipline of naming who wants what is rare.

**How to adapt this prompt:**

Antitrust students: the targeted firm is concentrated against; consumers and competitors are dispersed for. Tariff students: import-competing industries concentrated for; consumers and exporters dispersed against.
- *For ChatGPT / Gemini:* Works as written.
- *For Claude Code:* Not the primary tool here.
- *For a Claude Project:* Append.

**Connection to previous chapters:** Ch 11's regulatory-capture analysis is the close cousin of Ch 18's political-economy analysis. Both ask: who actually gets the policy they want?
