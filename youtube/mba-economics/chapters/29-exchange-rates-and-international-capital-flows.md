# Chapter 29 — Exchange Rates and International Capital Flows

*The price of one currency in terms of another — what determines it, what it does, and the policy choices that constrain it.*

---

In 2014, one euro bought about $1.40. By 2017, it bought about $1.05. By 2024, about $1.10. Over the same decade, the Japanese yen depreciated from roughly ¥80 per dollar to ¥150 per dollar — meaning a dollar bought nearly twice as many yen as it had ten years earlier.

These movements are not noise. They determine who wins and who loses in the global economy. When the dollar strengthens, U.S. exports become more expensive for foreign buyers (Boeing sells fewer jets to European airlines) while imports become cheaper for American consumers (European vacations get more affordable). When it weakens, the effects reverse. The same shift that helps one group hurts another.

Behind the visible currency movements is a market — the **foreign exchange (FX) market** — that is the largest financial market in the world by trading volume. Roughly $7 trillion changes hands daily, dwarfing any stock or bond market. The FX market is where currencies are exchanged by importers, exporters, investors, central banks, hedge funds, and tourists. It operates 24 hours a day across global financial centers.

This chapter is about how exchange rates work: what moves them, what effects they have on the macroeconomy, and the policy choices countries make about how to manage — or not manage — the price of their currency.

---

## What an exchange rate is

An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another.

When we say the dollar-euro rate is 1.10, we mean one dollar buys 1.10 euros, or equivalently, one euro buys $0.91. The rate is bidirectional — you can quote it either way.

**Appreciation** — a currency rises in value relative to another. The dollar appreciates when it buys more euros than before.

**Depreciation** — a currency falls in value relative to another. The dollar depreciates when it buys fewer euros.

A currency can appreciate against one currency and depreciate against another at the same time. A more useful aggregate is the **trade-weighted exchange rate** — an index of a currency's value against a basket of trading partners' currencies, weighted by trade volume. When economists say the dollar "strengthened" in 2022, they typically mean it appreciated against most major currencies simultaneously, reflected in the trade-weighted index.

---

## The foreign exchange market

The FX market exists because every international transaction involves converting one currency to another. The main participants:

**Importers and exporters** generate underlying transaction demand. A U.S. company importing German machinery needs euros. A German automaker selling cars in the U.S. needs to convert dollar revenue back into euros.

**Investors** need currency conversion for cross-border portfolio flows. A U.S. pension fund buying Japanese government bonds needs yen. A European fund buying U.S. Treasuries needs dollars. These capital flows generate far more FX volume than trade flows.

**Central banks** intervene to influence exchange rates, buying or selling their currency in large quantities when they want to lean against market movements.

**Speculators** trade on expected currency movements. By volume, this is the dominant category — most FX trading is not tied to real transactions but to bets about future price movements.

**Tourists and individuals** are small in aggregate but visible in daily life.

The U.S. dollar is involved in about 90 percent of all FX transactions. Most currency pairs — Swiss franc/Japanese yen, for instance — are actually traded by first converting each currency to dollars and then to the other. The dollar's centrality in global finance is both a cause and a consequence of its reserve-currency status.

---

## What determines the exchange rate

Exchange rates are determined by supply and demand in the FX market — the same framework from Chapter 3, applied to currencies as a special good.

**Demand for a currency** comes from foreign entities wanting to buy assets, goods, or services denominated in that currency. To buy U.S. Treasuries, you need dollars. To import American software, you need dollars. Speculators expecting the dollar to appreciate want to hold dollars.

**Supply of a currency** comes from domestic entities wanting to buy foreign assets, goods, or services. To import German cars, a U.S. company sells dollars. To invest in Japanese real estate, a U.S. investor sells dollars to buy yen. Speculators expecting the dollar to weaken sell dollars.

When demand exceeds supply at the current rate, the currency appreciates. When supply exceeds demand, it depreciates.

Several factors reliably shift these curves:

**Interest rate differentials.** When U.S. interest rates rise relative to other countries', global investors want to earn the higher U.S. return. They buy dollars to invest in U.S. assets. Dollar demand rises. The dollar appreciates. This is why the Fed's 2022–2023 rate hikes — the fastest cycle in forty years — produced a substantial dollar appreciation. Foreign investors were selling euros, yen, and pounds to buy dollar-denominated assets offering now-attractive yields.

**Inflation differentials.** Countries with persistently higher inflation than their trading partners see their purchasing power erode. Their currencies tend to depreciate over time to compensate — this is the mechanism behind purchasing power parity, discussed below.

**Growth differentials.** Countries with stronger expected growth attract more investment, supporting their currencies.

**Confidence and political stability.** Investors move money toward countries with stable institutions and credible rule of law. Political turmoil or institutional breakdown typically produces currency depreciation.

**Trade balances.** Persistent surplus countries see rising foreign demand for their currency to pay for exports, supporting appreciation. Persistent deficit countries see the reverse.

**Speculation.** In the short run, currency movements often reflect large investors' positions ahead of expected events — Fed announcements, elections, geopolitical developments. These can produce substantial short-run swings that have little to do with any of the above fundamentals.

## Purchasing power parity

In the long run, exchange rates should adjust so that a basket of goods costs roughly the same in different countries after converting currencies. This is **purchasing power parity (PPP)**.

The logic: if the same good costs significantly more in country A than in country B (after converting at the market exchange rate), arbitrageurs can profit by buying cheap in B and selling dear in A. That flow of goods and money pushes prices and exchange rates toward equality.

The Big Mac Index — published by *The Economist* for several decades — uses a single standardized good to illustrate PPP across countries. If a Big Mac costs $5.50 in the U.S. and ¥450 in Japan, PPP predicts an exchange rate of ¥450 / $5.50 = ¥82 per dollar. If the actual rate is ¥150 per dollar, the yen is undervalued relative to PPP — a Big Mac costs only $3.00 in Japan at the market rate, roughly 45 percent cheaper than in the U.S.

PPP holds well in the long run for tradable goods, where physical arbitrage is possible. It holds poorly for non-tradable goods and services — housing, haircuts, medical care — whose prices reflect local wages and land costs that can't be arbitraged away. And it holds poorly in the short run, when currencies are moved by capital flows, interest differentials, and speculation that have nothing to do with prices.

PPP is most useful as a long-run benchmark for detecting large, persistent mispricing. It is not a reliable short-term predictor of exchange-rate movements.

## Exchange-rate effects on the macroeconomy

A weaker domestic currency changes the macroeconomy through two channels simultaneously.

**The net-export channel.** Cheaper domestic currency means domestic goods become cheaper for foreign buyers — exports rise. Foreign goods become more expensive for domestic buyers — imports fall. Net exports increase. This shifts aggregate demand rightward, an expansionary effect. Domestic output and employment tend to rise.

**The inflation channel.** When imports become more expensive after a depreciation, import prices rise. Imported consumer goods cost more. Imported intermediate inputs (steel, oil, components) cost more, raising domestic production costs. Inflation tends to rise after depreciation.

A stronger domestic currency produces the mirror image: net exports fall (AD shifts left), imported goods become cheaper, inflation moderates.

Central banks are acutely aware of these effects. A central bank trying to reduce inflation may welcome a stronger currency — it produces disinflationary pressure from cheaper imports without requiring as large an interest-rate increase. This is part of why large Fed rate hikes in 2022–23 were tolerated in the global economy: the stronger dollar they produced was partly doing the Fed's anti-inflation work by making imported goods cheaper for Americans.

The complication: a stronger dollar that helps U.S. consumers hurts foreign exporters and strains dollar-denominated debt in emerging markets. U.S. monetary policy has international consequences by design and by accident.

## Exchange-rate regimes

Countries face a fundamental choice about how their currency operates internationally. The options differ in the trade-off between exchange-rate stability and monetary-policy independence.

**Floating exchange rate.** The FX market sets the rate; the central bank does not directly manage it. The U.S., U.K., Japan, Canada, Australia, and the eurozone all have floating rates. Most large advanced economies do.

The advantages: monetary policy can respond to domestic conditions (the Fed can raise rates to fight U.S. inflation regardless of what the ECB is doing); exchange-rate movements automatically adjust for external shocks (a fall in export demand tends to depreciate the currency, restoring competitiveness without requiring deflation). The disadvantages: exchange-rate volatility creates uncertainty for exporters and importers; large speculative swings can disrupt the real economy even when fundamentals don't warrant them.

**Soft peg.** The central bank actively intervenes to keep the currency within a range against another currency or basket, but the range is adjustable. China maintained a soft peg against the dollar for many years — the renminbi appreciated slowly but was never allowed to fluctuate freely.

**Hard peg.** The central bank commits to a fixed rate without bands, often through a currency board that holds foreign reserves matching every unit of domestic currency issued. Hong Kong has maintained a hard peg against the U.S. dollar since 1983. The commitment is credible because it's backed by institutional structure.

The hard peg's advantage is maximum exchange-rate predictability — trade and investment with the anchor country face zero exchange-rate risk. The disadvantage is the complete surrender of independent monetary policy. When the Fed raises rates, Hong Kong's Monetary Authority follows, regardless of Hong Kong's domestic economic conditions.

**Currency union.** Countries share a single currency and a single central bank. The euro is the largest example — 20 countries, one currency, one European Central Bank. Within the union, exchange-rate risk between members is eliminated entirely. The cost: the ECB must set a single monetary policy for economies that often have very different conditions. When Greece was in deep recession in 2011–2013 while Germany was growing, the ECB couldn't cut rates enough for Greece without risking inflation in Germany. Greece could not depreciate its way back to competitiveness because it had no independent currency. The adjustment had to come through painful internal deflation — wages and prices falling — which is slower and more damaging than exchange-rate adjustment.

## The trilemma

These choices can be organized around one of the most important principles in international monetary economics: the **impossible trinity** (or trilemma).

A country can have at most two of the following three things at once:

1. **Free capital movement** — the ability of investors and firms to move money in and out of the country without restriction.
2. **Fixed exchange rate** — a committed, stable exchange rate against another currency or basket.
3. **Independent monetary policy** — the ability to set domestic interest rates based on domestic economic conditions.

Choose any two. The third is ruled out.

Why? If a country has free capital mobility and wants to maintain a fixed exchange rate, the exchange rate pins the country's interest rates to the anchor country's rates. If domestic rates were higher, capital would flood in to earn the higher return, appreciating the currency and breaking the peg. If lower, capital would flood out, depreciating the currency and breaking the peg. The only way to hold both the exchange rate and capital mobility is to match the anchor country's interest rates — surrendering monetary independence.

If a country wants free capital mobility and monetary independence, the exchange rate has to float. That's the U.S., the U.K., Japan, and the eurozone.

If a country wants a fixed exchange rate and monetary independence, it has to restrict capital flows. That's how China maintained a managed exchange rate for decades while conducting its own monetary policy — by controlling capital in and out.

The trilemma is not a theoretical curiosity. It defines the actual policy space for every country's international monetary arrangement. Understanding which two a country has chosen explains why it faces the constraints it does.

## Synthesis

Pull back. Exchange rates are prices set by supply and demand in the world's largest financial market. The main drivers are interest-rate differentials, growth and inflation differentials, capital flows, and speculation. In the long run, purchasing power parity provides an anchor; in the short run, capital flows and expectations dominate.

Exchange-rate movements affect the macroeconomy through net exports (a weaker currency boosts them, a stronger one reduces them) and through import prices (a weaker currency raises inflation, a stronger one lowers it). These effects make exchange-rate movements part of the monetary transmission mechanism — whether the central bank intends it or not.

Countries choose among floating rates, soft pegs, hard pegs, and currency unions based on how they value exchange-rate stability relative to monetary-policy independence. The trilemma constrains all combinations: free capital movement, fixed exchange rate, and independent monetary policy cannot coexist. Large advanced economies with deep financial markets have generally chosen floating rates — capital mobility and monetary independence at the cost of exchange-rate volatility. Smaller economies and currency-bloc members have made different trade-offs.

The dollar's dominant role in FX markets — 90 percent of all transactions — reflects and reinforces the U.S.'s position in the global monetary system. That position gives the U.S. unusual flexibility but also means that U.S. monetary policy has international consequences that other countries must manage whether they want to or not.
