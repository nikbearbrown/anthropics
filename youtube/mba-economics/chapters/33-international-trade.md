# Chapter 33 — International Trade

*Why countries trade — and the math that shows trade can leave both sides better off, even when one side is "better at everything."*

---

Look at the iPhone in your pocket. The aluminum in its frame was likely mined in Australia, refined in China, and machined in Vietnam. The display is from South Korea. The chips are designed in California, fabricated in Taiwan, and assembled into the device in China. The battery comes from Japan or South Korea. The rare-earth elements in dozens of small components come from China and parts of Africa. The software was written in California with contributions from teams across the world.

When you bought the phone, the price reflected the labor of hundreds of thousands of workers in dozens of countries, the design and engineering of thousands more, and capital invested across decades by Apple and its suppliers. None of these flows would have been possible in a closed economy. International trade is the institutional infrastructure that makes the global supply chain real.

This chapter is about why trade happens and what determines whether it leaves participants better or worse off. The answer to the first question involves a piece of logic so counterintuitive that intelligent people have been getting it wrong for two centuries — and so beautiful that understanding it for the first time produces something like the satisfaction of watching a mathematical proof close.

---

## Absolute vs. comparative advantage

Start with a simple setup. Two countries — call them U.S. and Brazil — both produce wheat and sugar. U.S. workers are more productive at both:

- One U.S. worker produces 6 tons of wheat per year *or* 4 tons of sugar.
- One Brazilian worker produces 2 tons of wheat per year *or* 3 tons of sugar.

The U.S. has **absolute advantage** in both goods — its workers produce more of either good per year. The naive conclusion: Brazil shouldn't bother trading with the U.S., because the U.S. can produce everything more efficiently.

The economist's conclusion is different. Absolute productivity is the wrong thing to compare.

Compare instead the *opportunity cost* — what each country gives up to produce one more unit of a good.

To produce one ton of wheat, a U.S. worker must stop producing sugar. With productivity of 6 wheat *or* 4 sugar, producing 6 tons of wheat costs 4 tons of sugar foregone. Per ton of wheat, the U.S. gives up 4/6 = 0.67 tons of sugar.

In Brazil, producing 2 tons of wheat costs 3 tons of sugar. Per ton of wheat, Brazil gives up 3/2 = 1.5 tons of sugar.

The U.S. has **comparative advantage in wheat** — its opportunity cost of wheat is lower (0.67 vs. 1.5 sugar per ton of wheat).

Now flip it. What's the opportunity cost of sugar in each country? In the U.S., producing 4 tons of sugar costs 6 tons of wheat foregone — 6/4 = 1.5 tons of wheat per ton of sugar. In Brazil, producing 3 tons of sugar costs 2 tons of wheat — 2/3 = 0.67 tons of wheat per ton of sugar.

Brazil has **comparative advantage in sugar** — its opportunity cost of sugar is lower.

The pattern is not coincidence. If one country has lower opportunity cost in one good, it necessarily has *higher* opportunity cost in the other good. Comparative advantage is always symmetric: if you have comparative advantage in wheat, your trading partner has comparative advantage in something else.

## The gains from trade — worked through

Now the payoff. Each country has 100 workers.

**Without trade**, suppose each country splits labor evenly between the two goods:
- U.S.: 50 workers × 6 wheat + 50 workers × 4 sugar = **300 wheat + 200 sugar**
- Brazil: 50 workers × 2 wheat + 50 workers × 3 sugar = **100 wheat + 150 sugar**
- World total: **400 wheat + 350 sugar**

**With trade**, each country specializes in its comparative-advantage good:
- U.S. devotes all 100 workers to wheat: **600 wheat**
- Brazil devotes all 100 workers to sugar: **300 sugar**
- World total: **600 wheat + 300 sugar**

Total wheat rose from 400 to 600 — a gain of 200 tons. Total sugar fell from 350 to 300 — a loss of 50 tons. The world is producing substantially more wheat at the cost of modestly less sugar. Specialization has expanded what the world can collectively consume.

Now trade. Suppose the exchange rate settles at 1 ton of wheat for 0.7 tons of sugar — within the range where both countries benefit (we'll verify this). The U.S. trades 200 tons of wheat to Brazil and receives 140 tons of sugar.

After trade:
- U.S. keeps 400 wheat, receives 140 sugar → **400 wheat + 140 sugar**
- Brazil receives 200 wheat, keeps 160 sugar → **200 wheat + 160 sugar**

Compare to the no-trade scenario:
- U.S. gains 100 wheat, loses 60 sugar
- Brazil gains 100 wheat, gains 10 sugar

Both countries are better off. Trade allows each to consume a combination that would have been impossible without specialization.

The exchange rate of 1 wheat = 0.7 sugar is within the range that benefits both countries because: the U.S. is willing to trade wheat for any price above its domestic opportunity cost (0.67 sugar/wheat); Brazil is willing to trade sugar for any price where the wheat it receives covers its opportunity cost (1.5 sugar/wheat). Any price between 0.67 and 1.5 (sugar per wheat) is mutually beneficial. The market settles somewhere in between.

## Why "better at everything" doesn't eliminate the gains

The U.S. has absolute advantage in both goods. This is the fact that confuses people. How can trade with Brazil benefit the U.S. if the U.S. is already better at producing everything?

The answer is opportunity cost. Every U.S. worker who produces wheat is a worker not producing sugar. The U.S. can't produce unlimited amounts of both simultaneously. When it shifts workers to the thing it's *relatively* best at — wheat — it frees Brazil to specialize in what Brazil is *relatively* best at — sugar. World total output rises.

The brain surgeon analogy sharpens this. A brain surgeon might type faster than her assistant. Should she do her own typing? No — not because she can't, but because the time she spends typing is time she doesn't spend performing brain surgery, where her advantage over the assistant is far larger. The surgeon specializes in surgery; the assistant types. Both end up with more of what they value than they would if the surgeon did everything herself.

Same structure, same logic. The U.S. should produce wheat — where its advantage is largest in relative terms — even though it could produce more sugar than Brazil. Brazil should produce sugar — where its *disadvantage* is smallest — even though it's less productive absolutely.

The irrelevance of absolute advantage is perhaps the single most important and most widely misunderstood result in economics. Political arguments about trade — "they can make it cheaper, so we can't compete" — almost universally commit the error of confusing absolute with comparative advantage. Every country, regardless of its absolute productivity, has comparative advantage in *something*.

## Modern trade — intra-industry and supply chains

The Ricardian framework predicts the most trade between countries that are very *different* — a productive country and a less productive one, each specializing in what they do relatively best. It predicts much less trade between similar countries.

The actual pattern of modern trade contradicts this prediction. Most international trade happens between *similar* developed economies. The U.S. trades heavily with Canada, Mexico, the EU, and Japan. Cars flow both ways across the Pacific: German Porsches sell in the U.S., American trucks sell in Germany. This is **intra-industry trade** — trade in similar goods between countries with similar productivity levels. Standard Ricardian theory doesn't predict it.

Three mechanisms explain it.

**Product differentiation.** Cars are not a commodity. A German luxury sedan and an American pickup truck are not substitutes. Consumers in both countries have heterogeneous preferences, and both products find buyers across borders. Each country has comparative advantage in its *specific differentiated variety*, not in the generic good category. The trade is in varieties, not in categories.

**Economies of scale.** Producing a particular model at scale is cheaper than producing many models at small scale. International trade allows each country to specialize in a narrow range of varieties and produce them at efficient volumes, then trade for other varieties. The gains come from scale, not from differences in relative productivity.

**Global supply chains.** Modern production is decomposed into fragments that cross borders many times. Apple designs in California, fabricates chips in Taiwan, assembles in China, and sells globally. What appears in trade statistics as an "import from China" is actually a collection of value-added steps distributed across the globe. The gross trade flows vastly exceed the net value-added at each location. South Korea's "export" of display panels to China for iPhone assembly is a component of what eventually gets counted as a Chinese export to the U.S.

The implication for trade theory: the Ricardian model captures an important truth about why specialization creates value, but most of the world's trade is governed by scale economies and supply-chain logistics rather than the classical story. Modern trade models incorporate all three mechanisms. The basic insight that trade creates value survives; the specific mechanisms are richer than Ricardo imagined.

## Dynamic gains beyond comparative advantage

The static gains from specialization and comparative advantage — the kind we computed in the wheat-and-sugar example — are real but limited. They come from reallocating existing resources to higher-valued uses. They don't explain why trade-open economies seem to grow faster than closed-economy peers over long periods.

The dynamic gains from trade are harder to measure but probably larger over time.

**Competitive pressure.** Firms that face foreign competition have no choice but to improve their productivity. Closed-economy firms can earn comfortable rents from domestic market power. Open-economy firms have to match the world's best or lose the business. The empirical evidence on this is consistent: opening to trade tends to raise the productivity of surviving domestic firms, while forcing out the least productive.

**Technology spillovers.** Trade creates contact between industries. Engineers and managers in trading relationships observe how foreign competitors design, manufacture, and distribute. Workers move between firms. Ideas travel with goods, even when intellectual property is protected. Countries that are more embedded in international trade tend to exhibit faster technology adoption.

**Specialization deepening.** Larger markets support more specialized industries. The U.S. has hundreds of specialized law firms, surgical centers, software niches, and advanced manufacturing capabilities that wouldn't be economically viable if the U.S. served only its domestic market. International trade is one reason the U.S. domestic market effectively exceeds its domestic borders.

**Investment scale.** The global market for a successful pharmaceutical makes the R&D investment worthwhile. The global market for a successful software platform makes the engineering investment worthwhile. Trade multiplies the payoff to innovation. Countries that trade more have more reason to invest in the innovations that trade lets them export.

These gains compound over time in ways the static model doesn't capture. The extraordinary development of South Korea from a low-income agrarian economy in 1960 to a high-income industrial economy today — with companies like Samsung and Hyundai at world scale — reflects dynamic gains from trade exposure that no static calculation of comparative advantage in 1960 would have predicted. The same pattern appears in Taiwan, Singapore, and more recently Vietnam and Bangladesh.

## Synthesis

Pull back. Countries trade because specialization according to comparative advantage raises world output — even when one country is more productive at everything. The mechanism is opportunity cost: what matters isn't absolute productivity but relative productivity, and countries differ in relative productivity even when one has absolute advantage across the board.

The gains from trade in the Ricardian framework are static: they come from reallocating existing resources to their highest-valued use. They are real and computable. The dynamic gains — competitive pressure, technology spillovers, specialization deepening, investment incentives — are harder to quantify but probably larger over long periods.

Modern trade is dominated by intra-industry exchange between similar countries, driven by product differentiation, economies of scale, and supply-chain fragmentation. The iPhone is the canonical image: hundreds of thousands of workers across dozens of countries contributing to a single product, with value-added distributed across the supply chain. Comparative advantage in the classical sense is only one part of the story.

The gains from trade are real and substantial. They are not evenly distributed. Workers in import-competing industries face concentrated costs while consumers and export industries capture diffuse gains. The next chapter takes up that distributional question — the political backlash against trade, the policy debates over protection and adjustment, and the institutional framework of the global trading system. Understanding the gains from this chapter is the prerequisite for evaluating those arguments with clear eyes.

## Exercises

**Warm-up**

*1. Identify comparative advantage.* Country A can produce 10 tons of steel or 5 tons of cloth per worker per year. Country B can produce 4 tons of steel or 8 tons of cloth per worker per year. (a) Which country has absolute advantage in steel? In cloth? (b) Compute the opportunity cost of steel (in cloth) for each country. (c) Compute the opportunity cost of cloth (in steel) for each country. (d) Which country has comparative advantage in steel? In cloth? (e) At what range of price ratios (steel per cloth) would both countries gain from trade? *(Tests: the core comparative advantage calculation; identifying the mutually beneficial price range.)*

*2. Why absolute advantage is irrelevant.* Country X is the most productive in the world — its workers produce 50% more of every good than any other country's workers. (a) Does Country X have comparative advantage in every good? Explain. (b) Can Country X benefit from trading with a less productive country? Use the brain-surgeon analogy to explain. (c) A politician argues: "Country X shouldn't import anything because its workers are better at making everything." What is the error in this reasoning? *(Tests: the irrelevance-of-absolute-advantage result; identifying the common political error.)*

*3. Gains from trade arithmetic.* Use the U.S.-Brazil wheat-sugar example. Each country has 100 workers. (a) Without trade, with a 50/50 labor split, compute each country's output and the world total. (b) With full specialization, compute each country's output and the world total. (c) If trade occurs at 1 wheat = 0.8 sugar, and the U.S. trades 150 tons of wheat, compute each country's final consumption. (d) Verify both countries gain compared to the no-trade scenario. (e) Is 0.8 sugar/wheat within the mutually beneficial price range? *(Tests: computing before/after trade outcomes; verifying that a specific price ratio is in the mutually beneficial range.)*

**Application**

*4. Intra-industry trade.* The U.S. and Germany both have large automobile industries. In 2023, the U.S. exported roughly $13 billion of motor vehicles to Germany and imported roughly $35 billion of German vehicles. (a) The Ricardian model doesn't predict this pattern — explain why. (b) Which of the three intra-industry trade mechanisms (product differentiation, economies of scale, supply chains) best explains U.S.-Germany auto trade? Provide a specific example for each. (c) If the U.S. imposed a 25% tariff on German vehicles, would the Ricardian model predict this harms the U.S.? What does the intra-industry trade model predict? *(Tests: explaining why standard theory fails for intra-industry trade; applying the three mechanisms; connecting to tariff analysis.)*

*5. iPhone supply chain value-added.* Apple's iPhone is manufactured across multiple countries. Suppose a $1,000 iPhone has the following rough value-added distribution: design/IP (U.S.): $400; chip fabrication (Taiwan): $150; display (South Korea): $80; assembly and logistics (China): $80; components from other countries: $150; Apple retail margin: $140. (a) If this phone is counted as a Chinese export to the U.S. of $1,000, how much of the $1,000 actually represents Chinese value-added? (b) How does this affect the interpretation of the U.S.-China trade deficit? (c) A tariff on iPhones assembled in China would primarily hurt which parties — Chinese workers, Chinese value-added, American consumers, or Apple shareholders? Use the value-added breakdown. *(Tests: supply-chain fragmentation and the gap between gross trade flows and value-added; tariff incidence in supply chains.)*

*6. Comparative advantage shifts.* In 1960, South Korea had comparative advantage in labor-intensive manufacturing (textiles, light assembly). By 2000, it had comparative advantage in capital-intensive industries (shipbuilding, steel, semiconductor chips) and consumer electronics. (a) What changed that shifted South Korea's comparative advantage? Name at least two factors. (b) Does this shift challenge or support the Ricardian framework? Explain. (c) A country with comparative advantage in low-wage assembly today might lose that comparative advantage as wages rise. What does this imply for countries currently specializing in low-wage manufacturing? *(Tests: dynamic comparative advantage; the connection between development and evolving trade patterns.)*

**Synthesis**

*7. Static vs. dynamic case for free trade.* A small country is considering eliminating all tariffs on manufactured goods. (a) Make the static comparative-advantage case for free trade — what gains are available from specialization? (b) Make the dynamic case for free trade — what gains beyond static reallocation might materialize over 20 years? (c) Make the strongest case *against* free trade for this country. Under what conditions does the case for protection have merit? (d) Overall, is the static or the dynamic case for trade more compelling for a small developing country? *(Tests: synthesizing both arguments; naming the legitimate exceptions to the free-trade case.)*

*8. The Heckscher-Ohlin extension.* The Heckscher-Ohlin model says a country exports goods that use its abundant factor intensively. The U.S. is capital-abundant and labor-scarce relative to many trading partners. (a) What does Heckscher-Ohlin predict about U.S. export patterns? (b) The "Leontief Paradox" found that the U.S. exported labor-intensive goods in the 1950s despite being capital-abundant. What does this suggest about the Heckscher-Ohlin model's predictions? (c) How does the rise of human-capital (skilled workers, engineering) as a production factor help reconcile the paradox — is skilled labor more like physical capital or unskilled labor in Heckscher-Ohlin terms? *(Tests: applying the factor-endowment model; engaging with a famous empirical anomaly; connecting to modern trade patterns.)*

**Challenge**

*9. Infant industry argument.* The chapter mentions "infant industries" as one condition where the gains-from-trade story weakens. South Korea's industrial policy in the 1960s–1980s explicitly protected infant industries (steel, shipbuilding, semiconductors) from foreign competition while they developed. Those industries are now globally competitive. (a) What is the theoretical argument for infant industry protection — why is it an exception to free-trade advice? (b) The problem with infant industry policy is the "sunset problem" — what is it? (c) South Korea's infant industry policy appears successful in retrospect. Does this validate the policy choice, or could the same development have occurred without protection? What evidence would distinguish between these? (d) Is there a more efficient policy than import protection for achieving the same goal of building up an infant industry? *(Tests: the infant industry exception; the sunset problem; evaluating a historical case; alternative policy instruments.)*

*10. The distributional problem.* The chapter notes gains from trade are real but "not evenly distributed." A steel town loses 2,000 jobs when a U.S. tariff on imported steel is removed. Meanwhile, 300 million American consumers each save $30 per year on cheaper steel-containing goods. (a) Compute the aggregate consumer gain and compare it to the aggregate worker loss (assume average steel worker earned $70,000/year). Who gains more in aggregate? (b) Why does the concentrated loss politically dominate the diffuse gain, even though the aggregate gain is larger? (c) A Trade Adjustment Assistance program provides two years of income support and retraining to displaced workers. At what annual cost would such a program fully compensate the steel workers? Is this cost below the consumer gain? (d) If the answer to (c) is "yes, consumers gain more than it would cost to compensate workers," why doesn't this compensation happen routinely? What political economy problem blocks it? *(Tests: distributional arithmetic of trade; the political economy of concentrated vs. diffuse interests; the logic and limits of compensation.)*

## LLM Exercises

**Exercise 1 — Solve a comparative-advantage problem.** Tell an LLM: "Country A produces 10 units of wheat or 5 units of cloth per worker. Country B produces 4 units of wheat or 8 units of cloth per worker. Which country has comparative advantage in wheat? In cloth? At what price ratio could both gain from trade?" Verify the math.

**Exercise 2 — Trace a supply chain.** Pick a complex product (smartphone, car, laptop, designer handbag). Ask an LLM to trace the major components and their countries of origin. The exercise teaches that "trade" is much more than finished-goods exchange.

**Exercise 3 — Stress-test the gains.** Ask an LLM: "Under what conditions does the simple comparative-advantage gains-from-trade story fail or weaken?" Push for the conditions: monopoly behavior, zero domestic productivity, infant industries, externalities. Notice which conditions the LLM identifies.

**Exercise 4 — Diagnose intra-industry trade.** Ask an LLM why the U.S. and Germany trade enormous quantities of cars in both directions. The Ricardian framework doesn't predict this — what does? Push for product differentiation, economies of scale, supply chain explanations.

**Exercise 5 — Estimate the dynamic gains.** Ask an LLM to estimate how much of South Korea's economic transformation since 1960 was due to access to global markets vs. domestic policy choices. The exercise teaches the difficulty of separating dynamic gains from other growth factors.
