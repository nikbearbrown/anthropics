# Chapter 31 — The Impacts of Government Borrowing

*What happens when a government borrows on a sustained basis — to private investment, to interest rates, to trade balances, to the next generation.*

---

In the late 1960s, President Lyndon Johnson was pursuing two expensive programs simultaneously: the Vietnam War and the Great Society's expansion of social programs. Rather than raise taxes to pay for both, his administration ran budget deficits and let the Federal Reserve accommodate the spending with monetary expansion. The combined effect — "guns and butter" financed by borrowing and money creation — substantially preceded the inflation surge that haunted the U.S. economy through the 1970s.

This wasn't entirely unprecedented. Earlier wars had been deficit-financed. What was distinctive about the LBJ era was that the deficit persisted in peacetime and established a multi-decade pattern. The U.S. has run budget deficits in nearly every year since 1969, with brief exceptions in the late 1990s. Accumulated debt has grown from about 30 percent of GDP then to over 100 percent today.

Persistent borrowing has consequences. Some are channels through which deficits affect macroeconomic outcomes right now. Some are constraints on future fiscal flexibility. Some are intergenerational — current beneficiaries of spending leave the bills for future taxpayers. This chapter walks through the major effects, organized by their logic rather than their political valence.

---

## The national saving-investment identity, expanded

In Chapter 23 we established the core identity:

$$S - I = NX$$

National saving minus national investment equals net exports.

Now expand national saving into its two components: *private saving* ($S_p$) by households and businesses, and *public saving* ($S_g$) — the government surplus, which is negative when there's a deficit.

So $S = S_p + S_g$.

Substituting:

$$(S_p + S_g) - I = NX$$

Rearranging, with the government deficit written as $(G - T)$:

$$S_p = I + (G - T) + NX$$

In plain language: **private saving must fund three things simultaneously** — (1) domestic investment by businesses and households, (2) the government deficit, and (3) net capital outflows to the rest of the world (which corresponds to a trade surplus; equivalently, when $NX$ is negative, foreigners are funding the gap).

This identity has no moving parts — it's true by construction, the same way a balance sheet must balance. It can't be wrong. What it does is reveal structural connections between fiscal policy, investment, and trade. A larger government deficit must be matched by some combination of:

- More private saving (people save more in response to anticipated future taxes),
- Less domestic investment (fewer resources available for the private sector),
- More borrowing from abroad (smaller NX, larger trade deficit).

In practice, all three adjust simultaneously in proportions that vary with economic conditions. But the identity guarantees that the fiscal effect goes *somewhere*, even if policymakers would prefer it went nowhere.

## The twin deficits

The U.S. has a chronic budget deficit. The U.S. also has a chronic trade deficit. The identity above says these two are not coincidence — they're connected.

When $(G - T)$ is large, the right-hand side of the identity has to balance. Private saving can't easily rise by the full amount of the deficit (households don't save with that kind of precision). Investment can fall, but falling investment has real costs — it reduces future productive capacity. The most flexible adjustor, especially for an economy with open capital markets, is the trade balance. Foreign investors buy U.S. Treasuries to fund the deficit, supplying capital that funds government borrowing. The inflow of foreign capital appreciates the dollar, which makes U.S. exports more expensive and imports cheaper, worsening the trade balance.

This is the **twin deficits** mechanism: fiscal deficits and trade deficits tend to move together, linked through capital flows and the exchange rate.

The connection is empirically real but not one-to-one. The adjustments are distributed across all three terms, and the proportions shift. In the 1980s, under Reagan-era deficits, the U.S. trade deficit widened substantially — a textbook twin-deficits episode. In the late 1990s, both deficits shrank together. Since 2001, both have been large simultaneously. The correlation isn't perfect because private saving and investment fluctuate independently — but the structural tendency is visible in the data.

The policy implication is sometimes stated as: if you want to reduce the trade deficit, reducing the fiscal deficit is more reliably effective than imposing tariffs. Tariffs affect the composition of trade (which goods, with which partners) without changing the underlying savings-investment arithmetic. Fiscal consolidation affects the arithmetic directly.

## Ricardian equivalence

Now a theoretical complication, and an important one.

David Ricardo, writing in the early 19th century, made an argument that sounds counterintuitive: if households are forward-looking and rational, it shouldn't matter whether the government taxes or borrows to finance given spending. The two modes of financing are *equivalent* in their macroeconomic effects.

The reasoning. The government cuts taxes today and borrows instead. Households receive more after-tax income now. But they also know — if they think about it — that to repay the debt, the government will need to raise taxes in the future. The present value of those future taxes equals the current tax cut. Rational, forward-looking households should therefore save the entire tax cut rather than spend it, building up the financial assets they'll need to pay the higher future taxes. The net effect on consumption demand: zero.

In this view, deficit-financed spending and tax-financed spending are equivalent. The government borrows; households save the equivalent; the total consumption in the economy is unchanged; the deficit produces no stimulus.

This argument is logically coherent under its assumptions. The assumptions are heroic.

For Ricardian equivalence to hold exactly, households would need perfect foresight about future taxes, perfect ability to borrow against their anticipated future income, no uncertainty, and an altruistic motive to leave their heirs enough to pay future taxes. In the real world, many households are liquidity-constrained (they can't borrow against future income even if they know it's coming), have finite planning horizons, and respond to current income, not anticipated future income.

Empirical tests consistently find that Ricardian equivalence is *partially* true but far from complete. When the government runs deficits, private saving does rise somewhat — but not by the full amount of the deficit. Households are at least partially forward-looking but not perfectly so. The deficit produces some stimulus, but less than the simple Keynesian multiplier without Ricardian offsets would predict.

The practical implication: deficit-financed fiscal stimulus has real short-run demand effects. They're not zero (Ricardian equivalence doesn't fully hold) and they're not as large as the mechanical multiplier suggests (Ricardian effects partially offset). Where exactly in that range the actual effect sits depends on the economic context, the nature of the stimulus, and the credibility of the government's fiscal commitment.

## Crowding out

The most discussed effect of government borrowing on the private economy is **crowding out**.

When the government borrows, it competes with private borrowers in the market for loanable funds. More demand for capital at the current interest rate creates upward pressure on rates. Higher interest rates reduce the attractiveness of private investment — firms don't borrow as much for new plant and equipment; households don't borrow as much for housing. The government's increased borrowing "crowds out" some private investment.

The mechanism in a closed economy is direct: government demand for savings raises the price of savings (the interest rate), which reduces the quantity of savings demanded by private investors. In an open economy, the mechanism has an additional step: higher domestic rates attract foreign capital; the dollar appreciates; exports fall and imports rise; the trade balance worsens. Both channels are real; the open-economy channel distributes the crowding out across domestic investment *and* net exports rather than concentrating it all in domestic investment.

How much crowding out actually occurs depends heavily on context.

**Slack in the economy.** In a deep recession with unemployed workers and underutilized capital, additional government borrowing can draw on those idle resources rather than bidding them away from productive private use. Crowding out is minimal when there's slack to absorb the spending.

**Foreign capital availability.** U.S. deficits have persistently been funded partly by foreign investors — central banks, sovereign wealth funds, pension funds — who want safe, liquid dollar-denominated assets. Their willingness to fund U.S. deficits at historically low rates has muted the interest-rate effect domestically. The cost, as the identity makes clear, shows up in the trade balance instead.

**Monetary policy stance.** When the Federal Reserve is actively holding rates near zero (as during 2008–2014), it can mechanically offset any crowding-out pressure from deficits. The crowding-out mechanism requires rates to rise; if the central bank prevents this, the mechanism is suppressed.

The empirical record reflects these conditions. The massive deficits of 2009–2014 coincided with falling, not rising, long-term rates — because the Fed was at the zero lower bound and foreign capital was flowing in. The deficits of 2022–2023 occurred while the Fed was raising rates to fight inflation; rates did rise sharply. How much of the rate rise was crowding out versus Fed-driven disinflationary tightening is genuinely difficult to disentangle. Both forces were operating simultaneously.

## Debt sustainability

The cumulative effect of sustained deficits is an accumulated stock of debt. At some level — nobody knows exactly where — this becomes a problem. The question is what determines when.

The key variable is the relationship between the interest rate on government debt ($r$) and the growth rate of the economy ($g$).

If $r < g$: the economy is growing faster than the cost of the debt. Even with a modest deficit, the debt-to-GDP ratio shrinks over time because the denominator grows faster than the numerator. This is the condition that prevailed for much of the postwar period in the U.S. — strong economic growth eroded the WWII debt burden without requiring surpluses.

If $r > g$: the economy is not growing fast enough to dilute the debt. Even a small primary deficit (government spending minus taxes, excluding interest payments) causes the debt-to-GDP ratio to rise without limit. Sustainability requires a primary surplus large enough to offset the interest-growth differential.

The U.S. has historically benefited from $r < g$ or $r \approx g$. In recent years, with higher interest rates and moderate growth, $r$ has moved above $g$. The Congressional Budget Office projects U.S. debt-to-GDP rising substantially over the next several decades on current policy, primarily driven by Social Security and Medicare costs as the population ages.

This doesn't necessarily trigger a crisis. Japan has maintained debt-to-GDP above 200 percent for years without crisis. The U.S. dollar's reserve-currency status creates strong global demand for U.S. Treasury securities, financing deficits at lower rates than other countries of comparable indebtedness could obtain. And the U.S. borrows in its own currency — it can always service dollar-denominated debt in nominal terms.

But sustainability in nominal terms is not the same as sustainability in real terms. Inflating away debt works once; do it persistently and the bond markets price in the inflation risk, raising nominal rates. The long-run sustainability question for the U.S. is less about immediate crisis risk and more about the slow erosion of fiscal flexibility and the intergenerational question of who bears the cost of current borrowing.

Greece in 2010 faced crisis at debt-to-GDP around 100 percent. Japan has been at 200 percent without crisis. The difference is not just the numbers — it's the currency (Greece borrowed in euros, not its own currency), the institutional structure (Greece didn't have the ECB's backing as a last resort in the way a sovereign with its own currency does), and the market's confidence in the trajectory. Debt sustainability is partly a function of fundamentals and partly a function of confidence, and confidence can shift.

## Synthesis

Pull back. Government borrowing has multiple effects on the macroeconomy, all of which trace through the national saving-investment identity. That identity guarantees that the deficit funds come from somewhere: higher private saving (Ricardian effect), lower private investment (crowding out), or more foreign borrowing (trade deficit). In practice, all three adjust simultaneously.

Ricardian equivalence is the theoretical limiting case where fiscal deficits have no real effects because households fully anticipate the future tax implications. Empirically, the offset is partial — households do save somewhat more when deficits rise, but not by the full amount. Fiscal stimulus has real demand effects, but they're partially offset.

Crowding out is the real, empirically variable mechanism through which deficit borrowing reduces private investment. Its magnitude depends on slack in the economy, the availability of foreign capital, and the stance of monetary policy. In the modern U.S., the open-capital-market version of crowding out tends to work through the exchange rate and trade deficit as much as through domestic interest rates.

Debt sustainability depends on whether the interest rate on debt exceeds the growth rate. The U.S. has structural advantages — reserve-currency status, own-currency borrowing — that provide more latitude than most. But the trajectory of debt-to-GDP on current policy is rising, and the long-run demographic pressures from Medicare and Social Security have barely started. The fiscal questions this chapter examines are not merely academic; they are among the largest unresolved issues in U.S. macroeconomic policy.
