# Chapter 3 — Demand and Supply

*Where price comes from — and what happens when you try to override it.*

---

Pick up an organic Fuji apple at the supermarket. Look at the sticker. $2.75 a pound.

Now pick up the conventional Fuji right next to it. $1.72 a pound.

Same fruit, by genetics. Often grown in adjacent orchards. The organic one might have traveled a shorter distance — small farms tend to be local. By the logic of "less travel, lower price," the organic apple should be cheaper. It is not. It costs sixty percent more.

Hold that puzzle. The answer turns out to be the entire content of this chapter.

The model that produces the answer is called *demand and supply*, and it is the most-used tool in economics. Once you have it, you can read prices the way a sailor reads weather — not perfectly, but well enough to know whether to put up the sails or take them down. We're going to build the two curves, watch them meet, and then see what happens when a government tries to override the place where they cross. That last part is where the model earns its keep.

---

## The two curves

Start with the buyers.

**Demand** is the relationship between the price of a good and the quantity buyers are willing and able to purchase. Two words in that sentence are doing real work. *Willing* — the buyer wants it. *Able* — the buyer can pay. Wanting without being able doesn't count. Demand requires money behind the want.

The **law of demand**: when price rises, quantity demanded falls. Other things equal — and that phrase is doing important work we'll come back to. Why does the law hold? Two effects that reinforce each other. First, when something gets more expensive, some buyers switch to substitutes. Beef goes up; people eat more chicken. Second, even buyers who stay with the product buy less, because their income now buys less of everything. Both effects push the same direction: higher price, lower quantity demanded.

Plot it. Vertical axis: price. Horizontal axis: quantity. The demand curve runs from upper-left to lower-right. Higher price, smaller quantity.

Now the sellers.

**Supply** is the relationship between price and the quantity sellers are willing and able to produce. The **law of supply**: when price rises, quantity supplied rises. Why? Higher prices mean more revenue per unit, which justifies pulling in higher-cost production. The marginal farmer who can grow apples but barely breaks even at $1 a pound enters the market when the price hits $2. The farm that needs $5/pound to be worth the bother enters at $5. As price climbs, more producers find the arithmetic worth doing. Higher price, larger quantity supplied.

The supply curve runs from lower-left to upper-right.

Now we have the picture: two curves sloping opposite directions, drawn on the same axes. They cross at exactly one point. That crossing is **equilibrium** — the only price at which the quantity buyers want exactly matches the quantity sellers will produce.

At any price above equilibrium, sellers want to sell more than buyers want to buy. The unsold inventory accumulates; some seller cuts the price; we slide back toward the crossing. At any price below equilibrium, buyers want more than sellers will produce; the queue grows; some buyer offers a higher price; we slide back up. The market is a corrective mechanism — noisy, imperfect, often slow — that pushes price toward that one crossing point.

Every market price is the story of how these two lines found each other."] -->

This is the workhorse model. Two curves, one crossing. Almost everything in microeconomics is some version of this picture.

---

## The distinction that matters most

Here is the mistake students make every year. It is the difference between a *movement along* a curve and a *shift of* the curve. Getting this wrong makes the rest of the model unusable.

When the *price* of a good changes — and nothing else changes — buyers move along the demand curve. The curve doesn't move; the point on it does. Higher price, move up and left. Lower price, move down and right. Same curve throughout.

When *something other than price* changes, the entire curve shifts. If consumer income rises, more people can afford the good at every price. The whole demand curve moves to the right — at every price, more is demanded than before. If income falls, the curve shifts left.

The non-price things that shift the demand curve are **shift variables**. Five of them matter most.

*Income* — usually obvious. Get richer, demand more of most things. A few goods run the other way, goods economists call *inferior* — ramen noodles, used cars, bus tickets — where demand falls as income rises because people can now afford the better substitute. We'll meet these properly in the consumer-choice chapters.

*Taste and preference* — when fashion changes, when health concerns spread, when a video tips a product from obscure to desired. The decade-long drift toward plant-based food shifted the demand curve for meat substitutes dramatically to the right. Demand for fur coats has been moving left for forty years.

*Prices of related goods* — this one splits into two cases. *Substitutes* are goods that stand in for each other: chicken and beef, Uber and Lyft, paperback and e-book. When the price of a substitute rises, demand for the original shifts right — more people defect to the cheaper option. *Complements* are goods consumed together: cars and gasoline, printers and ink, tennis rackets and tennis balls. When the price of a complement rises, demand for the original shifts left — the bundled experience gets more expensive, so the whole package becomes less attractive.

*Expectations* — if buyers expect the price to rise, they buy now. Right shift in current demand. If they expect a price drop, they wait. Left shift. This is why real-estate markets heat up when buyers expect appreciation: the expectation becomes self-reinforcing.

*Number of buyers* — more buyers means more demand. Population growth, demographic cohorts entering their peak-spending years, immigration — all shift demand right.

The supply curve has its own shift variables.

*Input costs* — wages, raw materials, energy. When they rise, supply shifts left because every unit now costs more to produce at every price. The supply curve didn't tilt; it moved.

*Technology* — usually shifts supply right. Better seed varieties, more efficient fabrication, smarter logistics. The same inputs now produce more output.

*Natural conditions* — drought shifts the wheat supply curve left; a bumper crop shifts it right. This is the oldest supply shock in the world.

*Government policy* — taxes shift supply left (the seller must pass some of the tax to the buyer or absorb it as cost); subsidies shift supply right.

*Number of sellers* — more producers means more supply. Right shift.

Here is the rule stated as plainly as I can: **a change in price is a movement along the curves. A change in anything else is a shift of a curve.** Never confuse them.

## The four-step analysis

We can now predict. Something changes in the world. How does it change the equilibrium price and quantity?

Four steps, every time.

**Step 1.** Draw the original diagram. Label the equilibrium price and quantity. Establish the baseline.

**Step 2.** Identify what changed. Does it affect buyers — a demand shift variable? Does it affect sellers — a supply shift variable? Both?

**Step 3.** Shift the relevant curve. Which direction? Use the rules above. Be specific: "demand shifts right because consumer income rose" or "supply shifts left because the input price of corn rose."

**Step 4.** Find the new equilibrium. Compare it to the old one. The comparison is your prediction.

Let's run one. In 2022, gasoline prices rose sharply across the United States. Sales of new SUVs softened. What does the four-step analysis say?

Step 1: Draw the SUV market. Old equilibrium at price $P_0$, quantity $Q_0$.

Step 2: What changed? The price of a *complement* — gasoline — rose. SUVs and gasoline are consumed together; you need fuel to run the car. Higher price of a complement shifts demand for the original good left.

Step 3: The demand curve for SUVs shifts left. The supply curve is unchanged — manufacturers' costs and technology are stable.

Step 4: New equilibrium has lower price and lower quantity. Prediction: SUV prices fall and units sold fall.

That prediction matched what happened. The model doesn't tell you *how much* prices fell or *how fast*. It tells you *direction* and *qualitative shape*. That is already enormously useful.

<!-- → [DIAGRAM: Two-panel four-step worked example — left panel: original SUV market with D₀, S, equilibrium at P₀/Q₀; right panel: demand shifts left to D₁, same supply curve S, new equilibrium at P₁ < P₀ and Q₁ < Q₀; caption: "The demand curve moved — not the supply curve. The new crossing is lower and to the left. That's the whole prediction."] -->

A small but important complication: when *both* curves shift at once, some predictions pin down and others don't. Suppose a hurricane wipes out the Gulf shrimp crop (supply shifts left) at the same moment a cooking-show trend doubles consumer interest in shrimp dishes (demand shifts right). Price clearly rises — both shifts push that way. Quantity? Ambiguous. The supply shift pushes quantity down; the demand shift pushes it up. Which wins depends on the magnitudes, and the model doesn't know the magnitudes. Use the diagram to see which outcomes the model pins and which it leaves open. A careful analyst names the ambiguity rather than hiding it.

## What happens when the government overrides the crossing

The demand-and-supply model's most important use is not predicting normal market movements. It is making visible what happens when a government tries to hold price away from its equilibrium value. This is where the model produces surprises.

A **price ceiling** is a legal maximum — the price cannot rise above this level. The classic example is rent control: a city declares that landlords cannot charge more than $1,500/month for a one-bedroom in some district. A ceiling is *binding* when it sits below the market equilibrium. If it sits above, nobody was charging that much anyway and nothing changes.

When a binding ceiling sits below equilibrium, two things happen simultaneously. Quantity demanded rises — the lower price draws in more buyers who couldn't afford the apartment at market rates. Quantity supplied falls — some landlords won't produce at the controlled price; some will convert units to condos or let them sit vacant. The gap between the two is a **shortage**: more demand than supply, at the controlled price, always.

Politicians often pass ceilings precisely because they want to keep housing affordable for people who'd otherwise be priced out. The ceiling solves the price problem for whoever *still gets* the apartment. It does not solve the underlying scarcity. The shortage gets allocated by something else: queues, lottery, "who you know," landlord screening that prefers tenants with stable income and few children, deferred maintenance, side payments under the table. The price didn't disappear. It transformed. Some of it became time spent waiting. Some of it became favoritism. Some of it became corruption. The mechanism that was doing coordination work got replaced by mechanisms that often do it worse.

<!-- → [DIAGRAM: Price ceiling diagram — demand curve D and supply curve S crossing at equilibrium P*; horizontal line labeled "Price Ceiling" set below P*; at ceiling price, quantity demanded QD marked on demand curve and quantity supplied QS marked on supply curve; horizontal bracket between QS and QD labeled "Shortage"; caption: "The gap between what buyers want and what sellers will supply at the controlled price doesn't close — it gets filled by queues, screening, and side payments"] -->

A **price floor** runs the other direction — a legal minimum. The classic example is the minimum wage: the price of labor cannot fall below this level. If the floor sits above equilibrium, quantity supplied (workers willing to work at that wage) exceeds quantity demanded (jobs employers will offer). The gap is a **surplus** of labor — unemployment.

The standard textbook conclusion is that a binding minimum wage causes some unemployment. The empirical picture is messier. Real labor markets have features the simple diagram doesn't include: firms with local market power over workers (called *monopsony*), search costs, information asymmetries. In some studies, modest minimum-wage increases haven't produced measurable unemployment effects. In others, they have. The model is right *about the direction* when markets are competitive and the floor binds substantially. It is right *less robustly* in labor markets than in rental markets, because the assumption of competition is harder to defend for most local labor markets than for apartment rentals in a large city.

Both controls share a structural feature: they fix the *price* variable and let the *quantity* variable absorb the pressure. The price takes the political attention. The quantity adjusts in ways the diagram makes visible and the political debate often doesn't discuss.

## What the diagram secretly contains

There is a third layer in the demand-and-supply picture that most introductory treatments bury. It changes how you read every market argument. It is called **surplus**.

Look at a market in equilibrium. Say the equilibrium price is $80. Some buyers would have paid $300 for the product — they'd have driven across town for it, they wanted it that badly. They get it for $80. The difference between what they were willing to pay and what they actually paid is a gain. Summed across all buyers, those gains are the **consumer surplus**. On the diagram it appears as the triangular area above the equilibrium price and below the demand curve. Buyers in that triangle won — they got more than they paid for.

Sellers earn $80 per unit. Some of them would have produced for $20 — they're efficient, their costs are low. They receive $80. The difference between what they receive and what it cost them to produce is their gain. Summed across all sellers, those gains are the **producer surplus**. On the diagram: the triangular area below the equilibrium price and above the supply curve.

Add both triangles: **total surplus**. This is the size of the gains-from-trade pie — the total value created by the existence of this market. A clean result from economic theory: the free-market equilibrium maximizes this pie. Not who gets which piece — that's a distributional question the model doesn't settle. The *size* of the pie. This is the strongest efficiency argument for letting markets clear.

<!-- → [DIAGRAM: Surplus diagram — standard D and S crossing at equilibrium P*/Q*; upper triangle (between demand curve and P*) shaded and labeled "Consumer Surplus"; lower triangle (between P* and supply curve) shaded differently and labeled "Producer Surplus"; combined area labeled "Total Surplus"; caption: "The triangle above the price is what buyers gained. The triangle below is what sellers gained. Add them: that's the value this market created."] -->

Now look at what a price ceiling does to that pie.

A binding rent ceiling lowers the price below equilibrium. Some surplus shifts from landlords to the tenants who still get apartments — a redistributive effect. But because the quantity transacted falls (fewer apartments are built, fewer are rented), some surplus that used to exist simply disappears. It doesn't go to landlords. It doesn't go to tenants. It evaporates. Economists call this the **deadweight loss**. On the diagram it appears as a triangle that was filled with surplus at equilibrium and is now empty. A price floor produces the same triangular hole.

This is what the simple model says. It does not say the price ceiling is wrong. It says any argument for the ceiling has to account for the deadweight loss — the value that vaporizes, the transactions that would have made both parties better off and now don't happen. The redistribution might still be worth it. But the cost is real, and the diagram is the tool that makes it undeniable.

<!-- → [DIAGRAM: Deadweight loss diagram — price ceiling below P*; shaded region showing surplus redistributed from producers to consumers who still get the good; separate triangle labeled "Deadweight Loss" showing surplus that existed at equilibrium and no longer exists at the ceiling price; caption: "Some surplus shifted. Some evaporated. The evaporated triangle is the deadweight loss — value that belonged to nobody after the control took effect."] -->

## Synthesis

Pull back. The demand-and-supply diagram has three layers. The first: the two curves themselves — buyers' willingness and sellers' willingness, each driven by the price relationship and shifted by everything else. The second: the equilibrium where they cross, and the four-step method for tracking what happens when a shift variable changes. The third: the surplus structure — the pie, who gets which slice, and how much of it evaporates when price is held away from the crossing.

Bring the apple back. Why does the organic Fuji cost $2.75 and the conventional one $1.72?

Use the model. Demand for organic produce has been shifting right for twenty years — changing taste, health concerns, rising income, more buyers entering. Supply for organic apples is constrained: organic certification is expensive, organic farming is labor-intensive, converting a conventional orchard to organic takes years and a waiting period. The organic supply curve sits to the left of the conventional supply curve at every price. Demand shifted right; supply sits left. The crossing is higher. The price is higher.

The price is not a moral statement about organic farming. It is a compressed summary of how many buyers want it, how hard it is to produce, and how those two pressures balance against each other. That $2.75 sticker contains the decisions of every organic apple buyer and every organic apple farmer in the market, averaged and distilled into a single number you can read in a supermarket aisle.

That is what the demand-and-supply model does. It turns prices from noise into signal — a dense, fast-moving summary of what everyone in the market is doing. For the next thirty chapters, we will either use this model directly or examine the cases where it breaks. Knowing which is which is the skill.

## Exercises

**Warm-up**

*1. Draw and label.* From memory, draw a demand-and-supply diagram. Label: both curves, both axes, the equilibrium price and quantity. Then add a second equilibrium point showing what happens when demand shifts right. Name the shift variable that caused it and state whether price and quantity rose or fell. *(Tests: mechanical ability to construct and read the diagram.)*

*2. Classify the change.* For each of the following, identify whether it causes a movement along the demand curve, a shift of the demand curve, or a shift of the supply curve — and name the specific shift variable if it's a shift: (a) the price of coffee rises and people buy less coffee; (b) a study links coffee to improved memory and demand rises; (c) a drought destroys the Colombian coffee harvest; (d) the price of tea falls (a substitute for coffee). *(Tests: the movement-along vs. shift-of distinction, which is the most common source of student error.)*

*3. Law of supply.* Explain in your own words why the supply curve slopes upward. Then name one real-world industry where you think the slope is very steep (supply responds little to price) and one where it is very flat (supply responds a lot). What determines the difference? *(Tests: understanding the mechanism behind the law of supply, not just the label.)*

**Application**

*4. Run the four-step.* In 2020, COVID-19 shut down most restaurants. Apply the four-step analysis to the market for commercial restaurant equipment. Identify the shift variable, which curve moved and in which direction, and predict what happened to equilibrium price and quantity. Then check: did used restaurant equipment prices rise or fall in 2020? Does the model's prediction match? *(Tests: applying the four-step to a real event; connecting model prediction to observable outcome.)*

*5. Complements and substitutes.* Electric vehicles and home charging stations are complements. Gasoline-powered cars and EVs are substitutes. If the federal government ends the $7,500 EV tax credit, trace the effect through both relationships: what happens to the market for home charging stations, and what happens to the market for gasoline-powered cars? Run a separate four-step for each market. *(Tests: using substitute/complement relationships across multiple markets; applying the four-step twice in sequence.)*

*6. Price floor consequences.* A city sets a minimum price for rideshare rides of $12, above the current equilibrium of $8. Using the diagram, predict: (a) whether a shortage or surplus results and why; (b) who benefits and who is harmed; (c) one unintended consequence the city council probably did not announce when passing the policy. *(Tests: price floor mechanics; connecting diagram outcomes to real distributional consequences.)*

**Synthesis**

*7. Two shifts, one ambiguous outcome.* In early 2022, the war in Ukraine simultaneously disrupted global wheat exports (supply shock) and prompted some countries to increase wheat stockpiling (demand shock). For global wheat prices: state which direction price moved and explain why that prediction is unambiguous. For global wheat quantity traded: explain why the model cannot tell you the direction without additional information, and name what information you would need to resolve the ambiguity. *(Tests: handling simultaneous shifts; distinguishing pinned predictions from ambiguous ones.)*

*8. Read the surplus.* A pharmaceutical company holds a patent on a drug. Patients who need it would pay up to $500/dose; the company charges $200. The marginal cost of production is $15/dose. On a diagram, sketch the consumer surplus, producer surplus, and identify whether total surplus is maximized. Now suppose a price ceiling of $50/dose is imposed. Trace what happens to each surplus triangle and identify the deadweight loss. Whose surplus shifted, whose evaporated, and what transactions no longer happen? *(Tests: surplus analysis applied to a real policy context; connecting the geometry to the distributional story.)*

**Challenge**

*9. When the model misfires.* The chapter notes that the minimum wage empirical literature is messier than the simple price-floor diagram predicts. Identify one specific assumption built into the standard supply-and-demand model that, if relaxed, could explain why modest minimum wage increases sometimes produce little measurable unemployment. Explain the mechanism — don't just name the assumption. Then consider: does this make the supply-and-demand model wrong, or does it make it incomplete? What's the difference? *(Tests: critical evaluation of model assumptions; distinguishing "wrong" from "incomplete" as categories of model failure.)*

*10. The organic apple, fully explained.* The chapter opens with the organic vs. conventional apple price puzzle and closes by resolving it. Now extend the analysis: suppose a new study convincingly links conventional pesticides to health risks, and consumer concern shifts demand for organic apples sharply right at the same moment a new organic certification process cuts certification costs by 40%, shifting organic supply right as well. For organic apple prices: is the new equilibrium price higher, lower, or ambiguous? For organic apple quantity: is it higher, lower, or ambiguous? Show your reasoning with the two-shift framework, and name what additional information would resolve any ambiguity. *(Tests: fluent use of the two-shift framework on a novel case derived from the chapter's hook.)*

## LLM Exercises

**Exercise 1 — The four-step in action.** Pick a current event that you suspect changed equilibrium price and quantity in some market — a hurricane, a tariff, a viral product launch, a tech layoff. Describe the event to an LLM and ask it to walk through the four-step analysis: original equilibrium, what changed, which curve shifted and which way, new equilibrium. Then push: ask the LLM to identify *which* demand or supply shift variable drove the change, by name. Evaluate whether it gets the named variable right or hand-waves.

**Exercise 2 — Movement along vs. shift of.** Give an LLM this list and ask it to classify each as a "movement along the demand curve" or a "shift of the demand curve":
1. The price of avocados rises and people buy fewer avocados.
2. A celebrity recommends avocado toast and avocado demand rises.
3. The income tax falls and people buy more avocados.
4. The price of bagels rises (a complement) and avocado demand falls.

Check the LLM's answers. The first one is the trap — it is a movement along, not a shift. If the LLM gets it wrong, ask why and see whether you can teach it.

**Exercise 3 — Predict the unintended consequence.** Tell an LLM you are considering a price ceiling on a specific good — insulin, eggs, gasoline, daycare. Ask it to predict three unintended consequences using only the demand-and-supply framework. Evaluate: does the LLM identify the shortage, the non-price rationing mechanisms, and the deadweight loss? Or does it slide into political talking points without naming the mechanisms? If the latter, push it back to the diagram.

**Exercise 4 — Build a surplus diagram in words.** Without drawing anything, describe a demand-and-supply equilibrium to an LLM in pure prose. Then ask it to identify (a) consumer surplus, (b) producer surplus, (c) total surplus. Then introduce a price ceiling below equilibrium and ask the LLM to redo the surplus identification, naming the deadweight loss. Evaluate whether the LLM keeps the geometry straight when it can't see a picture.

**Exercise 5 — Two shifts at once.** Tell an LLM: "A drought hits the Midwest, cutting U.S. corn production. At the same moment, a new federal regulation requires more ethanol — made from corn — in gasoline." Ask what happens to corn's equilibrium price and quantity. Press it on which prediction is *certain* and which is *ambiguous*. A careful answer notes that price clearly rises while quantity is ambiguous — it depends on which shift is larger. If the LLM gives confident answers in both directions, that's a tell.
