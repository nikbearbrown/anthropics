# Chapter 21 — Unemployment

*The most-watched single number in macroeconomics — what it counts, what it misses, and why it never quite reaches zero.*

---

In March 2020, the U.S. unemployment rate was 4.4 percent — close to its lowest level in fifty years. By April 2020, it was 14.8 percent. Twenty-three million people were out of work who had been working a month earlier. The COVID-19 pandemic and the lockdowns that followed produced the most abrupt change in U.S. unemployment since statistics began being collected systematically in the 1940s.

Then, more remarkably, it fell back. By April 2022 the rate was 3.6 percent. The recovery was the fastest from any major disruption in U.S. labor-market history. Some sectors — restaurants, travel, in-person services — took longer than others. But in aggregate, within two years, the labor market had returned to roughly its pre-pandemic state.

The COVID episode is the right frame for this chapter because it exposes things the standard unemployment story usually hides. The rapid increase showed how quickly jobs disappear when something disrupts normal economic activity. The rapid recovery showed how quickly they come back when the disruption is specific and short-lived. And the messy specifics of those two years — workers leaving the labor force entirely, sectors recovering at different rates, debates about labor shortages that may or may not have been real — showed how much the headline unemployment rate conceals.

This chapter is about unemployment: what it counts, what it misses, why it's never zero, and how to read it as evidence about what's actually happening in the labor market.

---

## What the rate counts — and what it doesn't

The Bureau of Labor Statistics defines the **unemployed** as people who don't have a job, are available to work, and have actively looked for work in the past four weeks.

The **employed** are people with a job — even if they're working only one hour per week.

The **labor force** is employed plus unemployed.

$$\text{Unemployment rate} = \frac{\text{Unemployed}}{\text{Labor force}}$$

$$\text{Labor force participation rate} = \frac{\text{Labor force}}{\text{Working-age population}}$$

The key distinction is between being *unemployed* and being *out of the labor force*. Staying-at-home parents, retirees, students, people with disabilities preventing work, people who have stopped looking after months of rejection — none of them are counted as unemployed. They are simply not in the labor force.

This produces an important measurement gap. When the labor market deteriorates badly, some people stop looking for jobs. They've been rejected so many times that they've given up. In the official statistics, they disappear from the unemployment count the moment they stop actively searching. The unemployment rate can actually *fall* when discouraged workers give up, even though the labor market has gotten worse for them.

During the 2009 Great Recession, the headline unemployment rate peaked at 10 percent. The BLS also publishes a broader measure — **U-6** — that includes people who want a job but have stopped looking (discouraged workers), plus people working part-time who want full-time work (the underemployed). U-6 peaked at about 17 percent during the same recession. The difference captures around 7 million additional people whose situation was invisible in the headline.

The **labor force participation rate** captures the other side of the gap. In the late 1990s, about 67 percent of U.S. working-age adults were in the labor force. In the mid-2020s, it's about 62 percent. Five percentage points of working-age adults — roughly 13 million people — have left the labor force and aren't counted as employed or unemployed. Some retired earlier than they would have. Some left during COVID and didn't return. Some are in the informal economy. The headline rate doesn't see them.

Reading unemployment requires reading both the rate *and* the participation rate together. A falling unemployment rate combined with a falling participation rate is a weaker signal than a falling unemployment rate with a stable or rising participation rate.

## The historical pattern

Some regularities in U.S. unemployment over the past seventy years are worth knowing before we build the framework.

**Normal range.** In peacetime expansions, the rate typically sits between 4 and 6 percent. It has rarely fallen sustainably below 3.5 percent, and outside of the Great Depression, rarely exceeded 11 percent.

**Recessions push the rate up sharply.** Every recession since the 1950s has produced a jump of 2 to 5 percentage points within 12 to 18 months of the peak. The speed of increase is consistently faster than the speed of recovery.

**Recoveries are slower than collapses.** Unemployment typically takes 3 to 7 years to return to pre-recession lows after a recession. The COVID recovery is an exception because the underlying disruption was specific — a health shock — and cleared quickly once it passed. Recoveries from financial crises, which damage the credit infrastructure that businesses depend on, are characteristically slower.

**Demographic gaps.** Younger workers, less-educated workers, and workers from racial minority groups consistently face higher unemployment rates than the population average. The gaps narrow in expansions and widen in recessions. These are not temporary statistical artifacts — they reflect durable structural features of the labor market.

**Cross-country variation.** European unemployment rates have historically been higher than U.S. rates — partly because European labor laws make it harder to fire workers, which makes firms more cautious about hiring in the first place. Some countries (Spain, Greece) have sustained double-digit unemployment for years.

## Three kinds of unemployment

The headline rate mixes together three distinct phenomena, and understanding them is essential because each has a different cause and a different policy response.

**Cyclical unemployment** rises and falls with the business cycle. When real GDP contracts in a recession, demand for goods and services drops, firms reduce production, and they lay off workers or fail to replace those who leave. This is the unemployment that shoots up in recessions and gradually recedes in recoveries.

The mechanism behind it runs through demand and then through prices and wages. Demand for the firm's product falls. In principle, the firm could lower prices to maintain sales — but lowering prices without lowering costs means collapsing margins. And lowering costs means lowering wages, which is harder than it sounds for reasons we'll come to. So the firm keeps prices up and reduces the quantity it sells, which means fewer workers. Cyclical unemployment is what happens when macroeconomic demand contracts and wages don't fall fast enough to restore equilibrium.

**Frictional unemployment** is the time it takes workers and jobs to find each other — even in a roaring economy. The new college graduate looking for her first position. The engineer who left a job she disliked and is searching for the next one. The worker who gave notice last week and starts the new job in three weeks. These people are unemployed by the BLS definition (no job, available, looking), but their unemployment reflects a *process* — the matching process between workers and openings — rather than a failure of the labor market.

Some frictional unemployment is actually healthy. It reflects workers taking the time to find a good match rather than accepting the first job available. A labor market with zero frictional unemployment would be one where workers took any job instantly, with no search — not an outcome associated with good welfare. The relevant policy question about frictional unemployment is whether the matching process is working efficiently: are job boards comprehensive? Are workers' skills accurately represented? Are geographic barriers preventing matches?

**Structural unemployment** is the kind that arises when workers' skills or location don't match available jobs. The 50-year-old steel-mill worker in a region whose mills have permanently closed. The truck driver whose long-haul routes are being automated. The specialist in a field where the industry has shrunk permanently.

Structural unemployment is categorically harder than frictional unemployment. Frictional unemployment resolves itself — the worker eventually finds a match. Structural unemployment requires retraining, relocation, or accepting a different type of work, all of which are costly, slow, and don't always succeed. The loss is often permanent in the sense that the worker may never return to earnings comparable to what they had. This is the unemployment associated with technological change, trade shifts, and industry decline — the kind that shows up in political discontent as well as economic statistics.

## Sticky wages — why unemployment persists

In the supply-and-demand model from Chapter 3, any surplus of workers willing to work at the going wage should drive the wage down until the market clears. More workers than jobs at the current wage: wages fall, employment rises, equilibrium restored. So why doesn't this happen? Why does unemployment — especially cyclical unemployment — persist for months or years rather than clearing out quickly?

Wages are **sticky** — they adjust slowly to changing market conditions, and especially slowly downward. Several mechanisms produce this stickiness, and they reinforce each other.

**Contracts.** Long-term employment contracts, collective-bargaining agreements, and even informal understandings create wages that can't be cut quickly. If the firm promised a wage for the duration of a contract, it pays that wage even when demand has fallen. The contract structure that provides security to workers also prevents wages from adjusting when the market turns.

**Morale effects.** Cutting wages produces resentment, reduced effort, and turnover. It signals to workers that the firm doesn't value their contribution. Many firms find it more efficient to lay off some workers at the current wage than to cut wages for all workers — even though, in terms of total wage bill, the effect might be similar. The distinction between layoffs and wage cuts is partly economic and partly about organizational culture, but it has real macroeconomic consequences.

**Efficiency wages.** Some firms pay above the market-clearing wage deliberately, to attract higher-quality workers, reduce costly turnover, and improve morale and effort. If this is the optimal strategy at the firm level, those wages don't fall even when unemployment exists. The high-wage firm isn't choosing between paying its current workers or paying the unemployed worker at a lower wage; it's maintaining a pay structure that it believes produces better results.

**Minimum-wage and regulatory floors.** Legal minimums prevent wages from falling below a set level regardless of market conditions.

**Adverse selection.** When wages are cut, the workers with the best outside options are most likely to leave. A firm that cuts wages may end up with a worse workforce — those who stayed because they couldn't get jobs elsewhere. This adverse selection problem can make wage cuts counterproductive even from the firm's purely economic perspective.

The result is that wages in a recession don't fall far enough or fast enough to clear the labor market. Workers who would accept a job at lower wages can't find them. Firms that would hire more workers at lower wages can't credibly commit to that wage. The unemployment persists until something else changes — demand recovers, or enough time passes that contracts expire, or the structural mismatch resolves by other means.

## The natural rate

Even in the deepest expansions, U.S. unemployment doesn't fall to zero. It typically bottoms around 3.5 to 4 percent. This residual represents frictional and structural unemployment — the unemployment that exists regardless of where the business cycle is — combined with some workers who are always between jobs, always in training, always in the matching process.

This residual is called the **natural rate of unemployment** (sometimes the NAIRU — Non-Accelerating Inflation Rate of Unemployment). The name is misleading; nothing about it is natural. It depends on labor-market institutions, education and training quality, demographics, the speed of technological change, and how well the matching infrastructure works. The natural rate in the U.S. was estimated around 7 percent in the late 1970s (high frictional unemployment, poor matching technology, a demographic surge of young workers entering the market). By the late 1990s, improved job boards, an older and more experienced workforce, and stronger labor-market institutions had pushed it toward 5 percent. Current estimates are around 4 percent.

The natural rate matters because it defines the target around which monetary policy operates. If the unemployment rate falls *below* the natural rate, the labor market is exceptionally tight — more jobs chasing workers than workers chasing jobs. Wages begin to rise rapidly. Inflation picks up. The Federal Reserve treats this as a signal to tighten monetary policy to cool the economy. If the rate rises *above* the natural rate, the economy is underperforming — cyclical unemployment exists that could be reduced by stimulus.

The challenge is that the natural rate is not directly observable. It can only be estimated, the estimates are imprecise, and they change over time. When the Fed misjudges the natural rate, it either tolerates unnecessary unemployment (if it thinks the natural rate is higher than it is) or tolerates unnecessary inflation (if it thinks it's lower). Getting this judgment right is central to the Fed's mandate.

## Synthesis

Pull back. Unemployment is not one thing. Three distinct phenomena — cyclical, frictional, structural — are compressed into the headline rate, and distinguishing them matters both for understanding the economy and for evaluating policy responses.

The headline rate itself understates the true state of the labor market because people who stop looking are excluded from the count. The U-6 measure and the labor force participation rate together give a more complete picture.

Sticky wages are the mechanism that connects business-cycle downturns to persistent unemployment. Wages don't fall fast enough; the market doesn't clear; workers remain unemployed while they could have been employed at lower wages. The stickiness is not irrational — it reflects contracts, morale effects, efficiency-wage strategies, and adverse selection — but it has macroeconomic consequences.

The natural rate — the floor of frictional and structural unemployment — is the target around which monetary and fiscal policy operate. It is not fixed. It has fallen substantially over the past forty years as U.S. demographics shifted, matching technology improved, and labor-market institutions evolved.

The COVID episode showed that when the underlying cause of a labor-market disruption is specific and short-lived, the recovery can be much faster than historical patterns suggest. It also showed that even in a fast recovery, there can be persistent changes — like the lower labor force participation rate — that don't fully reverse. Each recession and each recovery is different in its mechanics even as the same framework — cyclical, frictional, structural, sticky wages, natural rate — applies to all of them.

The next chapter takes up inflation: how it's measured, why it varies, and the relationship between unemployment and inflation that monetary policy has to navigate.
