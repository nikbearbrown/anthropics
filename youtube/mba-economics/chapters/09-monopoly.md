# Chapter 9 — Monopoly

*The market with one seller — and what it costs everyone else.*

---

In May of 1773, the British Parliament passed an obscure piece of corporate-rescue legislation. The East India Company — a London trading conglomerate that had effectively governed a third of the world for a century — was struggling financially. Parliament's solution was to grant the company a monopoly on tea sales to the American colonies, combined with a tax the colonies were already protesting.

By November, the colonists had had enough. On a December evening, men dressed as Mohawk warriors boarded three East India Company ships in Boston Harbor and dumped 342 chests of tea — roughly 92,000 pounds — into the water.

Two and a half years later, the colonies declared independence.

The proximate cause wasn't the tax. Taxes had been the issue for years. The proximate cause was the *monopoly grant*, which made it impossible for colonial merchants to compete in the tea trade and turned the colonies into a captive market for a company they had no political power over. A monopoly imposed by force, used to extract value from people with no exit option, became the spark for one of history's larger political revolutions.

Monopolies are not an economic curiosity. They are an institutional choice with consequences that have started wars, reshaped industries, and generated more antitrust litigation than any other single economic concept. This chapter explains why — starting with where monopolies come from, moving to how they set prices, and ending with a precise account of what they cost.

---

## Why monopolies exist

A monopoly is a market with one seller and no close substitutes. The interesting question is not what it is but why it persists. In Chapter 8 we saw that positive economic profit attracts entry, and entry competes profit down toward zero. For a monopoly to survive with sustained profit, something must block that process. The thing that blocks it is a **barrier to entry**.

Barriers come in several forms, and they are worth distinguishing carefully because the appropriate policy response depends on which kind you're dealing with.

**Natural monopoly.** Some industries have cost structures where one firm can serve the entire market at lower average cost than two firms could. Water utilities are the textbook case: laying pipe is an enormous, almost entirely fixed cost; delivering an additional gallon once the pipe exists is cheap. Two competing water companies, each running their own pipe network, would each face higher average cost than a single firm covering the whole city. The monopoly emerges not from anyone's intention but from the economics of the cost curve — specifically, from an LRAC that keeps falling across the entire relevant range of output. When that's true, splitting the market between competitors makes everyone worse off. Same logic applies to natural gas distribution, electricity transmission, and the original telephone network.

The natural monopoly creates a genuine policy puzzle that we'll return to: you can't simply let competition solve it, because competition would produce a higher-cost outcome.

**Legal monopoly.** A government grants exclusive rights. Patents give an inventor twenty years of monopoly protection on a drug or technology. Copyrights give an author decades of monopoly on a creative work. Trademarks give exclusive use of a brand. Licenses and franchises grant geographic exclusivity. These are not accidents — they are deliberate policy choices. The justification is that without the period of monopoly profit, the invention, the book, or the investment might not happen. The cost is the period of higher prices. Whether the trade-off is worth it depends on the industry, and it is a genuine empirical question, not a settled one.

**Control of a key resource.** If one firm owns essentially all of a critical input, no one else can produce. Alcoa controlled most of the world's bauxite for decades. De Beers controlled most of the world's rough diamond supply for the better part of the twentieth century, deliberately managing scarcity to sustain prices that bore almost no relationship to production cost. Resource-control monopolies are now less common than they once were, but they remain decisive when they exist.

**Network effects.** A product becomes more valuable as more people use it. The first telephone was useless. The hundredth made the network worth having. The millionth made it nearly impossible to give up. Social networks, payment networks, and operating-system platforms all exhibit this property. A new entrant faces not just the cost of building a competing product but a chicken-and-egg problem: no one wants the new product until other people are on it. Network effects can produce monopoly-like dominance with no economies of scale, no IP protection, and no resource control — just the self-reinforcing logic of adoption.

In real markets, multiple barriers often operate simultaneously. Apple has economies of scale in hardware *and* network effects in the App Store *and* IP protection *and* significant switching costs *and* brand power. The barriers compound. Disentangling them is part of what antitrust investigations try to do.

## How a monopoly decides

The competitive firm from Chapter 8 was a *price taker*. It faced a horizontal demand curve at the market price. Its marginal revenue was always exactly equal to the market price. It produced where P = MC.

The monopoly is a *price maker*. Because it is the entire market, the demand curve it faces is the *market* demand curve — downward-sloping. To sell more, it must lower the price. And here is where the analysis becomes genuinely interesting: when the monopoly lowers the price to sell one more unit, it lowers the price not just on that new unit but on every unit it would have sold at the previous price.

This means the monopoly's **marginal revenue is below its price**. Every time the monopoly cuts the price to expand output by one unit, it gains revenue from the new sale but loses revenue from the price reduction on all existing sales. The two effects work against each other, and the net gain — the marginal revenue — is less than the price received for the new unit.

The marginal revenue curve lies below the demand curve at every quantity. For a linear demand curve, the marginal revenue curve has the same intercept but twice the slope — it falls twice as fast.

The numbers make this concrete. Suppose a monopolist faces a demand curve: at $10, it can sell 1 unit; at $9, 2 units; at $8, 3 units; at $7, 4 units. Total revenue at each level: 1×$10 = $10; 2×$9 = $18; 3×$8 = $24; 4×$7 = $28. Marginal revenue — the change in total revenue from each additional unit sold — is $10, $8, $6, $4.

The gap is already visible. To sell the 4th unit, the firm receives $7 from the buyer. But the marginal revenue is only $4, because selling that 4th unit forced the firm to lower the price from $8 to $7 on the 3 units it was already selling. The price cut on existing units eats into the new-unit revenue.

<!-- → [TABLE: Monopoly marginal revenue example — columns: Quantity | Price | Total Revenue | Marginal Revenue | Note; rows: 1 / $10 / $10 / $10 / MR = Price (first unit); 2 / $9 / $18 / $8 / MR < Price; 3 / $8 / $24 / $6 / Gap widens; 4 / $7 / $28 / $4 / MR is $3 below price; caption: "The MR column is always less than the Price column after the first unit — because selling more requires a price cut on everything already sold"] -->

The profit-maximizing rule is the same for a monopoly as for any firm: produce up to the quantity where MR = MC. But because MR < P, this quantity is *lower* than the quantity where P = MC would hold. The firm does not produce up to the socially efficient level. After finding its profit-maximizing quantity via MR = MC, the monopoly charges the highest price the demand curve will bear at that quantity — it reads the price off the demand curve, not off the MR curve.

Two consequences follow immediately. The monopoly produces *less* than a competitive industry would. The monopoly charges *more* than a competitive industry would.

The gap between Pm and Pc, and between Qm and Qc, is where the costs live"] -->

The gap between price and marginal cost — between what consumers pay and what the last unit actually costs to produce — is what economists call the *markup*. In perfect competition the markup is zero. In monopoly it's positive, and the size of the markup depends on the elasticity of demand. A monopoly facing inelastic demand can sustain a larger markup; one facing elastic demand cannot. The more captive the customers, the more that can be charged over cost.

## What it costs the rest of us

Now put the two pictures next to each other.

A perfectly competitive industry produces up to the point where price equals marginal cost — the quantity where the demand curve crosses the supply curve. At that point, the marginal value of the good to consumers exactly equals its marginal cost of production. Every unit that would benefit anyone more than it costs to make gets made. Total surplus is maximized. Nothing is left on the table.

A monopoly produces only up to MR = MC, then charges the demand-curve price at that lower quantity. That price is higher than the marginal cost of producing the last unit. The gap — P minus MC — represents units that *would* be worth making (consumers would pay more than the cost of production) but the monopoly will not make them, because making them would require lowering the price on all units and the marginal revenue from doing so has gone negative.

This is the **deadweight loss of monopoly**: the value of transactions that would have made both parties better off and simply don't happen. On the diagram it appears as a triangle, bounded by the demand curve, the MC curve, and the vertical line at the monopoly's quantity. That area is social value destroyed — not transferred, destroyed.

<!-- → [DIAGRAM: Monopoly welfare analysis — same axes as pricing diagram; shaded rectangle between Pm and ATC at Qm labeled "Monopoly profit (transfer from consumers)"; shaded triangle between Qm and Qc, bounded above by demand curve and below by MC curve, labeled "Deadweight loss (value destroyed)"; remaining consumer surplus above Pm labeled "Consumer surplus"; caption: "Two shaded regions, two different things: the rectangle is a redistribution, the triangle is an erasure — it belonged to no one after the monopoly chose not to produce those units"] -->

The rectangle between the monopoly price and the average cost curve, at the monopoly quantity, is the monopoly's economic profit. This is *not* deadweight loss — it is a *transfer* from consumers to the monopolist. Whether that transfer is troubling depends on values; it doesn't represent aggregate efficiency loss. The deadweight loss triangle is the thing that troubles economists who think about efficiency, because it's value that belongs to no one — it evaporated when the monopoly chose not to produce those units.

For scale: estimates of total deadweight loss from monopoly power in the U.S. economy range from under 1 percent of GDP in classical estimates to several percent in more recent work that tries to account for how modern platform firms exercise market power. The estimates vary because measuring a competitive counterfactual is genuinely hard. But monopoly is never free, even when the firm earned its position through legitimate means.

There is a second cost worth naming: productive inefficiency. Without competitive pressure, a monopoly has weaker incentives to control costs. Harvey Leibenstein coined the term *X-inefficiency* in the 1960s for this phenomenon — the tendency of firms shielded from competition to operate above the bottom of their cost curves. A competitive firm that wastes resources goes out of business. A monopoly that wastes resources continues. The static welfare loss from deadweight loss is compounded by this dynamic cost slackness.

## The natural monopoly dilemma

Natural monopoly produces a regulatory problem that has no clean solution, and it's worth naming precisely.

If you let a natural monopoly set its own price, it uses the MR = MC rule, charges above MC, produces below the efficient quantity, and earns economic profit at consumers' expense. That's the standard monopoly story.

If you force it to price at marginal cost — the textbook efficiency condition — the price will be *below* average cost. Why? Because in a natural monopoly, average cost is still falling over the entire relevant range. When ATC is falling, MC lies below it. Marginal-cost pricing puts the regulated price below average cost. The firm loses money on every unit. It exits the market, or requires permanent subsidy.

Real regulatory practice compromises. The most common solution is *average-cost pricing* — set the price equal to average cost, so the firm breaks even. This prevents monopoly profit and prevents losses, but price still exceeds MC and allocative efficiency is not achieved. It also blunts cost-control incentives: if the regulator guarantees cost recovery, the firm has limited reason to keep costs down.

Other approaches include capping the rate of return on invested capital (common in U.S. utility regulation), operating the asset as a public entity, or auctioning the monopoly franchise periodically to the firm that promises the lowest price. Each trades off efficiency, incentives, and political accountability differently. There is no free lunch in natural monopoly regulation — the question is which trade-off is least bad for the specific situation.

## Synthesis

Pull back. A monopoly is what happens when the conditions of Chapter 8 fail — when entry is blocked, when one firm has structural cost advantages no competitor can match, when network effects concentrate adoption, when patents exist for good reasons but produce real costs. In each case, one seller faces the full market demand curve rather than a horizontal price line.

Because the monopoly faces the downward-sloping demand curve, its marginal revenue is below its price. Because MR < P, it produces less than the socially efficient quantity and charges more than the socially efficient price. The gap produces a deadweight loss — value that would have existed in a competitive market and doesn't in this one. The monopoly's profit is a transfer; the deadweight loss is the waste.

The deeper point is this: the case for competitive markets is not a case for doing nothing. It is a case for *competition itself*, which sometimes requires active institutional work to maintain — antitrust enforcement, patent expiration, regulation, occasionally public ownership. The Boston Tea Party was the political response to a monopoly imposed by an empire on a captive market. Modern antitrust is the institutional response to monopolies that arise from technology, network effects, and strategic acquisition. The mechanics differ. The underlying problem is identical: one seller is too few, and the costs fall on everyone else.

The next chapter moves into the territory where most real industries actually live — markets with several firms that are neither perfectly competitive nor pure monopolies. That's where most of the interesting action is.

Then ask: at which quantity is marginal revenue zero, and what does that tell you about the price elasticity at that point?

Ask the LLM to identify (a) the consumer surplus transferred to the monopoly as profit, and (b) the deadweight loss to society. A correct answer distinguishes the two clearly.

**Exercise 4 — Argue both sides of a patent.** Ask an LLM to argue both sides: why a 20-year drug patent is justified, and why it's harmful. Evaluate which argument is stronger, and which costs and benefits the LLM tends to underweight. The exercise teaches that legal monopolies create real value and real costs simultaneously.
