# Chapter 17 — Financial Markets

*How savings find their way to the projects that need them — and what happens when the system breaks.*

---

In 2006, the total value of U.S. home equity reached $13 trillion. Three years later, in 2009, it had fallen to roughly $6 trillion — a destruction of about $7 trillion of household wealth in 36 months. The financial crisis that produced this collapse pushed the U.S. into the deepest recession since the 1930s, triggered emergency interventions by the Federal Reserve and the Treasury, and reshaped financial regulation for the following decade.

The crisis began in one specific market — mortgage-backed securities — and then flowed through every other financial market in the economy. Banks failed. Credit markets froze. Money-market funds threatened to "break the buck." Stock markets fell roughly 50 percent. The interconnections that normally make the financial system useful — matching savers to borrowers, pricing risk, allocating capital — became the channels through which one market's collapse spread to all the others.

This chapter is about that system in its normal operation, so that when it fails, you can read the failure.

The financial market is the institutional machinery that takes savings — money households and firms aren't currently spending — and routes it to entities that want to borrow or raise capital. When it works, it is one of the most powerful productivity-enhancing inventions in human history: capital flows from people who have it but can't use it productively to people who can. When it doesn't, it can take down whole economies. Understanding both requires understanding the instruments, the incentives, and the information structures that make the system go.

---

## How firms raise money

A firm that wants to invest — build a factory, develop software, expand into a new market — needs capital. Four main sources.

**Reinvested profits.** The firm uses its own retained earnings. This is the cheapest source in one sense: no external claim to pay back, no interest rate, no equity dilution. It is also limited to what the firm has already earned, which makes it inadequate for anything that needs to move faster than profitability allows.

**Bank loans.** The firm borrows from a bank. The bank intermediates between depositors (savers) and borrowers, performing the credit-evaluation work and bearing some of the default risk. A loan has specific terms — interest rate, repayment schedule, usually collateral — and is the dominant financing channel for small and medium-sized businesses.

**Bonds.** The firm issues bonds — contracts to pay back a specified amount on a specified future date, with periodic interest payments along the way. Bond buyers are lenders who get a defined cash flow. The firm gets a lump sum now in exchange for those future payments.

**Stocks.** The firm sells equity — fractional ownership claims on the company's assets and future profits. Stock investors don't get fixed payments. They get a share of whatever the company is worth and earns, after everyone else has been paid.

The choice among these depends on the firm's stage, profitability, and strategic situation. Early-stage startups rely heavily on equity — founders, angels, venture capital — because they don't have the cash flows to support debt. A startup that borrowed to fund development would face debt service payments at exactly the moment it's burning cash. Mature, profitable firms often prefer bonds, which are cheap when rates are low and don't dilute existing shareholders. The mix changes over a firm's lifecycle.

The aggregate scale of these flows is enormous. The total value of U.S. corporate stocks is in the tens of trillions. The U.S. corporate-bond market is similarly large. The financial system is the circulatory system of the modern economy: oxygen reaches tissue by capillary, and capital reaches firms by these channels.

## Bonds — promises to pay

A bond is a contract with three defined terms: the **face value** (also called principal — the amount borrowed), the **coupon** (the periodic interest payment), and the **maturity date** (when the principal gets paid back).

A simple example. A corporation issues a ten-year bond with $1,000 face value and a 5 percent coupon. The buyer pays $1,000 today. The corporation pays $50 per year for ten years, then returns $1,000 at the end. Over ten years, the buyer collects $1,500 on a $1,000 investment.

Bonds trade in secondary markets after they're issued. When they trade, prices change as market interest rates change, and this is the fact about bonds that trips people most often.

Here is the logic. If new bonds are being issued at 6 percent, an existing bond paying 5 percent is less attractive. Investors would rather buy the new bond. The only way to sell the old 5 percent bond is to lower its price until the effective return (coupon payments plus the gain from buying below face value) equals the going 6 percent rate. So the price falls. Conversely, if new bonds are being issued at 4 percent, the old 5 percent bond pays more than alternatives. Buyers bid up its price.

The rule: **bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions.** When the Fed raises rates, existing bonds lose market value. When the Fed cuts rates, existing bonds appreciate. The longer the remaining maturity, the larger the price swing for any given rate change. A thirty-year bond is far more sensitive to rate moves than a one-year note.

The **yield** is the actual return an investor earns, accounting for both the coupon payments and any difference between the purchase price and the face value. Buy a $1,000 bond at face value with a $50 coupon and your yield is exactly 5 percent. Buy the same bond for $900 — because rates have risen and its price fell — and your yield is higher than 5 percent: you collect $50/year coupons on a $900 investment, and you'll receive $1,000 at maturity. Yield and price are inverses of each other.

Bond risk comes in two forms. **Default risk** is the possibility that the issuer won't pay. U.S. Treasury bonds are considered essentially default-free — the government can always print dollars to meet dollar obligations. Corporate bonds carry default risk that varies by issuer; rating agencies assign grades from AAA (very safe) down to D (in default). Bonds rated below BBB- are called high-yield or "junk" — they offer higher returns to compensate for higher probability of failure. **Interest-rate risk** is the possibility that rising rates will reduce the market value of a bond you already hold, even if it never defaults. You can avoid realizing this loss by holding to maturity, but if you need the money before then, you're selling in an adverse market.

## Stocks — claims on whatever's left

A stock is fundamentally different from a bond. The stockholder doesn't have a claim to a fixed amount. They have a claim to a fraction of whatever is left over after the firm pays its employees, suppliers, creditors, bondholders, and taxes. This is the **residual claim**, and its defining feature is that it can be very large or nothing at all.

Stockholders benefit two ways. **Dividends** are cash payments the firm distributes to shareholders — a portion of profits paid out rather than reinvested. **Capital gains** are the appreciation of the stock's price, which reflects the market's revised estimate of the firm's future earning power. Some companies pay generous dividends and grow slowly — utilities, mature consumer companies. Others pay nothing and grow fast — tech firms in development, biotechs awaiting FDA approval. The choice is a function of the company's capital needs and the tax treatment in its investors' hands.

Stocks carry more risk than bonds for two structural reasons. First, the residual-claim position means stockholders absorb losses first. When a company goes bankrupt, bondholders typically recover some fraction of what they're owed from the remaining assets; stockholders typically recover nothing. The bond contract is senior; the equity claim is junior. Second, stock returns are inherently variable. They depend on profits, which depend on competition, technology, the macroeconomy, and chance — all of which fluctuate, sometimes wildly and without warning.

The reward for accepting that risk is, historically, higher average returns. U.S. stocks have returned roughly 7 percent per year in real terms over very long periods — substantially higher than the 1 to 2 percent real return on long-term Treasury bonds. The gap between stock and bond returns is called the **equity risk premium**. It compensates investors for the volatility and the residual-claim exposure. If stocks and bonds paid the same return, no rational investor would hold stocks. The premium is what makes holding them rational.

## Diversification — the only free lunch

Different assets don't all move together. Some industries do well when others do poorly. Technology stocks may rise when energy stocks fall. Foreign markets often diverge from U.S. markets. By holding a portfolio of many securities, an investor can reduce risk *without* reducing expected return. This is **diversification**, and it is sometimes described as the only free lunch in finance, because you get something (lower risk) for nothing (no sacrifice of expected return).

The mathematics behind it is elegant. Portfolio variance depends on two things: the variance of each individual asset and how the assets move together — their **covariance**. If two assets are perfectly positively correlated (always move in exactly the same direction by the same amount), holding both reduces nothing — you're just duplicating the same risk profile. If two assets are uncorrelated (move independently of each other), holding both reduces variance because they don't all fall at the same time. If two assets are negatively correlated — they tend to move opposite directions — holding both reduces variance even further.

The practical result: a portfolio of fifty randomly chosen stocks has substantially less variance than any one of those stocks held alone, even though the expected return is approximately the same. The reduction comes from averaging out the idiosyncratic ups and downs — the firm-specific shocks that are uncorrelated across companies. What diversification cannot eliminate is **systematic risk** — the market-wide shocks that hit all stocks in the same direction at the same time. The 2008 crash, the March 2020 COVID selloff — these affected every portfolio, no matter how diversified. Diversification removes the risk of owning any particular company; it doesn't protect you from owning the economy.

**Mutual funds** and **exchange-traded funds (ETFs)** exist primarily to make diversification accessible. Buy one share of an S&P 500 index fund and you own a tiny piece of 500 large U.S. companies. Buy a global bond index fund and you hold a slice of dozens of countries' debt. The fund pools investors' money, buys the securities, and charges a small fee. For most investors, this is the most efficient structure available.

## The random walk and efficient markets

The question every student eventually asks: can you beat the market by picking winning stocks?

The honest answer: almost certainly not, on average, after costs.

The argument is in two steps.

The **efficient-market hypothesis** says current stock prices already reflect all publicly available information. If there's a publicly known reason to think a stock is undervalued, professional investors have already acted on it — they've bought the stock, pushed up the price, and eliminated the undervaluation. If there's a reason to think it's overvalued, they've sold it. In equilibrium, prices reflect the best available collective estimate of each company's future cash flows. There are no $20 bills on the sidewalk, because someone would have picked them up already.

The **random-walk theory** says future price changes occur in response to *new information*, which by definition cannot be predicted in advance. Old price patterns contain no reliable information about what prices will do next. The changes in a stock's price behave statistically like a random walk — each step unpredictable from the ones before.

Both of these are simplifications. Markets are not perfectly efficient. There's evidence that prices sometimes diverge from fundamentals, that some traders possess private information not yet reflected in prices, that bubbles and crashes happen. But the simplifications are right enough to explain the observed facts: most actively managed mutual funds underperform low-cost index funds over long periods, after fees. Most individual stock-pickers do worse than the market. Most expert forecasters of individual stock prices produce track records indistinguishable from chance.

Why do so many people believe they can beat the market? Partly selection bias: the people who beat the market tell the story; the people who lose don't. Partly attribution error: in a bull market, almost everyone beats a cash benchmark, and people attribute this to skill. Partly the financial industry's incentive to sell the belief in active management, since index funds charge almost nothing and active managers charge much more.

The practical conclusions are short. Diversify broadly. Don't try to pick individual winners. Use low-fee index funds. Hold for the long term. Don't trade on news, since the news is already in the price by the time you read it. These are boring conclusions. They are also the conclusions that win, on average, over long periods.

## When the system breaks

The 2007–2009 crisis was a failure of the financial system in several places simultaneously, and working through it makes the structure of the system visible in a way normal operation doesn't.

The trigger was the U.S. housing market. Home prices had risen sharply through the early 2000s, fueled partly by mortgage lending that, in retrospect, had abandoned basic underwriting standards. Adjustable-rate loans with teaser rates that reset sharply upward after two years. "No-documentation" loans where income wasn't verified. Sub-prime borrowers approved for mortgages they had little prospect of repaying. As long as house prices kept rising, defaults stayed low — borrowers in trouble could refinance or sell. When prices stopped rising and fell, the loans began defaulting at rates that no risk model had considered possible.

The mortgages had been packaged into **mortgage-backed securities** and sold to investors worldwide. The packaging mechanism — with sophisticated tranching and credit-default swap insurance — had been designed to convert risky loans into very safe bonds. When the underlying loans defaulted at unanticipated rates, the securities collapsed in value, and the institutions holding them suffered losses they hadn't modeled.

The losses cascaded through the system's interconnections. Banks short on capital cut lending. Businesses short on credit cut hiring and investment. Households with shrunken home equity cut spending. The real economy contracted. The Federal Reserve and Treasury intervened at enormous scale — emergency liquidity facilities, capital injections into banks, the nationalization of Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, the bailout of AIG. The interventions prevented a complete collapse of the banking system. They also generated lasting controversy about who bore the costs and who captured the gains from the rescue.

The pattern here — excessive leverage, misaligned incentives, mispriced risk, cascading failures — recurs in financial crises with enough regularity that financial economists treat crises as a structural feature of the system rather than a series of unrelated accidents. The savings-and-loan collapse of the 1980s, the Mexican peso crisis of 1994, the Asian financial crisis of 1997, Long-Term Capital Management in 1998 — each has a different proximate cause and the same underlying structure. The regulatory responses try to reduce the probability and cost of the next crisis, typically by addressing the failure mode of the previous one. The system evolves faster than the regulation.

## Synthesis

Pull back. The financial system intermediates between savers and borrowers, routing capital to its highest-value uses. The four financing channels — retained earnings, bank loans, bonds, stocks — serve different needs at different firm stages. Bonds are debt with defined cash flows and two sources of risk (default and interest-rate). Stocks are equity with residual claims, higher historical returns, and higher volatility. Diversification reduces risk without reducing expected return. The efficient-market hypothesis explains why individual stock-picking doesn't beat the market on average — and the empirical record of active managers is consistent with it.

The same interconnections that make the system efficient in normal times make it fragile in stress. Risk that appears diversified in calm markets can become correlated in a crisis. Models built on historical volatility fail when the underlying distribution shifts. Leverage amplifies both gains and losses. These aren't bugs in a system that could be fixed with better management; they are structural features of a system that prices risk under uncertainty, using institutions built by people with imperfect information and conflicting incentives.

For an individual investor, the chapter's lessons are short and not glamorous. Diversify. Use low-fee index funds. Don't try to time the market or pick winners. Hold for the long term. Treat any hot tip as a warning sign. The investing strategies that win over long periods are the boring ones.
