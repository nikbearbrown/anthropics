# Chapter 15 — Poverty and Economic Inequality

*Two different questions about how a society's income gets distributed — and what to do about each.*

---

In September 2011, a group of protesters set up tents in Zuccotti Park in lower Manhattan. They called themselves Occupy Wall Street. Their slogan was a number: "We are the 99 percent." The number referred to the income share captured by the top 1 percent of U.S. households — roughly 20 percent of all pre-tax income by 2011, about double its share in 1980.

The movement faded within months. The number stuck.

By 2024, "the one percent" was a phrase in everyday political conversation, business journalism, and academic research that had barely existed in 1980. What Occupy changed was not policy but framing: it established that inequality is a distinct feature of the U.S. economy worth measuring and debating, separate from poverty. That distinction is the subject of this chapter.

Two different questions. *Poverty* asks how many people fall below some threshold of acceptable living standards — how many are in absolute or near-absolute deprivation. *Inequality* asks how the total income of the society is distributed across the population — how the gap between top and bottom evolves over time. The two are related but not the same. A society can reduce poverty while leaving inequality high — China since 1980 is the clearest example. A society can have low inequality with significant poverty — some low-income countries are there. Most political debate conflates them, sometimes deliberately. The first move of serious analysis is to keep them separate.

---

## Poverty — measuring deprivation

The **poverty line** is a threshold income below which a household is classified as being in poverty. The U.S. poverty line, as of the early 2020s, sits at roughly $14,000 for a single-person household and roughly $30,000 for a four-person household, updated annually for inflation.

The official line was set by Mollie Orshansky at the Social Security Administration in the 1960s, using a method that has stayed largely unchanged for sixty years. Take the cost of a basic food budget. Multiply by three — because food was estimated to be about one-third of household expenditures at the time. Adjust annually for inflation. That's it.

The method has been criticized continuously, on grounds that are mostly right:

Food's share of household budgets has fallen well below one-third since the 1960s. Housing costs now dominate, and they vary enormously — a poverty-line income in rural Mississippi buys something very different than the same income in Manhattan. The line uses pre-tax cash income, which excludes the EITC, food stamps, Medicaid, and housing vouchers — benefits that substantially change actual living standards for recipients. The Census Bureau publishes an alternative, the Supplemental Poverty Measure (SPM), that addresses some of these shortcomings. The SPM typically shows a somewhat different poverty rate than the official measure, and most researchers regard it as conceptually superior, even though the official measure remains the one most often cited in political discussions.

U.S. poverty rates over the past sixty years tell a story with a sharp middle section and a long flat end. Poverty fell sharply between 1959 and 1973 — from roughly 22 percent to roughly 11 percent — driven by general economic growth, the expansion of Social Security, and the early War on Poverty programs. From 1973 to 2019, the rate bounced between 11 and 15 percent: higher in recessions, lower in expansions, but with no sustained downward trend for nearly five decades. The recent (early 2020s) official rate sits around 11–12 percent.

Poverty is not evenly distributed. Children face higher poverty rates than working-age adults. Single-parent households, especially those headed by women, face sharply higher rates than two-parent households. Black and Hispanic Americans face higher rates than white Americans. People with disabilities face higher rates than those without. These disparities reflect both labor-market outcomes and program design, and they persist even in periods when aggregate poverty rates fall.

## Inequality — measuring the spread

Poverty asks who is below the line. Inequality asks how the whole distribution looks.

The most common summary measure is the **Gini coefficient**, derived from the **Lorenz curve**. The construction is worth understanding, because the intuition is in the geometry.

Sort every household in the country from poorest to richest. Plot cumulative income share on the vertical axis against cumulative population share on the horizontal axis. Start in the lower-left corner. Walk right: at 10 percent of the population (the poorest tenth), mark how much income they have collectively. At 20 percent, mark their cumulative share. Continue to the upper-right corner, where 100 percent of the population has 100 percent of the income.

In a perfectly equal society — every household earns exactly the same — the curve is a straight diagonal line. Every 10 percent of the population holds exactly 10 percent of the income. In an actual society, the curve bows below that diagonal. The poorest 10 percent hold less than 10 percent of income; the poorest 50 percent hold less than 50 percent. The further below the diagonal the curve bows, the more unequal the society.

The Gini coefficient is the ratio of two areas: the area between the diagonal and the actual Lorenz curve, divided by the total area below the diagonal. It runs from 0 (perfect equality — the curve is the diagonal) to 1 (perfect inequality — one household has everything and the curve hugs the bottom). Real economies land in between. The U.S. Gini for household income, post-tax, is around 0.40. Sweden's is around 0.27. Brazil's is around 0.53. The U.S. number has risen from roughly 0.35 in the late 1970s — a 14 percent increase over four decades — driven by the wage divergence we examined in Chapter 14.

A useful complementary measure is the **quintile share**. Sort the population into five equal groups and look at what fraction of total income each receives. In the U.S., as of recent data, the bottom quintile receives roughly 3 percent of pre-tax income. The top quintile receives about 50 percent. The top 5 percent alone receives about 22 percent. The top 1 percent about 20 percent. This is the number Occupy Wall Street was pointing at.

Some things these measures don't capture. The Gini and quintile shares are snapshots. They don't capture *mobility* — the chance that someone in the bottom quintile this year is in a different quintile in ten years. They don't capture *life-cycle* differences, since the young and old earn systematically less than the middle-aged in any society. They don't capture *consumption* inequality, which is smaller than income inequality because of redistribution and savings patterns. And they say nothing about non-income dimensions of wellbeing — health, education, social inclusion, autonomy, time.

That said: the U.S. has unusually high inequality among wealthy democracies. Within the OECD, only Mexico, Chile, and Turkey have higher Gini coefficients. The Northern European countries — Denmark, Sweden, Norway, Finland — cluster at the low end. The contrast reflects deliberate policy choices about labor-market institutions, taxation, and social insurance, not fundamental differences in pre-tax wage distributions. In fact, pre-tax wage distributions in the U.S. and Scandinavia are surprisingly similar; the divergence in post-tax inequality comes almost entirely from different redistribution choices.

## The poverty trap

A structural problem arises at the intersection of poverty programs and work incentives.

Suppose a government provides $20,000 per year in cash assistance to households below the poverty line, phased out at a rate of $1 in lost benefits for every $1 in earnings. The household gets $20,000 if it doesn't work. If it earns $20,000, it has lost $20,000 in benefits. Net change in household income: zero. The program imposes an effective marginal tax rate of 100 percent on the first $20,000 of earnings, leaving zero financial incentive to work within that range.

This is the **poverty trap**. Real safety-net programs rarely have 100 percent phase-out rates individually, but the *combined* effective marginal tax rate from stacking multiple programs — cash assistance, food stamps, housing vouchers, healthcare subsidies, childcare subsidies — can exceed 70 or even 80 percent in some income ranges. Households moving from welfare to work find that a full-time job leaves them only marginally better off than before. The trap is not a failure of the recipient's motivation. It is a feature of the program design.

The most influential structural response is the **Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC)**, introduced in 1975 and substantially expanded since. The EITC is a wage subsidy — the government pays a credit that *increases* with earnings up to a threshold, then phases out gradually. A worker earning $15,000 receives more from the EITC than a worker earning $5,000; the structure rewards work rather than penalizing it. The phase-out is gradual enough that the implicit marginal rate stays well below the trap level.

The empirical record on the EITC is strong. It substantially reduces poverty among working households. It measurably increases labor-force participation, particularly among single mothers — the group facing the most severe pre-EITC poverty traps. It distributes benefits more directly to households with earned income than almost any other U.S. anti-poverty program. And it has maintained bipartisan support for decades: the political right values its connection to work, the political left values its income support. Its limitations are real: it reaches only those who work, it helps relatively little with the deep poverty of households with no labor-market attachment, and the phase-out zone itself — where the credit begins declining — creates a band of elevated effective marginal rates.

## The U.S. safety net

The U.S. anti-poverty system is a patchwork of programs, each with its own eligibility rules, benefit formulas, and administering agencies. No single program covers everything; all of them together cover something.

**TANF (Temporary Assistance for Needy Families)** provides cash assistance to low-income families with children, with work requirements and time limits. It is the successor to the older AFDC program and is substantially smaller in reach since the 1996 welfare reform.

**SNAP (Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program)** is food stamps — currently serving about 40 million Americans, with eligibility based on income and household size.

**Medicaid** provides health insurance to low-income individuals and families. It's a joint federal-state program; the Affordable Care Act expanded eligibility substantially in states that chose to participate.

**The EITC** is described above — the wage-subsidy strand of the system.

**SSI (Supplemental Security Income)** provides cash to elderly, blind, and disabled people with limited income and assets.

**Section 8 vouchers and public housing** provide rental assistance to low-income households, though demand vastly exceeds supply.

The combined system does substantial work. Estimates using the SPM — which counts in-kind benefits — suggest it reduces the poverty rate by roughly 5 to 10 percentage points relative to a counterfactual without any of it. Using the official measure, which doesn't count most of the benefits, the effect looks smaller. The discrepancy is largely a measurement artifact: the system works more than the official measure suggests.

## The equality-efficiency trade-off

Now the harder question.

The case for more redistribution has both utilitarian and equity components. On the utilitarian side: a dollar transferred from a rich household to a poor one likely produces a larger gain in wellbeing than loss — diminishing marginal utility of income is a reasonable empirical assumption, even if the precise rate is contested. On the equity side: the social-contract argument that those who benefit most from market institutions, legal systems, and public goods owe something back to the system that makes their outcomes possible.

The case against — or more precisely, the case for caution — is that redistribution has efficiency costs. High marginal tax rates reduce work effort, savings, investment, and entrepreneurial risk-taking at the margin. Safety-net programs with high phase-out rates create poverty traps. The empirical question is *how large* these efficiency costs are per dollar transferred.

This is what Arthur Okun called "the big tradeoff" in 1975: the idea that income redistribution is like moving resources in a leaky bucket. Some value leaks out in the process. How much leaks depends on what specific intervention you're designing. How large a leak you accept in exchange for less inequality is a values question that economics can frame but cannot answer.

The simple version of the trade-off has limits that matter for policy. Some redistributive interventions — well-designed early childhood education, the EITC, broad healthcare access — appear to *increase* economic growth in the long run by improving human capital and reducing the drag of concentrated poverty. Others — poorly designed transfer programs that trap recipients, high marginal rates on capital in small open economies — may reduce growth more than they reduce inequality. The trade-off exists, but its magnitude depends entirely on what specific policy you're discussing.

One additional complication: the U.S. inequality story since 1980 is largely a *pre-tax* income story. The Gini has risen substantially in pre-tax, pre-transfer income. The U.S. redistributive system is moderately progressive, but it redistributes a market income distribution that has become more unequal. Redistribution can offset some of that shift, but it cannot directly change the wage dynamics — globalization, technological change, declining unionization, growing market power — that are generating it. Addressing *pre-tax* inequality requires either rebuilding labor-market institutions, changing trade and technology policy, or some combination. The U.S. has mostly pursued redistribution as the response, with results visible in the data Occupy Wall Street pointed at: the post-tax inequality is lower than pre-tax, but both have risen.

## Synthesis

Pull back. Two distinct questions; two sets of data; one big trade-off.

*Poverty* — measured by the official or supplemental threshold — has been roughly stable since 1973, declining in growth periods and rising in recessions, with no sustained downward trend despite decades of safety-net spending. The most effective anti-poverty intervention in the U.S. by most estimates is the EITC, precisely because it avoids the worst poverty-trap mechanics by rewarding work rather than penalizing it.

*Inequality* — measured by the Gini, the income shares, and the top-1-percent statistics that Occupy made familiar — has risen substantially since 1980. It reflects both the wage divergence documented in Chapter 14 and the growth in capital income at the top. Redistribution has offset some of it; the underlying pre-tax trend has been stronger.

The equality-efficiency trade-off is real but not fixed. Some interventions sit closer to pure efficiency gains; others are pure transfers; most are mixed. The honest policy brief identifies where a specific proposal sits on that curve and what evidence would move it.

The bigger structural challenge is that the tools most suited to reducing inequality — labor-market institutions, trade policy, technology governance — are largely outside what the safety net does. The safety net redistributes what the market has already generated. Whether that redistribution is sufficient, or whether it should be accompanied by changes to the forces generating the pre-tax distribution in the first place, is the question that remains open at the end of this chapter and throughout the policy debates that follow it.
