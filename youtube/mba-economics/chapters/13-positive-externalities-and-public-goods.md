# Chapter 13 — Positive Externalities and Public Goods

*When the market underproduces — vaccines, basic research, lighthouses, and why your phone's GPS is free.*

---

In your pocket right now is a piece of technology that knows where you are on the surface of the Earth, accurate to within a few meters, anywhere in the world, in real time. It works through a constellation of about thirty satellites in medium Earth orbit, broadcasting precisely timed signals that your phone uses to triangulate its position. The whole system was built and is operated by the U.S. Department of Defense at a cost of tens of billions of dollars over decades. You use it, every day, for free.

Why did the government build GPS instead of waiting for a private company to do it?

Because no private company would have. The benefits of GPS — better navigation, safer aviation, more efficient shipping, easier logistics, the modern app economy — accrue to everyone who uses it, and almost no one pays a fee directly for the satellite signal. A private firm that built the constellation and tried to charge per use would face two fundamental problems. First, once the signal is broadcast, anyone with a receiver can pick it up. Excluding non-payers is technically and politically very difficult. Second, one more person using the signal does not reduce its availability to anyone else. There is no scarcity at the margin.

This combination — *hard to exclude non-payers* and *one more user doesn't subtract from the rest* — is what economists call a **public good**. The market underproduces them. Sometimes it produces none at all. GPS sits at one end of a spectrum that runs through basic scientific research, vaccination programs, and national defense — goods that are enormously valuable to society and that markets, left alone, would fund inadequately or not at all.

This chapter develops the diagnosis and the standard responses. The structure is the mirror of Chapter 12, which examined negative externalities — cases where markets over-produce because firms don't pay for the harms they impose on others. Here the market under-produces because producers can't capture the benefits they generate for others. Same structural insight; opposite direction.

---

## Positive externalities — the mirror case

Start with the concept.

A market transaction involves a private benefit to the buyer and a private cost to the seller. When the transaction also produces *benefits to third parties who don't pay for them*, there's a **positive externality**. The key word is spillover — value that flows outward from a transaction and accrues to people who had no part in it.

The examples span a wide range of scale.

**Vaccines.** The person vaccinated benefits directly — reduced probability of contracting the disease. But everyone who comes in contact with that person also benefits, because the vaccinated person can no longer transmit the pathogen. Even the unvaccinated receive some protection from others' decisions. This is herd immunity. The person deciding whether to get vaccinated bears the private cost (time, discomfort, risk) but captures only part of the social benefit. If enough people reason this way, vaccination rates stay below the socially optimal level.

**Education.** The educated person earns more. The community around that person benefits from a more productive workforce, a more informed electorate, lower crime rates, and a more innovative economy. The student makes the decision based on private expected earnings; the broader social returns don't fully enter the calculation. Hence the argument for public subsidy of education — subsidizing the private decision to bring it closer to the social optimum.

**Basic research.** A scientist develops a new technique or discovery. The employing university or firm captures some of the value through licensing or consulting fees. Most of it spills over — to other scientists who build on the work, to firms that eventually commercialize the applications, to consumers who benefit from those products decades later. The inventor of PCR (polymerase chain reaction) enabled modern forensic DNA testing, genetic disease diagnosis, and vaccine development, among other things. The private return to the invention was substantial; the social return was orders of magnitude larger.

**A beautiful garden visible from the street.** Modest scale, same structure. The gardener bears the cost; everyone who walks by gets a free aesthetic benefit without contributing. The market produces fewer beautiful gardens than people would collectively pay for.

The economic consequence is the same in every case: the unregulated market produces less of the good than is socially efficient. The reason is that the market quantity equates private marginal benefit to marginal cost. The socially efficient quantity would equate *social* marginal benefit — private plus external — to marginal cost. Because the social benefit exceeds the private benefit, the socially optimal quantity is higher. The market falls short.

The diagram makes this concrete. Draw a demand curve representing private marginal benefit, and a supply curve. Their intersection is the market quantity. Now draw a second demand curve above the first, shifted up by the dollar value of the external benefit per unit. This is the social-marginal-benefit curve. Where it crosses supply is the socially efficient quantity — higher, and at a higher price. The gap between the market quantity and the efficient quantity is the underproduction.

The policy toolkit mirrors Chapter 12 in structure: change the price (subsidies instead of taxes), change the property rights (patents and copyrights), or step in directly (government provision). Which tool fits depends on the specific externality.

## R&D and the rate-of-return gap

The most quantitatively important positive externality in a modern economy is research and development. The numbers make the scale of the problem concrete.

Empirical studies of corporate R&D consistently show that the social rate of return is substantially higher than the private rate of return. Typical estimates put the social return in the range of 30–50 percent per year and the private return at 7–15 percent. Every dollar a firm invests in R&D generates perhaps $0.10 of profit for the firm and $0.40 of value for society. The difference — the gap — is value the firm cannot capture.

Why does the gap exist? Knowledge diffuses. A firm develops a new technology or process. The firm captures some of the value through sales, market share, or licensing agreements. But the technology eventually informs other firms' work. Engineers leave and carry ideas with them. Patents expire. Reverse-engineering happens. Academic papers describe the underlying principles. Total social value of the innovation — summed across all the firms and consumers eventually affected — is much larger than what the original inventor keeps. If the inventor could capture all of it, they would invest more. They can't, so they don't. The market under-invests in research by a substantial margin.

Policy responses:

**Patents.** Grant the inventor a temporary monopoly — typically 20 years from filing. The monopoly raises the private return enough to motivate investment that otherwise wouldn't happen. The cost is the deadweight loss we examined in Chapter 9: monopoly pricing during the patent period, consumers paying more than marginal cost, some trades that would have made both parties better off not occurring. The trade-off is direct and inescapable. Patents work *because* they create monopoly power; they hurt *for the same reason*. Whether the dynamic gain (more innovation) exceeds the static cost (monopoly pricing) depends on the industry, and the empirical literature has contested this for decades without settling the question for any single sector.

**Direct public funding of research.** The U.S. National Institutes of Health spend roughly $50 billion a year. The National Science Foundation about $10 billion. Defense research adds tens of billions more. The output funds a large fraction of the basic-research literature that eventually becomes commercial applications — the kind of upstream research too far from commercial value for any single firm to fund efficiently. Basic research is a public good within R&D: the knowledge, once produced, is non-rival (one scientist reading a paper doesn't exhaust it for others) and nearly non-excludable (published findings become part of the commons).

**R&D tax credits.** The U.S. Research and Experimentation Tax Credit reduces a firm's tax bill in proportion to its qualifying R&D spending. Most OECD countries have similar instruments. The mechanism: lower the after-tax cost of private R&D, raise the after-tax private return, get more R&D without directing specific projects.

The combination of these instruments produces total U.S. R&D spending of roughly 3.5 percent of GDP — about half public, half private. Most other developed economies spend between 2 and 4 percent. Whether more would be better is a real open question, and not one that theory can answer from first principles. The under-investment is well-documented; the optimal level is not.

## Public goods — the extreme case

Some goods have positive externalities so extensive that private firms can't capture any meaningful share of the social benefit. These are **public goods**, defined by two jointly necessary properties.

**Non-rivalry.** One person's consumption doesn't reduce the amount available to others. A lighthouse beam guiding one ship in safely doesn't dim for the next. A radio broadcast heard by one listener is equally audible to the next. National defense protecting one citizen protects every citizen at the same moment, at no marginal cost per additional person defended.

**Non-excludability.** It is very hard or impossible to prevent non-payers from consuming the good. Once the lighthouse is operating, every ship in the area benefits regardless of whether it paid a lighthouse tax. Once the radio program is broadcast, anyone with a receiver can listen. Once the air is cleaned, everyone breathes it.

A good with both properties is a *pure public good*. The private market cannot provide it in socially adequate quantities, because the rational individual response is to wait for someone else to pay and then consume for free. This is the **free-rider problem**, and it is the defining diagnostic of public-goods failure.

The logic runs as follows. Suppose ten people each value a clean-air program at $100 per year. Total social value is $1,000 per year. A program costing $500 per year is worth doing. But each individual reasons: "If the other nine pay, the air will be clean regardless of what I do — contributing $100 is wasted. If they don't pay, my $100 alone won't fund the program — contributing is still wasted. Either way, I shouldn't contribute." Every person reasons identically. No one contributes. The program isn't funded. The outcome is collectively irrational even though every individual decision is locally rational. This is the structure of a public-goods failure, precisely.

<!-- → [DIAGRAM: Free-rider decision tree — two-by-two payoff matrix; rows: "I contribute" / "I free-ride"; columns: "Others contribute" / "Others don't contribute"; cells: (contribute, others contribute) → "I pay $100, get clean air — worse than free-riding"; (contribute, others don't) → "I pay $100, air still dirty — worst outcome"; (free-ride, others contribute) → "I pay $0, get clean air — best outcome"; (free-ride, others don't) → "I pay $0, air still dirty — bad but I didn't waste $100"; dominant strategy arrow pointing to "Free-ride" in both columns; caption: "Free-riding dominates contributing regardless of what others do — that's why the program doesn't get funded voluntarily"] -->

The standard response is **collective provision through government** — tax everyone, fund the program, distribute the benefit. The coercion is not incidental; it is the entire point. Voluntary contributions don't solve the free-rider problem. What solves it is making the contribution non-optional. Governments exist, in part, to solve exactly this problem.

GPS is one clean instance. The satellite constellation benefits everyone, its costs can't be recovered from users who can be selectively excluded, and one more phone using the signal subtracts nothing. No private firm builds it voluntarily. The military builds it because the military is a coercive arm of the state, and the civilian benefits flow as a byproduct.

Basic scientific research is another. The knowledge produced by a physics experiment at a national laboratory, once published, can be used by anyone. It isn't diminished by use. Exclusion requires either never publishing results or patenting discoveries — both of which create their own distortions. Publicly funded research avoids these distortions by treating the knowledge as part of the commons from the start.

## The four-quadrant typology

Non-rivalry and non-excludability are not binary — they're matters of degree — but classifying goods along both dimensions produces a useful four-quadrant map.

|  | **Excludable** | **Non-excludable** |
|---|---|---|
| **Rival** | Private good | Common-pool resource |
| **Non-rival** | Club good | Public good |

**Private goods** — rival and excludable — are the standard case. An apple. A haircut. Your economics textbook. One person consuming it reduces availability for others; non-payers can be excluded. Markets handle these well.

**Common-pool resources** — rival but non-excludable — are exhaustible goods from which non-payers cannot be excluded. Ocean fish. Groundwater. Clean air as a sink for pollution. One person's use reduces what's available for others; yet excluding individuals is impractical. The result is what Garrett Hardin called the "tragedy of the commons" in 1968: everyone individually rational, the shared resource collectively depleted. Elinor Ostrom's Nobel-winning work showed that communities often solve this through institutions — fishing quotas, communal norms, enforceable agreements — without necessarily requiring government or private property. We'll return to this.

**Club goods** — non-rival but excludable — can be provided privately by charging members. Cable television. A gym membership. A toll road. Admission to a national park. The signal or the road can serve one more user at near-zero marginal cost, but the provider can exclude non-payers with a turnstile or a password. Markets can handle these reasonably well, though they produce some inefficiency — excluding users whose willingness-to-pay is above marginal cost but below the price.

**Public goods** — non-rival and non-excludable — are the case this chapter has developed. Free-rider logic dominates. Collective provision is the standard fix.

Private goods: fine as-is. Club goods: private provision works. Common-pool: needs scarcity rules. Public goods: needs collective provision."] -->

The typology is not a moral prescription about what *should* be publicly provided. It is a structural analysis of what *can* be provided by markets and what can't. The fishery problem and the GPS problem are both market failures, but they require different instruments. A fishing quota addresses a common-pool resource by creating scarcity rules. Public funding addresses a public good by solving the free-rider problem through coercion. Mixing up the categories produces bad policy.

<!-- → [TABLE: Policy instruments matched to externality type — columns: Externality / Market failure type | Market failure | Standard instrument | Why this instrument | Example; rows: Positive externality (partial) / Underproduction / Subsidy or tax credit / Raises private return toward social return / Vaccine subsidy, education funding; Positive externality (complete) — public good / No private provision / Government provision + taxation / Coercion solves free-rider problem / GPS, national defense, lighthouses; R&D gap / Private return << social return / Patents + public research funding / Patents raise private return; public funding funds what patents can't / NIH, NSF, patent system; Common-pool resource / Over-extraction / Quotas, cap-and-trade, property rights / Scarcity rules prevent tragedy of commons / Fishing quotas, emissions trading; caption: "Match the instrument to the failure mode — not to political preference for public or private provision"] -->

## Synthesis

Pull back. Chapter 12 showed that negative externalities cause the market to over-produce: the firm doesn't pay the full social cost, so it produces more than is efficient. This chapter shows that positive externalities cause the market to under-produce: the producer doesn't capture the full social benefit, so they produce less than is efficient. Both failures trace to the same structural cause — market prices that don't reflect social costs and benefits. Both are corrected by instruments that close the gap: taxes and regulations on one side, subsidies and public provision on the other.

Public goods are the extreme version of the under-provision problem. When non-rivalry and non-excludability are both strong, no private firm can recover costs from the people who benefit, so the free-rider logic prevents private provision entirely. The coercive solution — tax and provide — is not a preference for government involvement. It's the only mechanism that solves the free-rider problem at scale.

The R&D rate-of-return gap is the most quantitatively consequential application in this chapter. Every year, the gap between social and private returns to research represents an enormous amount of value that the economy could have had and didn't, because private firms couldn't capture enough of it to justify the investment. Public funding of basic research is the institutional response. It isn't guaranteed to be efficient — governments pick some bad projects, and the allocation is imperfect. But the alternative, leaving basic research entirely to the market, would produce even less of a good that has demonstrated enormous social returns.

The satellites in GPS orbit are a small monument to this logic. The Defense Department built them for military navigation. The civilian GPS receiver in your phone was an unanticipated byproduct. The modern app economy — ride-sharing, food delivery, logistics, location-based services — was an unanticipated byproduct of a byproduct. The return to the original investment, summed across all its applications, is incalculably large. No private firm would have made that investment. The market failure was real, the public solution worked, and you use the result every day without thinking about it.
