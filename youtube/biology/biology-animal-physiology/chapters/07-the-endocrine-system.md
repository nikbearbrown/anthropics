*One molecule in the water, one enzyme nudged upward, and a vertebrate's sex is no longer what its chromosomes said it should be.*

---

In a roadside pond in central Iowa, in June of 2001, a biologist named Tyrone Hayes pulled a male northern leopard frog out of the water and dissected its gonads. Inside the testis, where there should have been only spermatocytes, he found ova. Eggs. In a male frog. He pulled out another frog. Same thing. Across his sample of wild-caught males from atrazine-contaminated water, roughly ten percent had developed mixed-sex gonads — ovaries fused to testicular tissue, oocytes growing inside organs that should have been making sperm.

Atrazine is a herbicide. It kills weeds. It is sprayed on roughly half the corn in the United States and ends up in groundwater across the Midwest. It has no obvious chemical resemblance to any frog hormone. And yet it had reached inside this male frog's body and rewritten his sex.

The mechanism: a male frog builds testes rather than ovaries partly because an enzyme called *aromatase* is kept low in the developing gonads. Aromatase converts testosterone into estradiol, and a male body needs testosterone to stay male. Atrazine — through mechanisms still partly debated — increases aromatase expression in exposed amphibians. The testosterone in the developing male's body gets converted to estradiol. Estradiol binds estrogen receptors in the gonad. The gonad, reading its chemical environment, starts building ovary tissue alongside testis. One molecule in the water, one enzyme nudged upward, and a vertebrate's sex is no longer what its chromosomes said it should be. ([Hayes et al., 2002, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.082121499))

Now place a tadpole in the same chapter. Two weeks ago it was fully aquatic — gills outside, tail thrashing, gut twenty times its body length for digesting algae. Today its hindlimbs are erupting, its tail is visibly shorter, its mouth is reshaping into a carnivore's jaw, and it is gulping air at the surface because lungs are forming. In another week it will climb out onto land, having absorbed its tail entirely, remodeled its gut by two-thirds, and rebuilt its whole body plan.

The agent of that transformation is one hormone: thyroid hormone — the same molecule that in your body sets your basal metabolic rate and decides whether you feel cold at room temperature.

Then one more: a tobacco hornworm caterpillar finishes its fifth larval instar and stops eating. Over twelve hours its cuticle hardens into a pupal case, its larval mouthparts dissolve, imaginal discs unfold into legs and wings, and three weeks later what emerges is a moth. The molecule driving this is *ecdysone* — a steroid that vertebrates do not make, do not use, and would not recognize as a signal.

These three stories share an architecture and disagree about a molecule. The architecture is the endocrine system. The frog's aromatase disruption, the tadpole's metamorphosis, and the caterpillar's pupation are all operated by chemical messengers traveling in blood or hemolymph, binding receptors in distant tissues, switching gene programs on or off. What differs is which molecules are used and what the receptors have been wired to do. That distinction — conserved architecture, contingent molecule, target-dependent response — is what this chapter is about.

## Chemistry forces the mechanism

Start with a fact about cell membranes.

A cell membrane is a lipid bilayer — two sheets of fatty molecules with oily tails facing inward, water-loving heads facing outward. Lipid is hostile to charged things. A charged molecule trying to cross a membrane is like a drop of water trying to slip through oil. It is turned back.

This single fact splits the entire hormone catalog into two families with different speeds, different persistences, and different effects on the cell.

**Steroid hormones** are derived from cholesterol — a fat. Steroids are lipid-soluble. They pass through cell membranes without difficulty. Cortisol, estradiol, testosterone, aldosterone, and ecdysone are all steroids. So is thyroid hormone, which is technically derived from tyrosine but carries enough iodine to be lipid-soluble. A steroid does not need a surface receptor to deliver its message. It walks in.

**Peptide and protein hormones** are chains of amino acids — water-soluble, charged, often large. Insulin, glucagon, growth hormone, oxytocin, ADH, FSH, LH, ACTH, TSH, and the hypothalamic releasing factors are all peptides. None can cross a lipid bilayer. They deliver their message at the surface.

The first big idea: **lipid solubility predicts receptor location.** A water-soluble hormone binds receptors on the cell surface. A lipid-soluble hormone binds receptors inside the cell. Nothing else has been decided yet — no cascade, no gene activation — and the entire downstream physiology is already constrained by whether the molecule dissolves in oil.

Follow what each chemistry forces.

A peptide hormone — glucagon — lands on a surface receptor on a liver cell. The receptor spans the membrane: outside catches the hormone, inside changes shape. The shape change activates a G-protein, which activates an enzyme (adenylyl cyclase) that converts ATP into cyclic AMP. cAMP activates protein kinases. The chain compounds: one hormone molecule → hundreds of cAMP → many kinases → many phosphorylated substrates. A tiny signal becomes a large internal response within minutes. Then a specific enzyme (phosphodiesterase) chews up cAMP, phosphatases remove the phosphate groups, and the cell returns to baseline. Fast on, fast off, measured in minutes. ([Sutherland, 1971 Nobel lecture on cAMP](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/1971/sutherland/lecture/))

A steroid hormone — cortisol — does something completely different. Cortisol diffuses through the membrane into the cytoplasm. It binds an intracellular receptor (the glucocorticoid receptor) sitting in the cytoplasm. The hormone-receptor complex enters the nucleus. It binds directly to specific DNA sequences called response elements. It acts as a transcription factor: it turns on or off the rate at which specific genes are copied into RNA. The RNA is translated into protein. The protein does the work.

This takes hours. New protein synthesis is not instantaneous. But once the proteins are made, they persist. A single steroid-binding event can reprogram a cell for the rest of the week.

The trade-off is the heart of the chapter: **peptide hormones are fast but transient; steroid hormones are slow but deep.** Neither is better — each is optimized for a purpose. You want adrenaline to clear in a minute; if the tiger leaves, you want to stop being terrified. You want cortisol to last for hours; if the famine is real, you need sustained metabolic restructuring. Vertebrate flight-or-fight runs on a peptide-related catecholamine (epinephrine) because the response has to be fast and reset quickly. Vertebrate metamorphosis runs on a steroid (thyroxine) because the change has to be slow, sustained, and tissue-deep.

## The coordinator at the base of the brain

The endocrine system needs a brain — literally. The structure that coordinates most hormonal signals in a vertebrate body is physically brain tissue: the **hypothalamus**, sitting at the base of the brain directly above the pituitary gland.

The hypothalamus receives nearly every kind of input that matters. Light intensity. Blood temperature. Blood osmolarity. Blood glucose concentration. Stress signals from the limbic system. Day length, integrated through the pineal gland. The hypothalamus is the cellular committee where the body's internal state and the external environment meet, and where decisions about what to release are made.

The decisions exit through the **pituitary gland**, which dangles beneath on a thin stalk. The pituitary has two parts with unrelated embryological origins.

The **anterior pituitary** is glandular tissue — not brain. It makes six peptide hormones: growth hormone, prolactin, ACTH, TSH, FSH, and LH. But it only makes them when commanded. The commands come from the hypothalamus as small peptides called **releasing hormones** (CRH, TRH, GnRH), traveling through a specialized portal blood system that runs directly from hypothalamus to anterior pituitary — a short-circuit so the releasing hormones don't dilute in the body's general circulation. When TRH arrives, the anterior pituitary releases TSH; TSH travels to the thyroid; the thyroid releases thyroid hormone. TSH is a **tropic hormone** — a hormone whose job is to control another gland. ([Guillemin, 1977 Nobel lecture on hypothalamic releasing hormones](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/1977/guillemin/lecture/))

The **posterior pituitary** is not a gland at all. It is a collection of axon terminals — nerve endings — projecting down from neurons whose cell bodies sit in the hypothalamus. These neurons make ADH and oxytocin in their cell bodies, ship them down the axons, and store them in the posterior pituitary until a neural signal triggers release. The posterior pituitary is brain pretending to be a gland.

The architecture is a three-layer hierarchy: hypothalamus → anterior pituitary → target gland. It is maintained by negative feedback. When the target gland's hormone (cortisol, thyroxine, sex steroid) rises in the blood, it acts back on both the anterior pituitary and the hypothalamus, suppressing the releasing hormones and the tropic hormones. The rising output of the system silences the signals that produced it. The coordinator hears its own output and adjusts.

This architecture — conserved across all vertebrates — is what gets plugged into different target glands to run different axes. The same hierarchical loop, three different wiring schemes.

## The HPT axis — and the disassembly of a tadpole

The **HPT axis** — hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis — controls the thyroid gland, and it is the easiest place to see how the same architecture does radically different things in different animals.

In an adult mammal: the hypothalamus releases TRH, the anterior pituitary releases TSH, TSH travels to the thyroid, the thyroid produces T4 (thyroxine, four iodines) and T3 (triiodothyronine, three iodines). T4 is the major output, but T3 is more potent — peripheral tissues convert T4 to T3 by removing one iodine. T3 binds nuclear receptors in essentially every cell, switching on genes that increase mitochondrial activity, oxygen consumption, and heat production. Too little: cold-intolerant, slow, mentally dulled. Too much: heat-intolerant, agitated, rapid-pulsed. The dial is held in place by negative feedback. ([NIH overview on thyroid hormone action](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK500006/))

Now run the same axis in a tadpole.

The hypothalamus releases corticotropin-releasing factor (CRF) — not TRH, as in amphibian larvae this CRF-driven mechanism initiates thyroid axis activation during metamorphosis [verify — Denver 1997 documents this CRF-driven mechanism]. The anterior pituitary releases TSH. The thyroid produces T3 and T4. T3 binds the same family of nuclear receptors. The difference is in what those receptors do.

In a tadpole, T3 receptors sit on top of an entirely different downstream gene program. When T3 binds, the program executes: tail muscle and epidermal cells switch on apoptosis — programmed cell death — and resorb themselves. Limb-bud cells switch on growth programs and erupt as hindlimbs. Intestinal cells, currently shaped for digesting algae, dismantle their long coiled architecture and rebuild as a shorter carnivorous gut. Gill cells regress. Lung cells expand. The eyes migrate forward. Skin cells switch keratin types. The entire body plan changes. ([Brown & Cai, 2007, Dev Biol](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ydbio.2006.12.026))

One hormone, the same molecule that in your body merely adjusts your metabolism, is the demolition foreman, the architect, and the carpentry crew. The hormone is the same. The response depends on what the target tissues have wired up to its receptor. Evolution did not invent a new hormone for amphibian metamorphosis. It rewired what the existing hormone tells the cell to do.

There is also a temporal sequence. T3 does not peak and trigger everything at once. It rises gradually, and different tissues respond at different thresholds. Limb development starts at low T3. Gill regression at moderate T3. Tail resorption requires peak T3. The ordered sequence of metamorphic changes — legs first, then gills, then the explosive loss of the tail — is not orchestrated by a program. It is the outcome of different receptor densities and downstream pathway sensitivities across tissues. One hormone, multiple thresholds, an apparent program. ([Shi, 2000, *Amphibian Metamorphosis*](https://www.elsevier.com/books/amphibian-metamorphosis/shi/978-0-12-640540-7))

The HPT axis is also a seasonal sensor. In birds, thyroid hormone levels rise and fall on an annual cycle driven by photoperiod signals through the hypothalamus. Migrating birds use thyroid changes to time molt. Salmon use thyroid signals to coordinate smoltification — the physiological switch from freshwater juvenile to seawater-tolerant adult before downstream migration. ([Björnsson et al., 2011, Gen Comp Endocrinol](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ygcen.2010.07.003))

One axis: metabolic rate dial in mammals, wholesale body-plan rebuilder in amphibians, seasonal physiological switch in fish and birds. Same molecule, same hierarchy, different downstream wiring.

## The HPA axis — what stress means in this body

The **HPA axis** — hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis — is the slower of the two stress responses. (The faster is the sympathetic nervous system firing on the adrenal medulla, dumping epinephrine in seconds; you saw this in Chapter 4.)

The HPA cascade: hypothalamus releases CRH → anterior pituitary releases ACTH → adrenal cortex synthesizes and releases a glucocorticoid steroid. The steroid binds nuclear receptors in essentially every tissue: gluconeogenesis up, immune response down, water retention up, muscle protein catabolism up, reproductive effort down.

The hormone is a steroid — slow-onset, long-acting. This matches the function. The HPA axis is designed for prolonged challenges, not for the rabbit-jumps-out-of-the-bush moment. The week-long famine, the month-long drought, the chronic injury, the sustained threat.

The specific steroid varies by lineage. In mammals and most fish, the major glucocorticoid is **cortisol**. In birds, reptiles, and amphibians, it is **corticosterone** — chemically very similar, differing by a single hydroxyl group. Even rodents use corticosterone despite being mammals. ([Bentley, 1998, *Comparative Vertebrate Endocrinology*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/comparative-vertebrate-endocrinology/63B9F3DAB1A8C24C0F2C71F4B6F3D1F8)) The end-result function — drive gluconeogenesis, suppress immune response, restructure metabolism for prolonged stress — is shared. The chemical identity of the messenger is not. This is the pattern: across vertebrates, the architecture is conserved; the precise molecule is not.

The HPA feedback loop is also what fails in chronic stress. Rising glucocorticoid binds receptors in the hypothalamus and anterior pituitary, suppressing CRH and ACTH. The system self-limits. But suppression depends on functional glucocorticoid receptors in the brain. When glucocorticoid stays elevated for months — chronic stress, depression, long illness — the receptors in the hippocampus can become desensitized or downregulated. The feedback signal weakens. The system fails to shut itself off. Cortisol or corticosterone stays elevated. Sustained immune suppression, sustained gluconeogenesis, hippocampal atrophy, insulin resistance accumulate. ([Sapolsky et al., 2000, Endocr Rev](https://doi.org/10.1210/edrv.21.1.0389))

This is the comparative-endocrine reading of why chronic stress hurts every vertebrate studied. The architecture is built for acute challenges — a week-long flood, a month-long migration — and does not gracefully scale to year-long sustained threats. The receptors that close the loop wear out.

## The HPG axis — and the daylight problem

The **HPG axis** — hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis — controls reproduction. GnRH from the hypothalamus → FSH and LH from the anterior pituitary → gonads produce sex steroids. Gonadal steroids feed back on the hypothalamus and pituitary, closing the loop.

In humans, the HPG axis runs approximately continuously after puberty. In most other vertebrates, this is not how it works.

A salmon breeds once in its life, in autumn, after migrating from ocean to its natal stream. Reproductive maturation is triggered by environmental cues — photoperiod, temperature, water chemistry — channeled through the hypothalamus into rising GnRH and rising gonadotropins. Sex steroids climb dramatically. Eggs and sperm mature simultaneously. The fish spawns. Many Pacific salmon then die. The HPG axis is fired once, hard.

A migratory songbird breeds in summer. As days lengthen in spring, photoreceptors in deep brain regions — not in the eye — sense the change. Signals routed through the hypothalamus increase GnRH. Gonads, which have regressed to almost nothing over winter, regrow within weeks. As days shorten in late summer, the signal reverses. Gonads regress again. Each spring, the bird rebuilds its reproductive system from scratch. ([Dawson et al., 2001, J Biol Rhythms](https://doi.org/10.1177/074873001129002033))

A hamster uses the **pineal gland** as the intermediate. The pineal secretes melatonin in darkness. Long winter nights → long melatonin secretion → HPG axis suppression. Short summer nights → brief melatonin → HPG axis release. Melatonin is the chemical translator of day length into a hormonal signal.

**Photoperiodism** — using day length as an environmental cue to time biological events — is the dominant comparative variable across the vertebrate HPG axis. Humans are unusual for not showing strong photoperiodism in reproduction. Most vertebrates breed seasonally, and the timing is set by day length sensed through the brain and translated into hypothalamic output.

The lesson: a reproductive axis is not just a feedback loop. It is a feedback loop with an environmental input wired into the controller. Add one arrow to the textbook HPG diagram — environment → hypothalamus — and the difference between an animal that breeds once a year and an animal that breeds whenever becomes a single connection.

## Insects do not have any of this

Vertebrate endocrinology is built on TRH, TSH, CRH, ACTH, GnRH, FSH, LH, cortisol, thyroxine, estradiol, testosterone. Insect endocrinology uses none of these molecules.

A larval insect — a caterpillar — is encased in a rigid cuticle it cannot grow beyond. It has to shed the cuticle periodically, synthesize a new larger one, and grow into the gap. The molt is triggered by a steroid hormone called **ecdysone**, made by a paired gland in the front of the thorax (the prothoracic gland). Ecdysone synthesis is itself triggered by a peptide neurohormone, **prothoracicotropic hormone (PTTH)**, released from neurosecretory cells in the brain. The architecture is recognizable — brain peptide signals a gland to make a steroid — but every molecule is different. PTTH is not a vertebrate hormone. Ecdysone is not a vertebrate hormone. The prothoracic gland is not homologous to anything in vertebrates. The whole system was invented separately. ([Riddiford, 1993, Adv Insect Physiol](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2806%2808%2960084-3))

Ecdysone is a steroid. It crosses cell membranes. It binds the ecdysone receptor, which is a member of the nuclear receptor family — same general family as the vertebrate thyroid hormone and glucocorticoid receptors. It turns on the genes that drive molting. The molt happens.

But ecdysone alone does not tell the insect what to make. A caterpillar molts; what comes out could be another caterpillar, a pupa, or — from a pupa — an adult moth. What decides?

The second major insect hormone: **juvenile hormone (JH)**, a sesquiterpenoid made by paired glands called the corpus allatum, located near the brain. JH is not a steroid, not a peptide — it is a small fatty molecule structurally unlike any vertebrate hormone. It binds an intracellular receptor and modulates gene expression in cooperation with the ecdysone receptor. ([Jindra et al., 2013, Annu Rev Entomol](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-ento-120811-153700))

The key fact: **the ratio of JH to ecdysone determines what the next molt produces.**

- High JH + ecdysone pulse → larval-to-larval molt. Another caterpillar.
- Declining JH + ecdysone pulse → larval-to-pupal molt. Metamorphosis begins.
- Very low JH + ecdysone pulse → pupal-to-adult molt. The moth emerges.

JH is the "stay a juvenile" signal. As long as JH is high, every ecdysone pulse produces another larva. As development proceeds, JH production by the corpus allatum declines stage by stage. Eventually it falls low enough that the next ecdysone pulse triggers metamorphosis rather than another larval molt.

This is different control logic from vertebrate metamorphosis. In a tadpole, metamorphosis is driven by *rising* thyroid hormone. In a caterpillar, metamorphosis is driven by *falling* juvenile hormone against stable ecdysone pulses. The threshold-based timing logic looks superficially similar, but the molecular machinery is unrelated.

Insects and vertebrates solved the developmental-transition problem with completely different chemistry. Both use steroid hormones binding nuclear receptors. The conservation is at the level of "use a lipid-soluble messenger to do something slow and deep." Below that, everything diverges. Vertebrates picked thyroxine. Insects picked ecdysone with a juvenile-hormone modulator. The two systems do not talk to each other. A frog cannot use ecdysone; a fly cannot use thyroxine.

## Worked example — running the metamorphic gauntlet

Take one tadpole. Track its thyroid axis from premetamorphosis to climax.

A *Xenopus laevis* tadpole at premetamorphic stage 47-50 has very low circulating T3 — near the detection limit of many assays [verify — Brown & Cai 2007 and Shi 2000 review TH dynamics; specific concentrations vary by species and stage]. Hindlimb buds are present but small. Gills are full. Tail is full length. The tadpole is a swimming herbivore.

The HPT axis ramps up. The hypothalamus releases CRF [verify — Denver 1997 documents this mechanism in larval anurans]. The anterior pituitary releases TSH. The thyroid responds, increasing T3 and T4. Circulating T3 climbs.

At prometamorphosis (roughly stages 54-58), T3 has risen. At this concentration, the lowest-threshold T3 target tissues respond. Hindlimbs accelerate growth. Forelimbs, developing under the operculum, become visible. Intestinal remodeling begins.

At metamorphic climax (roughly stages 61-63), T3 has peaked — on the order of tens to a hundred pg/mL in some measurements, though the exact peak varies by species and methodology [verify]. The high-threshold tissues respond. The tail switches on cell-death programs. Caudal myocytes apoptose. The tail shortens visibly day by day. Gills regress and are reabsorbed. The mouth reshapes. The gut loses roughly two-thirds of its length, switching cell types, rebuilding as a carnivore's gut. Lungs become functional.

Then T3 falls. The climax is brief — a matter of days in *Xenopus*. By stage 66, the animal is a froglet.

The same T3 that produced goiter-level hyperthyroidism in a mammal has produced an entirely new animal from a larval one. The hormone changed by perhaps a hundredfold from baseline to peak — a dynamic range that would be a medical emergency in a mammal. In the tadpole it is the developmental program.

The lesson is general: one hormone is enough to orchestrate the rebuilding of a whole organism, provided the target tissues have differential receptor density and the downstream programs are pre-wired for differential thresholds. The hormone does not need to know what each tissue should do. The tissue knows. The hormone is the timing signal.

And the limit is equally important: this only works because evolution had already constructed the downstream programs. A mammal injected with high T3 does not metamorphose; it goes hyperthyroid. The same hormone, in the absence of the downstream wiring, produces only metabolic dysregulation. The hormone is the trigger. The body plan is in the genome.

## Endocrine disruption — what made the frog leak ova

Come back to Hayes's frogs.

An **endocrine disruptor** is a foreign molecule that interferes with hormonal signaling — usually by mimicking a hormone (binding its receptor and activating it inappropriately), blocking a hormone (binding the receptor and preventing activation), or altering the synthesis or breakdown of a hormone. Atrazine is the third kind. It does not bind estrogen receptors directly; it increases aromatase expression, so the male amphibian's own testosterone gets converted to estradiol, and estradiol drives gonadal feminization.

Bisphenol A (BPA), in many plastics, is a direct estrogen receptor mimic. It binds estrogen receptors and activates estrogen-responsive genes inappropriately. Effects span reproduction, metabolism, and neural development in exposed organisms. ([Vandenberg et al., 2009, Endocr Rev](https://doi.org/10.1210/er.2008-0021))

Polybrominated diphenyl ethers (PBDEs), flame retardants, bind thyroid-binding proteins in the blood, displace endogenous T4, and lower circulating T3. Developmental and neurological effects have been documented in studied species. ([Costa et al., 2014, Toxicology](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tox.2014.04.005))

The general lesson: **the endocrine system is a chemical signaling network, and any foreign molecule that can dock at one of its receptors, or interfere with one of its enzymes, can rewrite the signal.** The system's vulnerability is the inverse of its versatility. The same receptors that allow one hormone to act broadly across tissues allow one disruptor to act broadly across tissues. The same long-range broadcasting that makes hormones useful makes them vulnerable to environmental contamination.

Insect-specific endocrine disruptors make the same point in the opposite direction. Juvenile hormone analogs — insecticides like methoprene — lock larvae in a juvenile-like state by keeping JH levels artificially high, preventing adult emergence. They are used commercially against mosquitoes precisely because they exploit the JH:ecdysone ratio logic. One molecule, ratio disrupted, developmental program arrested. ([Henrick, 2007, J Agric Food Chem](https://doi.org/10.1021/jf063060f))

The endocrine system, in any animal lineage, is a small number of molecules controlling enormous downstream programs. That economy is its strength and its weakness.

## What the chapter is really arguing

Two claims run through everything here.

The first: **the hormone is not the response; the target tissue is**. Thyroid hormone runs metabolism in adult mammals and orchestrates metamorphosis in tadpoles. Cortisol restructures stress metabolism in mammals and helps trigger smoltification in salmon. Even within one organism, the same hormone can drive completely different programs in different tissues — that is exactly why metamorphosis works, with tail, limb, and gut all responding differently to the same T3 signal. The response is the receiver's.

The second: **the architecture is conserved; the molecule is contingent**. Every vertebrate runs the same three-layer hierarchy — hypothalamus releasing hormone → tropic hormone → target gland hormone → negative feedback. But the specific molecules filling those slots vary by lineage. Cortisol in mammals, corticosterone in birds and reptiles. TRH in mammals, CRF in larval amphibians at the same position in the HPT cascade. The architecture was built once, in stem vertebrates, and conserved. The molecules are accidents of evolutionary history.

Insects built a separate system from scratch — same general logic (slow, deep, steroid-mediated gene regulation) but every molecule different, no shared ancestry. Knowing that insects picked ecdysone while vertebrates picked thyroxine tells you which features of the endocrine system are structural constraints and which are contingent. The constraint is: for a wholesale, sustained developmental transition, you need a lipid-soluble messenger with an intracellular receptor that drives transcription. The contingency is: everything else.

And the leopard frog connects both claims. The architecture's versatility — a small number of molecules controlling enormous downstream programs — is exactly what allows one foreign molecule in water at parts-per-billion concentrations to rewrite a sex. The endocrine system is powerful because it is economical. It is vulnerable for the same reason.

### Exploration

Once you have a working simulator, run these comparisons:

1. **Same axis, different species, same input.** Set axis to HPG. Slide day length from 8 to 16 hours. Compare the gonad response in human, salmon, and songbird. Why does the human gonad barely respond while the songbird gonad regrows from nothing?

2. **Same hormone, different downstream wiring.** Set axis to HPT. Compare adult human and tadpole at the same T3 concentration. Watch the metamorphic stage indicator for the tadpole. Why does the mammal merely raise its metabolic rate while the tadpole disassembles its body?

3. **Insect logic.** Select insect and step through a sequence: pulse ecdysone with high JH, then again with declining JH, then again with very low JH. What is the JH:ecdysone ratio at each transition?

4. **Break the feedback.** Modify the simulator to add a "feedback strength" slider that lets you weaken the negative feedback on the HPA axis. What happens to cortisol over time when feedback is at 50%? At 10%? At 0%? Relate this to what is hypothesized to happen in chronic stress.

Chapter 8 covers how animals convert metabolic energy into mechanical work — muscles, skeletons, the cellular machinery of force generation. Endocrine signals set the metabolic stage. ## What would change my mind

If a careful re-analysis of the Hayes atrazine data — or independent replication in a different lab on different amphibian populations — showed that the gonadal feminization is an artifact of laboratory rearing conditions or co-contaminants rather than atrazine itself, I would substantially weaken the chapter's "endocrine disruption is the system's structural vulnerability" framing and lean harder on the BPA and thyroid-disruptor literature, where the receptor-level mechanism is better established.

## Still puzzling

I do not yet fully understand why amphibian metamorphosis uses CRF rather than TRH to drive the thyroid axis at the hypothalamic level — and why the same molecule (CRF) drives both the stress axis and the metamorphic axis in larval anurans, when in mammals these are separate pathways. The cross-wiring of stress and developmental transitions in amphibians looks like it should be telling me something deeper about the integration of environmental challenge and life-history transitions, but the mechanistic and evolutionary story is not yet clear to me.
