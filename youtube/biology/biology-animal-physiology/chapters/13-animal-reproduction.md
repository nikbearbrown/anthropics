---

A sockeye salmon, *Oncorhynchus nerka*, leaves the North Pacific in summer and heads up the Fraser River. It has not eaten in months and will not eat again. Its body is rebuilding itself in transit — fat reserves shrinking, jaw lengthening into a hooked *kype*, immune system shutting down, digestive tract atrophying. Every gram of protein not needed for swimming is being rerouted to gonads. The fish is, in a strict metabolic sense, eating itself.

It reaches the stream where it hatched four years ago. The female digs a redd in the gravel. The male fertilizes. A few thousand eggs are buried under stones. Within days — sometimes within hours — both parents are dead.

This is not failure. This is the plan. The shutdown of the digestive tract and the immune system is not a side effect of the journey; it is a feature — a way of converting all remaining tissue into reproductive output. The salmon's body is engineered to leave no resource unspent at the moment of spawning, because there will be no next year.

Hold that image. Now consider a different animal.

In the deserts of the American southwest, the whiptail lizards of genus *Aspidoscelis* have no males. [verify — Crews and Fitzgerald (1980) on *Cnemidophorus uniparens*; *Aspidoscelis* is the modern name after a 2002 revision] Every individual is female. They reproduce by **parthenogenesis** — eggs develop into clonal daughters without fertilization. A single founder can produce a population.

But here is the strange part. These all-female lizards still perform mating behavior. One female mounts another in what looks like a male-female display from the closest sexual species. The mounted female lays eggs at a higher rate than unmounted females. [verify — Crews's lab documented this through the 1980s] The hormonal cycles of the two roles look like male and female cycles in sexual whiptails, even though every individual is female and every offspring is a clone. Sex behavior has outlived sex.

Now one more.

An ocean sunfish, *Mola mola*, releases up to roughly 300 million eggs at a single spawning event — the highest fecundity recorded in any vertebrate. [verify — the 300 million figure is from Schmidt (1921) and treated as an order-of-magnitude estimate in current textbooks] Each egg is about a millimeter across. The female invests almost nothing per egg and abandons them all immediately. Most are eaten within hours. Out of 300 million, across the species' evolutionary history, roughly two per spawning event reach reproductive age themselves. The other 299,999,998 were necessary to get those two.

Three animals. Three strategies that look like they belong to different worlds. The salmon spends its body in a single event. The lizard skips the male altogether. The sunfish multiplies gametes to a number that strains the imagination. The question is not which one is right. The question is why all three exist, all reach stable evolutionary equilibria, and all do the same arithmetic.

This chapter is about that arithmetic, and the comparative physiology built to perform it.

## Sex or no sex: the deepest decision

If you look at the cost of sex from a purely accounting standpoint, sex is an obviously bad idea.

A female who reproduces asexually passes 100 percent of her genome to each offspring. A female who reproduces sexually passes 50 percent. The other half comes from a male who is not, in most species, contributing further investment. Sex cuts the female's per-offspring genetic contribution in half. This is the **two-fold cost of sex**, first formalized by John Maynard Smith in 1971. [verify — Maynard Smith (1971), "The origin and maintenance of sex"; the two-fold cost framing is also in Williams (1975)]

The other costs pile on. A sexual animal has to find a mate — which takes time, energy, and exposure to predators. Mating is risky; many predators specialize on copulating prey because the prey is distracted and slow. Building and maintaining sex-determination machinery requires extra chromosomes and developmental apparatus. Asexual reproduction, by contrast, is straightforward: a hydra buds a daughter from its own tissue; a sea star fragments; an aphid in spring produces live daughters without a male and a single founder becomes a colony in days.

Given all this, why do most animal species reproduce sexually?

The leading answer is **the Red Queen hypothesis** — named from *Through the Looking-Glass*, where the Red Queen tells Alice that "it takes all the running you can do, to keep in the same place." Leigh Van Valen proposed it in 1973: species interacting with parasites and pathogens must constantly evolve just to maintain the same fitness, because their parasites are evolving too. [verify — Van Valen (1973), "A new evolutionary law," *Evolutionary Theory* 1: 1–30]

Applied to sex: imagine a population of clones. They share a genome. A parasite that bypasses one clone's immune defenses bypasses all of them. The parasite spreads unchecked and the population crashes. Now imagine a sexually reproducing population — every offspring a new genetic combination. The parasite, having co-evolved to bypass one common defense, finds itself in a population where most individuals carry a different defense. Both sides run forever, neither pulls ahead, and the two-fold cost of sex is paid for by staying one step ahead of the parasite arms race.

The Red Queen is not the only explanation. The **mutational meltdown** argument (Muller, 1932) says asexual lineages accumulate deleterious mutations with no recombination to purge them and eventually go extinct. **Lottery models** say sex is favored in heterogeneous environments where no single genotype wins everywhere; diversity hedges bets. These are complements, not competitors — different cost-of-sex problems likely apply in different lineages.

What we do not have is a clean test that distinguishes the Red Queen from its alternatives across all sexual species. The evidence is suggestive: populations under heavy parasite pressure tend to be more sexual; freshwater snails that switch between sexual and asexual reproduction become more sexual when parasites are common. [verify — Curt Lively's New Zealand mud snail work, notably Lively (1987) *Nature*] But it is not closed.

Here is what matters for this chapter: asexual reproduction is metabolically cheap and works fine in stable conditions. Sex is metabolically expensive and pays its costs through the variation it generates. Almost every vertebrate has settled on sex, with occasional asexual exceptions. The exceptions tell you where the costs and benefits tip.

A note on the framework. The Red Queen is a tool, not a verdict. When you invoke it for a particular species, ask: is this species under heavy parasite pressure? Are there closely related asexual species, and are they restricted to parasite-poor environments? A claim that sex evolved "because of parasites" without those checks is decoration with a famous name, not science.

## External or internal: where to put the gametes

Given sex, the next question is where the gametes meet.

In water, sperm can swim. Eggs and sperm can be released directly into the surrounding medium — water is supportive (sperm don't desiccate), buoyant (gametes float and disperse), and continuous (no air gaps). This is **external fertilization**, used by most fish, most amphibians, and most aquatic invertebrates.

External fertilization reaches its most spectacular form in **broadcast spawning** — entire reefs of coral releasing trillions of eggs and sperm on a single moonlit night, synchronized to within hours; herring populations spawning so densely the water turns milky over miles of shoreline. [verify — coral mass-spawning was first formally described in Harrison et al. (1984) on the Great Barrier Reef] The strategy is large numbers, weak coordination, and almost no parental care. The probability that any individual gamete will meet a partner, that the zygote survives larval drift, that the larva finds food, that it avoids predation — every step has a survival probability close to zero, and the only way to end with one surviving offspring is to start with a stupendous number.

**Internal fertilization** moves the meeting inside the female's body. Sperm are transferred directly — by penis, spermatophore, or cloacal apposition — and meet the egg inside the female reproductive tract. Internal fertilization was a precondition for terrestrial life: water is not available outside the body to carry sperm, so the body must provide the medium internally.

The consequences are large. Once fertilization is internal, the female needs far fewer gametes — sperm find eggs efficiently in a tract built for the purpose. Internal fertilization is the route to fewer, larger, better-provisioned eggs. It makes internal development of the embryo possible. And it makes elaborate parental investment not just possible but sometimes necessary: the female carrying an embryo cannot abandon it without losing her metabolic investment.

Internal fertilization also creates the asymmetry that drives most of sexual selection. The female carries the embryo; the male, in most species, does not. The male's per-offspring investment ends at fertilization; the female's is just beginning. This asymmetry — variable but always present — is the engine behind mating system asymmetry throughout the animal kingdom.

## After fertilization: the spectrum of maternal investment

Once a zygote exists, the next question is where it grows. The animal kingdom has tried three answers, and the three are not categories — they are points on a continuum.

**Oviparity** — the egg is laid outside the parent's body, with all the nutrition the embryo will need packed into the yolk. The parent's metabolic transfer to offspring ends at laying (sometimes extended by incubation or brooding, but not by internal feeding). Birds, most reptiles, monotreme mammals, most fish, most amphibians, and almost all invertebrates are oviparous. Advantages: the parent can lay and leave, multiple offspring can be produced without the parent carrying their weight, eggs can be hidden where parents cannot go. The cost is exposure — an egg cannot run from predators, cannot thermoregulate beyond what its environment provides, and dies if placed incorrectly.

**Ovoviviparity** — eggs are fertilized internally and retained inside the female's body, but the embryo is nourished entirely by the yolk. No maternal blood connection. The female provides shelter, temperature regulation, and protection from predators — but not food. Many sharks, some snakes, some lizards, and some fish fall here. [verify — the classification of shark reproductive modes is nuanced; see Wourms (1977) for the canonical taxonomy] The offspring emerge well-developed relative to oviparous hatchlings, but at the cost of the parent carrying them.

**Viviparity** — the embryo develops inside the female's body and is nourished directly by the mother's blood via a **placenta** or placenta-analog. Almost all mammals (except monotremes) are viviparous. So are some sharks, some snakes, some lizards, and some fish. [verify — Hamlett and Hysell (1998) reviewed shark placentation] Viviparity is the most metabolically expensive route. The mother's calcium goes into fetal bones, her protein into fetal tissues, her glucose into fetal energy. Uterine remodeling, breast development, immune suppression at the placental interface, cardiovascular adjustment — her whole body reorganizes. In elephants, the cost is approximately 22 months of gestation plus four years of lactation per calf. [verify — Sukumar (2003) gives gestation of 21–22 months; weaning at 3–5 years]

The benefit is a more developed offspring at birth. The viviparous offspring has spent weeks or months in a controlled environment — stable temperature, continuous nutrition, protection from predators, regulated oxygen. It emerges further along the developmental curve than an oviparous hatchling of the same lineage would.

The deeper point: these three modes are a single axis of maternal investment. At one end, the parent contributes everything in the egg and walks away. At the other, the parent contributes everything in the egg plus months of internal feeding plus years of lactation. Every decision the lineage has made — sexual versus asexual, external versus internal fertilization — has narrowed the range of investment levels available to it. By the time you reach placental mammals, the system is committed to very high per-offspring investment, and the entire body plan has been built around that commitment.

## r and K, semelparity and iteroparity

So far we have been making decisions about a single reproductive event. The next layer is how many events there are in a lifetime, and how much each one costs. Two axes organize the strategy space.

**r-selection versus K-selection** — from Robert MacArthur and E.O. Wilson's *The Theory of Island Biogeography* (1967) and developed by Eric Pianka through the 1970s. [verify — Pianka (1970), "On r- and K-selection"] The names come from the logistic growth equation: *r* is the intrinsic rate of increase, *K* is the carrying capacity.

An **r-selected** species maximizes intrinsic rate of increase: many offspring, little investment per offspring, early reproduction, high mortality. Insects, weeds, most fish, most rodents. Boom and bust. Colonize open habitat. Play the numbers lottery.

A **K-selected** species maximizes competitive ability at carrying capacity: few offspring, high investment per offspring, late reproduction, low mortality. Elephants, great apes, albatrosses, tortoises. Stable habitats, competition for resources, careful production.

The framing has aged. Pure r and pure K are abstractions no real species fully occupies. Modern life-history theory prefers multidimensional descriptions — generation time, age at first reproduction, mean clutch size, total reproductive output. But Pianka's framing still does useful work: it makes the trade-off between offspring number and per-offspring investment visible, and it reminds you that selection has explored the entire continuum, not just the two ends.

**Semelparity versus iteroparity** — introduced by Lamont Cole in 1954. [verify — Cole (1954), "The population consequences of life history phenomena," *Quarterly Review of Biology*] *Semel* = once, *iter* = repeatedly.

A **semelparous** species reproduces exactly once and then dies. Pacific salmon. Mayflies — adult mayflies live hours to days, do not feed, mate once, die. The century plant (*Agave americana*) grows for 10–30 years, sends up one flowering stalk, and dies after seeding. Octopus females brood a single clutch without feeding and die when the eggs hatch.

An **iteroparous** species reproduces multiple times over a lifetime. Most vertebrates, most long-lived invertebrates, most plants.

Why does semelparity evolve at all? The naive argument — an animal that reproduces many times should always outperform one that reproduces once — is wrong. The relevant question is *expected* offspring count, weighted by survival probability.

A salmon that "saved" a kilogram of muscle and immune tissue for the return trip to the ocean, rather than converting it to gametes, would buy some probability of surviving until next year — say, 5 percent. Five percent of next year's reproductive opportunity has to be worth more than the eggs that kilogram could have made this year. If that kilogram could have produced 1,000 eggs now, and 5 percent of next year's reproduction is, on average, 50 eggs, keeping the muscle is the worse bet. The salmon that converted everything to gametes wins. Selection finds this answer, commits to it, and the post-spawning maintenance machinery becomes vestigial — the body literally cannibalizes itself because there is nothing left worth maintaining.

Iteroparity wins when the body can be maintained cheaply and future reproductive events are likely: low predation pressure, stable food, low energetic cost of survival. Mammals, birds, and most long-lived vertebrates live in this regime.

The r-K continuum and the semelparity-iteroparity continuum are not independent. Strongly r-selected species are often semelparous — mayflies, century plants, Pacific salmon, octopi. Strongly K-selected species are almost always iteroparous — elephants, great apes, albatrosses. But combinations in between exist and are biologically real: a herring is r-selected and iteroparous; a giant Pacific octopus is semelparous but produces relatively few large eggs with strong maternal care.

Life-history strategy is a metabolic budget allocation problem in two dimensions: how much per offspring, and how many events. Selection has found local optima in every corner of the strategy space.

## The hormones that run all of this

Strip away the comparative anatomy and the life-history math, and the same vertebrate endocrine axis is doing the work in almost every species we've discussed. The salmon's ovaries respond to the same hormones as a mouse's gonads. So do a sparrow's, a frog's, and a lizard's. The architecture is conserved; what changes is the timing.

The axis is the **hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis**, three layers stacked from brain to body.

**Layer one — hypothalamus.** A small group of neurons produces **gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH)**, released in pulses into the **hypophyseal portal blood** — a private capillary system running directly to the anterior pituitary. [verify — the portal system was first described by Popa and Fielding in 1930] Pulse frequency carries information: slow pulses (one every ~90 minutes) favor **FSH** release downstream; faster pulses favor **LH**. The system encodes signals in pulse rate the way a digital communication channel does.

**Layer two — anterior pituitary.** GnRH binds gonadotrope cells, which release **follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH)** and **luteinizing hormone (LH)** into systemic circulation. In females, FSH drives follicle growth and estrogen production; LH triggers ovulation and supports the corpus luteum. In males, FSH supports sperm production; LH stimulates testosterone synthesis. The gonadal hormones feed back to the hypothalamus and pituitary, closing the loop.

**Layer three — gonads.** The effectors. Their outputs — estrogen, progesterone, testosterone — set the downstream reproductive physiology and complete the feedback circuit.

The comparative question is not how the axis works — it works the same way in almost every vertebrate — but what turns it on and off.

### Estrus and menstruation

Most mammals do not menstruate. They have an **estrous cycle** instead — a sharply demarcated period of receptivity and fertility, with the endometrium reabsorbed (not shed) if pregnancy does not occur. No bleeding. The animal cycles silently until pregnancy or seasonal shutdown. Dogs, cats, horses, cows, deer, mice, and sheep are all estrous.

**Menstruation** — overt shedding of the endometrium with bleeding — is restricted to primates, some bats, and one rodent (the elephant shrew). [verify — Emera et al. (2012) review menstruation across mammals] Why primates menstruate is not settled. The leading hypothesis is that primate uteri are unusually invasive at implantation, the endometrium is rebuilt aggressively each cycle in preparation, and if implantation does not occur, the prepared tissue is too metabolically expensive to reabsorb and is shed instead. [verify — Emera et al. (2012); the earlier Profet (1993) pathogen-flushing hypothesis has not held up] It is a current but open proposal.

The hormonal architecture is the same: same HPG axis, same follicular phase, same ovulation, same luteal phase. What differs is one step in the cycle — how the uterus handles the failed implantation.

### Photoperiodism — running the calendar from photons

A reindeer in northern Norway gives birth in May or June every year — the calving window is usually within four weeks. To achieve that, it must begin mating about eight months earlier, in September or October. The cue cannot be temperature (Arctic temperature is variable and unreliable). It cannot be food availability (the food signal arrives too late). The cue that works — used by almost every seasonally breeding mammal at temperate or polar latitudes — is **day length**.

**Photoperiodism** is the use of day length as a seasonal cue. The mechanism in mammals runs through the **pineal gland** and **melatonin**.

Light enters the eye, and a subset of retinal signals goes to the **suprachiasmatic nucleus** of the hypothalamus — the daily clock. The suprachiasmatic nucleus controls the pineal gland via a multi-step neural pathway through the superior cervical ganglion. The pineal secretes melatonin only at night; during the day, retinal signals suppress secretion. Melatonin therefore acts as a chemical proxy for night length: short summer nights produce a few hours of melatonin; long winter nights produce many hours. The body reads the duration of melatonin elevation as a measure of the season.

Melatonin modulates the HPG axis. **Long-day breeders** — animals that mate in spring or early summer — are activated by short nights (low melatonin). Hamsters, ferrets, horses, and most birds are long-day breeders. [verify — Goldman (2001) reviews mammalian photoperiodism] **Short-day breeders** — animals that mate in fall for spring young — are activated by long nights (high melatonin). Sheep, goats, and deer. The pattern matches gestation length: short-gestation animals breed in spring so young arrive in summer; long-gestation animals breed in fall so young arrive in spring.

Birds use day length too, but partly via **deep brain photoreceptors** — light-sensitive neurons in the hypothalamus itself, detecting light through the skull. [verify — Foster and Soni (1998) review extraretinal photoreception in birds] These feed directly into the HPG axis without going through a pineal-melatonin step.

There is a practical consequence worth noting. Horses are long-day breeders; mares ovulate in spring when days are lengthening, and foals arrive eleven months later — the right time for grass. In the modern racing industry, breeders want foals born as early as possible in the calendar year (racehorses are aged from January 1, giving a developmental advantage to early foals). Breeders keep mares under artificial lighting in winter, simulating long days, and the mares ovulate months earlier than they would naturally. Photoperiodic control is a real physiological lever, not a textbook abstraction.

Tropical species experience little variation in day length across the year. Tropical mammals tend to be non-seasonal breeders, cycling continuously or responding to rainfall, food availability, or social signals. Humans are tropical-origin and largely non-seasonal, though residual photoperiodic effects on human reproduction have been claimed. [verify — birth rate seasonality in humans is documented but small and contested as to mechanism]

The HPG axis is the same machinery in a mouse, a horse, and a reindeer. What differs is the input that turns it on. Photoperiod, transduced through melatonin, runs the calendar that the hypothalamus reads.

## Worked example — lifetime reproductive success across three species

Let me run the numbers explicitly. The goal is to show how three dramatically different reproductive physiologies all arrive at approximately the same evolutionary endpoint.

I will use **lifetime reproductive success (LRS)** — the expected number of offspring an individual produces over its lifetime that themselves survive to reproduce. At evolutionary equilibrium in a stable population, LRS converges on roughly 1.0 per parent. If LRS were systematically above 1.0, the population would grow; if below, it would shrink.

**Pacific salmon (*Oncorhynchus nerka*), sockeye.** Semelparous, r-leaning.

Spawning events: 1. Eggs per spawning: ~3,000. [verify — sockeye fecundity ranges ~2,000–5,000; varies with body size] Survival from egg to spawning adult: ~0.1 percent. [verify — Bradford (1995) gives egg-to-smolt survival ~5–10%, smolt-to-adult ~2–5%, multiplying to ~0.1–0.5%]

$$\text{LRS} = 1 \times 3{,}000 \times 0.001 = 3 \text{ adult offspring}$$

Each parent's expected contribution: about 1.5 reproducing descendants. Roughly at replacement.

**Atlantic cod (*Gadus morhua*).** Iteroparous, extreme r.

Spawning events per lifetime: ~10. [verify — cod reach maturity at 4–6 years, live 15–25 years unfished] Eggs per event: ~1,000,000. [verify — large females release several million; 1 million is a reasonable average] Survival from egg to reproducing adult: ~10⁻⁷. [verify — Houde (1989) reviews fish egg survival; the 1-in-10-million figure for egg-to-recruit is commonly cited for cod]

$$\text{LRS} = 10 \times 1{,}000{,}000 \times 10^{-7} \approx 1 \text{ adult offspring}$$

At replacement.

**African elephant (*Loxodonta africana*).** Iteroparous, extreme K.

Calves per lifetime: ~7. [verify — Sukumar (2003) and Moss (2001) give mean lifetime calf production of 4–10] Calves per reproductive event: 1. Survival from birth to reproductive age: ~50 percent. [verify — calf first-year mortality ~10–30% in wild populations; cumulative survival to reproductive age ~10–12 years is ~0.5 as a rough mid-range]

$$\text{LRS} = 7 \times 1 \times 0.5 = 3.5 \text{ adult offspring}$$

Adjusted for sex ratio: about 1.75 reproducing descendants per elephant. Roughly at replacement.

Three species. Three physiologies that share almost nothing visually. Three lifetime reproductive successes that all converge on the same evolutionary answer: roughly one reproducing descendant per parent.

Now flip the analysis and look at per-surviving-offspring investment — where the comparative physiology is actually visible.

**Salmon.** Each sockeye egg weighs about 0.15 grams; 3,000 eggs is about 450 grams of total investment. Per surviving adult offspring (three, in this example), the investment is about 150 grams of parental tissue.

**Elephant.** Gestation 22 months, lactation roughly four years. Combined biomass transfer from mother to offspring is on the order of 100 kilograms per calf. [verify — back-of-envelope: peak daily milk production perhaps 10L × ~1,200 lactation days plus gestational transfer; rough order of magnitude, needs sourced number] Per surviving calf (about 3.5), the investment is roughly 100 kilograms.

The per-surviving-offspring investment ratio between salmon and elephant is on the order of 100,000 to 1. [verify — precise ratio is sensitive to accounting choices; the order-of-magnitude difference is robust] The physiologies look completely different because they occupy completely different parts of the strategy space. Both converge on the same evolutionary outcome.

The lesson: reproductive strategy is a metabolic budget allocation problem. The budget is the parent's lifetime energy intake. The allocation is between maintenance, growth, current reproduction, and future reproduction. Different ecologies favor different allocations. All workable allocations converge on the same long-run average: replacement.

The limit: LRS is genuinely hard to measure in the wild. Tagging individuals across full lifetimes is logistically brutal for long-lived species — the Amboseli Elephant Research Project has been running since 1972 and is still one of the longest-running mammal studies ever conducted. [verify — Cynthia Moss founded the Amboseli project in 1972] For high-fecundity species like fish, per-egg survival is so low that direct measurement is nearly impossible; most estimates come from cohort-fitting methods with their own assumptions. Treat the numbers above as orders of magnitude, not measurements.

## What the chapter is really arguing

Every visible feature of a reproductive system — gamete number, gestation length, whether the parent dies after spawning — is a different way of dividing the same finite energy budget among offspring. Natural selection has converged on dramatically different anatomies that all arrive at the same evolutionary endpoint: roughly one reproducing descendant per parent per lifetime.

The salmon and the elephant are not at opposite ends of a scale from primitive to advanced. They are at opposite corners of a two-dimensional strategy space — how much per offspring, how many events — that selection has explored exhaustively. The salmon's corner requires 3,000 eggs and accepts 0.1 percent survival; the elephant's corner requires 22 months of gestation and four years of lactation and accepts 50 percent survival. Both corners produce about 1.5 reproducing descendants per parent.

The hormonal machinery that runs all of this is more conserved than the anatomy. The HPG axis — same hypothalamus, same pituitary, same gonadotropins, same gonadal steroids — appears in virtually every vertebrate. What changes is the input signal that turns the axis on. In most non-tropical species, that signal is day length, transduced through melatonin, integrated by the suprachiasmatic nucleus, and delivered to the hypothalamus as a pulse-frequency change in GnRH. The salmon's spawning hormones are recognizably the same as the elephant's, even though the animals are separated by 400 million years of evolutionary time and have invested their bodies in completely different reproductive bets.

That conservation is what makes comparative reproductive physiology possible as a discipline. Without it, every species would have to be studied from scratch. With it, a mechanism understood in one lineage illuminates all the others — not because all lineages are the same, but because they are all running variations on the same machinery, gated by the same calendar, in the service of the same arithmetic.

## LLM Exercises — building the reproductive strategy comparator

The end-of-chapter simulator for this chapter is `13-reproductive-strategies.html`. The simulator lets the user pick a species, adjust life-history parameters via sliders, and see where the species lands on the r-K continuum, what its lifetime reproductive success is, and how the seasonal photoperiod signal would gate its breeding.

You will build this simulator by collaborating with a large language model. The exercise has four parts — Show, Say, Constrain, Verify — that you should run in order. Then there are two extension prompts to push the simulator further.

### Show

Before you ask the model to write code, show it what the simulator should look like. Sketch (in words or a hand-drawn diagram) the following layout.

**At the top:** a species selector with seven options — Pacific salmon, Atlantic cod, African elephant, house mouse, common chimpanzee, green sea turtle, honeybee. Each option, when selected, populates the sliders below with default values for that species.

**Below the selector:** four sliders: age at first reproduction (log scale), clutch/litter size (log scale), parental care duration (linear, in months), and maximum lifespan (log scale).

**Below the sliders:** a main visualization panel with three regions side by side.

*Region 1 — r/K continuum.* A horizontal line labeled "r-selected" on the left and "K-selected" on the right. A marker shows where the currently selected species sits, computed from the slider values.

*Region 2 — Trade-off curve.* A log-log plot of offspring number versus per-offspring survival probability. The curve should be roughly hyperbolic. The current species appears as a labeled dot.

*Region 3 — Photoperiod panel.* A circular calendar showing day length across the year for a configurable latitude. The breeding season is marked. The student can drag the latitude slider and watch the breeding window change.

**Below the visualization:** a comparison table showing the current species and the previously selected species side by side.

### Verify

Once the model has produced the code, open the HTML file and run through these checks.

1. **Select Pacific salmon.** LRS should be approximately 3 (3,000 eggs × 1 event × ~0.001 survival). Drag the clutch-size slider down to 300. LRS should drop to about 0.3. The r/K marker should not move much.

2. **Select African elephant.** LRS should be in the range of 1–5. The r/K marker should sit far to the right (K). Drag clutch size to 100 — LRS should jump dramatically. Note this is a teaching simplification.

3. **Select Atlantic cod.** Drag latitude to 60° N. The breeding window on the photoperiod panel should narrow as seasonality intensifies.

4. **Compare salmon and cod** using the side-by-side table. Both have low parental care, both r-leaning, but cod has many more lifetime events and many more eggs per event.

5. **Select honeybee queen.** r/K position should be far left. Note this is a eusocial edge case the simulator's formula does not handle cleanly.

6. **Inspect the code.** For each life-history constant in the defaults table, ask the model: *"Where does this number come from? Cite a source or mark it as a fitting parameter."* Mark any unsourced number with `/* VERIFY: source needed */`.

### Exploration

Use the simulator to probe questions the chapter raised.

- Find the latitude at which the breeding window for a long-day breeder collapses below one month. What does this tell you about the upper latitudinal limit for that species' viable reproductive cycle?

- Set up a "rare disturbance" scenario. Pick an r-selected species (salmon or cod). What happens to LRS if you cut clutch size by 50 percent? Now do the same for a K-selected species. Which type recovers faster, according to the simulator's logic?

- The simulator computes per-offspring survival from a simple formula. Inspect that formula. Where would you expect it to fail compared to real ecology? Propose one specific modification — for example, adding a parental-care multiplier to survival — and predict how that would change the salmon vs. elephant comparison.

- Toggle between chimpanzee and elephant, both K-selected, both with lifetimes around 50–60 years. They land in similar r/K territory but the elephant's parental care is much longer per offspring. Why doesn't the chimpanzee, with shorter parental care, produce more offspring per lifetime?

## What would change my mind

A demonstration that lifetime reproductive success across animals does *not* converge on replacement — that long-term average LRS is systematically above or below 1.0 in stable populations — would force a major revision of this chapter's framework. So would a clean demonstration of long-term asexual success under heavy parasite pressure in a vertebrate lineage. The bdelloid rotifers come close: apparently asexual for tens of millions of years and still thriving. What they actually do with parasites is one of the most interesting open questions in the field. [verify — Mark Welch and Meselson (2000) made the original case for ancient asexuality in bdelloids; subsequent work has complicated but not overturned it]

## Still puzzling

Why bdelloid rotifers are apparently successful in long-term asexuality when the Red Queen hypothesis says they should not be. Why menstruation exists in primates and a small handful of other mammals but not in most mammalian lineages. Whether semelparity in Pacific salmon evolved primarily because of the energetic cost of upstream migration or because post-spawning marine predation would have made return migration nearly fatal anyway — the comparative phylogenetics across salmonid species has not cleanly distinguished these.
