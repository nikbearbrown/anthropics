## The crow and the slug

In 2002, in an aviary at Oxford, a New Caledonian crow named Betty was given a simple task: a small bucket of food sat at the bottom of a vertical tube, too deep to reach with her beak. Two pieces of wire lay nearby — one bent into a hook, one perfectly straight. The straight wire was useless. The hooked one was the tool. The experimenters expected Betty to grab the hook.

Another bird grabbed the hook first and flew off with it.

Betty picked up the straight wire, wedged one end into a crack in the apparatus, pulled the free end sideways with her beak until she had bent a passable hook, and used the new tool to lift the bucket. ([Weir, Chappell & Kacelnik, *Science* 2002](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1073433).)

A New Caledonian crow weighs about three hundred grams. Its entire brain is the size of a walnut. Its forebrain is built differently from a mammal's — no six-layered neocortex, no folded gyri, none of the structures most textbooks identify as the seat of intelligence. And yet Betty just performed a feat of insightful tool manufacture that most three-year-old humans, and almost all non-human mammals, cannot do.

Here is the first puzzle: if the neocortex is what makes mammals smart, why does a corvid without one outperform most mammals on tool-use tasks?

The second puzzle is smaller and sharper. A sea slug called *Aplysia californica* has about twenty thousand neurons — fewer than a single cubic millimeter of your cortex. Eric Kandel showed in the 1970s that this slug can learn. It habituates to a repeated harmless touch. It sensitizes after a noxious shock. And the cellular changes that produce those behavioral shifts are visible under a microscope — you can watch a synapse get stronger or weaker. ([Kandel, Nobel lecture, 2001.](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/kandel-lecture.pdf)) Twenty thousand neurons is enough for the molecular logic of memory to be fully on display.

Two animals on opposite ends of the comparative spectrum: a crow that learns and reasons without a neocortex, and a slug that learns with almost no brain at all. The vertebrate brain is not the only way to make a mind. The mammalian brain is not the only kind of vertebrate brain. This chapter takes the brain apart along its evolutionary seams, examines what each piece does, and then puts it back together — including the autonomic limbs that run the body and the reflex arcs that bypass the brain entirely.

## The master idea: brains are tuned, not designed

Before any anatomy, the framing.

The naive picture is that there is one master vertebrate brain plan — a fish brain that fills in gradually, culminating in the primate brain at the top. This is wrong in detail and wrong in spirit. It is wrong in detail because the major brain divisions were present in the earliest vertebrates. A fish brain is not a half-built mammalian brain; it is a complete vertebrate brain with a different set of regions emphasized. It is wrong in spirit because it imports a value judgment ("primate at the top") into a description ("regions vary across lineages").

Better framing: **brain regions are enlarged or shrunk according to behavioral demand.** Each vertebrate lineage has tuned the same small inventory of regions to its own ecological problem. A cod swimming in dark water tunes up olfaction. A pigeon navigating in three dimensions tunes up the cerebellum and visual processing. A primate sitting in a tree tracking troopmates tunes up the neocortex.

This is not a metaphor. The expansion of one region relative to another is a real evolutionary signal, readable the way an ecologist reads a foot. A horse's foot is built for running on plains. A bat's wing is built for catching insects at dusk. A cod's olfactory lobe is built for finding food in turbid water. The brain is body, and its proportions reveal life.

## The three-division plan

A vertebrate embryo, at about four weeks of gestation in a mammal, has a neural tube that swells at its anterior end into three bumps. These are the primordial brain divisions, common to every vertebrate that has ever existed. From back to front: **hindbrain**, **midbrain**, **forebrain**. Every adult vertebrate brain is built from these three primordia. The differences across lineages are differences in how each primordium developed.

![every vertebrate brain from cod to human is built from these three primordia — the differences across lineages are in which primordium expanded.](images/05-brain-neural-integration-fig-01.png)
*Figure 5.1 — Vertebrate brain plan *

### The hindbrain

The hindbrain is what keeps you breathing while unconscious. It contains three structures.

The **medulla oblongata** — the bottom-most piece, continuous with the spinal cord — holds the nuclei that control breathing rate, heart rate, blood pressure, swallowing, vomiting, and coughing. Damage to the medulla is almost always fatal. There is no backup for these functions.

The **pons** sits just above the medulla. It contains additional respiratory pattern-generating nuclei (the medulla sets the basic rhythm; the pons smooths the transition between inhale and exhale) and a large mass of fibers running crosswise — the bridge shape that gives it its name. It is a major relay station between the cerebellum and the rest of the brain.

The **cerebellum** sits behind the pons. It is the motor refinement engine. It does not initiate movement; it makes movement accurate. Reaching for a cup, walking, hovering at a flower — the cerebellum compares what was intended (signals from motor cortex) with what is happening (proprioceptive feedback, vestibular input, vision) and emits correction signals in milliseconds. A person with cerebellar damage can still walk, but the walking is wide-based and visibly uncertain; the hand reaching for a cup overshoots, corrects, overshoots again — *dysmetria*, bad measure. The motor commands are intact. The execution machinery is intact. The comparison loop is broken.

The cerebellum's role in motor coordination is conserved across all vertebrates. Its *size*, however, varies dramatically — and that variation is one of the cleanest comparative signals in the brain.

### The midbrain

The midbrain sits between hindbrain and forebrain. In an adult mammal it has shrunk to a small structure. In fish, birds, and reptiles it is much more prominent, because in non-mammals it is doing serious sensory work.

The dorsal surface of the midbrain contains two pairs of bumps — the **colliculi**. The **superior colliculi** handle visual processing: in non-mammals, they are the primary visual center, doing the work the visual cortex does in mammals. A goldfish sees through its optic tectum. The **inferior colliculi** process auditory information; they are an obligatory relay for sound in every vertebrate.

The midbrain also contains the **substantia nigra** — dopamine-producing neurons whose axons project into the forebrain. This cell population dies in Parkinson's disease. Its existence in the midbrain is a comparative reminder that the chemistry of reward and movement initiation is an old vertebrate invention, not a primate one.

### The forebrain

The forebrain is where the comparative story becomes interesting. It divides into the **diencephalon** (small and centrally located) and the **telencephalon** (the large mass that envelops it).

The **thalamus** — twin egg-shaped nuclei in the diencephalon — is the relay through which almost every sensory signal on its way to higher processing centers must pass. But "relay" understates it. The thalamus *gates* signals. During deep sleep it dampens incoming sensory traffic; this is why you can sleep through ambient noise that would startle you awake in the daytime. The thalamus filters the world.

The **hypothalamus** — below the thalamus — is the master regulator of homeostasis. Body temperature, hunger, thirst, sleep-wake cycles, sexual behavior, autonomic nervous system output, and the gateway to the endocrine system (it controls the pituitary, which controls nearly everything else hormonal, covered in Chapter 7). Small as a marble in a human. Disproportionately important. The heuristic: if it is automatic and necessary for survival, the hypothalamus is involved.

The **telencephalon** is the cerebrum. It contains the cerebral cortex in mammals and its functional equivalents in other vertebrates, plus several deep nuclei.

Two deep nuclei matter immediately. The **hippocampus** — Greek for seahorse, for its cross-sectional shape — is essential for forming new long-term memories. The patient H.M. had his hippocampus surgically removed bilaterally in 1953 to treat intractable epilepsy ([Scoville & Milner, 1957](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC497229/)). The seizures stopped. So did his ability to form new declarative memories. He could remember his life before surgery. He could learn new motor skills without any conscious memory of having practiced. But he could not remember anything that happened after surgery for more than a few minutes. Every morning was the first morning. Every meeting was a first meeting. For fifty-five years. H.M. is the clearest single piece of evidence that the hippocampus does something the rest of the brain cannot substitute.

The **amygdala** — Greek for almond — sits just in front of the hippocampus. It is the fear and threat-evaluation center. Animals with amygdala damage approach predators they should avoid. It does not just store fear memories; it tags experiences as emotionally significant, which is why emotionally charged events are remembered more vividly than neutral ones.

## Comparative variation — reading brain proportions as behavior

Now three brains side by side: a cod, a pigeon, a cat. Each roughly the size of a walnut. The differences in proportion are striking.

**The cod** has prominent olfactory lobes at the very front — two bulbous protrusions dominating the anterior brain. A cod detects amino acids leaking from wounded prey at parts-per-billion concentrations. The optic tectum is well-developed; the cerebellum is moderate; the cerebrum is small. This brain is tuned for an aquatic predator that smells more than it sees.

**The pigeon** is built for flying. The optic lobes flanking the midbrain are very large — birds fly at speeds where visual reaction time is the constraint. The cerebellum is enormous relative to the rest. A hummingbird hovering makes microsecond-scale motor corrections to remain stable in air; a bird cerebellum supporting that work is proportionally one of the largest in the vertebrate kingdom. Olfactory bulbs are tiny — most birds rely little on smell. Complex cognition runs through the nidopallium rather than a layered cortex, and birds solve cognitive problems with nucleated pallial tissue that is developmentally homologous to mammalian neocortex even though it looks nothing like it ([Olkowicz et al., *PNAS* 2016 — corvids and parrots have primate-like neuron densities in the forebrain](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1517131113)).

**The cat** shows the mammalian signature: large, folded neocortex — gyri (ridges) and sulci (valleys) that increase cortical surface area within a fixed skull volume. Prominent amygdala (a predator evaluates threat from both prey and larger predators). Well-developed cerebellum. Small olfactory bulbs relative to a dog, which hunts more by smell.

Three lifestyles, three different tuning patterns built from the same component inventory.

The **encephalization quotient** (EQ) quantifies this by asking how actual brain mass compares to what body mass predicts. One common formulation:

$$\text{EQ} = \frac{\text{actual brain mass}}{0.12 \times \text{body mass}^{0.67}}$$

(where mass is in grams; verify constants — this is the Jerison (1973) formulation for mammals. Use one consistent source throughout any comparison.)

A domestic cat has an EQ near 1.0 — about what a typical mammal of that body size would be expected to have. Chimpanzees are around 2.5. Modern humans around 7, meaning our brains are roughly seven times larger than predicted for our body mass.

EQ is useful. It is also limited. It was built for mammals; applying it to a cod or pigeon gives nonsense numbers because body-to-brain scaling differs across vertebrate classes. Corvids have small absolute brain mass but extremely high neuron density in their pallium — their cognitive substrate is much greater than mass alone suggests. Cephalopods (octopuses, squids) have most of their neurons not in their brain at all but distributed through their arms. EQ is a useful first approximation, not a verdict on cognition.

The deeper point: **vertebrates have converged on different architectures for similar cognitive jobs**. Mammals use layered neocortex. Birds use nucleated pallium. Cephalopods use a distributed peripheral system we are still characterizing. Treating the mammalian solution as the only one is a parochialism, not a finding.

![three lifestyles, three brains. The same regions, differently proportioned — the proportions are the behavioral fingerprint. Olfaction dominant in the fish; flight-coordination and vision dominant in the bird; cortical integration dominant in the mammal.](images/05-brain-neural-integration-fig-02.png)

## The autonomic nervous system — the brain's grip on the body

The brain controls the body. Most of that control runs below conscious awareness through the **autonomic nervous system** — ANS. Heart rate, blood pressure, pupil diameter, salivation, sweating, bronchial smooth muscle tone, gut peristalsis, arterial diameter: you do not decide these the way you decide to lift a finger.

### The two-neuron chain

The ANS uses a **two-neuron chain** to reach its targets, in contrast to the somatic motor system's single neuron from spinal cord to muscle.

A **preganglionic neuron** has its cell body in the brainstem or spinal cord. Its axon exits the CNS and travels to an **autonomic ganglion** — a cluster of nerve cell bodies outside the CNS. There the preganglionic axon synapses onto a **postganglionic neuron**, whose axon then travels from the ganglion to the target organ. CNS → preganglionic axon → ganglion → postganglionic axon → effector. Two neurons. One synapse outside the CNS. The two-tier design lets a single preganglionic signal coordinate effects across multiple organs via widely branching postganglionic neurons.

### The two divisions

The two ANS divisions oppose each other. This opposition is the organizing principle of autonomic physiology.

The **sympathetic** division mobilizes the body for action. It speeds the heart, dilates pupils, opens bronchi, shunts blood from gut to muscle, dumps glucose into blood. Anatomically: preganglionic cell bodies in the **thoracolumbar** spinal cord. Their axons synapse in a chain of **paravertebral ganglia** running alongside the spine — the sympathetic chain — coordinating a body-wide response. One node in this chain is the **adrenal medulla**, which releases **epinephrine** and **norepinephrine** directly into the bloodstream rather than onto a local target. When you feel adrenaline, that is the adrenal medulla distributing sympathetic chemistry through blood to every tissue simultaneously.

The **parasympathetic** division runs the body at rest: slows the heart, constricts pupils, narrows bronchi, increases gut motility, promotes glandular secretion. Preganglionic cell bodies sit in the brainstem (**cranial outflow**, traveling on cranial nerves III, VII, IX, and especially X, the **vagus**) and sacral spinal cord (**sacral outflow**). The parasympathetic ganglia sit close to or inside target organs rather than in a spinal chain, which is why parasympathetic effects tend to be localized rather than body-wide. The vagus alone provides parasympathetic innervation to the heart, lungs, stomach, and most of the small intestine.

### The chemistry

Every preganglionic neuron — sympathetic and parasympathetic alike — releases **acetylcholine** at the ganglion, where it activates nicotinic receptors on the postganglionic neuron. Same neurotransmitter, both teams, at the first synapse.

The postganglionic neurons differ. **Parasympathetic postganglionic neurons release acetylcholine** onto muscarinic receptors at the target organ. **Sympathetic postganglionic neurons release norepinephrine** onto adrenergic receptors (alpha and beta subtypes, each with characteristic tissue distribution).

This is where the pharmacology lives. Atropine — the alkaloid from deadly nightshade — competitively blocks muscarinic receptors. It shuts down parasympathetic effects (heart rate climbs, pupils dilate, gut slows, saliva dries) without touching sympathetic ones. Propranolol blocks beta-adrenergic receptors and shuts down sympathetic effects on the heart without touching parasympathetic. The two-neurotransmitter design at the target organ makes selective pharmacological intervention possible.

### Dual innervation

Most major organs receive input from both divisions — **dual innervation**. Your resting heart rate is not a command. It is the net of two opposing inputs: a parasympathetic brake (the vagus, holding the rate around 60 bpm) and a sympathetic accelerator (cardiac sympathetics, ready to push it higher). When you stand suddenly and blood pools briefly in your legs, baroreceptors in your carotid arteries detect the pressure drop and signal the brainstem within seconds: sympathetic outflow increases, parasympathetic decreases, heart rate rises, pressure recovers. You notice none of this. The brain has two hands on the dial rather than one.

The two-division architecture buys speed and dynamic range. Going from rest to full mobilization in seconds — then back to rest in seconds — is much easier with an accelerator and a brake than with a single dial swung across its full range. The design costs complexity and pays in control bandwidth.

![same neurotransmitter at the ganglion, different neurotransmitter at the organ — that asymmetry is what makes selective drug action possible.](images/05-brain-neural-integration-fig-03.png)
*Figure 5.3 — Autonomic two-neuron chain *

The **enteric nervous system** — embedded in the gut wall and containing roughly 500 million neurons, about as many as a cat's spinal cord — runs autonomously enough that cutting all sympathetic and parasympathetic connections to the gut leaves peristalsis intact. It is a semi-autonomous nervous system within an organ, doing local computation the CNS modulates but does not micromanage. Chapter 9 (digestion) treats it properly.

## Reflex arcs — the brain is not in the loop

A reflex is what happens when the nervous system needs to act faster than the brain can think.

A **reflex arc** converts a sensory signal directly into a motor response, bypassing the brain. The fastest arcs are monosynaptic — one sensory neuron, one motor neuron, one synapse in the spinal cord.

The stretch reflex is the canonical example. Tap the patellar tendon; the knee kicks. Trace it carefully.

1. The hammer stretches the quadriceps muscle tendon slightly.
2. **Muscle spindles** inside the muscle belly — sensory receptors wrapped around specialized intrafusal fibers — detect the stretch and fire.
3. Sensory neuron axons enter the cord through the dorsal root. Cell bodies sit in the dorsal root ganglion just outside the cord.
4. Inside the cord, these axons synapse directly onto alpha motor neurons in the ventral horn. One synapse. Glutamate released. Motor neurons fire.
5. Motor axons exit through the ventral root, travel the femoral nerve to the quadriceps, release acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction. The muscle contracts. The leg kicks.

Forty to fifty milliseconds, start to finish. The brain learns about it afterward, when slower ascending pathways carry the signal to consciousness. You feel the kick after it has happened.

Why does this circuit exist? Because the stretch reflex is your postural maintenance system, running continuously below awareness. Gravity tugs your knee toward flexion; the quadriceps spindles detect the incipient stretch; the reflex contracts the quadriceps and keeps you standing. The clinician's tap amplifies the response to make it visible.

Most reflexes are polysynaptic — interneurons between sensory and motor neurons in the cord. The **withdrawal reflex**: touch a sharp object, the hand is already pulling away before you feel pain. Sensory neurons fire from nociceptors in the skin → enter cord → synapse onto interneurons in the dorsal horn → interneurons synapse onto flexor motor neurons → arm retracts. The brain is informed after the withdrawal has begun.

The architecture allocates compute by urgency. Decisions that can be made in fifty milliseconds are made in fifty milliseconds in the cord. Decisions that need conscious deliberation are sent to the cortex. The reflex arc is not unintelligent — it is differently intelligent, optimized for speed over flexibility.

## Learning — synapses that change

The slug's twenty thousand neurons can learn. How?

### Habituation and sensitization in *Aplysia*

Touch a sea slug's siphon; it withdraws its gill. Touch it again and again. After a few dozen repetitions the withdrawal becomes smaller and slower. This is not fatigue — the motor neurons and muscles are still functional. The slug has learned the stimulus is not worth responding to.

Kandel showed that habituation in *Aplysia* corresponds to a measurable change: the synapse between the sensory neuron (from the siphon) and the motor neuron (driving the gill) releases less neurotransmitter per action potential. The postsynaptic response shrinks. Kandel could record from the neurons directly and watch the synapse weaken in real time.

Sensitization — heightened response after a noxious shock — works through a different cascade: a modulatory interneuron releases serotonin onto the sensory terminal, triggering second-messenger events that increase neurotransmitter release. The synapse strengthens.

The breakthrough Kandel won the 2000 Nobel Prize for was not that *Aplysia* learns. It was that learning could be reduced to specific, identifiable molecular changes at identified synapses in an animal simple enough to trace the entire circuit. Memory has a cellular substrate. You can find it and watch it change.

### Associative learning and Pavlov

Non-associative learning involves one stimulus. **Associative learning** is when an organism learns that two events go together.

Pavlov's dogs: a dog salivates when food is placed in its mouth — an unconditioned reflex. Ring a bell each time before the food. After enough pairings, the dog salivates to the bell alone. The dog has learned the bell predicts food. ([Pavlov, *Conditioned Reflexes*, 1927.](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Pavlov/index.htm))

Operant conditioning is the complement: a behavior followed by a consequence changes in frequency. Press a lever; a pellet appears. The animal presses more often. Behavior reinforced by its consequence. Both forms of associative learning require a **coincidence detector** — a piece of machinery that responds when two events occur together but not when either occurs alone.

### Long-term potentiation — the molecular AND gate

In 1973, Tim Bliss and Terje Lømo were recording from neurons in the hippocampus of an anesthetized rabbit. A brief high-frequency stimulus train was followed by a persistent increase in the magnitude of synaptic responses — the synapses had been strengthened by their own activity, and the strengthening lasted for hours. ([Bliss & Lømo, *Journal of Physiology*, 1973.](https://physoc.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1113/jphysiol.1973.sp010273)) They called it **long-term potentiation** — LTP.

The mechanism centers on the **NMDA receptor**, a glutamate receptor that is a molecular coincidence detector. It opens only when *both* conditions are met simultaneously:

1. Glutamate is bound (from presynaptic release).
2. The postsynaptic membrane is already depolarized (from other inputs).

At resting potential, a magnesium ion sits in the channel pore and blocks ion flow. It pops out only when the postsynaptic membrane depolarizes. So glutamate alone is insufficient. Depolarization alone is insufficient. You need both, at the same synapse, at the same time. This is the molecular AND gate.

When both conditions are met, calcium flows through the NMDA receptor into the postsynaptic cell. The calcium triggers a cascade that, over minutes to hours, strengthens the synapse: more AMPA receptors are inserted into the postsynaptic membrane, the presynaptic terminal releases more neurotransmitter per spike, the dendritic spine holding the synapse may physically enlarge.

This is the molecular implementation of **Hebbian plasticity** — "cells that fire together, wire together," proposed by Donald Hebb in 1949 before the molecular machinery was known. ([Hebb, *The Organization of Behavior*, 1949.](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2346268_3/component/file_2346267/content)) The NMDA receptor is the machine Hebb predicted.

This is also why Pavlov's dogs learn. The bell-sensing and food-sensing neurons both project onto cells that drive salivation. When bell and food occur together, those convergent inputs depolarize the salivation-driver cells simultaneously — NMDA receptors at the bell-input synapses open, calcium flows, those synapses strengthen. Bell alone now drives salivation. The association is etched into specific synapses by a molecular AND gate.

Memory is not stored in any one place. It is stored as patterns of synaptic weights across networks of neurons, written by experience through the NMDA-calcium machinery, and continuously rewritten as experience continues. A vertebrate brain is not a recording device. It is a network whose connectivity is sculpted by what it does.

**Synthesis**

7. *Aplysia* habituation involves *weakening* of the sensory-motor synapse. LTP involves *strengthening* of a synapse. Explain at the molecular level why the NMDA receptor's AND-gate property is *not* strictly required for *Aplysia* habituation, but *is* essential for the associative learning Pavlov demonstrated in dogs. What would go wrong with associative learning if NMDA receptors were replaced by a receptor that opened whenever glutamate bound, regardless of postsynaptic membrane potential? *(Tests: distinguishing non-associative from associative learning; NMDA coincidence detection)*

8. The encephalization quotient was derived from mammalian brain-to-body-mass scaling and gives an EQ of roughly 1.0 for a typical mammal. Identify three specific reasons EQ is an inadequate measure when comparing cognition across vertebrate classes (use corvids, cephalopods, and the formula's mammalian derivation as your three examples). Then propose what additional measurement would be needed to make cross-lineage cognitive comparisons more rigorous. *(Tests: EQ limitations; cross-lineage comparison problems)*

**Challenge**

9. A patient's substantia nigra is selectively destroyed by a toxin. Within months they develop tremor at rest, rigidity, slowness of movement, and difficulty initiating walking. Explain the mechanism linking substantia nigra loss to these motor symptoms: name the neurotransmitter lost, the target structure deprived of input, and why loss of that input produces the specific symptom pattern (rather than, say, weakness or sensory loss). Then predict what would happen to symptoms if the patient were given a drug that increased dopamine signaling in the brain — and explain why that treatment, while helpful, cannot stop the underlying disease. *(Tests: substantia nigra; dopamine; basal ganglia circuit; symptomatic vs. disease-modifying treatment)*

## Common misconceptions

**"Mammals are the smartest animals."** By every applicable behavioral measure of complex cognition — tool use, problem solving, social cognition, self-recognition, planning — corvids, parrots, dolphins, and cephalopods perform at levels comparable to non-human primates. Some corvids outperform some primates on specific tasks. Birds solved complex cognition with nucleated pallial architecture. Octopuses solved it with a distributed peripheral nervous system. Treating the mammalian solution as the only one is a parochialism, not a finding.

**"The brain is fully formed at birth."** Almost the opposite is true. The human prefrontal cortex does not fully mature until the mid-twenties. The visual system requires post-natal patterned input to wire correctly — a kitten deprived of pattern vision during a critical developmental window is permanently visually impaired even if its retina is normal. Vertebrate brains are partly finished at birth; the rest of the construction is done by experience.

**"Cortex thinks; brainstem does life support."** The cerebellum contributes to cognition, not only motor coordination — cerebellar damage produces measurable deficits in working memory, language processing, and emotional regulation. The basal ganglia are involved in reinforcement learning. The hippocampus contributes to spatial reasoning well beyond memory storage. The functional boundaries between brain regions drawn in introductory textbooks are convenient approximations, not anatomical truths.

**"Autonomic means uneditable."** Slow controlled breathing reliably lowers heart rate. Meditation training alters autonomic baselines measurably. Biofeedback can teach people to warm their hands by influencing peripheral vasodilation. The cortex talks to the hypothalamus, which modulates autonomic outflow. The system is not under voluntary control the way skeletal muscle is, but it is not sealed off from cortical influence.

## What would change my mind

I would revise the chapter's reading that brain region size is a behavioral fingerprint if comparative neurobiology produced systematic counterexamples — animals whose brain proportions cannot be predicted from their ecology, or whose ecology cannot be predicted from their brain proportions, across enough lineages to suggest that region size and behavior are only weakly coupled. The current evidence supports the coupling, but it is observational rather than experimental, and reconstructing selection pressures on brain proportions is difficult to do rigorously.

## Still puzzling

Why some lineages (corvids, cephalopods) evolved high cognitive complexity from radically different neural architectures while other lineages (most reptiles, most fish) retained the ancestral vertebrate plan with only modest elaboration. Complex cognition is clearly selectable, but the conditions that make a lineage take that path remain genuinely unclear.
