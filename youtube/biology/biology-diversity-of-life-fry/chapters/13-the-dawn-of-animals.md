# Chapter 13 — The Dawn of Animals

The rock splits along a seam no thicker than a fingernail, and inside it something is looking back at you. It is the last day of August, 1909, on a ridge above the town of Field, in the mountains of British Columbia. The air at eight thousand feet is thin and cold even in late summer, and the man with the hammer is fifty-nine years old, the Secretary of the Smithsonian Institution, which means he runs the largest museum complex on Earth and could be anywhere — and he is here, on a scree slope, prying open shale. The story that grew up later says his horse stumbled on a loose slab. The truth is less tidy and more patient: Charles Doolittle Walcott had been working this mountainside on purpose, and on this seam the dark stone parts to show a film of silver, a flattened smear of carbon pressed into the rock, and the smear has legs.

It has more than legs. As Walcott and, over the next fifteen summers, his family chip out slab after slab — they will carry more than sixty-five thousand specimens down off that ridge by 1924, the old man hauling rock until he is seventy-four — the silver films resolve into creatures that have no business existing. There is a thing the size of your forearm with two spined claws curling out from its face like grasping antlers and a mouth that is a ring of plates, a circular trap. There is a creature with five eyes on stalks and a single nozzle springing from the front of its head, ending in a claw, as though someone had drawn an animal from a description shouted across a noisy room. There is a worm-thing walking on stilts, with a row of spines down its back, and for seventy years nobody could tell which end was the head or even which way was up — they reconstructed it upside down, walking on its spines, and only later turned it over and let it stand on its legs.

These are *Anomalocaris*, *Opabinia*, *Hallucigenia*. The names came later, and two of them are jokes. *Anomalocaris* means "abnormal shrimp," because the first piece anyone found of it was a detached front claw that looked like the tail of a prawn. *Hallucigenia* was named for exactly what it does to your sense of the possible. And the rock they came out of, the Burgess Shale, is about five hundred and eight million years old.

Stop on that number for a moment, because everything in this chapter hangs from it. Five hundred and eight million years. The dinosaurs, the ones every child can name, are a recent event by comparison — they did not appear until around two hundred and thirty million years ago, less than halfway back from us to this ridge. When *Anomalocaris* was hunting, there were no animals on land at all. There were no plants on land. The continents were bare rock and the only green was a scum of microbes at the water's edge. The whole drama of animal life — every body you have ever seen, every body you are — was happening underwater, and most of the basic designs had already been invented.

That is the thing the Burgess Shale showed, and it is the thing this chapter is about. Not the strangeness of the creatures, though they are strange. The strangeness of the *moment*. Somewhere not long before this rock formed, in a stretch of time that geologists call the Cambrian, the animal kingdom did almost all of its fundamental inventing at once — and then, in a sense, it stopped. To understand why, we have to ask a question that sounds too simple to be worth asking, and turns out to be one of the hardest in biology.

What is an animal?

## What it takes to be an animal

You are an animal. So is a sea sponge, which has no nerves, no muscles, no mouth, and does not move. So is a jellyfish, which is mostly water. So is a tapeworm. The category has to be wide enough to hold all of these and still mean something, and the temptation is to define it by what animals can do — eat, move, sense the world. But the sponge breaks every one of those. It does not move. It does not chase food. It barely senses anything. And it is, beyond any argument, an animal.

So we cannot define an animal by behavior. We have to define it by how it is built and, more deeply, by how it builds itself.

Start with the first and most obvious fact: an animal is made of many cells. But so is an oak tree, and so is a mushroom, and neither is an animal. Multicellularity is necessary but it is cheap — life invented it more than two dozen separate times, in plants, in fungi, in seaweeds, in slime molds. Being made of many cells is the price of admission, not the membership itself.

What animals do that the others do not is fold. Watch an animal embryo in its first hours. It begins as a single fertilized cell, which divides into two, then four, then a hollow ball of cells, like a soap bubble made of beads. And then, at a particular moment, one side of the ball pushes inward — the way you might press your thumb into a half-inflated balloon — and the bubble becomes a cup, an outside and an inside with a hole leading in. This folding has a name that tells you exactly what it makes: **gastrulation**, from the Greek *gaster*, the belly or the gut. Gastrulation is the embryo making itself a gut. It is the moment a ball of cells becomes an organism with an inside.

That fold does something profound. It sorts the cells into layers — an outer layer that will become skin and nervous system, an inner layer that will line the gut, and in most animals a middle layer that becomes muscle and blood and bone. These are the **germ layers**, *germ* in the old sense of seed or origin, the founding tissues from which everything else grows. No plant gastrulates. No fungus does. The infolding gut is the signature of the animal kingdom, the move that nothing else in life makes, and it is roughly four times older than the oldest dinosaur.

Now the embryo has an inside, and that raises the question of arrangement. A gut is a tube, and a tube has two ends and a direction. Something has to decide which end is the head. Something has to lay down the long axis of the body — front to back — and then divide it into regions and tell each region what to become: mouth here, gut there, anus at the far end. And here we arrive at the mechanism that is the real subject of this chapter, the one I want to build from the ground up, because it explains both the explosion of forms in the Cambrian and the strange fact that the explosion was, in a sense, a one-time event.

## The toolkit that draws the body

Imagine you had to give instructions for building a centipede. You could try to specify every leg individually — leg one goes here, of this length, with this many segments; leg two goes here — and you would need an instruction for every leg, and a centipede can have well over a hundred. That is the expensive way, and evolution almost never pays for it.

The cheap way is to write one instruction — *make a body segment with a pair of legs* — and then write a second set of instructions that says *do that, over and over, down the length of the body, and number them as you go.* The first instruction builds the part. The second instruction places it, decides how many, and tells segment forty that it is segment forty and not segment four, so it can do something slightly different if needed.

Animals build themselves the cheap way, and the genes that write the second kind of instruction — the placement instructions, the *you are here* signals along the head-to-tail axis — are called **Hox genes**.

A Hox gene does not build an eye or a leg. It is a switch. More precisely, the protein it makes is a switch that turns other genes on and off, a **transcription factor** — *transcription* being the cell's act of copying a gene's instructions out of the DNA archive, and a transcription factor being a molecule that decides which genes get copied and which stay shut. What makes the Hox switches special is two things. First, they are read out in order along the body: the Hox gene that marks "head end" is active at the front, the next one back from it marks the next region, and so on, a strip of address labels running nose to tail. Second — and this is the part that took biologists a long time to believe — they are nearly the same genes in animals as different as a fly and a mouse and you. The gene that tells a region of a fruit fly "you are thorax, grow a wing here" is recognizably the same gene that, in a mouse, marks the position of the ribs. You can take the mouse version, put it into a fly that is missing its own, and the fly will use the mouse instruction correctly. The address system is that old, and that conserved.

Hold those two facts together and you can see what they imply. If the body is laid out by a small set of master switches that say *where*, and the switches are shared across all animals, then evolution does not have to invent a new genetic language every time it wants a new kind of body. It only has to change *when* and *where* the existing switches fire, or *what* they switch on. Move the boundary of one Hox gene and a fly that should have small balancing organs behind its wings grows a second pair of wings instead. The instruction did not change. The address it was read at did. A small edit to the placement instructions can produce an enormous change in the finished animal — a new number of segments, a leg where an antenna should be, a body region repurposed for something entirely new.

This is the toolkit. A modest set of regulatory genes — Hox among them, and a handful of other master switches — laying out axes, marking regions, switching the construction crews on and off. And it was already in place, broadly shared, before the Cambrian began. The animals of the Burgess Shale were not running on different software from us. They were running on the same software, deployed differently.

## The explosion, and what it actually was

The Cambrian period opens around five hundred and thirty-nine million years ago, and within something like the next thirteen to twenty-five million years — a long time by a human clock, an eyeblink by a geological one — the fossil record fills up with animals. Not just more animals: more *kinds* of animal, more fundamentally different body plans, than at almost any time since. Most of the major groups of animals alive today — the arthropods with their jointed legs, the molluscs, the worms of half a dozen separate phyla, the ancestors of the line that leads to us — make their first clear appearance in this window. Biologists call it the **Cambrian explosion**, and the word is doing real work: it was fast, and it scattered designs in every direction.

The temptation, once you know about the toolkit, is to say: the Hox genes did this. The animals got the genetic switches, and the switches let them throw out new body plans, and the explosion followed. It is a clean story and it is almost certainly too clean.

Here is the honest version. Yes, gene duplications in the homeobox family — Hox and its relatives — do correlate in time with the radiation; lineages with more of these switches tend to be the ones with more complex, more regionalized bodies. But correlation in deep time is a slippery thing, and the reviews that have looked hardest at this are careful to say what the evidence supports: the toolkit was an **enabling condition**, a permission, not a trigger. Having the switches made the explosion *possible*. It did not make it *happen*. Something has to light the fuse, and the toolkit is the fuse, not the match.

What was the match? We do not fully know, and you should be suspicious of anyone who tells you they do. The candidates are environmental and ecological. There is evidence — from Stanford researchers in 2024, working on the chemistry of ancient seawater — that atmospheric oxygen rose by a modest amount around the time of the radiation, and more oxygen means more energy available, which means animals can afford to be larger, more active, more predatory. There is the ecological argument: once one animal evolves to eat another, it sets off an arms race, predator against prey, shell against jaw, and arms races generate diversity fast. There is the genetic-permission argument, the toolkit. The likeliest answer is that all of these conspired, that the explosion was overdetermined, a fuse and a match and a room full of fuel all arriving in the same geological instant.

And it is worth being precise about one more thing, because it is a common error. The Cambrian explosion is not the moment animals appeared. The molecular clocks — the technique of counting mutations to estimate when two lineages split — place the origin of animals well before the Cambrian, deep in a period called the Ediacaran and possibly before that, before the great planet-wide glaciations of "Snowball Earth" some seven hundred to six hundred and thirty-five million years ago. There were animals before the explosion. We even have a fossil to prove it: a flat, ribbed, oval creature called *Dickinsonia*, found in rocks older than the Cambrian, that for decades nobody could classify — plant? lichen? something with no living relatives? In 2018 researchers extracted the molecular residue of cholesterol from a *Dickinsonia* fossil, and cholesterol is an animal signature; plants and fungi make different sterols. The molecules settled it. *Dickinsonia* was an animal, and it lived tens of millions of years before *Anomalocaris*.

So the explosion was not the dawn of animals. It was the dawn of *animal diversity that fossilizes well* — the moment animals got big, got hard parts, started eating each other, and started leaving the kind of record that a man with a hammer could find on a ridge in British Columbia four hundred million centuries later.

## The trade-off written into the body plan

Now we come to the cost, because every design choice in biology is a choice against something else, and the animal body plan made an expensive one early.

A body plan laid down by master switches in the embryo is wonderfully **evolvable at the edges** and almost impossible to rewrite at the core. Consider what that means. Evolution can fiddle endlessly with the periphery — the length of a leg, the shape of a tooth, the number of segments, the color of a wing — because these are downstream consequences, late edits, things specified after the basic framework is built. A small change there is cheap and survivable. But the deep structure — the decision of how many body axes there are, which Hox gene marks which region, the fundamental order in which the embryo folds itself — is laid down so early, and so much of the rest of the animal is built on top of it, that changing it breaks everything downstream. An embryo that tries to rewire its core developmental logic almost always dies before it is born. The mutations that would test a genuinely new body plan are lethal the moment they occur.

This is the trade-off. The toolkit gives you a body that is endlessly tunable in its details and frozen in its foundations. It is the reason the Cambrian could explode and then, in a sense, stop. In that early window, before the planet was full of established animals, before the developmental programs had grown so deeply layered, there was room — ecological room and perhaps developmental room — to try out fundamentally different architectures. Once those architectures were established and built upon for tens of millions of years, the foundations set like concrete. New phyla essentially stopped appearing. Not because evolution ran out of ideas, but because the cost of a foundational change had become prohibitive. Every animal alive today is a variation on a body plan that was settled in the Cambrian. You are a tube within a tube, head at one end, laid out by Hox genes, gastrulating into germ layers — and so was *Anomalocaris*, and the difference between you and it is a matter of edits at the edges, accumulated for half a billion years, on a frame that neither of you could change.

The system optimized for **rapid diversification within fixed designs** at the expense of **the ability to invent new designs later**. It front-loaded its creativity. The explosion was not just an event; it was the closing of a door.

## A bush, not a ladder

Eighty years after Walcott, a paleontologist named Stephen Jay Gould wrote a book about the Burgess Shale called *Wonderful Life*, published in 1989, and it landed on the bestseller lists and changed how a generation pictured the history of animals. Gould's argument grew out of the work of younger researchers — Harry Whittington and his students Derek Briggs and Simon Conway Morris — who had spent the 1970s reopening Walcott's specimens and discovering that the old man, brilliant as he was, had forced his strange creatures into familiar categories. He had filed *Anomalocaris* as three separate animals — a shrimp, a jellyfish, a sea cucumber — because it did not occur to him that one creature could be that odd. The reconstruction of *Opabinia*, with its five eyes and grasping nozzle, was so peculiar that when Whittington presented it at a scientific meeting in 1972, the audience reportedly laughed. They thought it was a joke. It was not.

Gould took these reinterpretations and drew a conclusion about the shape of life's history. The Cambrian, he argued, was a time of enormous **disparity** — wildly different body plans, far more fundamental designs than survive today — which was then **decimated**, most lineages dying out, leaving only a handful of designs to inherit the future. The history of animals was not a ladder climbing toward us. It was a bush that grew explosively wide and was then pruned back hard, and which branches got pruned was substantially a matter of luck. "Replay the tape of life," Gould wrote, and you would get a different world — perhaps one with no creature like us in it at all. He called this **contingency**, and it was a quiet demotion of our own species. We are not the goal of the process. We are one twig that happened not to be cut.

You do not have to accept every part of Gould's argument — some of the strange Burgess creatures turned out, on closer study, to be early members of groups that do survive, which softens the "disparity then decimation" claim somewhat. But the central reversal holds. Stand on that ridge above Field, holding a slab with *Opabinia* pressed into it, and you are not looking at a primitive draft of yourself. You are looking at an experiment that ran, that swam, that hunted, that left no descendants — one of the doors that opened in the Cambrian and then closed. The animals alive now are the survivors of a contest that was decided before there was anything on land to witness it.

## Who was first?

There is one more question, and it is the one biologists are arguing about most fiercely right now, as I write this. If you walk the animal tree all the way back to its base — past the arthropods and molluscs and worms, past the jellyfish and corals, to the very first branch — which animal split off first? Which living group sits closest to the root, the sister to everything else?

For a long time the answer seemed settled: the sponges. They are the simplest animals — no nerves, no muscles, no gut, just a porous body that pumps water through itself and filters out food. It made intuitive sense that the simplest animal would be the oldest branch, the one closest to the single-celled ancestor we all came from. Simple first, then complexity built on top.

Then, starting around 2008 and intensifying since, molecular phylogenetics — reading the genomes themselves and letting shared mutations vote on the branching order — kept pointing somewhere else: to the **ctenophores**, the comb jellies, delicate transparent swimmers that beat rows of fused cilia like the teeth of a comb and shimmer with running light. The comb jellies have nerves and muscles. If they branched off first, before even the sponges, then the story inverts. Either nerves and muscles are older than anyone thought, present in the very first animals and then *lost* in the sponges, or — stranger still — nerves evolved twice, once in the comb jellies and once in the lineage leading to everything else, two separate inventions of the same thing.

In 2023 a team led by researchers including Daniel Rokhsar published, in *Nature*, what looked like the strongest evidence yet for ctenophores-first. They did not just count mutations, which can mislead over half a billion years. They looked at **synteny** — the arrangement of genes along the chromosomes, which genes sit next to which. Certain chromosome fusions and rearrangements happen rarely and are nearly impossible to undo, which makes them excellent fossils of ancestry: if two lineages share the same chromosome fusion, they almost certainly inherited it from a common ancestor, because it is far too unlikely to happen the same way twice. The synteny patterns, the team argued, place ctenophores as sister to all other animals.

That should have closed the case. It did not. In 2025 a paper in *Molecular Biology and Evolution* went after the statistics underneath the synteny argument — arguing that the method used to test how surprising the shared gene arrangements were had treated all the species as if they were equally distant from one another, which they are not, and so had overstated how significant the patterns really were. If that critique holds, the synteny evidence is weaker than it looked.

And then the part that should make you trust science more, not less, even though it sounds like chaos. In November of 2025 a paper appeared in *Science* — Steenwyk and King — that swung back the other way, reporting that an integrative analysis put the **sponges** at the root after all, with hundreds of statistical tests agreeing. It was a serious paper from serious people. And in February of 2026, three months later, it was **retracted**. The authors themselves found errors in their computational pipeline; when critics reanalyzed the genes that the corrected method agreed on, those genes pointed back toward ctenophores.

So here is where we honestly stand, at the time of this writing. The leading hypothesis is ctenophores-first — the comb jellies as sister to all other animals — and it has been strengthened by the retraction of the highest-profile sponge-first claim. But the statistical critique of the synteny evidence still stands unanswered, the molecular signal genuinely flips depending on which genes and which species you sample, and no one with a clear conscience would call this resolved. This is not a failure of biology. This is what a live question looks like from the inside — claims advanced, tested, retracted, re-argued, the answer flickering between two possibilities at the very root of our own kingdom. We do not know which animal came first. We know the question is real, we know how to attack it, and we are watching the attack happen in real time.

## What the rock is for

Return to the ridge one last time. Walcott carried sixty-five thousand specimens down off that mountain, and he was, by training and temperament, a cataloguer — he tended to file the strange into the familiar, to see shrimp where there were no shrimp. It took the next generation, willing to be laughed at, to look at *Opabinia* and admit they had never seen anything like it and that the honest thing was to say so.

That is the discipline the Burgess Shale teaches, and it is the discipline this whole chapter has tried to honor. The animals down there are not crude drafts of us. They are finished, functioning solutions to the problem of being alive underwater half a billion years ago — some of them solutions that ran for a while and then ended. The toolkit that built them is the toolkit that builds you, deployed at different addresses. The explosion that scattered their designs was a door that opened once and closed, which is why your body plan and the comb jelly's and the sea sponge's were all settled before there was a single green thing growing on dry land.

And the deepest question — which of them came first, who sits at the root of our own kingdom — is still open tonight, flickering between a sponge and a comb jelly, the argument running in journals as you read this. We have learned to read the rock and to read the genome, and the two do not yet fully agree, and we are honest enough to say so. The dawn of animals is five hundred and thirty-nine million years behind us. We are still, carefully, arguing about what we saw there.
