# The Joint-Legged

The sky over northern Kenya is wrong. Not dark with cloud — it is a clear morning in early 2020 — but textured, shifting, the light coming through it broken into a million flickers, as though someone had thrown a handful of brown confetti at the sun and the confetti refused to fall. Then the sound arrives. A dry, papery roar, the rustle of a forest that has somehow gotten airborne. And then they land.

They land on the sorghum and the maize and the acacia, on the corrugated roofs and the road and the backs of cattle, on every surface that will hold weight, and many that won't. A farmer walks into her field and the ground moves away from her feet. Each animal is the length of a finger, a yellow-brown insect with hind legs built like a catapult and wings that have carried it, on the wind, sometimes a hundred and fifty kilometers in a single day. There are, in a square kilometer of the densest swarms, as many as eighty million of them. A swarm that size eats in one day what thirty-five thousand people eat. And in 2020 a single swarm in Kenya covered around 2,400 square kilometers — roughly the area of Luxembourg. It was the worst outbreak the country had seen in seventy years, and in Ethiopia and Somalia the worst in twenty-five.

What you are watching is not a disaster that arrived from outside. It is a phase change in an animal that was already there. The desert locust, *Schistocerca gregaria*, spends most of its life as a shy, solitary, green grasshopper that avoids its own kind. But crowd it — let a wet season pack the survivors close, let their legs keep brushing each other — and the touching itself flips a switch. Over a few generations the solitary animals breed into the gregarious phase: they change color, change shape, change behavior, and begin to seek each other out instead of fleeing. The population can multiply roughly twenty-fold in three months. The plague is the same animal, edited by contact into a different version of itself.

Notice what the switch actually is, because it is the leitmotif of this whole book hiding inside a crop pest. The solitary locust and the swarming locust are not two species and not two life stages. They are the same genome reading itself two different ways depending on what the animal feels against its own legs. Crowding — specifically, repeated touching of the hind legs, which experiments have isolated as the trigger — sets off a cascade that ends in a different body color, a different shape, different behavior, even different brain chemistry, with the signaling molecule serotonin among the switches that flip. Nothing new is added to the animal. The instructions for the gregarious phase were in the solitary locust all along, waiting, and contact turns them on. The plague is not a new creature. It is an old creature, edited into a different version of itself by the press of its neighbors. Hold that — the same instructions, read differently, becoming something that looks like a different animal — because it is the pattern the rest of these chapters keep handing you.

And the locust is one insect among perhaps five and a half million. The insects are one group within a larger one. Step back far enough and the swarm over Kenya is not an aberration at all. It is a glimpse, briefly made visible, of who actually owns this planet.

## The body that ate the world

Roughly four out of every five animal species that have ever been described — about eighty-six percent — belong to a single phylum. Not the chordates, the group that includes us and every other creature with a backbone. Not the molluscs, with their eighty-five thousand snails and squid. One phylum holds the overwhelming majority, and you have been swatting it your whole life.

They are the arthropods. The name is Greek: *arthron*, a joint, and *pous*, *podos*, a foot. The joint-footed. Insects, spiders, scorpions, ticks, mites, centipedes, millipedes, crabs, lobsters, shrimp, barnacles, the horseshoe crabs that are older than trees. To understand why this one design swamped every other, you have to understand the design itself — not what it is called, but what it does.

Begin with the problem every animal has to solve: muscle has to pull against something. A muscle can only contract; it cannot push. So to move a limb, a muscle must be anchored at one end to something rigid and attached at the other end to the part it intends to move. Your muscles pull against bones buried inside you. The arthropod solved the problem the opposite way. It put the rigid part on the outside.

An arthropod's body is sheathed in a cuticle — a layer secreted by the skin, built largely of chitin, a tough polysaccharide, often stiffened further with proteins or, in crabs and lobsters, hardened with calcium carbonate, the same mineral as chalk and seashell. This sheath does two jobs that in your body are done by two different systems. It is armor, a hard external shell that turns aside a beak or a raindrop. And it is the skeleton, the rigid surface the muscles pull against — except the muscles are on the *inside* of the shell, anchored to its inner walls. An exoskeleton: the skeleton worn on the outside.

A rigid box, though, cannot move. A knight in a single seamless suit of armor would be a statue. So the cuticle is not seamless. At intervals it thins into flexible membranes, and at those thin places the shell bends. Those bends are the joints — the *arthron* in arthropod. Each segment of a leg is a hard tube; between the tubes is a hinge of soft cuticle, and across that hinge run muscles, anchored to the hard tube on either side. Contract one muscle, the joint flexes; contract its opponent, the joint extends. A jointed leg is a rigid tube turned into a machine, a sequence of levers worked from inside by paired muscles. String enough of them together and you can walk, dig, swim, grasp, spin silk, sting, and — once — fly.

That is the whole trick. A shell that is armor and skeleton at once, broken at intervals into hinges so it can move. It is so good a trick that the arthropods have used it, with endless modification, for over half a billion years.

## What the armor costs

Here is the place where most explanations stop, satisfied. They have told you the exoskeleton is strong and versatile, and moved on. Go one rung deeper, because the design's greatest weakness is hidden inside its greatest strength, and the weakness explains nearly everything about how arthropods live.

A box made of hardened cuticle cannot grow. Your bones grow because they are living tissue, laced with blood vessels, constantly dissolved and rebuilt from the inside; a child's femur lengthens year by year. The arthropod's shell is, for the most part, dead — secreted, hardened, and then inert, like a fingernail. A crab cannot inflate its shell the way you inflate a balloon. The animal inside keeps growing. The box does not. Something has to give.

What gives is the box. Periodically the arthropod must shed its entire exoskeleton and build a new, larger one. The process is called molting, or ecdysis — from the Greek *ekdysis*, a stripping off, a getting-out-of. The animal first grows a soft new cuticle underneath the old one, then secretes enzymes that dissolve the inner layer of the old shell, splits the old shell along a seam, and crawls out — antennae, legs, eye coverings, gut lining, all of it. For a few hours or days it is a soft, pale, swollen thing. It gulps air or water to puff itself larger than it was, and the new cuticle, made roomy by the swelling, then hardens around the bigger body.

Now name the trade-off plainly. *This works because* the exoskeleton is the most successful body plan in the history of animal life, armor and skeleton and lever system in one structure, cheap to build and endlessly modifiable. *It fails when* the animal must grow, because to grow it must abandon its armor entirely and spend hours or days soft, swollen, and nearly helpless — unable to support its own weight on land, unable to flee, a meal waiting to be eaten. Every arthropod that has ever lived has had to make itself defenseless, repeatedly, in order to get bigger. A newly molted crab hides in a crevice. A cicada that mistimes its molt is a casualty. The cost of the shell is paid in those hours of softness, and it is paid over and over.

A shaded band beneath the sequence marks the window of vulnerability when the animal is soft and cannot escape.](images/17-joint-legged-fig-02.png)

The molt also caps body size, especially on land. Out of the water there is nothing to hold the soft new body up; gravity flattens it before the cuticle sets, and the bigger the animal the worse the problem. It is no accident that the largest arthropods alive are aquatic — the Japanese spider crab spans nearly four meters claw to claw, buoyed by water — while the largest land insects are the size of a mouse. The shell that conquered the planet also drew a ceiling over it.

There is a second ceiling, and it is in the way an insect breathes. You breathe by pumping air into lungs, dissolving oxygen into blood, and pumping the blood, by a muscular heart, to every cell that needs it. An insect does none of this. It has no lungs and its blood carries almost no oxygen. Instead its body is honeycombed with a branching network of fine air-filled tubes — the tracheae, from the Latin for windpipe — that open to the outside through holes along the body called spiracles and carry air, as air, directly to the tissues. Oxygen reaches an insect's flight muscle not by being pumped there but by diffusing down those tubes from the open air. Diffusion is fast over short distances and hopeless over long ones; a tube can only feed tissue that lies within a few millimeters of an air supply. So the tracheal system works beautifully for a small animal and fails for a large one — past a certain size the innermost tissues simply cannot get enough air down a passive tube. The insect's breathing apparatus is part of why there are no insects the size of a dog, and why the largest insects that ever lived — the dragonfly-like *Meganeura* of the Carboniferous, with wings spanning two-thirds of a meter — flew at a time when the atmosphere held far more oxygen than it does now, when diffusion down a tracheal tube could reach a little farther. *This works because* delivering air directly as gas, with no blood and no pump, is light and cheap, ideal for a small flyer. *It fails when* the animal grows large, because gas can only diffuse so far down a tube. The same design that lets a gnat be a gnat forbids an insect from being a horse.

Put the two ceilings together — a skeleton that must be shed to grow, and a breathing system that cannot reach the center of a large body — and you have the answer to a question children ask and adults stop asking: why are there no giant insects? The horror-film tarantula the size of a car could not exist. It could not breathe, and the first time it tried to molt, it would collapse under its own weight, soft and suffocating. The arthropod body plan is the most successful on Earth precisely at small scale, and it is small scale that the design enforces. The triumph and the limit are the same structure, read from two sides.

This habit of shedding a cuticle is not unique to arthropods, and that fact rewrote a piece of the animal family tree. For most of the twentieth century, biologists grouped arthropods with the segmented worms — the annelids, the earthworms and bristleworms — because both have bodies built from repeated segments. The grouping was called Articulata, and it seemed obvious: segments are segments. Then in 1997 a team led by Anna Marie Aguinaldo, working not with anatomy but with the sequence of a ribosomal RNA gene, found something else. The molting animals — arthropods, but also the roundworms, the velvet worms, the water bears, several phyla of obscure marine worms — formed one branch, defined not by segments but by the shared machinery of shedding a cuticle. They called it Ecdysozoa, the molting animals. The segmented worms went off to a different branch entirely. Segmentation, it turned out, had evolved more than once; molting had evolved once and been kept. The tree had been read wrong, and a single gene corrected it.

## The two edits that took the air

The exoskeleton explains why arthropods are everywhere. Two further edits, each evolved exactly once and never given back, explain why the *insects* in particular are most of everything.

The first is flight. Insects are the only animals without a backbone ever to have evolved powered flight — not gliding, not parachuting, but the active, muscle-driven beating of wings that lets an animal go where it chooses against the wind. And the insects did it long before anything else. For something like a hundred million years, between the Carboniferous and the appearance of the flying reptiles, the only things in the air over the swamps and forests were insects. An animal that can fly can find food, mates, and new habitat that grounded animals cannot reach; it can cross a desert, colonize an island, escape a predator into a dimension the predator cannot follow. The locust swarm over Kenya is that ancient edit, still running: eighty million animals using a power their ancestors invented and no other invertebrate ever matched.

The second edit is stranger, and you have witnessed it without thinking. A caterpillar is a soft, legged, leaf-eating tube. A butterfly is a winged, nectar-drinking flyer with compound eyes and a coiled tongue. They are the same animal. Between them lies the pupa, the chrysalis, inside which the larva is very nearly taken apart and rebuilt. This is complete metamorphosis — holometaboly, from the Greek for "whole change" — egg to larva to pupa to adult, and it is one of the deepest tricks in the insect book.

Ask what it is *for*, and the answer is a trade-off resolved by separation. A young animal and an adult animal of the same species are competitors. They want the same food, the same space; the babies compete with the parents. Holometaboly abolishes the competition by making the larva and the adult into effectively different animals living different lives. The caterpillar's whole job is to eat and grow; it is a feeding machine, all gut and mandible, exploiting a food supply — leaves — that the adult ignores entirely. The adult's whole job is to disperse and reproduce; it drinks nectar, or nothing at all, and flies. They never compete because they never overlap. One genome, two organisms, two niches, no rivalry between the generations.

The strategy worked the way the exoskeleton worked — overwhelmingly. The insects that undergo complete metamorphosis, the Holometabola — the beetles, flies, butterflies and moths, bees, wasps and ants — make up roughly eighty-five percent of all insect species. Run the arithmetic forward: insects are the great majority of arthropods, arthropods are about eighty-six percent of described animals, and the holometabolous insects are about eighty-five percent of insects. Something close to four in five animal species on Earth are insects that turn to soup in a box and come out as something else. The beetles alone — one order within that group — account for roughly a quarter of all described animal species. When the biologist J.B.S. Haldane was supposedly asked what the study of creation had taught him about the Creator, the line attributed to him was that He had, if nothing else, an inordinate fondness for beetles.

## Twenty quadrillion

Hold the planet in your mind and try to weigh its animals, and the numbers tip somewhere you would not expect. In 2022 a team led by Patrick Schultheiss compiled the field counts and produced the best global estimate yet for a single group of insects: there are, walking the Earth at any moment, roughly twenty quadrillion ants. Two times ten to the sixteenth — a two followed by sixteen zeros. About two and a half million ants for every living human. Their combined dry weight rivals that of all the world's wild birds and mammals put together.

The entomologist E. O. Wilson, who spent his life among them and described over four hundred ant species himself, called the small invertebrates "the little things that run the world." He did not mean it as a flourish. Pull the ants and termites out of a tropical forest and the soil stops turning, the dead stop being cleared, the seeds stop being moved, and the system seizes. The insects are not decoration on the living world. They are much of its working machinery, the part that runs underneath, the way the locust swarm runs underneath the green calm of the savanna until the rains come and it surfaces.

And every one of those twenty quadrillion ants is the same design, edited and re-edited: a body sheathed in chitin, broken into jointed limbs, anchored from within, shed and rebuilt to grow. The catapult legs of the locust and the dragging mandibles of the leafcutter and the four-meter span of the spider crab are not separate inventions. They are the same exoskeleton, the same hinge, the same molt, modified across half a billion years into a few million forms. Nothing here started over. The locust that darkened the sky over Kenya is wearing armor that was already old when the first fish hauled itself toward land — armor that has never once been redesigned from nothing, only altered, joint by joint, into the most numerous animals the planet has ever carried.
