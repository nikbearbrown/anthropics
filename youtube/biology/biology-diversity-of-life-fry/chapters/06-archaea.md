# Archaea and the Extremes

The water is pink.

Not the soft pink of a sunset reflected, not a trick of the light — the water itself, in this shallow rectangular pond carved into a coastal flat, is the deep saturated rose of watermelon flesh. There are dozens of these ponds stretching to the horizon, each a different shade, ranging from pale straw through orange to this almost violent red, divided by low earthen walls. The air is thick with salt. A white crust rims every pond and grows out across the mud in feathery blooms. This is a saltern — a solar evaporation works, where seawater is run through a chain of ponds and left to bake in the sun until only salt remains, to be raked up and sold. People have made salt this way for thousands of years.

Walk to the edge of the reddest pond and dip a finger. The water is not just salty. It is *brutally* salty — past the point where seawater stings a cut, past the point where any fish or crab or seaweed could survive a minute in it. The salt concentration here is approaching saturation, nearly ten times that of the open ocean. Nothing should live in it. By every rule you know about life, this is a chemical desert, a brine so concentrated it would pull the water straight out of any normal cell and leave it a shriveled husk. Osmosis alone should kill anything that tries.

And yet the water is pink. Something is making it pink. Something is not merely surviving in this killing brine but flourishing in it, in numbers so vast that its color saturates an entire pond — billions upon billions of cells, each packed with red-orange pigment, thriving exactly where nothing should.

The thing making the water pink is called *Halobacterium* — the name means "salt rod," and it is one of the most extreme salt-lovers known, demanding a brine of around five molar sodium chloride, near saturation, to live at all. Put it in mere seawater and it bursts. It needs the desert.

![An aerial view of a coastal solar saltern: a grid of shallow evaporation ponds in graded colors from pale straw through orange to deep watermelon red, divided by low earthen walls, with white salt crust rimming the edges.](images/06-archaea-fig-01.png)

*Figure 6.1 — A solar saltern. As seawater is concentrated pond by pond toward saturation, the brine grows too salty for fish, crustaceans, or algae — and turns pink, colored by the carotenoid pigments of *Halobacterium* and its relatives, archaea thriving in a brine roughly ten times as salty as the sea.*

And here is the thing you cannot see by looking at the pink water, the thing that took biology until 1977 to discover: this organism is not a bacterium. The name lies. It is no more closely related to the bacteria in your gut than you are. It belongs to a whole separate domain of life, a third great branch of the tree that for three centuries of microscopy nobody knew was there — hiding in plain sight, in the boiling springs and the killing brines and the airless mud, in all the places we had written off as too hostile for anything to bother living.

These are the Archaea. And to understand how they live where nothing else can, you have to understand the skin they live in.

## The membrane that does not melt

Every cell that has ever existed is, at bottom, a bag. A boundary that separates *inside* from *outside* — that keeps the cell's carefully assembled chemistry in, and the disorderly chemistry of the world out. Lose that boundary and you are no longer a cell; you are a smear. The membrane is not an accessory. It is the first and most basic condition of being alive.

That membrane is built from a particular kind of molecule — a lipid, a fat — with a clever shape. One end of the molecule loves water; the other end hates it. Drop millions of these molecules into water and they organize themselves, with no help, into a double sheet: the water-hating ends turn inward to hide from the water, lining up tail-to-tail, and the water-loving ends face outward into the wet on both sides. Two layers, back to back, forming a flexible film just two molecules thick. That film is the membrane of every living cell. It is one of the most reliable acts of self-assembly in all of chemistry.

Now, the molecules that build this film come in two different designs, and the difference between them is the sharpest chemical line in all of biology — sharper than the line between an animal and a plant, sharper than between a mushroom and a man. It is called the lipid divide, and it splits the living world cleanly in two.

On one side are the bacteria — and, surprisingly, us, and every plant and fungus and protist, the whole eukaryotic world. We all build our membranes the same way. We take straight chains of fatty acid, and we attach them to a small backbone molecule called glycerol using a chemical link called an *ester* bond. Straight tails, ester links, glycerol oriented one particular way. That is the design we share with the bacteria, and it tells you something about how deeply related we all are, even across the gulf between a bacterium and a blue whale.

On the other side, alone, are the archaea. And they build their membranes by three different rules at once.

First, where we use straight fatty-acid tails, they use *isoprenoid* chains — branched, studded with little methyl side-groups sticking off at regular intervals, like a tree branch versus a smooth rope. Second, where we link those tails to glycerol with an ester bond, they use an *ether* bond — a carbon-oxygen-carbon link instead of the ester's arrangement. And the ether bond is the key to everything, because an ether bond is tougher. It resists being broken apart by water, by heat, by acid. An ester bond, by comparison, comes apart relatively easily when the temperature climbs or the pH drops — it hydrolyzes, it dissolves, it lets go. The ether bond holds. Third — and this is the strangest one — the glycerol backbone in archaea is built as the *mirror image* of ours. There are two ways to arrange the same atoms around glycerol, left-handed and right-handed; bacteria and eukaryotes use one, archaea use the other, exclusively, with no exceptions. A choice made at the very foundation of the lineage and never once reversed.

Three differences, all at once, all the way down. This is not a minor variation on a theme. It is a different solution to the problem of building a bag, arrived at — or at least frozen in place — so early that it now marks one of the deepest splits in the tree of life.

On the right, the archaeal design: branched isoprenoid tails ether-linked to a mirror-image glycerol backbone, with a third panel showing a single tetraether lipid spanning the whole membrane as a fused monolayer.](images/06-archaea-fig-02.png)

*Figure 6.2 — The lipid divide. Bacteria and eukaryotes build membranes from straight fatty acids on ester bonds; archaea use branched isoprenoid chains on tougher ether bonds, with the glycerol backbone built as a mirror image. In the hottest archaea, single lipids span the entire membrane, fusing the two leaflets into one seamless sheet that does not come apart when heated.*

And then there is the trick that lets *Halobacterium*'s cousins survive temperatures that would cook any ordinary cell. Many archaea, the ones living in the hottest places, take the design one step further. Instead of two layers of lipid stacked tail-to-tail — the standard double-sheet of every other cell — they grow lipids long enough to span the *entire* membrane, top to bottom, in a single molecule. These are called tetraether lipids, and they turn the two-layer membrane into a single fused sheet, a monolayer, with the two faces glued into one continuous slab. A normal double-layer membrane, heated enough, comes apart at its seam — the two leaflets peel away from each other, and the cell leaks and dies. A monolayer membrane has no seam to come apart at. It is one piece. You can heat it past the boiling point of water and it holds.

That is the secret of the extremes. Not magic, not some exotic biochemistry — just a tougher bag, built from tougher molecules with tougher bonds, fused where ours is merely stacked. The archaeal membrane is a membrane engineered, by four billion years of selection, to not let go.

## How hot is too hot

Numbers make this real. Pull on the data and the limits of life come into focus.

The hottest place a living thing has ever been caught growing is around 122 degrees Celsius — well past the temperature at which water boils at sea level, possible only because the organism lives under crushing deep-sea pressure that keeps the water liquid. The record-holder is an archaeon called *Methanopyrus kandleri*, a methane-maker living at deep-sea vents, the most heat-tolerant organism known to science. Let that sink in. There is a cell — a complete, functioning, dividing organism with DNA and proteins and a membrane — that treats the boiling point of water as merely pleasant, and grows hotter still.

Just below it on the thermometer is *Pyrococcus furiosus*, whose name translates from the Latin as "rushing fireball" — an archaeon that grows between 73 and 103 degrees Celsius, with its happiest temperature, its optimum, right around 100. A hundred degrees. The point at which we boil eggs and sterilize instruments to kill everything is, for *Pyrococcus*, the comfortable middle of its preferred range.

And there is a ceiling. Life seems unable to push much past about 150 degrees, and the reason is not the membrane — the archaea solved the membrane — but the rest of the cell. DNA, the molecule that stores the genetic code, begins to come apart at high enough temperatures; its two strands unzip and its chemistry degrades. Proteins, the molecular machines that do the cell's work, unfold and tangle and stop functioning, the way an egg white turns from clear to white and solid and never goes back. Above roughly 150 degrees, the very molecules of life lose their cohesion no matter how tough you make the bag around them. So there is a hard edge, somewhere up there in the superheated dark, beyond which the chemistry simply cannot hold itself together. The archaea live right up against it.

The salt-lovers and the heat-lovers and the acid-lovers — archaea that thrive in battery-acid pH, in airless mud, in the airless guts of cows where they make the methane that becomes the cow's belch — they all have one thing in common. They have moved into the rooms of the planet that everyone else found uninhabitable. The boiling vent. The saturated brine. The acid spring. The oxygen-free ooze.

## The trade-off: buying the desert

Here is the bargain, and like every bargain in biology it has two faces.

By specializing for the extremes — by building that tougher membrane, those mirror-image lipids, those heat-proof fused monolayers — an archaeon gains exclusive real estate. The brine pond where the water turns pink, the vent where the water boils, the acid spring that would dissolve any ordinary cell: these are habitats with almost no competition, because almost nothing else can get in the door. *Halobacterium* does not have to fight anything for its brine pond. It has the place to itself. When you can live where nothing else can live, you never go hungry, because no one is at the table but you.

That is the upside. The downside is the mirror of it.

Specialization is a one-way commitment. The very adaptations that let *Halobacterium* thrive at five molar salt make it helpless in mere seawater — drop it into the ordinary ocean and it bursts, because its whole physiology is tuned to pump and balance against a brine, and without the brine the balance tips and the cell floods and pops. The heat-lovers are the same in reverse: cool *Pyrococcus* down to a temperature you'd find comfortable and it stops working, its proteins built to fold correctly only when the heat is feeding them energy. The extremophile cannot move back to the gentle middle. It has traded the crowded, competitive, temperate world — the warm shallows where everything wants to live and everything competes ferociously for every scrap — for a private kingdom in a place so hostile that the kingdom comes with no neighbors and no exit. It owns the desert. It can never leave the desert.

This is the same shape of trade-off you will meet again and again in this book, because selection is always making it. You can be a generalist, able to live many places but the master of none, fighting for room in the crowded middle. Or you can be a specialist, supreme in one narrow place and stranded there. Evolution does not prefer one over the other. It pushes lineages toward whichever pays, in whichever place they happen to find themselves — and for the archaea that wound up at the boiling vents and the killing brines, the price of having those places all to themselves was that they could never have anywhere else.

Pull the lens back from the single pink pond and the single boiling vent, and the scale of what this trade-off has won the archaea comes into view. They are not a fringe — not a handful of oddballs clinging to the planet's worst neighborhoods while the real action happens in the temperate middle. They are everywhere the middle is not, and the places that are not the middle add up. The deep, cold, dark sediment under the seafloor — that buried biosphere from the last chapter, holding more cells than the ocean and the soil combined — is thick with archaea living slowly on rock chemistry, cell divisions measured in centuries. The airless mud of every marsh and rice paddy and lake bottom is run by methane-making archaea, and the methane they exhale is a greenhouse gas powerful enough that their slow metabolism helps set the temperature of the whole planet. They live in the guts of cows and termites, making the methane those animals belch and pass, a contribution to the atmosphere large enough to show up in climate models. And out in the open, sunlit ocean — the temperate middle itself, the place they were supposedly shut out of — one particular lineage of archaea, the ammonia-oxidizers, turns out to be among the most numerous organisms in the sea, quietly running a key step of the planet's nitrogen cycle. The archaea did not retreat to the extremes and surrender the world. They took the extremes, and then, with the same toughened chemistry, leaked back into a great deal of the middle as well, and we simply never knew, because we had no instrument that could tell them from bacteria.

## The molecule that redrew the map

For three hundred years, ever since the first lens turned on a drop of pond water, life came in two kinds. There were the things with a nucleus — plants, animals, fungi, and the microscopic creatures that swam and hunted — cells with their DNA wrapped in a membrane-bound vault at the center. And there were the things without — the bacteria, naked DNA loose in the cell, the simple ones, the primitive ones. Two kinds. Eukaryotes and prokaryotes. Everyone agreed.

And everyone was wrong, because they had been sorting life by the wrong evidence. They were sorting by shape — by what a cell *looks* like under a microscope. And by shape, the archaea look exactly like bacteria. Same size, same lack of a nucleus, same loose DNA, same featureless little rods and spheres. For three centuries the archaea sat in the bacterial drawer, misfiled, because nobody had a tool that could tell them apart. The pink salt-lover got called a *Halobacterium*. The vent-dwellers got called bacteria. They looked the part.

The tool that finally separated them was not a better microscope. It was a molecule.

In the mid-1970s, a scientist named Carl Woese, working at the University of Illinois, was doing something tedious and strange. He was reading the sequence of a particular molecule found inside every living cell — a piece of RNA that forms part of the ribosome, the machine that builds proteins. Every cell that has ever lived has ribosomes; they are too essential to do without, and too essential to change much, which means this molecule changes very, very slowly over evolutionary time. And that slowness is exactly what makes it useful. A molecule that barely changes can carry a record reaching back billions of years. The small differences that *do* accumulate, between one lineage and another, are a clock and a family record at once. Compare the sequence of this molecule in two organisms, and the number of differences tells you how long ago their lineages split.

It was punishing work — in those years, reading even a fragment of sequence meant weeks of radioactive labeling and developing films and reading spots by hand. Woese ground through organism after organism, building a library of these ribosomal-RNA signatures, comparing them, sorting life by its deepest molecular bookkeeping rather than by its shape.

And then, in 1977, working with George Fox, he hit something that made no sense. He was reading the ribosomal RNA of some methane-making microbes — organisms that everyone, including Woese at the start, had filed as bacteria. They were small, they had no nucleus, they looked like bacteria in every way. But their ribosomal RNA did not match the bacteria. It did not match the eukaryotes either. It was *its own thing* — as different from bacteria as bacteria are from us. By the only evidence that reached back far enough to matter, these methane-makers were not a kind of bacteria at all. They were a third primary form of life, a separate trunk rising straight from the base of the tree, that had been sitting in the bacterial drawer the entire time because it had the bad luck to look like a bacterium.

Woese and Fox published it that year, in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, under a flat, enormous title: *Phylogenetic structure of the prokaryotic domain: the primary kingdoms.* They called the new group the archaebacteria — the "ancient bacteria" — a name later trimmed to Archaea, from the Greek *archaios*, ancient, for the suspicion that these were among the oldest lineages on the tree.

![Two trees of life side by side. On the left, the old two-kingdom view: a split into Prokaryotes and Eukaryotes, with archaea hidden inside the bacterial branch. On the right, Woese's three-domain tree of 1977: three trunks rising from a common root, labeled Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya, with the archaea pulled out of the bacterial drawer into a branch of their own.](images/06-archaea-fig-04.png)

*Figure 6.4 — Before and after the molecule. Sorting life by shape kept the archaea misfiled with the bacteria for three centuries. Sorting by the slow ribosomal-RNA molecule revealed a third primary branch, equal in rank to all bacteria and all eukaryotes — two-thirds of the tree that nobody had known was there.*

The reaction, at first, was disbelief and ridicule. Established biology had two kinds of life, the textbooks were settled, and here was an obscure microbiologist with his radioactive films claiming there was a whole third of the tree nobody had ever seen. It took the better part of two decades — and a great deal more sequence, from a great many more organisms — for the field to come around. But come around it did, because the molecule does not lie, and every new sequence confirmed the same three-way split. By the 1990s the three domains — Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya — were the standard map of life, and Woese had done what almost no one ever does: he had discovered that two-thirds of the living world was a thing nobody knew existed.

Pull the lens back and hold the scale of it. A man reading the same slow molecule across organism after organism, in a basement lab, with films and a pencil — and out of that tedium came the recognition that the entire previous map of life had been drawn with a third of the continents missing. Not a new species. Not a new family. A new *domain*, equal in rank to all of animal and plant and fungal and protist life put together, hiding behind the fact that it happened to be the same shape as something else. The archaea were never rare and never hidden. They were in every hot spring and every salt pond and every cow's gut and a fair fraction of the open ocean. We simply had no instrument that could see what they actually were, until one man learned to read the right molecule.

## First, or third?

There is a coda to the Woese story, and it is still being written, and honesty requires that it be told plainly rather than tidied away.

Woese drew three domains, equal, rising side by side from a common root — Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya, three trunks from one base. That is how most people first meet the tree of life, and it is how this chapter has presented the archaea: a third domain, the third form of life. For the purpose of seeing that the archaea are *not* bacteria, three domains is exactly right, and the evidence is overwhelming.

But the deeper question — how the three relate, which split off from which, and in what order — has been reopened by better tools, and the answer now emerging is stranger than three equal trunks. When biologists sequence not one slow molecule but hundreds of genes across many archaeal and eukaryotic lineages, a different picture keeps surfacing. In that picture there are not three domains but *two*: Bacteria on one side, Archaea on the other — and the eukaryotes, the things with a nucleus, *us*, do not form a separate trunk at all. We come up from *inside* the archaea, as one branch nested among them, more closely related to certain archaea than those archaea are to other archaea.

If that is right — and the debate is genuinely live, with the answer shifting depending on which organisms and which genes you include — then the archaea are not the third domain. They are something closer to the *first*. They are the lineage out of which our own deepest ancestry grew, the stock from which the entire complex, nucleated world, every plant and animal and fungus and you, eventually emerged.

How that happened — how a cell from this ancient, extreme-loving, tough-membraned lineage could have given rise to something as elaborate as the cell you are made of — is the deepest question in this whole book.

And it begins with a sample of mud, pulled up from the bottom of the Arctic Ocean, near a vent named for a god of fire.
