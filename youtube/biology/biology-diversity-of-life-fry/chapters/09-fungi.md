# Chapter 9 — Fungi

There is a forest in eastern Oregon, on the dry side of the Blue Mountains, where the conifers die in slow rings. You walk in under ponderosa pine and Douglas fir, and at first the trees look healthy enough. Then you notice the pattern. A stand of standing dead, gray and needleless. Beyond it, trees that are still green but thinning at the crown. Beyond that, the healthy forest again. The dying moves outward in a ragged circle, year by year, like a stain spreading on cloth.

You could dig. If you peeled the bark from the base of one of the dying trees, you would find, pressed between the bark and the wood, sheets of white tissue — flat, fan-shaped, faintly the texture of suede. If you traced it down into the soil, you would find black strands like bootlaces, running from root to root, tree to tree, threading the ground. And if you sampled the white tissue at the far edge of the dying circle and compared its DNA to the tissue under a tree half a mile away, you would find they are the same. Not the same species. The same individual.

The thing killing this forest is one organism. It is a single clone of *Armillaria ostoyae*, the dark honey fungus, and the patch of ground it occupies in the Malheur National Forest covers 2,385 acres — about three and a half square miles. It is, by area, the largest single living thing yet found on Earth.

How old is it? Here the honest answer matters more than the dramatic one. Nobody has weighed it or counted its rings, because it has no rings and most of it cannot be reached. The age comes from genetics and arithmetic: you measure how fast the fungus spreads through soil in a year, then divide the size of the clone by that rate. Do the calculation and you get a minimum of about 2,400 years, with an upper estimate that runs to roughly 8,650. That is an estimate built on a growth rate, not a measurement of the organism itself. Call it somewhere between two and a half and nearly nine millennia. The mass — often quoted as 35,000 tons — is the same kind of number, a model output, not a reading off a scale.

![Aerial view of a forest with a ragged ring of dead conifers spreading outward from a center.](images/09-fungi-fig-01.png)

*Figure 9.1 — The dying expands as a slow circle. Each ring of dead trees marks the advancing front of a single underground fungal individual in the Malheur National Forest, Oregon.*

Set the uncertainty aside for a moment and hold the shape of the fact. The largest organism on Earth is a fungus. It is mostly invisible, mostly underground, and it makes its living by killing trees. It has no center, no head, no face. It is a network — a single connected mesh of threads, fanning through soil and wood across an area you could not walk across in an afternoon.

To understand how a living thing can be shaped like that — all edge, no middle — you have to understand the one trick that defines the entire kingdom. A fungus does not eat the way you do. It does not have an inside.

## A Body That Is All Surface

Consider what eating means for you. You take food into a chamber — your gut — and digest it there, on the inside, in a private pool of enzymes and acid, safe from the world. The breakdown happens within a wall you built around the meal. Then you absorb the small molecules through the gut lining. The strategy is: bring the food in, then take it apart.

A fungus does the reverse. It takes itself out to the food and digests it where it lies.

The working body of a fungus is not the mushroom. The mushroom is a fruiting structure, a temporary spore-launcher that appears for a few days and rots. The actual organism — the part that lives for years, or millennia — is the **mycelium**: a mat of threads, each thread a single file of cells called a **hypha** (plural *hyphae*, from the Greek for "web"). A hypha is extravagantly thin — between about 1 and 30 micrometers across, a fraction of the width of a human hair — and it grows only at its very tip, pushing forward into whatever it is feeding on. A mushroom's worth of mycelium, unspooled into a single line, would run for kilometers.

Here is the move that makes the kingdom what it is. As the hypha grows into wood, or skin, or soil, or rock, it secretes **enzymes** out through its own wall and into the surrounding material. These are **exoenzymes** — *exo* for "outside." They are molecular scissors, each kind cutting a particular bond: cellulases that snip apart the cellulose of plant cell walls, ligninases that attack lignin, the rigid brown polymer that makes wood woody, proteases that cut proteins into their component pieces. The enzymes do the digestion *in the substrate*, outside the fungus, breaking long indigestible polymers into small soluble fragments — sugars, amino acids. Those fragments then diffuse back across the hypha's wall, and the fungus absorbs them.

The fungus, in other words, has turned its gut inside out. The world is its stomach. It digests externally and absorbs the result.

This is why the body has the shape it has. If your business is absorbing dissolved molecules across a surface, then surface is the only thing that matters, and you want as much of it as possible packed into as little volume as you can manage. A thread is the most efficient possible solution: nearly all surface, almost no interior. A mycelium is what you build when surface area is the entire point. It is not a body with a skin around it. It is skin, all the way through.

![Cutaway of a fungal hypha tip growing into wood, with enzymes secreted ahead of it and small molecules diffusing back in.](images/09-fungi-fig-02.png)

*Figure 9.2 — External digestion. The hypha secretes enzymes ahead of its growing tip; the enzymes break wood into soluble fragments; the fragments diffuse back across the wall and are absorbed. The fungus digests the world before it touches it.*

One more piece of the wall. The hypha is held rigid by **chitin** — the same tough, nitrogen-rich polysaccharide that stiffens an insect's exoskeleton and a crab's shell. Plants build their walls from cellulose; fungi build theirs from chitin. This is not a small detail. It is one of the clearest signatures that a fungus is not a plant — and, as we will see, a quiet hint about whose relatives fungi actually are.

So: a fungus eats by growing into its food and digesting it from the outside in. Now we can name what that strategy buys and what it costs, because every solution in biology is a bargain.

What it buys is a menu nothing else can touch. External digestion with a toolkit of polymer-cutting enzymes lets fungi eat things no animal can stomach: the lignin in dead wood, the keratin in hair and hooves and feathers, the cellulose in a fallen tree, even — with the right enzymes and a great deal of patience — extracting minerals from solid rock. Almost every dead thing on the land surface of this planet is ultimately taken apart by fungi. They are the reason a forest is not buried under ten thousand years of its own corpses. They unlock the dead and return it to the living.

What it costs is movement. A creature that digests by growing into its food cannot chase anything. The fungus is **sessile** — fixed in place at any given point, able to advance only by the slow extension of its tips. It cannot pounce, cannot flee, cannot decide. An animal that meets prey can run it down in seconds. A fungus that meets a fallen log waits, and grows, and waits. It does not hunt its food. It out-waits it. The *Armillaria* clone in Oregon has been out-waiting that forest for somewhere between two and nine thousand years. Slowness is not a flaw in the design. It is the design. When your strategy is to dissolve the world and drink it, you have all the time there is.

## The Trade Under the Forest

The fungus that kills trees is the dramatic case. Far more common, and far more important to the running of the planet, is the fungus that feeds them.

Pull up almost any plant — a blade of grass, an oak seedling, a stalk of wheat — and look at the fine roots under a microscope. In roughly nine out of ten land-plant species, you will find fungal hyphae wrapped around the roots, or threaded directly inside the root cells. This partnership has a name: **mycorrhiza**, from the Greek *mykes*, fungus, and *rhiza*, root. The fungus-root. About 90 percent of all land plants form one.

The arrangement is a trade, and the terms are clean. The plant can do something the fungus cannot: photosynthesis, the capture of sunlight to build sugar out of air and water. The fungus can do something the plant struggles at: reaching out through the soil — with that vast, thread-thin surface — to scavenge scarce minerals, above all phosphorus, which barely moves through soil and which a plant root, blunt and slow-growing by comparison, can strip from only the few millimeters around itself. The fungal mycelium extends the plant's reach a hundredfold. So the plant pays the fungus in sugar, and the fungus pays the plant in phosphorus and nitrogen and water. Carbon for minerals. It is one of the oldest trades on land, older than trees, older than seeds — as old, very nearly, as land plants themselves.

![A plant root sheathed and penetrated by fungal hyphae, with the hyphae extending far out into the soil toward mineral particles.](images/09-fungi-fig-03.png)

*Figure 9.3 — The mycorrhizal trade. Fungal hyphae extend the plant's effective root surface enormously, delivering phosphorus and nitrogen inward; the plant pays in photosynthetic sugar. Roughly 90 percent of land-plant species run on this exchange.*

Now, you may have heard a larger story than this — the "wood wide web." The idea that trees in a forest are wired together by a shared fungal network, that a mature tree pumps sugar through the fungus to feed its own shaded seedlings, that an injured tree sends chemical warnings to its neighbors through the mycelium, that the forest is in some sense a cooperative superorganism trading and signaling and caring for its young. It is a lovely story. It has been told in bestselling books and films and a great many breathless articles.

We need to be careful here, because the careful version is more honest and, in the end, more interesting than the breathless one.

The underground networks are real. Fungi do connect to many plants at once; minerals and carbon do move through fungal hyphae; the mycorrhizal trade is genuine and ancient and runs the chemistry of nearly every forest. None of that is in doubt. But in 2023, three researchers — Justine Karst, Melanie Jones, and Jason Hoeksema — went back through the scientific literature on these "common mycorrhizal networks" and checked, claim by claim, what the evidence actually supported. Their paper, in *Nature Ecology & Evolution*, was blunt. Three of the most repeated popular claims, they found, ran well ahead of the data.

The first claim is that these shared networks have been mapped and shown to be widespread in forests. In fact, the structure of such a network had been rigorously mapped in only about two of the world's roughly 73,000 tree species. The second claim is that the networks reliably help seedlings. The evidence for that turned out to be weak and inconsistent — sometimes seedlings near a network do better, sometimes worse, sometimes no different. The third claim — the most cinematic one — is that mature trees use the network to send resources and warning signals to their kin. The support for this was thinner still, and the popular literature had gotten louder than the studies by a process the authors called citation bias: each retelling cited the hopeful result and quietly dropped the caveats, until a tentative finding hardened, through sheer repetition, into a fact everyone "knew."

So here is the careful version. There is a trade economy under the forest, and it is one of the great running engines of life on land. There may also be something like a network linking many plants through shared fungi. What there is not — yet — is good evidence that the network is a cooperative, signaling, kin-nurturing web. The wonder is real. The fairy tale was added later. Keep the first; let go of the second.

## Whose Relatives These Are

We have been treating fungi as a thing apart — not plants, not animals, a kingdom of their own. That is correct. But the next question is the one that reorganizes the picture: if a fungus is not a plant, what *is* it nearest to?

The intuitive answer is wrong. Fungi look like plants. They are rooted in place, they grow toward their food, they have walls around their cells, and for most of the history of biology they were filed beside mosses and ferns as a peculiar kind of plant that had given up on sunlight. The intuition is built on appearance, and appearance, here, lies.

Look instead at the molecules, and a different family tree appears. Fungi and animals share an ancestor that plants do not share — a common forebear that lived after the plant lineage had already split away. Fungi are closer kin to you than they are to the grass they grow in. The clade that holds both animals and fungi has a name built from a shared structural detail: **Opisthokonta**, from the Greek *opisthen*, "rear," and *kontos*, "pole" or "flagellum." The defining feature is the position of the swimming tail. Where most cells that swim by a flagellum push it out in front and pull themselves along, the cells of this group — animal sperm, and the swimming spores of the most ancient fungi — trail a single flagellum behind and push. Rear-flagellum. A sperm cell and a primitive fungal spore swim the same way, by the same engineering, inherited from the same ancestor.

The evidence is not only the tail. Buried in a protein that every cell uses to build other proteins — translation elongation factor EF-1α, part of the machinery that reads the genetic code — there is a stretch of twelve amino acids that animals have and fungi have and plants and other lineages do not. A twelve-letter insertion, shared by the two kingdoms, absent from the rest. You do not acquire the identical twelve-letter insert twice by accident. You inherit it, once, from a common ancestor. The chitin in the fungal wall is part of the same story: animals make chitin too — it is in your tendons' cousins across the animal kingdom, in insect shells and crab armor — while plants do not make it at all.

![A simplified tree showing plants branching off first, then a later split between fungi and animals, both grouped as Opisthokonta and both trailing a single rear flagellum.](images/09-fungi-fig-04.png)

*Figure 9.4 — The rear-flagellum kinship. Animals and fungi share a common ancestor that plants do not — the clade Opisthokonta. A fungal swimming spore and an animal sperm cell push with a single flagellum trailing behind, inherited from one forebear.*

This is the kind of fact this book keeps handing you, and it pays to stop on it. The mushroom in the forest and the deer eating beside it are closer cousins than the mushroom and the tree it grows on. The thing that digests the dead is, on the deep family tree, on our branch — not the plants'.

## The Two-Made-One

There is one more kind of fungus to meet, and it is the strangest, because it is not, strictly, one organism at all.

Walk to a rock face after rain — a gravestone, a boulder, a stretch of bark — and look at the crusts and frills and leaf-like growths spreading across it in gray-green and orange and rust. These are **lichens**. People have looked at them for as long as people have looked at anything, and for most of that time they were taken to be a single odd kind of plant. They are not. A lichen is a partnership: a fungus and a photosynthetic partner — an alga, or a cyanobacterium, or sometimes both — living so tightly fused that they look like one body. The fungus builds the structure, the architecture that clings to the rock and holds water and shields against drought. The photosynthetic partner, sheltered inside that structure, captures sunlight and feeds the fungus sugar. The fungus has done what the mycorrhizal fungi do — found a partner that can photosynthesize — but here it has wrapped the partner up inside its own body and carried it everywhere it goes. A self-contained farm. A fungus that took its food source indoors.

The idea that a lichen is two organisms and not one was so counterintuitive that, when it was first proposed, the experts rejected it as absurd. The proposal came in 1867 from a Swiss botanist named **Simon Schwendener**, who looked closely at lichen tissue and argued that what everyone took to be a single plant was in fact a fungus that had captured and was farming a colony of algae. The leading lichen specialists of the day — men who had spent their careers naming and classifying lichens as autonomous individuals — called the dual hypothesis "unnatural" and "absurd." It violated the deep assumption that an organism is one thing, a self, an individual. It took decades to be accepted. Today it is the founding example of **symbiosis** — two unlike organisms living as one — and the word *symbiosis* itself was coined, a few years later, partly to describe exactly this.

There is a story often told alongside this one, and because it is half-true and half-myth, the details matter. The story goes that **Beatrix Potter** — yes, the Peter Rabbit Beatrix Potter — discovered the dual nature of lichens, wrote it up in a scientific paper, and was dismissed because she was a woman. The truth is more tangled and, in its way, sharper. Potter was a serious and gifted mycologist and scientific illustrator; her paintings of fungi are accurate enough that mycologists still consult them. In 1897 she did submit a paper to the Linnean Society of London — "On the germination of the spores of Agaricineae," about how fungal spores germinate, *not* about lichen symbiosis — and because she was a woman she could not attend the meeting or be a member, so the paper was read on her behalf by a man, received, "laid on the table," and never published. A century later the Society issued a posthumous apology. All of that happened. But the widely repeated claim that her rejected paper proposed the lichen dual hypothesis appears to trace back to a mistranslated footnote, and several historians argue she actually held the older view — that a lichen is a single organism. The injustice was real: a woman shut out of the room where her own work was read aloud. The discovery of lichen symbiosis belongs to Schwendener.

And the partnership turns out to be deeper than even Schwendener guessed. In 2016, a team led by Toby Spribille found that many lichens are not two partners but three: alongside the long-known fungus and alga, there is a second, hidden fungus — a basidiomycete yeast woven into the lichen's outer layer. It had been overlooked for a century and a half. Its presence explained a puzzle that had baffled lichenologists: two lichens that appeared to contain the identical fungus and the identical alga could nonetheless differ — one yellow and laced with a toxin, the other brown and harmless. The difference was the third partner no one had seen. A lichen, that thing we took for one organism and then learned to see as two, may be three.

---

Step back to the dying forest in Oregon, the slow gray circle widening through the pines.

We tend to rank life by what we can see, and what we can see is large, fast, and warm: the deer, the hawk, the standing tree. The fungus offends that ranking at every point. It is the largest organism on the planet and almost entirely invisible. It is among the slowest and among the oldest. It is, on the deep family tree, our own cousin, closer to us than to the plants it dissolves. And it is the reason the visible world does not choke on its own dead — the kingdom that took eating and turned it inside out, that grew a body of pure surface so that it could dissolve the world and drink it.

You will not often look at a forest and see the fungus. But it is the larger part of what the forest is. The trees are the part that catches light. The fungus is the part that handles the dead, feeds the living through the roots of nearly every plant, and, in one Oregon clone, has been quietly running the same patch of ground since before the founding of Rome. The conspicuous things are not the important things. The most of life, here, is the part you have to dig for.
