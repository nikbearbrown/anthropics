# Bacteria

There is a glass tube on a windowsill, and it is full of mud.

A few weeks ago it was just mud — grey-brown, uniform, dredged from the bottom of a pond and packed into the tube with a little shredded newspaper and a spoonful of egg yolk stirred through it for good measure. Someone topped it with pond water, capped it loosely, and set it where the morning sun would reach it. Then they walked away and let it sit.

Now look at it.

The mud has sorted itself. From the bottom up the tube has drawn itself into bands of color, as orderly as a geological core sample, except nobody laid these strata down over millions of years. They appeared in a fortnight, out of a single homogeneous slurry, in a sealed tube where nothing got in and nothing got out. At the very bottom, in the black anaerobic ooze where no oxygen reaches, the mud has gone inky and smells faintly of rotten eggs. Just above it, a band of black gives way to a smear of green, then a streak of purplish red catching the light like an oil slick. Higher up the column shades to rust, then to a cleaner green near the top where the water meets the air and sunlight pours in.

Nobody painted these bands. Nobody seeded them. They organized themselves, each from the population already present in the mud, the way a city sorts itself into neighborhoods. And each band is a different kind of life making a living a different way — a different chemistry, claiming a different slice of the tube where the conditions suit it and starving everywhere else.

This tube is called a Winogradsky column, after the man who first watched mud do this on purpose. And what it shows you, if you let it, is the single most important thing about the organisms that built it — more important than their shape, their size, their names. It is not what they look like. It is what they *eat*, and how they breathe, and where they get the energy to stay alive.

*Figure 5.1 — A Winogradsky column after several weeks. Oxygen seeps down from the surface and runs out within a centimeter or two; sunlight reaches deeper. Each band of color is a population of bacteria settled into the exact chemical zone where its way of making a living pays.*

These are bacteria. And the diversity that matters about them is invisible.

## The chemistry you cannot see

Hold up a bacterium and there is, frankly, not much to look at. A rod. A sphere. A comma, a spiral, a short curved sausage. *Escherichia coli*, the workhorse of every microbiology lab on Earth, is a rod about one to two micrometers long and a quarter to one micrometer wide — so small that two thousand of them laid end to end would barely cross the width of this letter *o*. Under the best light microscope they are featureless gray smudges, jostling. You could line up a hundred bacterial species and most of them would look like the same handful of geometric primitives.

If you tried to write a natural history of bacteria the way you'd write one of birds — by their forms, their plumage, the architecture of their bodies — you would have almost nothing to say. The bodies barely vary. A sphere is a sphere.

So the temptation, for two centuries, was to conclude that bacteria were simple. Boring, even. The primitive sludge at the bottom of the ladder of life, where nothing much was going on.

That is exactly backwards. The bodies are simple. The *chemistry* is the richest on the planet.

Here is the thing to hold onto. Every living cell needs two things to stay alive: a source of energy, and a source of carbon to build itself out of. Animals like us solve both problems the same lazy way — we eat other living things, which hands us energy and carbon in one bite. A cow's body, a leaf, a fish: it is all pre-assembled carbon with energy stored in its chemical bonds, and we take it apart and use both. That is the only trick we know.

Bacteria know all the other tricks. Every single one.

Some bacteria do what plants do — split water using sunlight, releasing oxygen, and use the captured energy to build sugar out of thin air. That is oxygenic photosynthesis, and bacteria invented it; the green algae and the trees only inherited it later, by swallowing a bacterium, which is a story for two chapters from now. Other bacteria run an older, dirtier version of photosynthesis that uses sunlight but splits hydrogen sulfide instead of water, leaving little beads of sulfur behind instead of oxygen — that is the purple-red band glinting in the column, anoxygenic photosynthesizers thriving in the lightless reek where oxygen would poison them.

And some bacteria perform a trick that has no equivalent anywhere in the visible living world. They make their entire living from rock chemistry. No sunlight. No eating. They take an inorganic molecule — ammonia, say, or hydrogen sulfide, or iron, or hydrogen gas — and they oxidize it, the way a fire oxidizes wood, except controlled, harnessing the trickle of energy that the reaction releases. Then they use that energy to pull carbon dioxide out of the air and assemble it into their own bodies. They eat the way a battery discharges. The whole organism runs on the slow rusting of minerals.

This is called chemolithotrophy — from the Greek, *chemo* for chemistry, *lithos* for rock, *trophos* for feeding. Rock-eating, literally. The cell feeds on the chemical disequilibrium of the mineral world, and it was the discovery of this — that something could be alive, could grow and divide and build complex bodies, without ever touching sunlight or another living thing — that the man with the mud column made.

![A diagram contrasting three ways a cell makes a living: an animal cell eating pre-made food for both energy and carbon; a photosynthetic cell using sunlight for energy and air for carbon; and a chemolithotroph oxidizing an inorganic molecule such as hydrogen sulfide or ammonia for energy while pulling carbon dioxide from the air.](images/05-bacteria-fig-02.png)

*Figure 5.2 — Three ways to stay alive. Animals take energy and carbon together by eating. Photosynthesizers take energy from light and carbon from air. Chemolithotrophs take energy from the slow oxidation of rock chemistry and carbon from air — a door into the living world that needs neither sunlight nor food.*

## Winogradsky's window

Sergei Winogradsky was a Russian working in Strasbourg in the mid-1880s, in the laboratory of Anton de Bary, peering at a bacterium called *Beggiatoa* — a long, gliding filament that lived in sulfurous mud and packed itself with bright granules of sulfur. The puzzle was where the sulfur came from and what the organism was doing with it.

What Winogradsky worked out, between 1885 and 1888, broke a rule that had seemed unbreakable. *Beggiatoa* was taking hydrogen sulfide — the rotten-egg gas — and oxidizing it, in the dark, to sulfur and then to sulfate. And it was living on the energy that reaction released. No light. No organic food. It needed nothing but the gas, some carbon dioxide, and a way to burn one against the other.

Every textbook to that point had assumed energy entered the living world through exactly one door: sunlight, captured by green plants. Everything else ate the plants, or ate the things that ate the plants. The sun at the top, and a long descending chain of mouths beneath it. Winogradsky had found a second door. He had found life that ran on geology.

He moved to Zurich and went after the bacteria of the soil that turn ammonia into nitrate — the invisible engine of every farmer's field, the reason fertilizer works. Between 1888 and 1891 he isolated the first pure cultures of these nitrifiers: *Nitrosomonas*, which oxidizes ammonia to nitrite, and *Nitrobacter*, which takes that nitrite the rest of the way to nitrate. Two organisms, two halves of a chemical relay, neither of which had ever needed an animal or a plant or a ray of sun. They lived on nitrogen the way *Beggiatoa* lived on sulfur — burning an inorganic molecule for the energy locked inside it.

The column on the windowsill is Winogradsky's idea made into a teaching object. Seal mud in a tube, give it light at the top, and let the bacteria already living in it sort themselves out. Oxygen seeps down from the air-water surface and runs out a centimeter or two below. Sunlight reaches deeper than oxygen does. So the tube becomes a stack of chemical environments — oxygen-rich and bright at the top, dark and oxygen-free and stinking of sulfide at the bottom, with every gradient in between. And each kind of bacterium settles into the exact layer where its particular chemistry pays. The green band is one trade. The red band is another. The black ooze is a third. Nobody assigned the territories. Each organism found the only stretch of tube where its way of making a living turns a profit, and there it bloomed.

That is the real diversity of bacteria. Not a zoo of shapes. A marketplace of chemistries, every conceivable energy-yielding reaction claimed by something that has learned to live on it.

## The unseen majority

Step back now, all the way back, and look at the scale of what we are talking about.

Count the bacteria and archaea — the prokaryotes, the cells without a nucleus — on the whole planet, and you arrive at a number on the order of four to six times ten to the thirtieth. Write it out: a four followed by thirty zeros. The estimate comes from a 1998 paper by William Whitman and his colleagues with the title that says everything — *Prokaryotes: the unseen majority.* It is not a number the human mind can hold. There is no comparison that makes it tangible, because nothing in our experience is that numerous. There are perhaps ten to the twenty-second stars in the observable universe. The bacteria on Earth outnumber the stars in the sky by a factor of a hundred million.

And almost none of them live where you'd look. Pull up the distribution from that same study. The open ocean holds about ten to the twenty-ninth bacterial cells — a vast number, the entire sunlit sea full of them. The soil holds more, around two and a half times ten to the twenty-ninth. But the real mass is underground, in the dark, in the rock. The cells living in the sediment and crust beneath the seafloor number around three and a half times ten to the thirtieth — more than the ocean and the soil combined, many times over. There is a buried biosphere down there, in the pore spaces of rock kilometers deep, living so slowly that some cells may divide once a century, running on exactly the kind of rock chemistry Winogradsky found. The living world we see — the forests, the herds, the reefs — is a thin bright film over the top of a far larger world that never sees the sun and never will.

Weigh it all, and the carbon locked up in prokaryotes comes to somewhere between sixty and a hundred percent of all the carbon held in every plant on Earth. The trees and grasses and kelp of the entire planet, set against the bacteria, roughly balance.

Now bring the lens all the way in, from the buried global biosphere to the warm, dark interior of a single body. Your body.

For most of a century the textbooks repeated a striking figure: that bacterial cells outnumber human cells in your body by ten to one — that you are, by the count of cells, ninety percent microbe and ten percent you. It was a wonderful line. It was also wrong. In 2016 Ron Sender, Shai Fuchs, and Ron Milo went back through the arithmetic carefully — how big the cells actually are, how much room the gut actually holds, where the bacteria actually concentrate — and the real ratio came out close to one to one. Around thirty-nine trillion bacterial cells, against roughly thirty trillion of your own. About 1.3 microbial cells for every human cell.

That is less dramatic and far stranger. You are not a human body lightly dusted with bacteria, nor a bacterial colony wearing a human costume. You are very nearly an even partnership — a single ecosystem walking around, half your cells your own and half of them a teeming, metabolizing nation of microbes, mostly packed into your gut, running chemistries your own cells never learned. They make vitamins you cannot make. They digest fibers you cannot digest. They are not passengers. They are staff.

## Why a small cell borrows instead of invents

Here is the trade-off at the heart of being a bacterium, and it is a beautiful one once you see it.

A bacterium is built for speed. Its genome — the full set of its DNA — is tiny and stripped of slack. *E. coli* carries about 4.6 million base pairs, the rungs of its DNA ladder, coiled into a single circular chromosome. A human cell carries something like three billion. The bacterium has thrown out almost everything it does not constantly use. There are no long stretches of junk, no luxurious redundancy, no spare parts. Lean, fast, efficient.

And it divides fast. In rich broth, *E. coli* can double its numbers every twenty minutes. Think about what that means. One cell becomes two, two become four, and if nothing stopped it — if the food never ran out and the waste never built up — a single bacterium would in a day produce a mass of offspring comparable to the planet. It never happens, because the food runs out almost immediately and the predators arrive and the waste turns toxic. But the *capacity* is there. The cell is a machine optimized to copy itself before anything can catch it.

There is a wrinkle here worth pausing on, because it tells you how hard these cells push. Copying the entire chromosome — unzipping all 4.6 million rungs and duplicating each one — takes the cell about forty minutes. But the cell divides every twenty. How do you finish a forty-minute job in twenty minutes? You don't. You start the next copy of the chromosome before you've finished the last one. A fast-growing *E. coli* has multiple copying machines running on the same chromosome at once, like a printer that begins the second run of a document before the first run is off the press. The cell is, in a real sense, always pregnant with its own future generations.

But speed costs you something, and here is the bill. A tiny, streamlined genome carries almost no reserve. There is no warehouse of unused genes to draw on when the world changes — when a new poison appears, a new food source, a new competitor. An animal facing a changed world has to wait for mutation and selection to slowly invent a solution over many generations. A bacterium does not have that kind of time, and its lean genome does not hold many spare answers.

So bacteria solved the problem a different way. They do not, mostly, *invent* what they need. They *borrow* it.

This is horizontal gene transfer, and it is the move that makes the bacterial world behave less like a family tree and more like a marketplace. A gene — say, one that confers resistance to an antibiotic, or the ability to digest a new sugar — can move sideways, from one living bacterium to another, even across species, without any parent-to-offspring inheritance at all. There are three doors through which a gene can walk.

The first is **transformation**: a bacterium simply picks up loose DNA from its surroundings — fragments shed by dead cells that have burst open — and stitches the useful pieces into its own chromosome. It is reading the wills of the dead and keeping what's valuable. This is the route Frederick Griffith stumbled onto in 1928, when he found that dead, harmless bacteria could somehow pass their virulence to living ones, the first hint that something heritable was moving between cells outside of reproduction.

The second is **transduction**: a virus — a bacteriophage, the kind that preys on bacteria — accidentally packages a scrap of its host's DNA when it assembles new copies of itself, and carries that scrap into the next cell it infects. The virus, meant to be a parasite, becomes a courier. Norton Zinder and Joshua Lederberg demonstrated this in 1952.

The third is **conjugation**: two living bacteria make direct contact, build a bridge between them, and pass DNA across it — usually a small ring of DNA called a plasmid, which carries genes the chromosome doesn't. Lederberg and Edward Tatum showed this in 1946. Conjugation is thought to be the main way antibiotic-resistance genes spread through a population, which is why resistance can sweep through a hospital ward faster than any single lineage of bacteria could possibly evolve it on its own. One cell figures out how to survive the drug; within hours, it can hand the recipe to thousands of strangers.

This is the deep difference between how bacteria adapt and how we do. We are stuck with the genome our parents gave us; novelty comes only through the slow accident of mutation, passed down. A bacterium lives in a world where useful genes circulate like currency, available to be acquired from anyone, alive or dead, related or not. The tiny genome is not a limitation it suffers. It is a strategy it exploits — stay lean, stay fast, and shop for whatever you need from the open market of the living and the dead around you.

## What this means for the tree

There is a quiet consequence here, and it is worth sitting with before we move on, because it complicates the whole picture this book is built around.

The grand idea of this book is that everything alive hangs on a single tree — one trunk, branching and branching, every organism a leaf on a lineage that runs unbroken back to one ancestral population almost four billion years ago. Among animals and plants and fungi, that tree is real and reliable. You inherit your genes from your parents and from no one else. The branches diverge and never touch again. A family tree is a clean diagram.

But horizontal gene transfer means the bacterial part of the tree is not clean at all. Genes leap between branches. A bacterium can carry, in its single chromosome, genes inherited from its ancestors *and* genes borrowed last week from an entirely unrelated cell, perhaps from a different phylum, perhaps from a virus. Trace any one gene back and you may get one tree; trace a different gene in the same organism and you may get a completely different one. The trunk, down here among the prokaryotes, is not a trunk at all in places. It is a thicket, a braid, a tangle of lineages constantly exchanging material across the gaps between them.

This does not break the idea of common descent. There is still one origin; all the genes still trace back, eventually, to that ancestral population. But it means that for bacteria the metaphor of a tree — neat, dividing, never-rejoining — is a simplification we impose for clarity. The reality is messier and, in its way, more generous. Life down here does not hoard its inventions within a lineage. It shares.

Which is why the diversity of bacteria can never be counted the way you'd count beetles or birds, by tallying up distinct, well-bounded species. The boundaries leak. Two bacteria that look identical may carry wildly different genes; two that share a recent ancestor may have swapped so much DNA with strangers that they no longer make a living the same way at all. The unit of bacterial diversity is not really the species. It is the chemistry — the trade, the metabolism, the particular way of pulling energy out of the world — and chemistries can be borrowed, lost, recombined, and passed around a population like rumors.

Go back to the tube on the windowsill. The bands of color are not an exhibit of how many kinds of bacteria there are. They are a map of how many ways there are to be alive — how many doors there are between the chemistry of the dead, mineral world and the chemistry of a living cell. Bacteria found all of them. They were finding them for a billion years before there was an animal to notice, and they will be finding them for a billion years after the last animal is gone, in the dark rock under the seafloor, oxidizing iron, fixing carbon, dividing once a century, the unseen majority quietly running the chemistry of the planet that the rest of us merely live on.

We are the leaves. They are the soil, and the air, and most of the tree.
