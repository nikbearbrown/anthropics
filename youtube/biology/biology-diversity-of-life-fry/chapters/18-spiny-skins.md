# Spiny Skins

The tide has gone out and left the mussel bed exposed to the gray light, a shelf of black shells packed so tight they overlap like roof tiles. Among them something is moving — slowly, the way a glacier moves, so slowly you have to look twice to be sure it is moving at all. An arm, then a second arm, hauling a five-rayed body up over the shells. On the underside of each arm runs a double row of small translucent stalks, hundreds of them, each one reaching, gripping, releasing, reaching again, a thousand tiny feet rippling in waves. The animal climbs onto a single mussel and folds itself over the shell like a hand closing.

Then it begins to pull.

The mussel's two shells are clamped shut by a muscle stronger, pound for pound, than anything in the predator on top of it. The animal cannot match that strength in a single heave. It does not try. It applies a steady, patient tension across the shell with hundreds of those little stalked feet at once, a few grams each, and it holds. The mussel's adductor muscle is built for a quick slam, not for an hour-long tug of war. Minute by minute it tires. The shells part — a hundredth of a millimeter, then a tenth, then enough to slide a sheet of paper through. And now the predator does something that no animal with a face could do. It pushes its own stomach out through its mouth, slides the everted stomach into the gap between the shells, and digests the mussel alive, inside the mussel's own armor, before drawing the dissolved meal back in.

You are watching a sea star eat. And almost everything strange about how it just hunted — the patience, the hundreds of little feet, the stomach turned inside out, the body with no front and no head — comes from a single piece of plumbing that no other phylum on Earth ever built.

## The animal that runs on seawater

Pick up a sea star, gently, and turn it over. The mouth is in the center of the underside. Running out along each arm is a groove, and lining the groove are those translucent stalks — the tube feet. There are hundreds, sometimes thousands. To understand how they work is to understand the whole phylum, because the tube feet are the visible ends of a hydraulic system that fills the animal's body, and that system runs not on blood, not on muscle pulling bone, but on seawater under pressure.

It is called the water-vascular system, and here is what it actually does. Somewhere on the animal's upper surface is a small, pitted plate, the madreporite — Latin for "mother-stone," because under a lens it looks like a piece of porous coral. The madreporite is a sieve, a filtered intake valve open to the sea. Water drawn in through it runs down a tube to a ring canal that circles the mouth, and from the ring canal radial canals run out along each arm. Off the radial canals branch hundreds of side channels, and each one ends in a tube foot.

Now look at a single tube foot closely, because this is the engine. The foot is a thin-walled tube of muscle, closed at its outer tip with a small sucker, and at its inner end it opens into a muscular bulb tucked up inside the arm — the ampulla, named for the round-bellied Roman flask. The whole thing is full of seawater. Squeeze the ampulla and the water it holds has nowhere to go but down into the tube foot, which lengthens and stiffens and reaches out, a tiny hydraulic cylinder. Pressure the tip against rock and pull back, and the sucker grips. Relax the ampulla and the foot shortens and lets go. One foot, on its own, moves a few grams and does nothing. But the animal coordinates hundreds of them in traveling waves — a row of feet reaching forward, gripping, pulling the body along, releasing, swinging forward to grip again — and the sum of all those small pressures is an animal that walks across the seafloor and prizes open a mussel by sheer accumulated patience.

Stop and notice what is missing. There is no heart driving this. There is no skeleton for muscle to lever against in the way your muscles lever against your bones. There is no central brain telling each foot what to do. Movement here is pressure, applied through thousands of small soft cylinders, each gripping and letting go on its own. It is locomotion built from hydraulics, and it belongs to one group of animals alone — the echinoderms, from the Greek *echinos*, a hedgehog or sea urchin, and *derma*, skin: the spiny-skinned. Sea stars, brittle stars, sea urchins, sea cucumbers, and the stalked, flower-shaped sea lilies. About seven thousand species, all of them living in the sea.

There is a second strangeness in the body, easy to miss because we expect a skeleton to be a single connected frame. The echinoderm has a skeleton — a hard one, built of calcium carbonate, the chalky mineral of seashell — but it is not assembled the way ours is, nor the way a crab's shell is. It is made of countless small separate plates and spines, called ossicles, embedded in the body wall and held together by connective tissue rather than fused into a rigid case. And that connective tissue can change its stiffness on command, in seconds, under nervous control — soft and pliable one moment so the animal can fold and creep, locked rigid the next so it can hold a posture for hours without spending a calorie of muscle. A sea cucumber threatened by a predator can liquefy its own body wall and pour through a crack, then stiffen again on the far side. The skeleton is not a frame the muscles drive; it is a fabric the animal can dial between cloth and stone. Between that adjustable skeleton and the seawater hydraulics of the tube feet, the echinoderm runs almost its entire body on systems we have no equivalent of — no bones-and-muscles, no pumping heart, no head.

All of them. This is worth holding onto. In something like half a billion years, the echinoderms have never once produced a single species that lives in fresh water or on land. Not a lake, not a river, not a damp forest floor — not one. They are the largest animal phylum with no member anywhere outside salt water. The reason is the very system that makes them work: a body run on seawater under pressure, with thin permeable tube feet open to the sea through that filtered madreporite, cannot easily survive where the water is fresh and would flood the animal's tissues, or where there is no water at all to fill the feet. The hydraulic trick that conquered the seafloor is also a leash that has never let go. *This works because* seawater is free, abundant, and incompressible, a working fluid the animal need never manufacture. *It fails when* there is no seawater — and so half a billion years on, the echinoderms remain prisoners of the ocean that built them.

## The animal that threw away its head

Now the strangeness gets deeper, and it goes to the heart of this book's quiet argument.

Look at the sea star's body plan: five arms radiating from a central mouth, no front, no back, no left, no right. Biologists call it pentaradial symmetry — five-fold radial symmetry. A radial animal has no head. It faces every direction at once and no direction in particular. For a slow bottom-dweller this is a sensible design: food, threat, and mate can arrive from any side, and the animal that points equally in all directions is never caught looking the wrong way. The cost is just as plain. With no front, there is no place to concentrate sense organs and nerves; with no head, there is no brain; with no preferred direction, there is no fast, committed pursuit. A sea star can go anywhere and chase nothing. *This works because* a radial body senses and feeds in every direction, ideal for an animal that creeps; *it fails when* the animal needs to go somewhere on purpose, quickly, head-first — which it cannot.

You would think, looking at that five-rayed adult, that the echinoderms are an ancient radial lineage, built around a center from the beginning, cousins to the jellyfish. They are not. And the proof is in their children.

A sea star does not hatch as a tiny five-armed star. It hatches as a microscopic free-swimming larva, drifting in the plankton — and that larva is bilaterally symmetric. It has a left side and a right side that mirror each other, a front and a back, the body plan of a fish or a worm or a person. It swims with a definite direction. Only later, when the larva settles and undergoes a wrenching metamorphosis, does the bilateral body get torn down and the radial adult built across it, the five-fold pattern imposed on top of a body that began with two clear sides.

Read that again, because it is the whole point. The echinoderm's radial form is not original. It is a later edit laid over an earlier bilateral plan that the animal still builds, every generation, in its larva and then discards. The sea star is descended from bilateral, head-bearing ancestors. It inherited the front end — and then, in the lineage, gave it up. The radial adult is not an animal that never had a head. It is an animal whose ancestors had one and whose larva still starts to make one, and which then throws it away to become a creature that faces all directions and goes nowhere fast.

This is the shape of fact that keeps recurring in these chapters, and you should be starting to feel it before it is ever named. Nothing in the living world begins from a clean page. The radial sea star is a modified bilateral animal. It did not invent radial symmetry out of nothing; it edited an existing two-sided plan into a five-rayed one, and the edit happens in front of you, in the metamorphosis of every larva, every generation. There are no new starts here. Only changes rung on what was already there.

The metamorphosis itself is worth lingering on, because it is one of the more violent rebuilds in animal life and it makes the editing literal. The echinoderm larva swims and feeds in the plankton for weeks, a transparent, ciliated, bilaterally symmetric creature with a clear front and back. Then, on one side of its body, a structure begins to grow — the rudiment, the seed of the future adult — and as it develops, the larval body is not so much remodeled as cannibalized. Much of the larva is resorbed and its materials reused; the adult is assembled, five-fold and radial, largely out of the dismantled parts of the bilateral animal that built it. A creature with a head and a direction is taken apart, and from its pieces a creature with neither is put together. It is the same trick as the sea squirt that swims with a notochord and then settles and digests its own brain — the body of the larva is the raw material for the body of the adult, edited past recognition. The bilateral larva is not a leftover the echinoderm has failed to shed. It is the construction site. The adult cannot be built any other way, because the genetic instructions for making an echinoderm begin, as they always have, with the instructions for making a bilateral animal, and only then add the steps that tear it down and lay the five-rayed plan across it.

## Which side of the family, and a clade that may not be one

If the sea star's ancestors were bilateral and had a front end, then the echinoderms belong on the great branch of two-sided animals, the Bilateria — the branch that holds the worms, the insects, the molluscs, and us. The question is which part of that branch. And answering it takes us to a piece of embryology that named a whole group of animals, and to a piece of recent science that is now quietly unnaming it.

Watch a fertilized animal egg divide. It becomes a hollow ball of cells, and then one side of the ball pushes inward, folding in to form the gut — a process called gastrulation, and the dimple where the infolding begins is the blastopore, the "mouth of the early embryo." Here animals split into two great camps by what that first opening becomes. In one camp, the blastopore becomes the mouth, and the anus forms second, as a new opening at the other end. Greek for first-mouth: these are the protostomes — the arthropods, molluscs, annelids, flatworms, most of the animal kingdom. In the other camp, the order is reversed. The blastopore becomes the *anus*, and the mouth forms second. Greek for second-mouth: the deuterostomes. And the echinoderms are deuterostomes — and so are we.

That shared quirk of development, the blastopore becoming the anus, is why echinoderms, the acorn worms called hemichordates, and the chordates — the group that holds the vertebrates, ourselves included — were bundled together for over a century into a clade called Deuterostomia. Your kinship to a sea star, the textbooks said, is written in the fact that you and it both built your back doors before your front ones. It is a strange and satisfying piece of family resemblance, and it has been taught as settled for more than a hundred years, ever since the zoologist Karl Grobben formalized the two camps in 1908.

Here is where honesty demands a stop. The "second-mouth" development is real, and the close kinship of echinoderms with the acorn worms — a pairing called Ambulacraria — is robust, well supported by many genes. But whether the chordates, our own group, truly form a single clade with the Ambulacraria, sharing a common ancestor that excludes the protostomes — whether Deuterostomia is one real branch of the tree at all — is no longer secure. In 2021, a team led by Paschalia Kapli published an analysis in *Science Advances* with a deliberately uncomfortable conclusion: the statistical support for a monophyletic Deuterostomia is, in their words, always equivocal and barely higher than the support for alternatives in which it is not a single branch. Worse, their simulations showed that the apparent support could be an artifact — a mirage thrown up by the difficulty of the problem itself. The branches deep in the animal tree are short and very old; the molecular models we use to read them are imperfect; and under those conditions a method can manufacture a grouping that is not really there. A 2025 paper in *Molecular Phylogenetics and Evolution* pressed the same doubt under a blunt title: is the deuterostome clade an artifact?

So the careful statement is this. Echinoderms develop in the deuterostome way; they are bilaterians; they are close cousins of the acorn worms; that much holds. But the comfortable old picture in which a single line called Deuterostomia split, long ago, into our branch and the sea star's branch — that picture may be an error of method rather than a fact of history. The tree is being redrawn here, in real time, and the drawing is not finished. The right posture is not to pretend the question is closed but to hold it open, which is what the working scientists are doing.

## A flower that is an animal

Pull the lens back one last time. Down in the deep sea, anchored to the bottom on a slender stalk, swaying in the slow current, grows what looks for all the world like a flower — a cup of feathery arms on a stem, pale and delicate. It is not a plant. It is a sea lily, a crinoid, one of the oldest echinoderm forms, and its relatives have been rooted to the seafloor since long before there were flowers, before there were forests, before anything with a backbone walked on land. Those feathery arms are catching food particles from the water with the same hydraulic tube feet that the sea star uses to climb a mussel. Same plumbing, different life. The flower is an edit of the star; the star is an edit of a bilateral ancestor with a head it gave away.

And every one of these animals — the patient star with its everted stomach, the urchin with its forest of spines, the rooted sea lily that mimics a bloom — runs on a body filled with the sea it has never been able to leave. Half a billion years, seven thousand species, and not one of them has stepped onto the land or into a freshwater stream, because the very system that lets them walk also chains them to the ocean that built it. The tide that went out this morning will come back in. It always does. It is the one thing the spiny-skinned animals have always been able to count on.
