# Chapter 10 — Onto Land

In a field in Aberdeenshire, in the northeast of Scotland, there is a layer of rock that does not behave like rock. It is chert — a dense, glassy stone, mostly silica, the stuff of flint. Cut a slab thin enough that light passes through it, polish it, and put it under a microscope, and you are looking not at minerals but at a swamp. A whole wetland, frozen mid-life. Stems still standing where they grew. The cells inside the stems still visible, one by one, their walls intact. Spores caught in the act of release. Tiny animals — mites, primitive insects, things with too many legs — preserved in the gaps. The water that fed all this is gone, replaced molecule by molecule with quartz, but the bodies it held are still here, rendered in stone with a fidelity that no ordinary fossil can match.

This is the **Rhynie chert**, and it is roughly 407 million years old. It formed the way a few rare places preserve life almost perfectly: a hot spring, like the geysers of Yellowstone, repeatedly flooded a low wetland with water so loaded with dissolved silica that when it cooled and the water drained, the silica crystallized inside and around everything it had touched — entombing the living community where it stood, down to the individual cell. It is the oldest preserved land ecosystem we have caught in place, rather than as scattered washed-in fragments. It is a photograph of the land at the moment life had only just begun to hold it.

And what is in the photograph is small. The plants of the Rhynie chert — they have names now, *Rhynia*, *Aglaophyton* — are green stems, leafless, rootless, knee-high at the most generous, many of them no taller than your thumb. There are no trees. There are no leaves. There are no flowers, no seeds, no grass. The land, 407 million years ago, was a film of green and a scatter of low stems, wet at the feet, with nothing standing tall enough to cast a shadow worth the name.

The chert itself was found by accident. In the 1910s a country doctor and amateur geologist named William Mackie was mapping the rocks around the village of Rhynie when he split open a slab and saw, embedded in the glassy stone, the unmistakable cross-sections of plant stems — not impressions or carbon films, the usual fossil ghosts, but the plants themselves, their tissues turned to quartz. It was a natural microscope slide, a whole ecosystem pre-sectioned by geology, and it gave us the first clear look at the anatomy of the earliest land plants. Almost everything we know about how those first stems were built inside — the water-conducting cells, the breathing pores, the spore capsules — we read off slices of the Rhynie chert.

To get even this far had taken an enormous amount of solving. Because the land, for a thing made of water and built for water, is a desert with a view. Everything that made life easy in the sea — the buoyancy that held you up, the water that surrounded every cell and kept it from drying, the medium through which sperm could simply swim to egg — all of it is gone the moment you crawl out. The question of this chapter is how life paid for the sunlight. And the answer, as it always is, is one trait at a time, each trait an edit of what came before, each with a cost attached.

Let us start with the deepest of those costs, the one the first land plants never escaped — and to see it, we have to look at the strangest thing about how a plant has sex.

---

## The Two Lives of a Plant

Here is a fact about plants that almost no one is taught plainly, and it is the key to everything that follows. A plant is not one organism living one life. It is two organisms, taking turns.

You are a single kind of body. Your cells carry two copies of every chromosome — one from each parent — and that doubled state, called **diploid** (from the Greek *diploos*, "double"), is what you are from fertilized egg to death. The only cells in your body that carry a single copy, the **haploid** cells (*haploos*, "single"), are your eggs or sperm, and they exist only briefly, to be passed on. You are a diploid organism that makes haploid cells. Simple.

A plant runs two full bodies, one diploid and one haploid, and they alternate, generation after generation, each building the other. This is **alternation of generations**, and once you see it, the whole plant kingdom rearranges itself in your mind.

Follow the cycle. Start with the diploid body — call it the **sporophyte**, the "spore-plant," because that is its job. Instead of making sperm and eggs directly, as you do, it makes **spores**: single haploid cells, produced by the same kind of halving division that makes your egg and sperm, but these spores do not fuse with anything. Each spore, on its own, grows into an entire separate organism — a haploid body, the **gametophyte**, the "gamete-plant." This second body is the one that makes the sex cells: haploid eggs and haploid sperm. Those fuse, restoring the double set, and the fertilized egg grows back into a sporophyte. Two bodies. Two lives. Diploid makes spores makes haploid makes gametes makes diploid again, around and around.

Now, the question that organizes the plants is: **which of the two bodies is the big one?** In the plants you know best — the trees, the grass, the ferns — the sporophyte is the plant. The gametophyte has shrunk to something tiny or hidden. But run the clock back to the first plants on land, the mosses and liverworts and hornworts, the group we lump as **bryophytes**, and the balance is reversed. In a moss, the green cushion you see, the soft plush carpet on the wet stone — *that is the gametophyte*. That is the haploid body. The sporophyte, the diploid body, is the thin brown stalk with a capsule on top that pokes up out of the moss cushion in season, and it is small, short-lived, and cannot even feed itself — it stays attached to the green gametophyte and lives off it, like a fruit that never quite leaves the branch.

![A diagram of alternation of generations in a moss, showing the dominant green gametophyte cushion with the small dependent brown sporophyte stalk rising from it.](images/10-onto-land-fig-02.png)

*Figure 10.2 — Alternation of generations in a moss. The green cushion you see is the haploid gametophyte. The brown stalk-and-capsule rising from it is the diploid sporophyte — small, short-lived, and nutritionally dependent on the gametophyte beneath it. In all later plants this balance reverses.*

Hold on to that image, because it tells you what the first land plants were and were not. And it sets up the cost they could not pay off.

## Why the First Land Plants Stayed Small and Wet

A moss is small. Not small the way a seedling is small, on its way to being large. Small as a permanent condition. A moss cushion is a few centimeters of soft green, and no moss has ever grown into a tree. Two limits hold it there, and they are worth taking one at a time, because the rest of plant evolution is the story of breaking them.

**The first limit: no plumbing.** To grow tall, a plant has to move water up. Roots take water from the soil; leaves, high up, lose it to the air and need it replaced. A tall plant must lift water meters into the sky against gravity, continuously, all day. To do that you need pipes — and bryophytes have none. They have no **vascular tissue**: no **xylem**, the file of reinforced, hollowed-out dead cells that later plants use as water pipes, and no **phloem**, the living tubes that carry sugar. Without pipes, a moss moves water the only way left to it — by **diffusion**, water seeping from cell to cell, and across the wet surface of the whole plant directly from the air and rain. Diffusion is a fine way to move water across a tenth of a millimeter. It is hopeless across a meter. The physics simply will not carry water that far without plumbing. So a plant with no pipes cannot be tall. It can only be as big as diffusion can supply, which is to say: a few centimeters, and damp. No plumbing, no height. The moss is the size that diffusion permits.

**The second limit: the sperm has to swim.** Recall the haploid body, the gametophyte, the one that makes the sex cells. In a moss, when it is time to reproduce, the gametophyte makes sperm — and bryophyte sperm have flagella, little whipping tails, because they are built to swim. They have to reach an egg held in a neighboring plant, and the only way they can get there is to swim through liquid water. A film of rain or dew across the surface of the moss cushion, after a wet night — that is the medium the sperm swims through, from the plant that made it to the plant that holds the egg. No water film, no journey. No journey, no fertilization.

This is the deepest tie to the old life, the one the move onto land did not cut. A moss has hauled its body out of the sea, given it a waxy skin in places, learned to live in the air — and then, at the single most important moment of its life, it has to wait for rain, because its sperm still swims the way its ancestors' sperm swam in the ocean. The bryophytes left the water. Their sex did not. They are chained to the damp not by their bodies but by their gametes.

Now name the trade plainly, because it is the trade that defines the whole conquest of land. **What the move to land bought: sunlight and air.** On land, sunlight pours down unfiltered by meters of seawater, and carbon dioxide — the raw material of photosynthesis — is freely available in the air, not scarce and dissolved. For a photosynthetic organism, the land is a feast of light and carbon that the crowded, shaded, competitive sea cannot match. **What it cost: water.** Everything that water did for free in the sea — holding the body up, keeping every cell from drying, carrying the sperm to the egg — now had to be paid for, trait by trait, or done without. The bryophytes paid the cheapest possible price: they stayed small enough not to need plumbing, and stayed near enough to water that their sperm could still swim when it rained. They took the sunlight and accepted the chains. For 470 million years, the bargain has held: mosses are still here, still small, still waiting for the rain.

## The Toolkit for Staying Dry

The plants that broke the limits did it by adding tools, one at a time, and you can read the tools right off the Rhynie chert and the rocks around it.

The first tool is a coat. A plant exposed to air dries out — water evaporates from every wet surface — so land plants cover themselves in a **cuticle**, a thin waxy layer secreted over the outer cells, waterproof, sealing the moisture in. But a perfect seal creates a new problem: photosynthesis needs carbon dioxide *in* and releases oxygen *out*, and a waterproof coat blocks the gas exchange along with the water loss. So the cuticle is punctured by adjustable pores called **stomata** (from the Greek *stoma*, "mouth") — tiny mouths in the leaf surface, each flanked by two cells that swell to open the pore and slacken to close it. Open the stomata and the plant breathes and loses water; close them and it conserves water but starves of carbon. The stoma is a valve that manages the central conflict of land plant life: every breath costs water. (These pores are old — the land-plant common ancestor likely had them, and some liverworts have since lost them, which tells you the lack of stomata in those liverworts is not primitive but a later simplification, a tool put down rather than never picked up.)

The second tool is the one that broke the height limit: **vascular tissue**, the plumbing. The key innovation is xylem — pipes built from dead cells whose walls are stiffened with **lignin**, the same rigid polymer that makes wood hard, so the pipes do not collapse under the suction needed to pull water up them. And the pulling is the clever part: a vascular plant does not push water up from below; it lets the leaves at the top lose water to the air through the open stomata, and that loss pulls the entire connected column of water upward through the xylem, the way sucking on a straw draws the drink up — the evaporation at the top doing the lifting, the lignin-walled pipes holding their shape against the pull. With pipes and a way to pull, a plant can finally stand tall, lift water meters into the air, raise leaves above its neighbors into the unshaded light. The ferns and their kin — the first vascular plants, the **seedless vascular plants** — grew with this toolkit into forests while the bryophytes stayed at ankle height. By the late Devonian and the Carboniferous that followed, the descendants of these tools were trees the height of office buildings.

Xylem — dead, lignin-walled cells forming pipes — lets evaporation at the leaves pull a continuous water column upward, freeing vascular plants to grow tall.*

But notice what none of these tools touched. The cuticle keeps water in. The stomata manage the breathing. The xylem lifts water high. None of them addresses the swimming sperm. Even the tall ferns of the Carboniferous, towering over the bryophytes, still released flagellated sperm that had to swim through a film of water to reach an egg. The ferns broke the height limit and kept the water chain. To break *that* — to have sex without standing water — took an invention so consequential it gets its own chapter. The seed and the pollen grain. That is the next cut to the cord, and it is the one that finally let plants leave the damp behind.

There is something to notice in how this whole sequence is built, because it is the shape of nearly every story in this book. The bryophytes did not become ferns. A moss is not a fern that has not grown up yet; it is a different lineage that solved the problem of land with a smaller toolkit and stopped there, and it is still here, still small, still doing fine after 470 million years. The fern did not become a seed plant. The tools — cuticle, stomata, xylem, and later the seed — are not steps on a single ladder that every plant climbed. They are inventions that arose in particular lineages and were inherited by their descendants, while the cousins who lacked them carried on without. The "ascending grades" picture, the old textbook image of plants marching upward from primitive moss to advanced flower, is misleading in exactly this way. There is no march. There is a tree, branching, with each branch keeping the tools its ancestors had and, in some lines, adding one more. The moss and the orchid are not early and late versions of the same thing. They are two leaves on a tree, both alive now, one of them carrying a great many more tools than the other — and both of them perfectly suited to where they live.

## When, and in What Order

Before we pull back, a word about dates, because the question of *when* plants came onto land is itself a small lesson in how we read deep time — and the two best ways of reading it disagree.

The first way is to find the oldest fossils. The earliest hard evidence of land plants is not stems or leaves but spores — specifically **cryptospores**, fused little clusters of spores of the kind bryophyte-grade plants make — and the oldest of those are about 470 million years old, from the middle of the Ordovician period, found in rocks from Saudi Arabia and North China. So the fossils say: land plants by ~470 million years ago.

The second way is to count mutations. Take the living plants, line up their DNA, count the genetic differences that have accumulated between lineages, and — knowing roughly how fast mutations pile up — run the clock backward to estimate when the lineages split. This is the molecular clock, and when it is applied to land plants it gives an older answer: the origin reaches back toward the Cambrian, around 500 million years ago, perhaps 30 million years before the oldest spore anyone has dug up. The gap is real, and it is the usual kind: the molecules record a lineage from the moment it splits, while the rocks record it only from the moment it left something hard enough to fossilize. A plant can exist for a long time, soft and small and rare, before it leaves a trace a geologist will ever find. So the honest statement is a range: land plants arose somewhere around 470 to 500 million years ago, with the molecules arguing for the early end and the fossils confirming the late.

There was an order question too, and it was not settled until recently. For a long time the textbooks drew the bryophytes as a "paraphyletic grade" — a series of ascending steps, mosses and liverworts and hornworts each splitting off one after another on the way up to the vascular plants, as if they were rungs of a ladder rather than branches of a tree. Whether that was right turned out to be genuinely hard to resolve; as late as 2018 the data could not rule out several arrangements. It took a flood of whole-genome sequences in 2020 and 2021, with better sampling of the long-neglected hornworts, to tip it. The current answer is that the bryophytes are a single branch after all — a clade, not a ladder — with mosses and liverworts as closest kin and hornworts beside them. The plants that stayed small and damp are not the failed first drafts of the plants that grew tall. They are a sister lineage that took the cheaper bargain and kept it.

## What Greening the Land Did to the Planet

We have been standing close, at the scale of a moss cushion and a stoma. Pull the lens all the way back, because what these small green things did, summed over the whole planet and tens of millions of years, changed the air, the rock, and the climate of the Earth.

Land plants run on carbon dioxide. They pull it from the air and lock it into their tissues — into sugar, into cellulose, into lignin, into wood. A single plant doing this matters not at all. But cover the continents in plants, century after century, and you are running a planetary-scale pump, drawing carbon dioxide out of the atmosphere and burying it in living and dead tissue faster than it returns. Carbon dioxide is a greenhouse gas; it holds the planet's heat. Pull enough of it out of the air, and the planet cools.

That is, in outline, what happened. The greening of the land in the Ordovician and Devonian — first by the bryophytes and their relatives, then by the rising vascular plants — drew down atmospheric carbon dioxide on a scale large enough to be implicated in global cooling and the spread of ice. Plants also accelerated the weathering of rock: their tissues and the acids around their growing parts broke down silicate minerals, and that chemical weathering consumes carbon dioxide too, locking it into new minerals washed to the sea. Small plants, vast numbers, deep time. The film of green that could not grow taller than your thumb helped pull the planet's thermostat down.

There is a tidiness to it that is almost unsettling. The plants came onto land to reach the sunlight and the carbon-rich air. In taking the carbon, they cooled the world that had offered it. The thing they fed on was the thing that warmed the planet, and by eating it they changed the climate they had crawled out into.

---

When the first cell crawled onto a Devonian shore and stayed, it did not conquer the land. It bargained with it. It traded the water that had held it up and kept it wet and carried its sex, in exchange for unfiltered light and free carbon — and then spent the next 470 million years paying down that trade one invention at a time. A coat to hold the water in. A valve to manage the breathing. Pipes and a pump to climb into the light. And, still to come, a sealed embryo and a grain of pollen to finally cut the sex free of standing water.

You can read the whole sequence as a single long sentence written in trade-offs: every gain on land was a loss somewhere else, paid for by a new structure. None of it started over. The moss did not become a fern; an ancestor it shared with the fern added pipes its own line never got. The fern did not become a pine; a line beside it sealed the embryo in a coat. Each step kept everything the last step built and edited only what it had to. The land was taken not by conquest but by accumulation — a film of green that learned, slowly, and never once from scratch, how to live where there was no water to hold it.
