# Chapter 1 — What Diversity Is

The man is forty feet up, or near enough, on a steel tower lashed into the canopy of a Panamanian forest, and he is about to poison a tree. Below him, on the forest floor, his team has spread sheets of fabric like funeral shrouds, white in the green gloom. It is 1982. The fogging machine coughs and catches and begins to push a fine mist of insecticide up into the crown of the tree above him — *Luehea seemannii*, a single species of tropical hardwood, one tree among the millions in this forest.

The mist drifts up through the leaves. And then it begins to rain insects.

They fall for hours. Onto the sheets, into the trays, a slow dense precipitation of bodies — beetles mostly, but also ants, flies, bugs, spiders, things with no common name at all. Many of them are tiny, a millimeter or two, the sort of creature you would never see unless it landed on your arm and you happened to be looking. They are the residents of the canopy, the layer of the forest forty and sixty feet up where most of the action of a rainforest actually happens — the flowering, the fruiting, the eating and being eaten — and which, until people learned to fog it and catch what fell, was as unexplored as the deep sea. The man's name is Terry Erwin, an entomologist at the Smithsonian, and when he and his colleagues sort what fell from that one kind of tree, across several individuals and seasons, they will count, among the beetles alone, **988 distinct species.**

Nine hundred and eighty-eight kinds of beetle, from one species of tree, in one forest, in one country. Hold that number. Then sit with the question Erwin scrawled, almost as an afterthought, in the last paragraph of the paper he published from this work. If a single tropical tree species hosts that many beetle species, and many of them live on that tree and no other, and there are tens of thousands of tropical tree species — how many kinds of insect are there in all the world's tropical forests?

He ran the arithmetic forward, layer by layer — beetles per tree, beetles as a fraction of all arthropods, that tree as one of many — and arrived at a number that detonated quietly in the field and has not finished going off since. **Thirty million.** Thirty million species of arthropod — the joint-legged animals, the insects and spiders and their kin — possibly sharing the planet with us. At the time, the standard estimate for the total number of *all* species on Earth, every plant and animal and fungus together, was around one and a half million. Erwin, from his tower and his sheets, had just suggested we might be off by a factor of twenty.

That is the first thing to understand about the diversity of life. We do not know how much of it there is. Not approximately. Not within a factor of two. The honest answer to "how many kinds of living thing are there?" is that nobody knows, and the disagreement is not at the edges — it is at the center.

How can that be? We have been at this for a long time. People have been naming creatures systematically since the 1700s. Surely, after three centuries of collecting and pinning and pressing and describing, we have at least *counted* what we have found?

We have. That part we know. The number of species formally described and named — given a Latin name, written up, lodged in the record — is roughly 1.2 million. Three centuries of work, museums full of drawers, expeditions to every continent, and the running total is about 1.2 million distinct kinds of living thing. That is the catalogue.

The problem is that the catalogue is not the inventory. The catalogue is what we have *found.* The inventory is what is *there.* And the gap between them is the entire question.

Here is the thing you are actually looking at when you ask "how many species are there?" You are not really asking a question about nature. You are asking a question about *sampling* — about how thoroughly we have searched a planet too large to search completely. And the way you answer a sampling question is not by counting harder. It is by figuring out how much you are likely to have missed.

Erwin's method was one way to do it. Take something you *can* measure exhaustively — the beetles on one tree species — and find a ratio that lets you scale up. Walk the chain he actually built, because seeing it is the only way to understand both why the number was so large and why it was so shaky. He had 988 beetle species off *Luehea seemannii.* He reasoned that roughly a fifth of them — call it 160 — were specialists, the kind of beetle that lives on this tree and no other. Then he estimated that beetles make up about 40 percent of the canopy arthropods, so the specialist arthropod count per tree might run past 400. He doubled it to account for the insects living on the trunk and the ground beneath as well as the canopy. And then came the multiplier that did the real work: there are something like 50,000 species of tropical tree. Multiply the specialists-per-tree by the number of trees, and the arithmetic detonates. Out the far end came thirty million.

Look at that chain again and you can feel where it bends. *A fifth are specialists* — is that right? Nobody had measured it; it was an educated guess. *Beetles are 40 percent of canopy arthropods* — in his samples, yes, but his samples were a few trees in Panama. *Fifty thousand tree species, each with its own dedicated fauna* — but how dedicated, really? Do tropical beetles each cleave to one tree, or do most of them wander among many, in which case you are counting the same species over and over? Every link in the chain is a guess with a number attached, and the guesses *multiply,* which means the errors multiply too. Shift the host-specificity assumption even modestly — say most beetles are generalists rather than specialists — and thirty million collapses toward seven million, or balloons past fifty. The estimate is only as solid as its shakiest ratio, and Erwin's chain had several shaky ones. He knew it. The number was a provocation, not a measurement, and he meant it as one. What he had really proved was not that there are thirty million arthropods. It was that the canopy was full of life nobody had counted, and that our confident total of a million and a half was a figure built on having looked only where it was easy to look — at eye level, on the ground, in the temperate North where most naturalists happened to live.

And before we go further, a question hides inside the counting that we have been gliding past: what *is* a species, that we should count them at all? It is worth being honest that the unit of the count is itself slippery. The working definition most people carry — a species is a group whose members breed with each other and not with outsiders — is a fine rule of thumb for birds and mammals, the creatures we know best. But it fails the moment you push on it. It says nothing about the bacteria, which do not breed in pairs at all but swap genes sideways with whatever is nearby. It says nothing about a fossil, or about the many plants and animals that clone themselves without sex. And even among the breeders, the boundary smears: closely related species often produce hybrids, and populations caught in the slow act of splitting are half one species and half two. We will spend the whole next chapter on this problem, because it turns out to be deep rather than a technicality. For now just hold the discomfort: when someone reports that there are 8.7 million species, they are counting a unit whose edges are genuinely fuzzy, and the fuzziness gets worse the smaller and simpler the organism. The grandest creatures are the easiest to count and the rarest. The most numerous creatures are the hardest to count and the most important. The count is biased, all the way down, toward the large and the visible — toward us.

There is a cleaner way to estimate the total, and it is worth seeing because it answers the question without leaving the museum. In 2011 a team led by Camilo Mora tried something that sounds almost like a trick. Instead of counting species, they counted *the categories above species* — the way biology files life into a nested hierarchy. Species group into genera, genera into families, families into orders, orders into classes, classes into phyla. The big boxes fill up first: we found nearly all the major groups — the phyla, the classes — long ago, because they are large and obvious and there are few of them. The small boxes, the species, are still filling. So the *rate* at which we keep discovering new families and orders, plotted against time, traces out a curve that is flattening — we are running out of big new groups to find. And the shape of that flattening curve, extrapolated down to the species level, predicts where the species count *should* settle once the catalogue is complete.

To make that concrete: when Linnaeus and his successors were filling in the tree, they found the big branches first. The major animal phyla — the great body-plan divisions, the things as different from each other as a starfish is from a snail — were essentially all known by the late 1800s. The classes filled in close behind. The orders came next, then the families, each rank discovered later and slower than the one above it, because each rank is finer and there are more of them to find. Plot the running count of known *families* against the year and the line climbs steeply and then bends, leveling off as we run out of new families to discover. That bend is information. A curve that is flattening tells you it is approaching a ceiling, and the height of the ceiling can be estimated from the shape of the approach — the way you can guess where a thrown ball will peak from watching the first half of its arc. Mora's team did this for each rank, then used the well-mapped upper ranks to predict the still-unfinished species count below. It is statistics standing in for centuries of fieldwork nobody will ever finish.

Their answer, for the eukaryotes — the organisms with complex cells, which is to say plants, animals, fungi, and their single-celled relatives — was about **8.7 million species,** give or take 1.3 million. Of those, roughly 2.2 million live in the ocean. Break it down by kingdom and the shape of life on Earth comes into focus: about 7.77 million animals, only 298,000 plants, 611,000 fungi, and a few tens of thousands each of the single-celled groups the old textbooks lumped as "protozoa" and "chromists." Animals dominate the count — and within the animals, as we will see, the insects dominate, and within the insects the beetles. Life, by species count, is mostly small things with six legs.

And here is the part that should stop you: if 8.7 million is right and we have described 1.2 million, then after three hundred years of taxonomy we have named perhaps one species in seven. About 86 percent of land species and 91 percent of ocean species have never been described. They are out there now, unnamed, most of them small, many of them already sliding toward extinction we will never record because we never knew they existed. Think about what that means for a moment. The great age of exploration is not behind us. It is mostly still ahead of us, and it is happening in soil samples and DNA sequencers rather than on sailing ships, and we are losing the unexplored continent faster than we are mapping it.

Now — a confession the number requires. That 8.7 million is itself an estimate with wide error bars, and it leaves something enormous out. Mora's method works by extrapolating the well-mapped hierarchy of complex life. It does not work for bacteria and archaea, the single-celled organisms without nuclei, because their hierarchy is not mapped the same way and, as we will see, they trade genes sideways so freely that the very idea of a clean species count starts to dissolve. So the 8.7 million is a count of the visible kingdom — and it deliberately excludes the kingdom that actually runs the planet.

And here is the trade-off that sits under this whole chapter, the cost of measuring diversity the way we instinctively do. **When you count diversity by counting species, you measure the wrong thing.** Species-counting is built for organisms that come in discrete, sexually-reproducing, countable kinds — beetles, birds, orchids. It works beautifully for them and it captures almost nothing of where life's real diversity lives.

Because the real diversity is not in form. It is in chemistry, and it is microbial. The number of bacterial and archaeal cells on Earth is estimated at four to six times ten to the thirtieth — a four with thirty zeros after it, a number so large that writing it out is pointless. Most of them live not on the surface but deep underground, in rock and sediment, in a buried biosphere we have barely sampled — by the estimate that produced that figure, more than nine cells in ten live in the dark, in soil and ocean sediment and the water trapped in rock kilometers down, in a hidden majority that never sees the sun.

These cells are nearly invisible and nearly uncountable as "species," but think about what the count is missing by leaving them out, because it is not just headcount — it is *invention.* A beetle and an oak and a jellyfish, for all their visible difference, make their living in nearly the same way: they take in carbon-rich food or sunlight, burn it with oxygen, and breathe out carbon dioxide. Three wildly different shapes, one underlying chemistry. The microbes are the opposite — modest in shape, extravagant in chemistry. Between them they perform almost every way of making a living that exists. Some pull nitrogen straight out of the air and fix it into a form living things can use, a reaction so demanding that human industry only learned to copy it in the twentieth century, at high heat and pressure, and it still consumes a couple of percent of the world's energy. Some eat rock. Some breathe metals instead of oxygen, exhaling rust. Some harvest light with pigments no plant ever evolved. The point is not that there are many kinds of microbe. The point is that the microbes hold the planet's whole chemical toolkit, and the animals and plants we count so carefully are all running on a tiny corner of it. A spoonful of garden soil holds more genuinely different *ways of being alive* — more distinct metabolisms — than a rainforest holds distinct body shapes. The beetles are the spectacle. The bacteria are the show.

Bring the lens all the way in, then, from the planet to your own body. You are, by cell count, roughly half not-you. A 2016 reckoning by Ron Milo's group put it at about 38 trillion bacterial cells riding in and on a 70-kilogram human body, against about 30 trillion human cells of your own — and even that human figure is mostly red blood cells, which carry no DNA. (You will sometimes hear that microbes outnumber your own cells ten to one; that was an old back-of-envelope guess that got repeated for decades until someone actually did the careful count, and it came down to roughly one to one. The truth is humbler and stranger than the myth: it is nearly a tie.) Most of those bacteria live in your gut, a dense and ancient ecosystem digesting the food you cannot break down on your own, manufacturing vitamins you need but cannot make, training your immune system from infancy, and holding off invading microbes by sheer crowding — leaving them no room and no food. Every one of them is a cousin of yours, a leaf on the same tree, separated from you by perhaps two billion years of independent editing — and there are roughly as many of them as there are of you. You are not an individual. You are a procession.

And then pull the lens back out, because diversity plays a final trick that undoes the very thing we have been counting. We have been counting *kinds* — distinct species, distinct cells. But it is not always clear what counts as one organism at all. In Utah, in the Fishlake National Forest, there is a stand of quaking aspen that looks like an ordinary forest: forty-seven thousand separate trunks spread across 106 acres, trembling in the wind. It is not forty-seven thousand trees. It is one tree. Every trunk is genetically identical, all of them connected underground by a single shared root system, all of them sprouted from one organism that has been alive — by some definitions — for thousands of years. It is called Pando, from the Latin for *I spread.* It weighs an estimated thirteen million pounds, which makes it, by mass, one of the largest single living things on Earth. And right now it is dying — its young shoots eaten by deer and elk faster than it can replace them, the great clone breaking apart in slow motion while we argue over whether it is one thing or a forest.

![A cross-section illustration of the Pando aspen clone: many genetically identical trunks above ground connected to one continuous root mass below ground, with a scale bar showing 106 acres.](images/01-what-diversity-is-fig-02.png)

*Figure 1.2 — Pando: 47,000 stems, one root system, one organism — diversity's count of "kinds" runs aground on the question of what one organism even is.*

So the count fails twice over. It undercounts the microbial majority, which is most of life's real variety. And it cannot even cleanly say what one organism is when the answer runs underground. The neat number — 8.7 million, or 30 million, or 1.2 million catalogued — is a useful fiction, a flashlight beam in a dark warehouse. It shows you something real. It does not show you the room.

J.B.S. Haldane, the British biologist, was supposedly once asked by a theologian what his study of creation had taught him about the mind of the Creator. He is said to have replied that the Creator must have had "an inordinate fondness for beetles." There is no good record that he ever actually said it, and biologists have repeated it for decades anyway, because whether or not he said it, it is true to the data. Beetles — the order Coleoptera — number something like 400,000 described species, which is to say roughly a quarter of every animal species we have ever named. One in four named animals is a beetle. When Erwin stood on his tower and fogged that tree and watched the beetles fall for hours, he was not seeing an anomaly. He was seeing the center of gravity of animal life, raining down on white sheets in the green dark.

He died in 2020. The thirty-million figure that made his name has since been revised downward — current estimates put arthropod species closer to seven million, still many times what we have named. He would not have minded the revision. The number was never the point. The point was the gesture: a man standing in a forest, looking up at one tree, and realizing that nobody — in three hundred years of science, on a planet we thought we had mapped — had any real idea how much life was up there.

And that is the quiet weight this chapter has been carrying toward you. We tend to imagine that the cataloguing of life is a finished thing, a closed book in a museum, the great work of the Victorian naturalists wrapped up and shelved. It is not. We are living inside the early chapters of that work, not the last ones. The honest summary of where we stand is this: we have named on the order of a million and a half kinds of life; the careful estimate says there are at least eight or nine million complex ones we could name if we looked; and that estimate does not even count the bacteria and archaea, which are most of the cells, most of the chemistry, and effectively uncountable by the methods we have. The map of life is mostly blank. Not blank at the edges — blank in the middle. And the territory it describes is shrinking while we draw it, lineages going extinct in soils and reefs and canopies before anyone gives them a name, edits to a four-billion-year-old manuscript being deleted before we have read them.

That is what diversity is. It is not the number on the page. It is the size of the part that never made it onto the page at all — the rain of unnamed beetles, the buried trillions of cells, the one tree pretending to be a forest. It is mostly the part we haven't met. And the only honest posture toward it is the one Erwin had on his tower: not the confidence of someone who has counted, but the vertigo of someone who has just understood how much there is left to count.
