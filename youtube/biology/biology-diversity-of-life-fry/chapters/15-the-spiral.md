# Chapter 15 — The Spiral

On the bench in front of you is a small flat worm, no longer than your fingernail, gliding across the glass of a dish in a film of pond water. It has two dark eyespots near the front that give it the look of a cross-eyed arrowhead, and it moves with a smooth, ciliated drift, no jerk to it, as though it were floating just above the surface it travels on. It is a planarian. Now you take a razor and you cut it. Not in half — into pieces. You divide the little worm into fragments, and one of the fragments you keep is tiny, a sliver, a fraction of the whole animal, perhaps a two-hundred-and-seventy-ninth of it, a piece holding maybe ten thousand cells. You put the sliver in its own dish of water and you wait.

In one to two weeks, the sliver has become a worm. A whole worm — with a head, with two eyespots, with a brain, with a gut, with a tail at the other end. Every fragment you cut does this. A middle slice, a piece with no original head and no original tail, grows a head at the end that used to face forward and a tail at the end that used to face back, sorting out which way is which and rebuilding the missing parts in their proper places. You did not give it instructions. You gave it a razor and a dish of water, and it rebuilt itself from a scrap.

This is the experiment Thomas Hunt Morgan ran at the turn of the twentieth century, before he turned to fruit flies and went on to found modern genetics and win a Nobel Prize for it. The planarian regeneration was early work, and it posed a question Morgan could not answer with the tools of his time: how does a sliver of an animal know what is missing, where its missing head should go, and how to build it? We can answer it better now, and the answer is a reserve of cells called **neoblasts** — pluripotent adult stem cells, *pluri* for many, *potent* for capable, cells that have not yet committed to being any particular kind of cell and so can become any of them. A planarian keeps a standing population of these uncommitted cells throughout its body, and when you cut it, the neoblasts near the wound divide and rebuild whatever the fragment is missing, including a new brain. They are the planarian's blank pages, and the worm carries a supply of blank pages everywhere, all its life.

But here is the thing that should stop you, and it is the puzzle this whole chapter unfolds from. The *Hydra* in the last chapter could be cut apart and regrow, and that was no great surprise once you saw how simple it was — two cell-layers, a nerve net with no irreplaceable center, no real head. The planarian has a head. It has a brain. It has a front and a back, a left and a right, eyes pointed in the direction it travels. It is, in other words, an animal that has *committed to a direction* — and by every expectation we built up in the last chapter, an animal that commits to a direction should be exactly the kind of animal that cannot regrow, because so much now depends on the careful front-to-back arrangement. And yet the planarian regrows from a sliver, head and all. To understand why — and to understand what this little worm tells us about your own body — we have to understand what it means to have a front end at all.

## The decision to have a front

Go back to the radial animals of the last chapter — the anemone, the jellyfish, the coral. Their bodies are arranged like wheels, the same in every direction around a central axis, and the price of that arrangement, you'll remember, was that they could catch food from anywhere but could not go *toward* anything with intent. A wheel has no leading edge. To navigate, you need a front.

The planarian has a front, and that single fact reorganizes the entire animal. Its body has **bilateral symmetry** — from the Latin *bi*, two, and *latus*, side: two matching sides, a left that mirrors a right, which immediately implies the other two distinctions that come with it. If there is a left and a right, there is a front and a back (the direction the two sides are heading), and there is a top and a bottom. The body now has axes. It has a definite orientation in the world. And here is the deep consequence, the mechanism I want to build carefully, because it explains where brains come from.

Think about what it means to move consistently in one direction. The front end of the animal is the part that meets the world first. It is the first to encounter food, the first to encounter danger, the first to arrive at anything new. Now imagine selection acting on this animal over many generations. A worm whose sense organs are spread evenly over its whole body — like the radial animal's — wastes most of them, because the back end only ever experiences places the front end has already passed through. But a worm that concentrates its senses at the *front*, where the news arrives, gets that news first and can respond before the rest of the body has even reached the spot. An eyespot at the front sees the shadow before the body is under it. A chemical sensor at the front tastes the food before the mouth arrives. So selection piles sensory equipment onto the leading end — eyes, chemical detectors, touch receptors — concentrating the animal's window on the world at the point where it meets the world.

And once the senses cluster at the front, the nerves that process them must cluster there too, because the processing has to happen close to the sensors to be fast. The diffuse nerve net of the cnidarian — no center, signals rippling out in every direction — is wrong for a directed animal. A directed animal needs the signals from its front-mounted senses gathered, compared, and turned into a decision, right there at the front, before it acts. So a swelling of nervous tissue forms at the leading end: a concentration of neurons, the first thing in evolution you could fairly call a **brain**. The process has a name — **cephalization**, from the Greek *kephalē*, the head — the formation of a head, the migration of senses and nerves to the front of a directed body.

Here is the sentence to carry out of this chapter: **the brain is what happens when you commit to a direction.** It is not an addition the animal chose to make. It is the inevitable consequence of moving head-first through the world. Once you have a front that always meets the future first, selection cannot help but build a control center there. The planarian's brain — two clusters of neurons behind its eyespots — is the first draft of every brain that followed, including the one you are reading this with, and it exists for one reason: the planarian goes somewhere, and something has to be in charge at the front of the line.

## Why a flat worm can stay flat

Look again at the planarian and notice what it does *not* have. It has no heart. It has no blood vessels. It has no lungs, no gills, nothing dedicated to moving oxygen around inside it. For an animal with a brain and a directed body, this is conspicuous. How does it supply oxygen and nutrients to its cells without a circulatory system?

The answer is in its shape, and it is the reason the planarian is flat. Oxygen and food molecules move into and through tissue by **diffusion** — the slow drift of molecules from where they are crowded to where they are sparse, no pump required, just the random jostling of molecules spreading themselves out. Diffusion is reliable but it is slow, and crucially it only works over short distances. Over a fraction of a millimeter, diffusion can supply a cell with all the oxygen it needs. Over a centimeter, it cannot — the molecules would take far too long to travel that far, and the cells in the middle would suffocate before the oxygen arrived.

So an animal that wants to live on diffusion alone, with no circulatory system to pump supplies to its interior, has exactly one option: it must make sure no cell is ever far from the surface. And the way to guarantee that is to be flat. A flat body — a sheet of an animal — has no deep interior. Every cell, top layer or bottom layer, sits close enough to the outside that oxygen can diffuse in and waste can diffuse out, directly, no plumbing needed. The flatworm's flatness is not an accident of style; it is the geometric solution to living without a circulatory system. The body is spread thin precisely so that diffusion can reach all of it. Even the gut is built to the same logic — a branching gut with a single opening, the mouth also serving as the exit, the branches reaching out toward all parts of the body so that food is distributed by the gut's own architecture rather than by any blood.

Now we have the trade-off of the whole chapter, and it is severe. Living on diffusion alone, by staying flat, frees the planarian from ever needing to build and run a heart, a vascular system, a respiratory system — a genuine saving, a simpler animal, fewer organs to make and maintain and rebuild. **This works because** every cell is near the surface and diffusion does the delivery for free. **It fails when** the animal tries to get big. The moment a flatworm thickens — the moment any part of it grows more than a fraction of a millimeter from the surface — the cells in the new interior begin to starve and suffocate, because diffusion cannot reach them. The flat body plan caps the animal's size forever. There is no large flatworm, and there cannot be, not without inventing a circulatory system, which is precisely the thing the flat body was designed to avoid. A planarian can be long; it can branch; it cannot be thick. It bought simplicity and paid with a permanent ceiling on size.

And now, finally, the puzzle from the opening resolves. Why can the planarian regrow from a sliver when it has a brain and a committed front-to-back body? *Because it stayed simple.* It has a head, but it has no irreplaceable complex organs — no heart that must be rebuilt with exact valves and chambers, no lungs, no kidneys with intricate plumbing. The flat, diffusion-supplied body that capped its size is the same flat, simple body that lets a fragment rebuild the whole. The neoblasts have an easier job than they would in a vertebrate, because there is less intricate machinery to reconstruct. The very trade-off that limits the planarian — simplicity bought at the cost of size — is what grants it the regeneration that astonished Morgan. The limit and the gift are the same body plan, seen from two sides.

## The fork in the road

We need to step back now and place the planarian in the larger structure, because it sits on one side of the most important split in the animal kingdom — a fork that, on one path, leads to it and the snails and the earthworms, and on the other path leads to the starfish and to you.

Every bilaterally symmetric animal — every animal with a front and a back, which is to say nearly every animal you can name — belongs to a vast group called the **Bilateria**. And early in the development of a bilaterian embryo, in those first hours when the ball of cells folds inward to make a gut, there is a fork. Recall from Chapter 13 that gastrulation begins when one side of the hollow ball pushes inward, making a cup with a hole leading in. That first hole, the original opening of the embryonic gut, is called the **blastopore** — *blasto*, the embryo, *pore*, the opening: the embryo's first mouth-or-not. And the fork is this: in some animals, the blastopore becomes the **mouth** of the adult, with the anus forming later as a second opening. In other animals, the blastopore becomes the **anus**, and the mouth forms second.

That sounds like a trivial detail of plumbing, a quirk of which end forms first. It is not trivial. It marks the deepest division in the bilaterian tree, a split that happened well over half a billion years ago and that sorts the animal kingdom into two great lineages that have been evolving separately ever since. The animals whose blastopore becomes the mouth are the **Protostomia** — *proto*, first, *stoma*, mouth: "mouth first." The animals whose blastopore becomes the anus are the **Deuterostomia** — *deutero*, second: "mouth second." You are a deuterostome. Your mouth formed second; the first opening in your embryonic gut became, of all things, your anus. The planarian is a protostome, and so are the snails, the clams, the earthworms, the insects, the spiders — the overwhelming majority of animal species on Earth.

It is worth pausing on how strange that is, because it is easy to read past. The first hole an animal punches in itself, during gastrulation, has to become something. In your lineage it became the back door. In the planarian's it became the mouth. That single difference — which end of the embryo's first tube becomes the eating end — is not a matter of taste; it is a fork that two enormous groups of animals took in opposite directions more than half a billion years ago, and they have never crossed back. There is no animal that is half protostome and half deuterostome, no intermediate, no blending. You inherited your developmental direction from a deuterostome ancestor; the planarian inherited the other from a protostome one; and the fork between them is one of the oldest legible decisions in the animal kingdom, written into the very first hours of how each of us assembles a gut.

The protostomes themselves divide into two further great branches, and this is the map for the next several chapters of the book. One branch is the **Ecdysozoa** — the molting animals, the ones that grow by shedding a rigid outer cuticle, which includes the arthropods and the nematode worms; we will spend a whole chapter on them, because they are the most numerous animals that have ever lived. The other branch — the one this chapter and the next belong to — is the lineage of the planarian, the snail, and the earthworm, and it is named for a signature written not into the adult but into the egg.

## The signature in the egg

Watch a planarian's egg divide, or a snail's, or an earthworm's. The fertilized cell splits into two, the two into four — and then, at the next division, something specific happens. The four cells do not divide straight up and down, stacking neatly. They divide at a tilt, so that the new upper cells come to rest not directly on top of the lower ones but nestled into the grooves between them, offset, rotated. And the rotation has a handedness — the upper tier is twisted clockwise or counterclockwise relative to the lower. The cells spiral.

This is **spiral cleavage**, and it is the defining inheritance of the clade we are now entering. *Cleavage* is just the word for these early cell divisions; *spiral* describes the tilted, rotated, offset way the cells stack. It is a precise and ancient choreography, and it has a second feature that matters even more than its geometry: in animals with spiral cleavage, the fate of each early cell is fixed very early. This is called **determinate** development — *determined*, settled, decided — meaning that if you were to remove one of those four spiraling cells, the embryo could not replace what it would have made; the missing cell's contribution is simply lost, because each cell's job was assigned almost from the start. This is the opposite of what happens in your own development, which is **regulative** — the early cells are interchangeable, and if you remove one, the others compensate and a whole embryo still forms. (This is why a human embryo can split in two and make identical twins, and a snail embryo cannot.)

The clade of animals defined by this spiraling, determinate egg is called the **Spiralia** — named directly for the spiral cleavage that is taken to be its ancestral signature. You will also hear it called the **Lophotrochozoa**, and that name is built from the two kinds of larvae its members make, so it is worth unpacking. *Lopho-* comes from the **lophophore** — from the Greek *lophos*, a crest or tuft, and *phorein*, to carry: a crest-bearer, a horseshoe-shaped ring of ciliated tentacles surrounding the mouth that some of these animals (the brachiopods, the bryozoans) use to filter food from the water. *Trocho-* comes from the **trochophore** — from *trochos*, a wheel: a top-shaped, free-swimming larva ringed with a band of beating cilia, like a tiny spinning toy, the larval form of the molluscs and the annelid worms we meet next chapter. So Lophotrochozoa means, roughly, "the animals that make lophophores or trochophores" — crest-larvae and wheel-larvae — united into one clade.

## How we learned the worm's true family

For most of the history of biology, the lophophore-bearers — the brachiopods with their hinged shells, the bryozoans encrusting rocks in mossy mats — were filed with the deuterostomes, the line that includes the starfish and the vertebrates. Their development had features that looked deuterostome-like, and anatomy was the only evidence available, and anatomy pointed that way. The flatworms, the molluscs, the annelid worms were sorted elsewhere, by their own anatomical features. The tree was drawn from how the bodies looked.

Then, in 1995, came one of the quiet redrawings of the animal tree, and it came not from anatomy but from a molecule. A team led by Kenneth Halanych, working with Texas and other collaborators, sequenced a gene that every one of these animals carries — the gene for **18S ribosomal RNA**, a piece of the ribosome, the cell's protein-building machine, which is ancient and present in all life and accumulates mutations slowly enough to record deep ancestry. They read this gene across the lophophore-bearers and the molluscs and the annelids and the flatworms, and let the shared mutations vote on who was related to whom. The vote came back clear and surprising: the lophophorates were not deuterostomes at all. They belonged with the molluscs and annelids, among the protostomes. The molecule had overruled the anatomy. Halanych and his colleagues gave the new clade its name — **Lophotrochozoa** — and the brachiopods and bryozoans were moved across the tree, out of the company of starfish and into the company of snails and worms.

This is the pattern the whole book keeps returning to: anatomy gives you a first draft of kinship, and the molecules give you the correction, because the molecules carry the record of descent more faithfully than the shape of a body, which selection can mold into convergent likenesses. A lophophore looked like a deuterostome feeding structure; the genes said the resemblance was a coincidence of function, not a sign of shared ancestry. And I should be honest, as this book always tries to be, that the work is not finished. The Spiralia is a well-supported clade — that much is solid. But its internal branches, the exact placement of the flatworms and the lophophorates within it, are still being revised as more genomes are read. The clade is real; its inner map is a draft.

## What the little worm holds

Step back to the scale of the whole kingdom for a moment, because it is easy to lose sight of how much this branch carries. The Spiralia — the lineage that the planarian on your bench belongs to — accounts for something like a quarter of all the major animal body plans, the phyla, that exist. From this one branch come the molluscs (the snails, the clams, the octopuses — the whole of the next chapter), the annelids (the earthworms, the leeches, the bristleworms), the flatworms, the brachiopods, the bryozoans, and more. A quarter of the fundamental ways of being an animal trace back to the spiraling egg and the determinate cell. The little flat worm gliding across your dish is not a curiosity at the edge of the kingdom. It is sitting near the base of one of its largest limbs.

And it holds, in its small flat body, the answer to where you came from in a deeper sense than its family tree. The planarian has a front. It committed to a direction, and committing to a direction built it a brain — the first draft of the move that, repeated and elaborated across half a billion years down a different branch of the same fork, built yours. You and the planarian took different paths at the blastopore — its first opening became a mouth, yours became something less dignified — but you share the deeper inheritance: a body with a leading edge, senses gathered at the front, a control center built where the world arrives first. The brain is what happens when you commit to a direction. The planarian committed early, in a flat body it could rebuild from a sliver. You committed in a thick body you cannot. The difference is the size you were willing to pay for, and the cost of size, as the next chapters will show, is that you can never grow your head back.

Watch the worm finish reassembling itself in its dish, head where a head should be, eyespots pointed forward, ready to go somewhere. It is doing something your body forgot how to do, in a body simpler than yours, on a branch of the tree a quarter of the kingdom hangs from. That is what the spiral holds.
