# The Notochord

The summer field season on Ellesmere Island is short and unforgiving. This is the far north of the Canadian Arctic, a few degrees of latitude from the pole, a place of frost-shattered rock and twenty-four-hour daylight where the only sound is wind and the crunch of boots on scree. It is 2004. A team of paleontologists has come back for the fourth summer, working a slope of reddish 375-million-year-old rock that was once the bed of a shallow, meandering, subtropical stream — Ellesmere sat near the equator when these sediments were laid down. They have spent three previous summers finding almost nothing. They are nearly out of seasons.

A man named Steve chips into the rock face and exposes a snout. Not a fish snout, exactly. Flat. Broad. The eyes, when more rock comes away, sit on top of the skull rather than on the sides — the arrangement of an animal that lies in shallow water and looks up. There is something like a neck, a thing no fish has, because a fish's head is fused to its shoulders so that turning the body turns the head. This animal could turn its head while holding its body still. And when they finally free the front fins from the stone and look inside them, the bones are wrong for a fish and right for something else. There is a single bone where the upper arm would be. Beyond it, two bones, side by side, where the forearm would be. Beyond those, a cluster of small bones arranged like the beginnings of a wrist.

They name it *Tiktaalik roseae*, taking the first word from Inuktitut, the language of the people whose land this is — *tiktaalik*, a large freshwater fish that lives in the shallows. The description appears in *Nature* on the sixth of April, 2006, under the names of Edward Daeschler, Neil Shubin, and Farish Jenkins. What they have found is a creature that is, depending on how you look at it, the last of the fish or the first of the things that would crawl. It has gills and lungs both. It has scales and fins, and inside one of those fins is the architecture of your arm. To understand why a flat-headed fish in Arctic rock is one of the most important animals ever dug up, you have to go back to a stiff little rod, and to the four-hundred-million-year project of editing a swimming worm into a creature that could stand up and look around.

## Four features, one of them you still have

Bury yourself in coarse sand on a warm seafloor and filter-feed, a translucent sliver of an animal the length of a finger, no jaw, no real head, no brain to speak of — just a body, a notch of a mouth, and a stiff rod running down your back. This is the lancelet, amphioxus, and it is about as simple as a member of our own great group gets. Watching it, you can see, stripped to its essentials, the four features that define a chordate — the four edits that everything from a hagfish to a hummingbird to you has inherited and modified.

The first is the one the group is named for: the notochord. The Greek is *notos*, the back, and *chorde*, a cord or string — the back-string. It is a flexible rod of turgid, fluid-filled cells running the length of the body between the gut below and the nerve cord above. Ask what it does, not what it is called. A worm with no internal stiffening, when its muscles contract on one side, simply shortens on that side and bunches up. But a worm with a stiff incompressible rod down its middle cannot shorten — so when the muscles on one side contract, the body bends instead, and when the muscles alternate side to side, the body flexes into the S-curves of a swimming fish. The notochord is what turns muscle into propulsion. It is the first thing that made a chordate swim like a chordate.

The second feature is the dorsal hollow nerve cord. Most animals with a centralized nerve trunk run it along their belly, and it is solid. Chordates run theirs along the back, and it is a hollow tube — built, in the embryo, by a sheet of cells that rolls up into a cylinder. That tube is the future brain and spinal cord. Yours formed exactly this way, a flat plate of cells curling into a tube in the third week after you were conceived; when the tube fails to close, the result is the birth defect called spina bifida. The hollow dorsal cord is not a metaphor for your nervous system. It is, literally and developmentally, your nervous system, in its first form.

The third feature is a set of pharyngeal slits — openings in the wall of the throat, behind the mouth. In the lancelet and in fish, water comes in the mouth and passes out through these slits, and the animal strains food, or later breathes, across them. The slits are ancient and they are persistent, and we will come back to what became of them, because the story of the chordate body is largely the story of the throat being repurposed.

The fourth feature is a tail that extends past the anus — a post-anal tail, muscle running beyond the end of the gut, there for one reason: propulsion. You had one. In the womb, around the fifth week, the human embryo has a tail with more than thirty vertebrae in it; it is reabsorbed before birth, leaving the coccyx, the few fused bones at the base of your spine that are the stump of a tail your ancestors swam with.

And the notochord, the first feature, has a sequel in your own back worth telling, because it shows how an edit leaves a trace it never quite erases. In a fish, the notochord persists for life as the stiffening rod. In you, it mostly does not — as the embryo develops, a chain of bony vertebrae forms around and largely replaces it, the backbone taking over the job of stiffening while allowing far more control of bending. But the notochord does not vanish without leaving something behind. Between each pair of your vertebrae sits an intervertebral disc, and at the center of each disc is a soft, gel-like core, the nucleus pulposus — and that gel is, in part, the remnant of your notochord, the leftover of the embryonic rod, walled into the spine as a cushion. When a disc "slips" and that gel-like core bulges out and presses on a nerve, the pain shooting down your leg is, in a real sense, your four-hundred-million-year-old notochord making itself felt. The rod that let the first chordates swim never entirely left. It was edited into a backbone, and its core was repurposed into the padding between the bones. Nothing thrown away cleanly; everything carried forward and reassigned.

A correction to an old textbook, while we are here. For a long time the lancelet was called the closest living relative of the vertebrates, the simple cousin that best showed what our own ancestor looked like. The molecules say otherwise. When the genes are read, the animals nearest to us are not the streamlined lancelets but the tunicates — the sea squirts, blobby filter-feeding sacs glued to docks and rocks, which as adults look nothing like a chordate at all. The link was first glimpsed in the 1860s by the Russian embryologist Alexander Kowalevsky, who looked at a sea squirt's *larva* — a free-swimming tadpole-shaped thing — and saw, plain as day, a notochord and a dorsal nerve cord. The sluggish adult sac had thrown its chordate body away after settling, but the larva still built it. The sea squirt is a chordate in its childhood and forgets it in adulthood, and a modern reading of the genome (Delsuc and colleagues, 2006) confirms what the larva hinted: that throwaway tadpole's lineage, not the tidy lancelet, is our nearest living kin.

## The vertebrate edits: a fourth germ layer, and a jaw made from a gill

The lancelet has the four chordate features and almost nothing else. It has no head worth the name, no brain, no jaw, no bone. The vertebrates — the animals with backbones — are chordates with a stack of further edits laid on top, and the most consequential of them is a population of cells that does not exist in the lancelet at all.

In a vertebrate embryo, where the neural tube rolls shut along the back, a band of cells peels off the top of it and migrates — streaming out through the body to destinations all over the embryo, turning into an extraordinary range of tissues once they arrive. These are the neural crest cells, and they are so versatile, so central to building a vertebrate, that biologists sometimes call them the fourth germ layer — a fourth fundamental tissue alongside the three (ectoderm, mesoderm, endoderm) that every animal embryo has. What do they build? The bones of the skull and face. The jaws. The teeth. The pigment cells of the skin. Much of the peripheral nervous system. The neural crest is, to a first approximation, what gives a vertebrate its head — and the vertebrates have sometimes been called, only half in jest, the animals that invented the head. The lancelet's lack of a real head and the vertebrate's possession of one come down, in large part, to this one migrating population of cells.

To see what the neural crest is worth, look at an animal that has it but uses it differently, and at the gap between two grades of vertebrate. A lamprey is an eel-shaped fish with no jaw — a round, sucking mouth lined with rasping horny teeth, which it fastens to the side of a living fish to feed on its blood and flesh. It has a skull, of sorts, but a soft one, and no biting apparatus. The lamprey and the hagfish are the last survivors of the first vertebrate grade, the animals that had a head and a backbone-of-sorts before anything had jaws. Set a lamprey beside a shark and you are looking across one of the largest divides in vertebrate history: the divide between the animals that could not close their mouths on prey and the animals that could. Both have neural crest; both build a head with it. But in the jawed animals the crest was put to a further use, and that further use rebuilt vertebrate life.

Now the deepest edit of all, and the one that names the leitmotif of this entire book without a sound. The first vertebrates had no jaws. The lampreys and hagfish that survive from that ancient grade still have none — round, sucking, rasping mouths, but nothing that bites. These are the agnathans, the jawless ones, from Greek *a*-, without, and *gnathos*, jaw. For the first hundred million years or so of vertebrate history, no animal could close its mouth on prey. Then, somewhere around 430 to 440 million years ago, in the Silurian, jaws appeared, and with them a new way of making a living — biting, gripping, tearing — and the jawed vertebrates, the gnathostomes, went on to become almost every backboned animal you have ever seen.

Where did the jaw come from? Not from nothing. Go back to those pharyngeal slits in the throat, the third chordate feature. In fish the gaps between the slits are supported by curved bars of cartilage or bone — the gill arches, a series of them, one behind another, holding the gill apparatus open so water can pass and oxygen can be extracted. The jaw is one of those arches. The frontmost gill arch, the one nearest the mouth, was remodeled across evolutionary time — its upper and lower bars hinged at the back and pressed into a new job. The support that had held a gill open became the structure that bit down. The mouth's new hardware was built out of the breathing apparatus's old hardware. The jaw is a modified gill arch.

Hold that sentence next to the sea squirt that forgets it is a chordate, the sea star that throws away its head, the locust shedding the armor it cannot grow inside. The same shape keeps surfacing. Nothing here was designed from a blank page. The jaw was not invented; an existing structure with one job was edited into a structure with another. Your ability to chew is a gill arch with a different assignment. Keep that in your pocket. It is going somewhere.

## The fin that was already an arm

Now back to Ellesmere Island and the flat-headed fish in the red rock, because *Tiktaalik* is the same principle written into deep time, and it answers a question that troubled biologists for a century: how does an animal built to swim ever come to walk?

The honest answer is that it does not build legs for walking. The bones were already there, doing something else. Look again at what the team found inside that front fin: one bone, then two bones, then a cluster of small ones — humerus, then radius and ulna, then the start of a wrist. That is the skeletal plan of your arm, and of the foreleg of a horse, the wing of a bat, the flipper of a whale. Every land vertebrate with limbs, the tetrapods, shares it: one bone, two bones, many bones. And here it is, around 375 million years ago, *inside a fish's fin*, in an animal that still had gills and scales and lived in water.

*Tiktaalik* did not have legs. It had fins. But inside those fins the limb skeleton already existed, robust enough that the animal could probably prop itself up on the streambed, lift its flat head out of the shallows, and push. The mobile neck — the thing no fish has — let it raise and turn its head independently, the way you would need to if you were going to spend time with your body in water and your eyes in air. The lungs were already there alongside the gills. The architecture of the land animal was assembled, piece by piece, in an animal that had not yet left the water and may never have fully left it. The fin became an arm not by inventing arm bones but because the bones that would become an arm were already in the fin, recruited from their old job into a new one.

Why did it happen at all — why would a fish that was perfectly good at being a fish start propping itself up at the water's edge? The Late Devonian streams that *Tiktaalik* lived in were shallow, warm, choked with plant debris, and very likely low in oxygen — and they were full of large predatory fish. A flat-headed animal that could lift its head into the air to breathe, push itself through inches of water too shallow for a deep-bodied predator, or haul out briefly onto a bank, had places to feed and to escape that a conventional fish did not. The limb did not evolve in order to walk on land. It evolved because, in shallow weedy water, an animal that could prop and push had an advantage over one that could only swim — and the bones that did the propping turned out, much later, to be exactly the bones a land animal would need. The land was not the goal. It was a destination the equipment reached after it had already been built for something else. This is how evolution always works: it cannot plan ahead, cannot build a leg for a future on land it has never seen. It can only edit what is in front of it for an advantage available now — and sometimes the edit, made for one reason, turns out to open a door no one was aiming for.

Neil Shubin, one of the three who described *Tiktaalik*, later wrote a book about exactly this — about how the human body is a fish body, edited. He called it *Your Inner Fish*. The title is the whole thesis in three words. Your arm is a lobed fin's interior, rearranged. Your jaw is a gill arch, hinged. Your nervous system is a hollow dorsal tube that rolled shut in your third week. There is, in the architecture of a person, almost nothing that was made new. It is fish parts, amphibian parts, reptile parts, all inherited and modified, carried forward and reassigned.

## The slow, expensive bet

There is a trade-off buried in the vertebrate body, and it runs exactly opposite to the arthropod's. The arthropod wears its skeleton outside, which means it must shed that skeleton to grow, going soft and helpless at intervals, and so it can never grow very large on land. The vertebrate buries its skeleton inside — an endoskeleton, of bone, laced with living blood vessels and rebuilt from within. And because the skeleton is living tissue, it grows with the animal. There is no molt. There is no moment of soft helplessness. There is, crucially, no hard ceiling on size. The largest animal that has ever lived — the blue whale, longer than three buses and heavier than the heaviest dinosaur — is a vertebrate, and it could only ever have been a vertebrate, because only an internal skeleton can keep growing under the body's own increasing weight.

Name the cost. *This works because* the internal skeleton grows with the animal, with no vulnerable molt and no size cap, which is why vertebrates reach sizes no insect or crab could approach. *It fails when* you weigh the development against the arthropod's: a large vertebrate takes years to grow up, years of slow, expensive, defenseless childhood before it can fend for itself. An insect can go from egg to flying adult in weeks and make thousands of offspring; a whale carries one calf for nearly a year and tends it for a year more. The vertebrate made the opposite bet from the joint-legged — grow big and slow and few, instead of small and fast and many — and the bet bought a body that can be the largest thing in the ocean, at the price of a long, fragile road to get there.

About seventy thousand species of vertebrate are alive today, the great majority of them fish, all of them running the same four chordate features modified past recognition: the stiffening rod that became a backbone, the hollow nerve tube that became a brain, the throat slits whose front arch became a jaw, the swimming tail that in you became three fused bones at the base of your spine. And every one of them traces back, through the deep red rock of Ellesmere and ten thousand seasons of rain, to a flat-headed fish that propped itself up in a subtropical stream, lifted its head, and looked at the air — using, to do it, an arm it had not yet finished inventing, because nothing in biology is ever invented from nothing, only edited from what the water had already built.
