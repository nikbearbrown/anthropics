# The Sixth

It is the fifteenth of May, 1989, in a cloud forest above Monteverde, in the mountains of Costa Rica. The air at this altitude is wet and cool and almost always full of mist; the trees drip; the ground is spongy with moss. A biologist named Marty Crump is watching a small patch of forest floor where, in a good year, the breeding pools fill with the most improbably colored animal in Central America — a toad the size of a thumb, the males a blazing, lacquered orange so vivid it looks artificial, like a thing dipped in enamel. The golden toad. For a few weeks each spring, hundreds of them gather at these pools to breed, the orange males swarming over the mottled females in a wet tangle of color.

On this day in 1989, she finds one. A single male, alone, orange against the brown leaf litter.

She never sees another. Neither does anyone else. The pools that had filled with hundreds of toads stand empty the next spring, and the spring after that, and every spring since. Surveys go out for years and come back with nothing. In 2004 the International Union for Conservation of Nature lists the golden toad as extinct. The probable causes braid together — an unusually dry season driven by a warm-water El Niño event in the Pacific, drying the breeding pools at the wrong moment, and very likely a fungal skin disease, the chytrid fungus, that has been scything through the world's amphibians. The golden toad may be the first species we can name whose disappearance was pushed, in part, by a changing climate. It existed nowhere else on Earth — its entire world was a few square kilometers of this one ridge — and now it exists nowhere at all.

The scene the chapter turns on is not the toad. It is the silence afterward. The empty pool in the spring of 1990, where there should have been hundreds of orange males and there were none, and no one understood yet that there would be none ever again. To grasp what that silence means — why it is different in kind from any other kind of loss, why it is the one thing this whole book has been walking toward — you have to understand what extinction actually is, and you have to have noticed, across twenty chapters, a pattern that has never once been named.

## A pattern you have been handed twenty times

Go back through the animals.

The jaw in your skull is a gill arch, remodeled — the front support that once held a fish's gill open, hinged and pressed into biting. The bones you hear with, the hammer and the anvil in your middle ear, were once the hinge of a reptile's jaw, shrunk and migrated up into the ear. The arm you are using to hold this book has its skeleton — one bone, two bones, many bones — inside a fish's fin, three hundred and seventy-five million years before any animal walked. The placenta that fed you before you were born is a reptile's egg, turned inside out and plugged into your mother. The bird at the window is a dinosaur that kept its feathers and lost its teeth. The flower is a modified shoot. The mitochondrion that powers every cell in your body is a bacterium that was swallowed whole, two billion years ago, and never let go. The sea star throws away, in every larva, a head its own ancestors invented. The radial urchin is a modified bilateral animal. The locust's armor is a cuticle shared, unbroken, with every molting animal back to the Cambrian.

Each of these was handed to you in its own chapter without comment, as a single strange fact. Set them in a row and the strangeness resolves into a law. It is the deepest fact in all of biology, and it is this: **every living thing is a modified version of something older. There are no truly new starts. Only edits.**

Life does not design. It does not draft on a blank page and build from nothing. It can only take what already exists and change it — stretch a fin bone, hinge a gill arch, swallow a bacterium, repurpose an egg membrane, shrink a jaw bone and move it. The reason is mechanical and unforgiving: each generation is built from the genetic instructions of the one before, copied with small errors, and selection keeps the errors that work. There is no step in that process where the page is wiped clean and the organism starts over. A four-billion-year-old chain of copies, each link a slight revision of the last, runs unbroken to every cell alive. Descent with modification, Darwin called it. Modification — editing — is the only move available. The blank page does not exist.

This is why the diversity of life is so vast and also why it is, underneath, a single thing. Every beetle and kelp and archaeon and orchid is a leaf on one tree, and the tree grew by editing — never by starting a second tree. And it is why the silence over the empty toad pool is the one loss that cannot be undone.

## What extinction is, exactly

We say a species "goes extinct" and the phrase sounds like a death — the death of the last individual, the last orange male in the leaf litter. But the species was already lost before that last toad died, and understanding why takes you down to the level of the genes.

A species is not its individuals. It is a pool of genetic variation — a great library of slightly different versions of each gene, spread across a population, the raw material that lets the lineage change as the world changes. When the climate warms or a disease arrives or a forest is cut, the species survives only if somewhere in that library are variants that happen to cope — individuals a little more heat-tolerant, a little more resistant, a little more flexible — who survive and breed and shift the whole population toward what now works. That is adaptation, and it runs on variation the way a fire runs on fuel.

Shrink a population and you burn the library. A small, isolated population loses gene variants to sheer chance — an allele present in only a few individuals simply fails to get passed on, and is gone. The survivors are forced to breed with close relatives, which exposes harmful recessive variants and weakens the young. Generation by generation the genetic library thins, and with it the lineage's ability to track a changing world. Below some threshold of variation, the population can no longer adapt fast enough to anything; it cannot respond, only decline. The golden toad was lost not on the day the last male died but in the seasons before, as its tiny ridge-top population fell below the size at which it could have adapted to a drying climate and a new fungus. The death of the last individual is the funeral. The lineage died earlier, when the library burned down.

Now put that next to the law. A lineage, once gone, does not come back, and here is the precise reason — not sentiment, mechanism. Because life only ever edits what already exists, and never starts from a blank page, a deleted lineage is a deleted edit. The information it carried — a particular four-letter sequence, refined over millions or hundreds of millions of years, encoding a particular way of being alive that worked — is not stored anywhere else. It cannot be reconstructed from first principles, because biology has no first principles to build from; it has only the editing of what is already there, and the thing to be edited is exactly what is gone. A cut branch never regrows from the cut. The tree can grow new twigs from surviving branches, given millions of years, but it can never rewrite the branch it lost, because the only copy of the instructions for that branch went into the ground with the last individual. Every extinction is the permanent deletion of information that took the tree billions of years to write and that nothing in biology can write again.

## That extinction is real at all

For most of recorded history, no one believed any of this could happen. The idea that a kind of creature could simply cease to exist seemed to insult the order of the world; a creation complete and perfect does not lose its pieces. Fossils of unfamiliar animals were explained away — they were creatures still living somewhere unexplored, or the remains of beasts drowned in the Flood.

It took a particular kind of stubborn anatomist to prove otherwise. In 1796, in Paris, Georges Cuvier read a paper to the National Institute comparing the bones of living elephants — the Indian and the African — with the great fossil bones being dug up across Europe and North America: the mammoth, and a second, distinct beast he would name the mastodon. Cuvier was the finest comparative anatomist of his age, and he showed, bone by careful bone, that these fossil animals were not elephants displaced or distorted. They were different species, built differently — and they were nowhere to be found alive, on any continent, despite the world being well explored enough by then that an animal the size of an elephant could not be hiding. The conclusion was inescapable and, for its time, disturbing: these were species that had once lived and now did not. Cuvier had proven that extinction is real — that the world has lost kinds of life, permanently, and that the fossil record is partly a record of the dead. It is the foundation everything in this chapter rests on. Before you can grasp a sixth extinction, someone has to establish that there were any at all.

And there were. Reading the rock, paleontologists eventually found five moments when the diversity of life did not merely decline but collapsed — five mass extinctions, in which something like three-quarters or more of all species vanished in a short geological span. Raup and Sepkoski formalized the "Big Five" in 1982. The worst was the end-Permian, about two hundred and fifty-two million years ago, which erased roughly ninety-five percent of marine species — life on Earth came within a hair of ending. The most famous was the end-Cretaceous, about sixty-six million years ago, when an asteroid strike and its aftermath killed about three-quarters of all species, including every dinosaur except the feathered branch that became the birds. Five times, the tree has been savagely pruned. Five times, it took ten million years or more to grow back to its former richness — and it never grew back the same, because the lost branches stayed lost, and the recovery had to edit forward from whatever survived.

## The sixth — and what that claim does and does not mean

Now the hard part, and it must be said with precision, because the precision is what makes it land.

Biologists today measure how fast species are being lost and compare it to the "background rate" — the slow, steady trickle of extinction that goes on in normal times, readable from the fossil record. The current rate is far above background. The careful estimates, led by work like that of Gerardo Ceballos, Paul Ehrlich, and colleagues in a 2017 paper in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, put it at something like one hundred to a thousand times the background rate, by some measures higher. Their follow-up work found that of around twenty-nine thousand land-vertebrate species, more than five hundred are down to fewer than a thousand individuals each — on the very brink. A 2023 study extended the accounting from species to whole genera, entire branches rather than single twigs, and found that the rate of genus-level loss since 1500 also runs far above background. The numbers on the *rate* are solid, repeatedly confirmed, and alarming.

This is the place where honesty has to do its hardest work, because the phrase that gets attached to these numbers — "the sixth mass extinction" — claims more than the numbers have yet proven. A mass extinction, in the strict sense the Big Five define it, means roughly three-quarters or more of all species lost in a short geological window. We are not there. We have not lost three-quarters of species. By total magnitude, the present event is nowhere near the end-Permian or the end-Cretaceous. What is established is the *rate* and the *trajectory* — that species are vanishing far faster than they should be, and that if the rate continues and accelerates, it points toward a mass extinction. The "sixth" is a warning grounded in how fast the tree is being pruned, not a tally already reached. Ceballos and Ehrlich's own phrase, "biological annihilation," is an argument about where the trend is heading, not a statement that the destination has arrived. To say otherwise — to claim the sixth mass extinction is an accomplished fact — would be to overstate the evidence, and overstatement is the enemy of exactly the case that needs making. The honest claim is sharper than the exaggerated one: we are pruning the tree at a rate the fossil record reserves for its catastrophes, and we are still early in it.

What we can count directly is sobering enough without exaggeration. The IUCN Red List, the running global assessment of extinction risk, had as of April 2025 evaluated about a hundred and sixty-nine thousand species and judged roughly forty-seven thousand of them — more than a quarter of those assessed — to be threatened with extinction. Recent group assessments found something like thirty-eight percent of the world's tree species threatened, and around forty-four percent of reef-building corals. These are not projections. They are the current standing of the species we have looked at closely.

Be precise about the word "biodiversity," because the word is younger than the crisis it names. It was coined in 1985 by the conservation biologist Walter Rosen, as a contraction of "biological diversity," for the title of a 1986 national forum in Washington. The biologist E. O. Wilson — who edited the 1988 volume that carried the new word out into the world, and who spent his life arguing that the small invertebrates run the planet — did not invent the term, but more than anyone he gave it weight, turning a contraction into a cause. What Wilson kept insisting, against a culture that measures the living world in charismatic large animals, was that the vast majority of life is small, obscure, undescribed, and structurally essential — and that we are pruning branches we have not even named yet. Most of the species being lost in the present event are not toads or tortoises. They are insects, fungi, microbes, and small marine invertebrates that no one ever counted, edited over hundreds of millions of years and deleted before they were ever read.

One more correction, because the word that is often draped over all of this — the Anthropocene, the "human epoch" — does not mean, officially, what people think. The idea is that human activity has so reshaped the planet that we have entered a new geological epoch named for ourselves. It is a powerful idea and a useful one. But in March 2024, the International Union of Geological Sciences, the body that governs the official Geologic Time Scale, formally rejected the Anthropocene as a unit of geological time. A specialist subcommission voted against it — twelve to four — and the executives ratified the rejection. We are still, officially, in the Holocene. The Anthropocene is not an epoch on the geological chart; it is an informal concept, a way of talking about human impact, and an honest chapter calls it that and not more.

## The trade-off, turned on us

Every chapter of this book has named a trade-off — what a body plan optimized for, and what it sacrificed. The exoskeleton bought armor and paid with the molt. The amniotic egg bought the dry land and paid in parental investment. The vertebrate endoskeleton bought unlimited size and paid in a long, vulnerable childhood. There is a trade-off in this chapter too, and for the first time the animal under examination is the one reading the page.

One species — *Homo sapiens* — optimized for something no other lineage ever managed at this scale: transforming the environment to suit itself, faster than the environment, or the other lineages in it, can adapt. We do not wait for our bodies to evolve fur for the cold; we make fire and clothing and houses in a single generation. We do not wait for digestion to evolve; we cook and farm. We rebuild the surface of the planet — its forests, its rivers, its atmosphere, its chemistry — within decades, a blink in evolutionary time. This is the trait that built everything we mean by civilization. It is also, precisely and exactly, the trait now pruning the tree. The thing we are best at — changing the world faster than evolution can keep up — is the thing doing the damage, because the other lineages, the toad on its ridge and the coral on its reef, can only adapt at evolution's pace, by editing across generations, and we are changing their world faster than they can edit. *This works because* rapid environmental transformation let one species spread across and reshape an entire planet. *It fails because* every other lineage can only keep up at the speed of descent with modification — and we have far outrun it. Our great gift and the present extinction are the same trait, seen from two sides.

## The silence over the pool

Here is where the whole book comes to rest, and it does not need raising the voice.

Stand again at the empty breeding pool above Monteverde in the spring of 1990, where there are no orange toads and there will never be again. Or stand at the Charles Darwin Research Station in the Galápagos on the twenty-fourth of June, 2012, the morning a giant tortoise named Lonesome George is found dead in his pen at over a hundred years old — the last Pinta Island tortoise, an animal whose keeper Fausto Llerena had cared for him across four decades, an "endling," the last of his entire lineage, dying without issue. When George's heart stopped, a branch of the tree that had grown for millions of years was cut at the trunk, and the only copy of the instructions for being a Pinta Island tortoise went into the ground with him.

This is the weight the book has been carrying toward, never announced, only built. Because life edits and never reboots, because there is no blank page and never has been, an extinction is not a death. A death is recoverable; the species makes more individuals. An extinction is the deletion of an edit — of information written by four billion years of copying-with-modification, refined across deep time into a working way of being alive, and held in only one place: the living lineage itself. Burn the lineage and the information is gone, not hidden, not stored, not retrievable, because biology cannot write from nothing; it can only revise what survives, and what is gone is exactly what can no longer be revised.

The golden toad was an edit four billion years in the making. So was Lonesome George. So is every one of the forty-seven thousand species the Red List now counts as threatened, and so are we — modified versions, every one, of something older, leaves on the single tree that has only ever grown by changing what was already there. We are the first lineage in that tree's history with the power to read its branches and the power to cut them, often at the same time, often without noticing which we are doing. The silence over the empty pool is the sound of an edit being deleted. It is the one sound, in all of biology, that cannot be taken back.
