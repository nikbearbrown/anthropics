# The Orphans

Under the lens, in a single drop of pond water, something is hunting.

It is shaped like the sole of a slipper, a flattened oval maybe a quarter of a millimeter long — small enough that a hundred of them would fit across your fingernail, large enough that with a cheap microscope you can watch it for an hour and never tire. Its whole surface is covered in fine hairs, thousands of them, and they are *beating* — not randomly, but in coordinated waves that sweep along the body in rows, like wind moving across a wheat field, each row a fraction of a beat behind the last. The waves drive the creature forward in a smooth corkscrewing glide. It meets an obstacle, reverses the beat, backs up, swivels, and sets off on a new heading. It finds a cloud of bacteria and slows, sweeping them into a groove in its side, funneling them toward an opening where they are swallowed whole.

It avoids. It pursues. It backs away from trouble and steers toward food. It will, in time, find another of its kind and exchange genetic material with it in a careful, ritualized act. Watch it long enough and you will swear you are watching an animal — a small, simple animal, going about the business of staying alive, with all the apparent purpose and decision that word implies.

It is not an animal.

It is not a plant either, and it is not a fungus. It is one single cell. Everything you have just watched — the hunting, the steering, the avoidance, the feeding, the mating — happens *inside one cell*, with no nervous system, no muscles, no brain, no body in the sense you mean by the word. The thousands of beating hairs are cilia, and they are driven by machinery built into the membrane of this one cell. The whole repertoire of behavior that you'd expect to need an animal is packed into a single bag of cytoplasm.

*Figure 8.1 — A whole creature's worth of behavior in one cell. The *Paramecium* swims, hunts, avoids obstacles, and mates — all with no nervous system, no muscles, and no body — using thousands of beating cilia, two kinds of nucleus, and a contractile vacuole that pumps out the fresh water constantly flooding in.*

This is a *Paramecium*. And for a century and a half it was filed, along with thousands of other single-celled hunters and drifters and swimmers, into a single great kingdom of life called the Protista — the kingdom of the simple, the primitive, the not-quite. That kingdom no longer exists. Not because the organisms vanished, but because the kingdom was a mistake — one of the largest and most instructive mistakes in the history of how we have tried to organize the living world. And the *Paramecium* gliding across the slide is going to show us exactly why.

---

## The kingdom that wasn't

In 1866, a German biologist named Ernst Haeckel — a great drawer of organisms, a great coiner of words, the man who gave us the terms *ecology* and *phylogeny* — looked at the swarming microscopic world that the microscope had been revealing for two hundred years and proposed a tidy solution. There were plants. There were animals. And there was everything else — all these single-celled swimmers and the simpler many-celled things that fit neither category. Haeckel gathered them into a third kingdom and named it the Protista, from the Greek *protos*, meaning *first*. The first ones. The primitive forms, as he saw them, from which the higher kingdoms had risen.

It was a reasonable guess for 1866. If your only tool is the eye and the microscope, and you are sorting life by how complicated it looks, then a *Paramecium* and an amoeba and a diatom all look like roughly the same grade of thing — small, single-celled, simple compared to an oak or a horse. Lump them together. Call them the first ones. Move on.

The trouble is that "looks simple" is not a relationship. It is an impression. And the entire enterprise of this book — the reason there is a tree of life at all — is that the way to organize living things is not by how they look but by how they are *related*, by who shares an ancestor with whom. Sorting organisms by their grade of complexity is like sorting a library by the thickness of the books. You will get neat shelves, and they will tell you nothing true.

For a century nobody had a better tool, so the Protista stood — a vast, baggy kingdom holding everything that was not visibly a plant, an animal, or a fungus. Then, in the closing decades of the twentieth century, the same molecular tool that pulled the archaea out of the bacterial drawer and revealed the eukaryote as a fusion of two domains was turned on the protists. Biologists began reading the genes — the slow ribosomal-RNA molecule first, then whole genomes — and asking not *what does this organism look like* but *what is it actually related to.*

The kingdom fell apart in their hands.

## A grade, not a clade

Here is what they found, and it is worth getting the logic exactly right, because it is the logic the whole tree of life runs on.

The organisms lumped together as protists were not each other's closest relatives. Not even close. When you sort them by ancestry instead of appearance, they scatter — all across the eukaryotic tree, into deeply separated branches, some of them more closely related to *you* than to another so-called protist swimming in the same drop of pond water. The *Paramecium* and the amoeba, which look like cousins, sit on branches as far apart as you are from a mushroom. The kingdom Protista was not a group of relatives at all. It was a grab-bag of unrelated lineages that happened to share one negative trait: none of them had become a plant, an animal, or a fungus.

This is the difference between two ideas that sound similar and are not. A **clade** is a true branch of the tree — an ancestor and *all* of its descendants, a complete twig you could snip off in one cut. Animals are a clade: one ancestor, every animal descended from it, nothing left out. A **grade**, by contrast, is a group defined by a shared *level* or a shared *lack* rather than by shared ancestry. "Protists" is a grade. It is the eukaryotes minus the three lineages — plants, animals, fungi — that happened to get large and famous. You cannot snip the protists off the tree in one cut, because to do so you would have to leave the plants and animals and fungi behind, and they are nested right in the middle of the protist branches. A protist, properly defined, is simply *a eukaryote that is not a plant, an animal, or a fungus.* It is a definition by exclusion. It tells you what the organism failed to become, not what it is.

So the word survives — it is useful, biologists still say "protist" every day — but it no longer names a kingdom. It names a *grade*, a level of organization, a convenient verbal shorthand. There is no kingdom Protista on the modern tree of life. There cannot be, because the thing it was supposed to name is not a branch.

What *is* on the tree, in place of that vanished kingdom, is something far more interesting: a handful of enormous, deeply ancient branches called the supergroups, each holding a wild assortment of organisms that look nothing alike but share, in their genes, the signature of a common ancestor. And the famous kingdoms — plants, animals, fungi — turn out to be small twigs tucked inside these much larger branches, not separate trunks of their own.

## The supergroups

Let me show you where things actually sit, because once you see it the old kingdom-of-protists picture becomes impossible to take seriously again.

The eukaryotes — every cell with a nucleus, the whole fused-from-two-domains lineage of the last chapter — gather into a small number of supergroups. The names are forbidding; ignore them for a moment and watch what they contain.

There is a supergroup called **Amorphea**, and this is the one that should rearrange your sense of things. It holds the amoebas — the shape-shifting blobs that ooze along on flowing extensions of themselves — and it *also* holds, on a sub-branch called Obazoa, both the animals *and* the fungi. Read that again. Animals and fungi are in the same supergroup, and amoebas are in there with them. You are more closely related to a mushroom than a mushroom is to an oak tree. The animal kingdom and the fungal kingdom, which seem like two plainly separate great divisions of life, are two twigs on a branch that also carries the amoebas — and they are nearer kin to each other than either is to a plant. The slime molds, which we'll come back to, live in this supergroup too. The whole thing is one branch, and "animal" and "fungus" are leaves on it.

There is a second great supergroup, **Diaphoretickes**, and it is where most of the photosynthetic life lives. It holds the Archaeplastida — the lineage that captured the cyanobacterium two chapters ago and turned it into the chloroplast, which means it holds the red algae, the green algae, and the land plants, the entire green world descended from that one swallowing. And alongside the plants it holds a clade nicknamed SAR, for the three groups inside it: the Stramenopiles (which include the diatoms and the giant kelps), the Alveolates (which include our *Paramecium*, along with the organisms that cause malaria and the dinoflagellates that light up the sea at night), and the Rhizaria (amoeba-like cells with elaborate mineral shells). So the diatoms, the kelps, the *Paramecium*, the malaria parasite, and the oak tree are all in one supergroup — related, distantly, by descent — while the *Paramecium* and the amoeba it resembles are in entirely *different* supergroups, not close kin at all.

And there are the **Excavates** — Discoba and Metamonada and a scatter of orphan lineages near the very root of the eukaryote tree, organisms with names like Malawimonada and the CRuMs and the ancyromonads that biologists are still trying to place, still arguing about, because the root of the eukaryote tree is genuinely not yet resolved. Where exactly these deep lineages attach is a live question, and the supergroup map is not frozen — it is a working draft, redrawn every few years as more genomes come in.

But the shape of the answer is clear and it is permanent: there is no kingdom of protists. There are these few great branches, and the plants and animals and fungi are *inside* them, three conspicuous twigs on a tree whose vast majority — by branch, by lineage, by sheer evolutionary depth — is microbial. The visible kingdoms we organized biology around are the exceptions. The single-celled eukaryotes are the rule.

## What one cell can and cannot do

Go back to the *Paramecium* on the slide, because it carries the trade-off at the heart of this whole chapter, and it is a trade-off about the limits of being one cell.

A single eukaryotic cell is an astonishing piece of engineering. The *Paramecium* packs into one membrane-bound bag everything it needs to live a rich, active, almost animal life. It has not one nucleus but two kinds: a large *macronucleus* that runs the cell's day-to-day business, churning out the instructions for ordinary metabolism, and a small *micronucleus* that holds the germline, the genetic material reserved for sexual exchange — a division of labor between the working copy and the archival copy, inside one cell. It has rows of cilia for swimming and feeding, an oral groove for funneling in bacteria, and a small organ called a contractile vacuole, which solves a problem you might not have thought of.

The *Paramecium* lives in fresh water, which means water is constantly flooding *into* it by osmosis — the cell's salty interior pulls fresh water across the membrane relentlessly, and without some defense the cell would swell and burst like an overfilled balloon. So the contractile vacuole acts as a bilge pump. It collects the incoming water into a central reservoir and then, with a sharp contraction, squirts it back out through a pore in the membrane. Bail, fill, bail, fill, all day, every day, for the entire life of the cell — a single organelle running a continuous pumping operation just to keep the cell from drowning in the very water it lives in. All of this — the two nuclei, the cilia, the oral groove, the bilge pump — coordinated and powered and maintained inside one cell.

That is what one cell can do, and it is more than enough for a versatile, mobile, behaviorally rich life.

Here is what one cell cannot do. It cannot get large. There is a ceiling, and the ceiling is set by simple physics: a single cell has to move materials around inside itself — food in, waste out, signals from one end to the other — and it does this largely by diffusion and by internal transport along its skeleton, processes that work beautifully across the width of a *Paramecium* and fail utterly across the width of a mouse. Double a cell's diameter and you eightfold its volume but only fourfold its surface, so the interior outruns the membrane's ability to supply it. There is no way around this for a single cell. To get big — to build a body the size of a fish or a tree — you have to stop being one cell and become many, with specialized tissues, plumbing to carry materials over distances diffusion can't bridge, a division of labor across cells rather than across organelles.

That is the trade-off the single-celled eukaryotes made, every one of them, and it is why most of them stayed small. They optimized for a complete, self-sufficient, behaviorally sophisticated life *within one cell* — a whole creature's worth of capability in a package a quarter-millimeter long. The price was a hard cap on size. The *Paramecium* will never be larger than a *Paramecium*. The lineages that broke the ceiling — that became plants, animals, fungi — did so by giving up the self-sufficient single cell and committing to the multicellular body, with all the coordination and cost that requires. Versatility in one cell, or size across many. You cannot have both, and the eukaryotic tree is split, over and over, between lineages that took one path and lineages that took the other.

## The cells that change their minds

There is one group that sits right on the seam of that trade-off, and it is worth a look because it shows the choice being made in real time, in a single lifetime.

The slime molds — in the supergroup Amorphea, the same branch as the animals and fungi — spend most of their lives as ordinary single cells, amoebas crawling through the soil and the leaf litter, hunting bacteria, minding their own business, each one a complete and independent organism. But when the food runs out, something extraordinary happens. The starving single cells begin to release a chemical signal, and they follow each other's signals, streaming together across the ground, tens of thousands of formerly independent cells flowing toward common gathering points. And there they *merge* — not into a crowd, but into a single coordinated body, a multicellular slug that crawls as one, that hunts as one, that finally raises a stalk and a fruiting head from which spores are flung to start the cycle again.

Single cells that, on cue, assemble themselves into a multicellular organism, and then disperse back into single cells. The slime mold lives on both sides of the great trade-off, switching between them as conditions demand. It is a living demonstration that the line between "one cell" and "a body" is not a wall but a threshold, crossable, and that the multicellular bodies we take for granted — the animals, the plants, the fungi — were built by lineages that learned, at some deep point in the past, to stop letting go of each other after they divided.

And it is a perfect emblem of why "protist" cannot be a kingdom. Here is an organism that is sometimes a single cell and sometimes a coordinated multicellular body, living in the same supergroup as the animals and the fungi, related more closely to *you* than to the *Paramecium* or the diatom that an older biology would have shelved right beside it. Sort it by appearance and it lands in the bin marked "simple microbe." Sort it by ancestry and it lands on your branch of the tree. The two answers are not close. One of them is true.

## The half of the air you cannot see

Now move the lens all the way out, from the one drop of pond water to the whole living planet, because the single-celled eukaryotes are not a curiosity at the edge of biology. They run a substantial fraction of the world.

Consider the diatoms — single-celled algae in the Stramenopile branch, each one living inside an intricate, ornate shell of glass. Not glass as a metaphor: glass as a fact. The diatom pulls dissolved silicon out of seawater and builds itself a two-part case of silica, the same material as window glass and quartz, sculpted into patterns of pores and ridges so fine and so regular that Victorian microscopists used them to test the resolution of their best lenses. The oceans are full of them — they take in something over six billion tonnes of silicon every year to build these glass houses — and they are doing photosynthesis inside those houses, on a planetary scale. Diatoms alone are estimated to generate on the order of twenty percent of the oxygen produced on Earth each year. One in five breaths you take, the oxygen in it was released by a single-celled alga living in a glass box in the sea.

*Figure 8.4 — Glass houses that make the air. Each diatom builds itself a two-part case of silica — the same material as window glass — and photosynthesizes inside it. Diatoms alone release on the order of 20% of Earth's oxygen; all marine phytoplankton together produce close to half, matching every land plant on every continent combined.*

Widen it further. Count all the marine phytoplankton — the diatoms plus the other photosynthetic drifters of the ocean, the dinoflagellates, the coccolithophores, the green and golden picoplankton — and together they account for something close to *half* of all the photosynthesis on the entire planet. Half. Every forest, every grassland, every jungle and crop and houseplant on every continent, all the land plants together, do roughly the other half. The drifting single-celled life of the sea, invisible without a microscope, individually smaller than a speck of dust, matches the entire green mass of the land in the work of turning sunlight and carbon dioxide into living matter and free oxygen. The air you breathe is, to a first approximation, half a gift from organisms most people have never heard of and could not see if they tried.

And then there is the other face of the single-celled eukaryotes, the one that has shaped human history more than any war. In the Alveolate branch, the same supergroup as our gentle *Paramecium*, lives a genus called *Plasmodium* — single-celled parasites that live half their lives in a mosquito and half in a human bloodstream, and that cause malaria. In 2023, by the World Health Organization's accounting, there were around 263 million cases of malaria and roughly 597,000 deaths, the great majority of them young children in sub-Saharan Africa. Not a virus. Not a bacterium. A protist — a single-celled eukaryote, a cousin of the slipper-shaped hunter on the slide — has been among the deadliest agents in all of human experience, and remains so today, killing more than half a million people in a single recent year.

Hold those two facts together, because between them they tell you why the vanished kingdom matters. The single-celled eukaryotes make half the air and harbor one of the deadliest diseases. They are not a primitive footnote on the way up to the real organisms. They are not the simple first draft that the plants and animals improved upon. They are running the chemistry of the ocean and writing themselves into every human death certificate that says malaria. The kingdom we built to file them away as "the simple ones, the first ones, the not-quite" was not just wrong about their relationships. It was wrong about their importance. We mistook the things we couldn't see for things that didn't matter.

## The orphans, named

Go back, one last time, to the drop of pond water and the slipper-shaped cell gliding across it, hunting bacteria with its rows of beating hairs.

For a century and a half it had a home. It was a protist, a member of a kingdom, filed alongside the amoeba and the diatom and the slime mold and the malaria parasite, all of them shelved together under one comfortable word. Then the molecules spoke, and the kingdom dissolved, and the *Paramecium* found itself an orphan — not because it lost its relatives, but because we finally learned that the relatives we'd given it were strangers. Its true kin are the dinoflagellates and the malaria parasites, off in the Alveolate branch; the amoeba beside it on the slide belongs to a different supergroup entirely, nearer to the animals and fungi; the diatom is off in a third branch, building its glass. The drop of pond water, which looks like a single community of simple cells, is in fact a chance meeting of lineages separated by more evolutionary distance than separates you from a fish.

That is the deep lesson of the orphans, and it runs underneath this entire book. Appearance is not kinship. The eye sorts the world into the simple and the complex, the small and the large, the important and the negligible — and the eye is wrong, again and again, because the tree of life was not built to look tidy. It was built by descent, one cell dividing into two, for nearly four billion years, with no regard for whether the results would fall into neat kingdoms. When we sorted life by how it looked, we got a kingdom of orphans — a bin of unrelated strangers united only by our failure to see what they were. When we learned to read the genes, the bin came apart, and the orphans turned out to be scattered across the whole tree, some of them sitting closer to us than we ever imagined, all of them carrying, in their single cells, the long unbroken record of where they actually came from.

There are no simple organisms. There are only old ones, and we are late, and the most important thing the *Paramecium* can teach you is that the tree does not care what you think it should look like. It only records, faithfully and indifferently, who came from whom.
