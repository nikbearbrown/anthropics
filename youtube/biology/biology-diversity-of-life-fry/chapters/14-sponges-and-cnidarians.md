# Chapter 14 — Sponges and Cnidarians

The water is dark and warm and you are floating above the reef on a night three or four days after the November full moon, and nothing is happening. Then everything happens at once. From the surface of the coral — the coral that has sat here, apparently inert, for centuries, that you would have sworn was rock — tiny bundles begin to lift off. First a few, pink and pale, drifting up like sparks rising backward from a fire. Then thousands. Then the whole reef, every coral head for miles along the Great Barrier Reef, releasing its bundles together in a single coordinated pulse, until the water column is so thick with rising eggs and sperm that your dive light cannot cut through it. The sea has turned to soup. This is coral mass spawning, and it is one of the largest synchronized events in the living world — an entire reef, an animal city built by creatures two cell-layers thick, reproducing on a single cue from the moon.

The cue matters, and it tells you something. The corals cannot see each other across the reef. They have no calendar. What they have is a sensitivity to moonlight — to the specific quality and timing of light from the moon — and by all spawning on the same lunar signal, they solve a problem that any rooted animal faces. A coral cannot move to find a mate. Its eggs and its neighbor's sperm have to meet in open water, and open water disperses everything in minutes. So the reef floods the sea with sex cells all at once, in the same hour, maximizing the chance that an egg from this colony meets sperm from that one before the current sweeps them apart. Synchrony is the answer to being rooted in place and needing to breed with a stranger.

The corals are **cnidarians**, and they are the subject of the second half of this chapter. But to understand them — to understand what it means that a thing without a brain can keep a calendar, fire a harpoon faster than a bullet, and build a structure visible from orbit — we have to go down one branch further, to the animals that are simpler still, and that take us closer than anything else alive to the question of how animals began at all. We have to start with a creature that is, in every sense, just a body that filters water. We have to start with the sponge.

## The cell that built the kingdom

Pick up a sponge — a real one, from a reef, not the synthetic block in your kitchen sink. It has no front and no back, no head, no mouth, no nerves, no muscles. It does not move. If you push your finger into it, it does not flinch, because there is nothing in it to flinch. It is, to a first approximation, a sack of cells around a system of channels, riddled with pores. The name of the whole phylum, **Porifera**, means exactly that — *pore-bearer*, from the Latin *porus*, a passage, and *ferre*, to carry. A sponge is a body that carries passages, and water moves through those passages all day long.

Why does water move through it? Because the sponge is pumping. And here is where we get to the mechanism — the single deep thing I want you to carry out of this chapter — because the pump is also the mouth, the stomach, and, in a sense, the entire animal. It is a cell, and it is called a **choanocyte**.

The word comes from the Greek *choanē*, a funnel, and *kytos*, a cell — the funnel cell, also called the collar cell, and once you see how it is built you will understand both names. Picture a single cell with a tail. The tail is a **flagellum**, a long whip-like filament that the cell can beat, lashing it back and forth. Now picture, ringing the base of that flagellum like a collar around a neck, a ring of fingers — between roughly twenty and forty thin projections called **microvilli**, standing up in a circle around the flagellum, and webbed together with a fine mesh of sticky molecules, a net strung between the fingers. That is the whole apparatus: a whip in the middle, a collar of mesh-strung fingers around it.

Watch what it does. The flagellum beats, and beating a whip in water does not push the water straight — it drives a current down the length of the flagellum and *out*, away from the cell, the way a propeller throws water behind it. Throwing water out one way means water has to flow *in* from the other way to replace it, and the only path in is through the collar — through the fine mesh strung between the microvilli. So the choanocyte creates a one-way current: water is sucked through the mesh of the collar and then jetted out past the flagellum. And the mesh is fine enough that bacteria and floating scraps of organic matter, drawn in with the water, snag on it. They cannot pass through. They stick. And then the cell engulfs them — wraps its membrane around each trapped particle and pulls it inside to be digested, a process called **phagocytosis**, *phago* for eating, *cyto* for cell: the cell eating.

That is the sponge. Line a sponge's internal chambers with thousands of these collar cells, all beating their flagella, and the combined current is enough to pull a great deal of seawater through the whole animal — a sponge the size of your fist can filter many liters a day — straining out the bacteria and trapping its food without ever moving a muscle, because it has no muscles. The sponge does not chase food. It builds a current and lets the food come to it, one bacterium at a time, caught in a collar of mesh.

## The cell that remembers where animals came from

Now I am going to show you why that one cell is the most important cell in this book so far, and it has nothing to do with sponges and everything to do with you.

Out in open water, not attached to any animal, swimming freely on its own, lives a single-celled organism called a **choanoflagellate**. The name should make you sit up: *choano*, the funnel-collar, *flagellate*, the whip-bearer. It is built the same way as the sponge's collar cell — a cell with a single flagellum, ringed by a collar of microvilli, beating water through the mesh to trap and eat bacteria. It is not an animal. It is a single free-living cell, and it is the closest living single-celled relative of the entire animal kingdom. Read the genomes and the answer is unambiguous: the choanoflagellates are our nearest unicellular cousins, sister to all animals, sponges and jellyfish and beetles and humans alike.

Sit with what that implies. The animal kingdom — every body that has ever crawled, swum, flown, or read a book — appears to have descended from something very like a colony of these collar cells. Picture a cluster of choanoflagellates that stopped drifting apart after dividing, that stayed stuck together, that began to specialize — some cells beating water, some cells doing other jobs — and you have, in outline, the road from a single cell to the first animal. The sponge is the living echo of that beginning. When you watch a sponge pump water through chambers lined with collar cells, you are watching a body that is still, structurally, very close to the colony of feeding cells that all animals came from. The mouth and the stomach and the pump are still the same cell. The division of labor has barely begun.

A note of honesty, because the voice of this book does not let a clean story past without checking it. The sponge's choanocyte is not a simple copy of the free-living choanoflagellate — it is a specialized feeding cell, packed with food vacuoles, tuned for its job inside a sponge body, and the two are cousins rather than one being the unaltered ancestor of the other. The collar-and-flagellum design is the shared inheritance; the details have diverged. But the kinship is real and the lesson holds: the first thing an animal cell learned to do, before nerves, before muscles, before brains, was to beat a whip and catch a bacterium in a collar. Everything else was built on top of that.

This is also where the question from the last chapter comes back to haunt us. I told you the animal root is contested — that the leading hypothesis places the comb jellies, not the sponges, as the first branch off the animal tree, though the evidence flickers and the highest-profile rival claim was retracted in early 2026. Notice what that does to the sponge's story. If sponges branched first, the picture is tidy: animals began simple, like a sponge, and built up. But if comb jellies branched first — comb jellies, which have nerves and muscles — then the sponge is not the simple ancestor. Then the sponge may have *lost* things, or the simplicity may be a different kind of simplicity than it looks. The collar cell still ties us to the choanoflagellates either way; that kinship does not depend on the root. But whether the sponge sits at the very base of the tree, the closest living mirror of the first animal, is exactly the question that is unresolved tonight. Hold both: the choanocyte is ancient and tells us where animals came from; *which* simple animal came first is still being argued in the journals.

## The first nervous system

Leave the sponge and go back up to the reef, to the corals spawning in the moonlight, and to their relatives — the jellyfish, the sea anemones, the tiny freshwater *Hydra*. These are the cnidarians, and they are the first animals in this book to have something the sponge entirely lacks: a nervous system.

The name **Cnidaria** comes from the Greek *knidē*, a nettle — the stinging plant — because the defining feature of these animals is that they sting. We will get to how in a moment. But first, the body, because the cnidarian body is the first to be organized in a way you would recognize as having a plan.

A cnidarian is built on **radial symmetry** — arranged around a central axis like the spokes of a wheel, so that it has a top and a bottom but no left and right, no front and no back. There are two basic forms, and most cnidarians are one or the other at some point in their lives. The **polyp** is the form that sits still — a tube fixed at the bottom, mouth pointing up, ringed by tentacles. A sea anemone is a polyp. A coral is a colony of tiny polyps. The **medusa** is the form that swims — the same body plan turned upside down and set free, mouth pointing down, tentacles trailing, the bell pulsing. A jellyfish is a medusa. Both have a single opening that serves as mouth and anus together, leading into a simple gut, with the mouth ringed by tentacles in either case.

And running through that body — through the tentacles, around the mouth, across the whole animal — is the first nervous system in the history of life: a **nerve net**. The phrase is exactly right. It is a net of neurons, nerve cells connected to their neighbors in a diffuse web spread through the body wall, with no center, no brain, no headquarters. When a neuron fires, the signal spreads outward through the net in all directions, like a ripple, rather than being routed to a control room and back. There is no decision being made anywhere in particular. There is only the net, sensing a touch on a tentacle and propagating the response outward, telling the nearby tentacles to bend toward the stimulus and the mouth to open.

This is worth dwelling on because it is so unlike how you think a nervous system works. You have a brain — a place where signals converge, are evaluated, and from which commands issue. The cnidarian has no such place. Its intelligence, such as it is, is distributed evenly through a mesh, with no part in charge. And it works. A *Hydra* with a nerve net can catch prey, move it to its mouth, swallow it, and respond to light and touch — all with no neuron more important than any other. The nerve net is the minimum viable nervous system, the first answer to the problem of coordinating a body, and it has been running, essentially unchanged, for well over half a billion years.

## The fastest thing in the animal body

Now, the sting — because it is the cnidarian's masterpiece, and because the numbers behind it are almost hard to believe.

Embedded in the tentacles are specialized cells called **cnidocytes**, the nettle cells, and inside each one is a structure called a **nematocyst** — *nēma*, a thread, *kystis*, a capsule: the thread-capsule. Picture a tiny sealed bladder, under enormous internal pressure, with a long hollow thread coiled up inside it like a harpoon stuffed into a barrel, the thread often tipped with barbs or spines and sometimes loaded with venom. On the outside of the cell is a trigger — a fine hair. When prey brushes that hair, the capsule fires. The lid pops, the internal pressure releases, and the coiled thread shoots out, turning itself inside out as it goes, driving its barbed tip into the prey and injecting venom.

Here are the numbers, and read them slowly, one at a time, because each is doing work. The discharge happens in as little as seven hundred **nanoseconds** — seven hundred billionths of a second. The accelerating tip experiences forces measured at around five million times the acceleration of gravity. The pressure at the point of impact, where the thread strikes the prey, has been measured at around seven gigapascals — comparable to the pressures used to drive industrial cutting. Put those three together: in under a millionth of a second, a structure smaller than a speck of dust accelerates harder than almost anything else known in biology and strikes with a pressure that can punch through the shell of a small crustacean. The nematocyst is among the fastest mechanical events in the entire living world, and it is fired by an animal with no brain to aim it. The cell aims itself. The trigger is local, the discharge is local, the whole thing happens faster than any nervous system could possibly route a signal — which is precisely why it is built to fire on its own, mechanically, the instant the trigger is touched. A nerve net is too slow for this. So the cnidarian did not use the nerve net for it. It built a self-firing harpoon into each cell and let physics do the aiming.

## The trade-off: a body with no direction

Here is the cost of the cnidarian design, and it is written into the radial symmetry itself.

A radial body — arranged like a wheel, the same in every direction around its axis — is supremely good at one thing. It can catch food coming from *any* direction equally well. A sea anemone does not care which way the current brings its dinner; its ring of tentacles, identical all the way around, is ready for prey arriving from any compass point. For an animal that sits still and waits, this is the optimal design. You never have to turn around. There is no behind you, because every direction is the front.

But that same symmetry forbids something fundamental: it cannot efficiently go *toward* anything. To move purposefully toward a target — to chase, to flee, to navigate — an animal needs a front end that leads and a back end that follows. It needs to commit to a direction of travel and concentrate its senses and decision-making at the leading edge, so it can steer. A radial animal has no leading edge. The jellyfish that swims is not steering toward prey; it is pulsing through the water and trailing its tentacles, catching whatever it blunders into. The anemone does not stalk. The coral does not hunt at all; it waits.

This is the trade-off, stated plainly. The radial body optimized for **catching food from every direction without having to orient** — perfect for an ambush predator that stays put — at the cost of **the ability to pursue, to flee with intent, to navigate toward a goal**. It traded direction for omnidirectional readiness. And that is the ceiling on the whole lineage. No cnidarian, in over half a billion years, has become a fast directed hunter, because the body plan that makes it a perfect waiting trap is the body plan that makes it a hopeless chaser. To get a hunter, evolution had to throw out radial symmetry and commit to a front and a back — which is exactly the story of the next chapter.

## Builders the size of countries

Now the scale shift, and it is the one that should make you put the book down for a moment.

The coral polyp is tiny — a few millimeters across, a soft tube of two cell-layers with a ring of tentacles, a nerve net, and a battery of nematocysts. It is among the simplest animals that exist. And yet the structures these animals build are the largest constructions made by any living thing on Earth, the only biological structures visible from orbit. The Great Barrier Reef stretches more than two thousand kilometers along the coast of Australia and has been assembled, layer by microscopic layer, by generations of these millimeter-scale animals secreting limestone beneath themselves and dying and being built upon. Reefs grow by perhaps a centimeter or so a year. The scale of the structure relative to the builder is as if a single human, working with no machinery, laid one brick and then was buried under the bricks of descendants until the wall was tall enough to be seen from the Moon.

How does an animal two cell-layers thick afford to build at that scale? It does not do it on filter-feeding alone — there is not enough food drifting past. It does it through a partnership, and the partnership is the key to the entire reef and, as we will see, its undoing.

Inside the tissues of a reef-building coral live single-celled algae called **zooxanthellae** — photosynthetic cells, relatives of the dinoflagellates, packed into the coral's cells by the millions. The algae do what plants do: they capture sunlight and use it to build sugar out of carbon dioxide and water. And because they live *inside* the coral, in clear sunlit tropical water, they hand most of that sugar straight to their host. The coral, in exchange, gives the algae a safe sunlit home, a supply of carbon dioxide from its own respiration, and nitrogen-rich waste the algae can use as fertilizer. It is a closed loop, a near-perfect economy: the coral's waste feeds the algae, the algae's sugar feeds the coral. This partnership is why a reef can flourish in tropical seas that are otherwise nutrient-poor — biological deserts, clear blue and nearly empty of drifting food. The reef is an oasis built on sunlight, captured by algae, spent by corals on limestone. That is how an animal too simple to have a head builds a structure visible from space.

## When the partnership breaks

And here, at last, is where the moral weight of the chapter arrives — not announced, but arriving, because it has been built up under everything else.

The coral–algae partnership is exquisitely tuned, which is another way of saying it is fragile. The algae photosynthesize best within a narrow band of temperature and light. Push the water too warm, hold it too warm for too long, and the photosynthesis inside the coral's cells begins to malfunction — the light-capturing machinery, overdriven, starts producing toxic byproducts instead of sugar. The relationship turns poisonous. And the coral does the only thing it can: it expels the algae. It evicts its partners, casting out the zooxanthellae from its tissues, and a coral can lose sixty to seventy percent of its algae this way in a stressed event.

The algae are also what give the coral its color. So when they are expelled, the coral turns white — you can see straight through the now-transparent animal tissue to the white limestone skeleton beneath. This is **coral bleaching**, and it is not the coral dying, not at first. It is the coral surviving by amputating the partnership that feeds it. A bleached coral is a coral that has thrown away its food supply to escape a poison, and it is now starving, living on its reserves, waiting for the water to cool so the algae can return. If the water cools soon enough, the algae recolonize and the coral recovers. If the heat holds, the coral starves to death, and the reef — the structure visible from orbit, built by millimeter animals over thousands of years — begins to die from the surface down.

The Great Barrier Reef has now suffered mass bleaching in 1998, 2002, 2016, 2017, 2022, and 2024 — the events clustering tighter together, the intervals shrinking. Each one is a sea grown too warm for the partnership to hold. The numbers from the opening of this chapter — the whole reef spawning on a single moonlit night to flood the water with a future — are the same reef now bleaching white on a single overheated summer. The animal that learned to keep a calendar by moonlight, that built a structure you can see from space, that fires the fastest harpoon in biology, cannot survive a few degrees of sustained warming, because the engine of the whole edifice is a partnership with an alga that photosynthesizes within a narrow window of temperature, and we are pushing the water outside the window.

There is no need to make a speech about it. The chapter began with the reef turning the sea to soup with life, every coral spawning at once. It ends with the same corals turning white, expelling the partners that feed them, because the water got too warm. The wonder and the warning are the same animal. That is the whole of it.

## What broke the old assumption

One last scene, because the cnidarians also broke a piece of received wisdom, and the story is worth telling.

In the summer of 1740, in a country house outside The Hague, a Swiss tutor named Abraham Trembley was looking through a lens at small green threads he had scooped from a ditch — things attached to pondweed, with arms that waved and caught tiny prey. He could not decide whether they were plants or animals. Plants do not move their parts to catch food; this thing did. Animals were thought not to grow back when cut; plants were. So Trembley did the experiment that any plant or animal might be subjected to: he cut one in half.

Both halves lived. More than that — the bottom half grew a new head with new arms, and the top half grew a new base, and within days he had two complete animals where there had been one. He cut them into more pieces; the pieces regenerated. He turned one inside out, like a glove; it survived and went on feeding. This was the freshwater *Hydra*, a cnidarian polyp, and Trembley's results — reported in a 1740 letter to the naturalist Réaumur, published in full in 1744 — broke an assumption that had stood since antiquity: that an animal is an indivisible whole, that you cannot cut it apart and get more animals, that regeneration on this scale belongs to plants. The *Hydra* did not know the rule, and so it broke it.

That capacity is not magic; it is the same theme that runs through this whole part of the book. A *Hydra* can regenerate because its body is simple enough — two cell-layers, a nerve net with no irreplaceable brain, no complex organs that must be rebuilt in exact relation to one another — that the remaining cells can rebuild the whole from a fragment. Simplicity is what makes it possible. The more an animal commits to a fixed, intricate, front-to-back body plan, the less it can regrow when cut, because too much depends on too much else. The sponge and the *Hydra* can be cut apart and live because they never committed very hard to being any particular shape.

Which is the perfect place to leave this chapter, because the next animal we meet has committed. It has a front and a back, a head and a tail, a brain at the leading end. And it can still, against all the odds, be cut into hundreds of pieces and grow a whole worm from each. To see why that is the surprise of the entire chapter to come, you had to first understand why a sponge and a *Hydra* can do it easily — and why, by all rights, a thing with a head should not be able to do it at all.
