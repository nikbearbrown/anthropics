# Introduction — The One Tree

A shop in Delft, autumn, the light failing early the way it does in the Low Countries. The man at the window sells cloth — wool, linen, the bolts stacked behind him in the dim — and he uses small lenses to count the threads, to check that the weave is honest before he sells it. That is his trade. But he has learned to grind glass better than anyone alive, beads of it no bigger than a pinhead, and tonight he is not looking at cloth.

He has caught some rainwater. Or water from the lake outside town, or from the gutter, or from a tooth he has scraped — over the years it will be all of these. He holds the drop to the lens, the lens to a candle, and he puts his eye close.

And the water is full of animals.

They tumble and dart. Some spin. Some have little legs that thrash. There are more of them in this single bead of water than — he will write later, struggling to make the Royal Society in faraway London believe him — than there are people in the whole of his country. He counts and recounts. He is not a trained philosopher; he is a draper named Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, and in October of 1676 he writes a letter describing what he calls his *animalcules* — little animals — and the educated men of Europe do not believe a word of it until they grind their own lenses and look for themselves.

What he had found, of course, was the rest of life. Not the rare exception, not the curiosity at the margin, but the majority — the overwhelming, planet-running majority of living things, which are single cells and which we had simply never seen. For all of human history up to that drop of water, "life" had meant what we could see with the naked eye: the things that walk and swim and grow in the garden. Leeuwenhoek had pulled back the curtain on the other ninety-nine percent.

This book is about everything behind that curtain and in front of it — the full sweep of living things, from the archaeon thriving in near-boiling acid to the blue whale, from the virus that may not even be alive to the aspen grove in Utah that is one organism and forty-seven thousand trees at once. The diversity of life. The variety is the easy part to feel; you have felt it on any walk through a forest, in any tide pool. The harder, stranger, more beautiful fact is the one underneath the variety, and it is the argument of this entire book:

**It is all one thing.**

Every organism alive — every beetle and kelp and orchid, every bacterium in your gut, the archaeon in the hot spring, you — is a leaf on a single tree. Not a metaphorical tree. A literal genealogy, an unbroken chain of parent-to-offspring that runs back through roughly three and a half to four billion years to one population of cells from which everything descends. The hermit crab and the mushroom and the moss on the wall are not separate creations that happen to resemble one another. They are cousins. Distant cousins — but the relationship is exact, traceable, and in its broad shape no longer in serious doubt.

How can we possibly know that? It is one thing to say a chimpanzee is your relative; you can see the kinship in the hands, the eyes, the way it sits. It is another to claim that a bacterium and a redwood share an ancestor. They have nothing visible in common. The redwood is three hundred feet of wood; the bacterium is a fleck of chemistry you cannot see. Where is the family resemblance?

It is in the writing.

Here is the thing you are actually looking at when you look at any living cell. Inside it is a long thin molecule, DNA, and along that molecule runs a sequence of just four chemical letters — call them A, C, G, and T. That sequence is an instruction set: it tells the cell which proteins to build, and proteins are the machines that do nearly everything a cell does. But the instructions are written in a code, and a code needs a dictionary — a rule that says *this run of letters means build this part*. The cell reads the letters three at a time. Each triplet of letters is a word, and each word specifies one amino acid, one bead to add to the growing chain of a protein. The triplet A-U-G means *start, and add the amino acid methionine*. The triplet G-G-A means *add glycine*. And so on down a table of sixty-four words.

Now here is the load-bearing fact. That dictionary — which triplet means which amino acid — is, with tiny exceptions, *the same in every living thing we have ever checked.* In the bacterium and the redwood and the archaeon and you, A-U-G means start, means methionine. The whole table holds. And there is no chemical reason it had to. The pairing of word to meaning is arbitrary — A-U-G could just as well have meant glycine, the way the sound "dog" could just as well have meant cat. There are a staggering number of possible dictionaries, more than there are atoms in the galaxy. Out of all of them, every organism on Earth uses the one same dictionary, give or take a few edited dialects.

There is only one good explanation for that. The dictionary is not being reinvented by each lineage. It is being *inherited* — copied, generation after generation, from a single common source, the way every Romance language inherited its grammar from one Latin no longer spoken. The shared code is the fingerprint of shared descent. It is the strongest single piece of evidence we have that the tree of life has one trunk and not a forest of separate trunks. Life, as far as we can tell, happened once. Everything after is variations.

And those tiny exceptions to the dictionary — the few cases where one organism reads a word differently? They do not break the argument; they make it sharper. In the little power-plants inside your own cells, the mitochondria, one triplet that means *stop* in the standard code instead means *add tryptophan*. A different dialect. But when you trace it, it is plainly a *change to* the universal code, a local edit, not an independent invention from scratch. Even the exceptions are modifications of the one original. Hold that thought. It is the quiet melody this whole book is built on, and I will not name it again until the end, but it is playing under every chapter: **every living thing is a modified version of something older. There are no truly new starts in biology. Only edits.**

The vertebrate jaw is a modified gill arch. The flower is a modified shoot. The bird is a modified dinosaur. The mitochondrion is a modified bacterium that got swallowed and stayed. We will meet these one at a time, and I will mostly let them pass without comment, because the point lands harder if you feel the pattern before anyone points at it.

The man who first drew the tree did not have the genetic code; he had only the resemblances, the fossils, and an extraordinary nerve. In a private notebook begun in 1837 — a notebook that went missing from Cambridge University Library for twenty years and was anonymously returned in 2022, left in a pink gift bag on a library floor — Charles Darwin sketched a branching diagram, lineages splitting from lineages, and wrote above it two words: *I think.* When he finally published *On the Origin of Species* in 1859, that book — dense with examples, hundreds of pages long — contained exactly one illustration, and it was a tree: branches dividing and dividing, some lines reaching the top of the page alive, most stopping partway, extinct. The single picture in the most consequential biology book ever written is a genealogy of everything.

A genealogy is the right word, but it hides a complication, and I would rather you have it now than feel cheated of it later. A tree is a powerful drawing and also a lossy one. It assumes that lineages only ever split apart and never rejoin — that once two branches part, no gene ever crosses from one to the other. For the animals and plants you can see, that is nearly true. But down among Leeuwenhoek's animalcules, the bacteria and archaea, genes leap sideways between unrelated lineages all the time, the way you might borrow a tool from a neighbor rather than invent it. The trunk of the tree, in places, is braided rather than clean. We will come back to this. For now, keep both ideas: the tree is the best map we have of how life is related, *and* the map smooths over some real tangles in the deepest, oldest part of the picture. Both are true. Good science holds the model and its limits in the same hand.

So here is the journey. We will start by asking what diversity even is — how many kinds of living things share the planet with us, and why that turns out to be a question about how hard we have looked rather than a thing anyone has counted. Then we will learn the grammar: how a name like *Homo sapiens* works, and how anyone reads kinship out of an organism in the first place — first from its anatomy, then, decisively, from its molecules. We will go down to the root, to the first cell and the deep chemistry that powered it, four billion years ago at the bottom of the sea. And we will pause at the strangest edge of the whole enterprise — the viruses, which carry the same four-letter code as everything else and yet may not be alive at all, and which force us to ask what we even mean by the word.

After that the book walks the tree the way time walked it: outward from the root, oldest lineages first, through the bacteria and archaea that ran the planet alone for two billion years, through the great fusion that built complex cells, out along the branches to the fungi, the plants climbing onto land, the flowers, and finally the animals — the sponges and corals, the worms and snails, the joint-legged insects that are most of everything, and at last the line that runs through fish and reptiles and the warm-blooded latecomers, to the one species reading this sentence.

It is a long way out to the twigs. But every twig connects back, without a single break, to that drop of water in Delft, and past it, down the trunk, to one beginning. When Leeuwenhoek put his eye to the lens and saw the water swarming, he did not know he was looking at his relatives. He was. So are you. Let us go and meet them.
