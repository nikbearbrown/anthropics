# The Fusion

The mud comes up cold and black and smelling of sulfur.

It is the summer of 2015, and somewhere in the Arctic Ocean, on the long ridge between Greenland and Norway, a research vessel has lowered a coring device a thousand meters and more down into the dark, into sediment near a field of hydrothermal vents — chimneys of mineral that the discoverers, finding them rising out of the black like castle towers, named for the trickster god Loki. The core comes up, and most of what it holds will never be looked at directly. The organisms in this mud are too slow, too strange, too dependent on conditions no laboratory can yet reproduce. No one will see them under a microscope. No one will grow them in a dish.

What the researchers do instead is grind the mud up and read the DNA in it — all of it, every fragment from every cell, sequenced in bulk and then sorted out by computer into the genomes it came from. And out of that slurry of genetic code, the team in Thijs Ettema's laboratory assembles the genome of a single kind of archaeon that no one had ever named, a microbe from the lineage of the ancient extremophiles, the cousins of the salt-lovers and the heat-lovers. They call it Lokiarchaeota, after the vents, after the god.

Then they read its genes, and the genes are wrong.

Not wrong as in damaged. Wrong as in *familiar*. An archaeon — a member of that ancient prokaryotic domain that has no nucleus and is supposed to be simple — should carry archaeal genes and only archaeal genes. But this one is carrying, scattered through its genome, the instructions for roughly 175 proteins that have no business being there. They are proteins for building and managing a complex internal architecture: for shaping membranes, for trafficking material around inside a cell, for the kind of internal skeleton and machinery that, until this moment, was thought to belong exclusively to the eukaryotes — to *us*. To plants and animals and fungi and every nucleated cell. These are called eukaryotic signature proteins, and they were supposed to be the dividing line, the thing that separated our kind of cell from the simple prokaryotic kind.

And here they are, in the genome of an archaeon pulled from cold mud near a vent named for Loki. The most eukaryote-like organism that does not have a nucleus that anyone has ever found.

To understand why this is one of the most important sentences in all of biology, you have to understand what kind of thing you are. Not a human — a *cell*. What kind of cell you are, and where it came from, and the answer is going to be stranger than anything in this book so far. You are not descended from one ancestor. You are descended from a collision.

---

## Two cells, one body

Open up any cell from your body and look inside. The difference between your cell and a bacterium is not a matter of degree. It is a matter of kind, and it is enormous.

Your cell is, first, *vast* by bacterial standards — often ten times wider, a thousand times the volume. It has a nucleus: a membrane-walled vault at its center where the DNA is kept, wound and organized and protected, read out through pores in the vault wall. It has an internal skeleton of protein filaments that it builds and tears down on the fly, hauling cargo along them like a freight system, changing its own shape, dividing its insides into compartments. It is a city, with districts and roads and walls and a central archive. A bacterium, by comparison, is a single open room with the DNA loose on the floor.

But the most telling difference is the power plant. Scattered through your cell — hundreds in some cells, thousands in others — are small, bean-shaped bodies called mitochondria. They are the cell's energy works. They take the food you've eaten, already partly broken down, and finish the job, burning it with oxygen to produce the chemical energy currency that powers everything the cell does. Without them you would be dead in minutes. They are the reason you can run, think, grow, heal — the reason a eukaryotic cell can afford to be a city instead of a single room, because a city needs a power grid, and the mitochondria are it.

Now look closer at a mitochondrion, and the strangeness begins.

It has its *own* DNA. A small ring of it, separate from the DNA in the nucleus — its own private genome, inside an organelle, inside the cell. It has its own ribosomes, its own protein-building machines, and they do not look like your cell's ribosomes. They look like a bacterium's. It builds its own internal membrane, and that membrane is not like the membranes of the rest of your cell. It divides on its own schedule, pinching in two like a bacterium dividing, not waiting for the cell around it to reproduce. In almost every way that matters, the mitochondrion behaves not like a part of your cell, but like a *guest* in it. A separate organism, living inside yours, carrying out its ancient trade, dividing to its own rhythm, reading its own genes.

Because that is exactly what it is.

One mitochondrion is enlarged to show its own circular ring of DNA, its own bacterial-style ribosomes, and its own inner membrane, with a label noting it divides on its own schedule.](images/07-the-fusion-fig-01.png)

*Figure 7.1 — A guest in the cell. The mitochondrion keeps its own ring of DNA, its own bacterial-style ribosomes, its own membranes, and its own division schedule — behaving less like a part of the cell than like a separate organism living inside it. Because that is what it once was.*

The mitochondrion was once a free-living bacterium. Read its DNA and compare it to the bacteria of the world, and it falls cleanly among the Alphaproteobacteria — a particular group of bacteria — with its nearest known relatives among an order called the Rickettsiales. Roughly two billion years ago, give or take, one of those bacteria ended up *inside* another cell, and instead of being digested, it stayed. It kept living. It kept dividing. And the cell that held it discovered that having a captive bacterium that could burn food with oxygen and hand over the energy was the best deal in the history of life. The two became one. The bacterium became the power plant. And every plant, every animal, every fungus, every nucleated cell alive today — every one — carries the descendants of that single captured bacterium in its body still, two billion years later, still ringed with its own membrane, still reading its own shrunken genome, still dividing on its own clock.

This is endosymbiosis — from the Greek, *endo* for inside, *sym* for together, *bios* for life: living-together-inside. One cell taking up residence inside another, permanently, until the two are no longer separable. And here is the claim that the cold mud near Loki's vents helps to settle: the cell that did the swallowing was an archaeon.

## The fusion of two domains

Hold the whole picture in your head now, because this is the deepest thing in the book.

There were two domains of simple cells: the Bacteria and the Archaea. We met them in the last two chapters — both small, both without a nucleus, both ancient, but built on different chemistries, with different membranes, different ribosomes, different genes. Two separate trunks of the tree of life, diverged for perhaps a billion years.

And then they fused.

An archaeal host cell — a relative of the Lokiarchaeota lineage, a cell from the ancient extremophile domain — somehow took in a bacterium, an alphaproteobacterium, and kept it alive inside itself. Not as food. As a partner. The bacterium kept doing what it did best: burning fuel with oxygen, generating energy. The host kept doing what *it* did best: living, dividing, holding the whole arrangement together. And over time the two wove themselves into a single thing that was neither one nor the other but something genuinely new — a cell with an archaeal core and a bacterial power plant, a chimera, a merger of two of the three domains of life into one.

That new thing is the eukaryote. It is you.

![A tree of life at its deepest level. Two branches, Bacteria and Archaea, split from a common root. An arrow shows a bacterium from the Alphaproteobacteria branch being taken up into an archaeal host cell on the Archaea branch, and from that fusion point a new branch rises, labeled Eukarya, carrying both ancestries.](images/07-the-fusion-fig-02.png)

*Figure 7.2 — The knot in the tree. The eukaryotic branch does not rise on its own from the root. It is a confluence — the archaeal line and the bacterial line, separated for perhaps a billion years, meeting in a single cell that became the ancestor of all complex life.*

This is why, when biologists now draw the deepest tree of life, the bottom of it does not look like three tidy branches splitting cleanly apart. It looks like two branches — Bacteria and Archaea — that split apart and then, once, came partway *back together*, the bacterial line and the archaeal line meeting in a single cell that became the ancestor of everything complex. The eukaryotic branch does not rise on its own from the root. It is a knot where two of the other branches tied themselves together. Your ancestry is not a line. It is a confluence — two rivers, separated for a billion years, joining into one.

And once the cell had its captive power plant, everything else became possible. This is the trade-off, and it is the grandest one in the book.

By outsourcing its energy to a captive organelle, the eukaryotic cell broke free of a limit that had held the bacteria and archaea small and simple for two billion years. A bacterium generates energy across its outer membrane, and the bigger it gets, the worse that works — surface area cannot keep up with volume, so a bacterium is capped at a size where its skin can still power its bulk. But a cell with hundreds of internal power plants is not limited by its skin. It can grow vast. It can afford the enormous energy cost of carrying thousands of genes, of building an internal skeleton, of maintaining a nucleus, of becoming a city. Every gene a cell carries costs energy to maintain and copy; a bacterium can afford only a lean genome; a eukaryote, with its mitochondrial grid, can afford a genome dozens of times larger and all the complexity that buys. The mitochondrion is the reason complex life exists at all. Multicellularity, large bodies, brains, this sentence — all of it sits on top of that one ancient act of swallowing.

That is the upside, and it is the whole visible living world. Now the cost.

Every eukaryote is chained, forever, to a borrowed organelle. The mitochondrion is no longer free-living; over two billion years it has shed almost all of its genes — most of them migrated into the host's nucleus, where the host now controls them — and what remains is a remnant genome, a handful of genes it cannot do without, that it still keeps and copies on its own. The cell cannot make a mitochondrion from scratch; it can only make new ones by dividing the old ones, which means the lineage of that captured bacterium must remain *unbroken*, generation after generation, for as long as eukaryotic life persists. Two billion years and counting, and not once has the line of mitochondria been allowed to lapse, because the day a cell has no mitochondrion is the day it cannot make energy and dies. We are powered by a captive whose continued existence we can never afford to lose. The deepest dependency in biology — the engine of all complex life — is a hostage we took two billion years ago and can never release.

Here is the leitmotif of this whole book, handed to you without comment, the clearest instance of it you will ever see: *the mitochondrion is a modified bacterium. The eukaryote is a modified archaeon wrapped around it.* Nothing started fresh. The most complex cell on Earth is two old cells, edited together.

## The second swallowing

And then, in one branch of the eukaryotes, it happened *again*.

If you are a plant, an alga, anything green — anything that turns sunlight into sugar — then your cell contains, alongside its mitochondria, a second kind of captive: the chloroplast, the green organelle where photosynthesis happens. And the chloroplast has the same tells as the mitochondrion. Its own DNA, a small ring of it. Its own bacterial-style ribosomes. Its own membranes. Its own division. Read its genes and they fall not among the bacteria generally but specifically among the cyanobacteria — the blue-green bacteria that, you'll recall, invented oxygen-releasing photosynthesis in the first place.

So it happened a second time, and once, in the ancestor of the group called Archaeplastida — the lineage that holds the glaucophytes, the red algae, the green algae, and the land plants. A eukaryotic cell, one that already had its mitochondrion, swallowed a cyanobacterium, around 1.6 billion years ago, and kept it. The cyanobacterium became the chloroplast. And now the cell had two captives — a bacterial power plant for burning fuel and a bacterial solar panel for making it. The plant is a triple being: an archaeal host, around a bacterial engine, around which a second bacterium, a cyanobacterium, was later folded in.

The leitmotif again, even cleaner: *the chloroplast is a modified cyanobacterium.* Every blade of grass, every leaf, every kelp frond is running its photosynthesis on the descendants of a free-living bacterium captured a billion and a half years ago, still ringed in its own membrane, still reading its own genes, the sunlight of the entire green world processed through a hostage taken before there were animals to walk on the plants.

Second, in one descendant lineage, that eukaryote engulfs a cyanobacterium, which becomes the chloroplast, producing the ancestor of plants and algae — a cell containing two captive bacteria.](images/07-the-fusion-fig-03.png)

*Figure 7.3 — Two swallowings. The mitochondrion came first, in the ancestor of all eukaryotes; the chloroplast came later and only once, in the ancestor of plants and algae. A plant cell is a triple being: an archaeal host, around a captive bacterial engine, around a captive cyanobacterial solar panel.*

## The woman who was right too early

The idea that the parts of our cells were once free-living bacteria sounds, the first time you hear it, slightly mad. It sounded exactly that mad in 1967, when a young biologist named Lynn Sagan — later, and better, known as Lynn Margulis — wrote it up and sent it out.

Her paper was called *On the Origin of Mitosing Cells.* In it she argued, marshaling what evidence the era allowed, that the mitochondria and the chloroplasts inside complex cells were the descendants of captured bacteria — that the eukaryotic cell was not a single thing that had gradually grown complex, but a community of once-separate organisms that had merged. It was an old idea in fragments — others had glimpsed pieces of it decades earlier — but she assembled it into a full, argued, modern case.

The establishment was not interested. The paper was rejected, by one account, by around fifteen journals before the *Journal of Theoretical Biology* finally took it. And even after publication it was largely ignored or dismissed for the better part of a decade — too strange, too speculative, too far outside the comfortable assumption that complexity arose gradually within a single lineage.

What rescued it was not argument but evidence — the same kind of molecular evidence that, in the same years, was letting Carl Woese pull the archaea out of the bacterial drawer. When biologists looked closely at the DNA inside mitochondria and chloroplasts, there it was: organellar genomes, bacterial-style ribosomes, the unmistakable molecular fingerprints of a bacterial past. By the 1980s Margulis's once-ridiculed thesis had become the standard explanation, printed in every textbook, taught to every student. She had been right, and she had been right roughly fifteen rejections too early — which is the ordinary fate of being right before the evidence that proves you arrives.

There is a pattern in these last three chapters worth naming. Woese, ridiculed for the archaea, vindicated by the molecule. Margulis, rejected fifteen times for endosymbiosis, vindicated by the molecule. The molecule keeps turning out to know things that the established picture, drawn from shapes and assumptions, got wrong. The tree of life as we now understand it was redrawn, again and again, by people who learned to read the genes instead of trusting the appearances — and who were dismissed, each time, until the genes spoke loudly enough to be heard.

## Seeing the host

For decades the host cell — the archaeon that did the swallowing — was a figure inferred but never seen. We knew there had to have been one. We could read its signature, faintly, in the archaeal half of our own genes. But no one had ever laid eyes on a cell from the right lineage, because the right lineage was the deep-sea, slow-growing, never-cultured archaea that come up in cores of cold mud and refuse to grow in any laboratory.

The Lokiarchaeota of 2015 was a genome without a face — assembled from sequenced fragments, never seen alive. But the genome was enough to set off a hunt, and within two years the relatives came pouring in: Thorarchaeota, Heimdallarchaeota, Odinarchaeota, more archaea named for the Norse gods, until they were collected into a single great superphylum and given the name Asgard, after the home of those gods. The Asgard archaea, every one of them carrying eukaryote-like genes, every one of them looking more like our deep ancestors than anything else alive.

Then, in 2020, after a cultivation effort that took twelve years — twelve years of coaxing a fragment of deep-sea mud to grow in a bioreactor, feeding it, waiting, the organism doubling perhaps once every couple of weeks — a team led by Hiroyuki Imachi in Japan finally grew one. They published it in *Nature*: the first Asgard archaeon ever cultured, named *Candidatus* Prometheoarchaeum syntrophicum, strain MK-D1. And for the first time, the inferred host had a face.

It was small — a sphere about 550 nanometers across, smaller than many bacteria. It grew agonizingly slowly. It could not live alone: it survived only in partnership with other microbes, breaking down amino acids and handing off the products to a partner in a tight metabolic dependency called syntrophy — feeding-together. And when the team imaged it, they saw the thing that made everyone catch their breath. The cell grew long, branching protrusions — tentacles, arms — reaching out from its body into the space around it, exactly the kind of structure you might imagine a host cell using to embrace, entangle, and eventually engulf a passing bacterium. Later work on other cultured Asgard archaea found, inside such cells, a genuine internal skeleton — actin filaments, the same kind of structural protein your own cells use to change shape and move things around, here named "Lokiactin," running in long networks through a prokaryotic cell where no such thing was thought to exist.

![A microscope-style image of the cultured archaeon *Candidatus* Prometheoarchaeum syntrophicum, strain MK-D1: a small spherical cell about 550 nanometers across, with long, branching tentacle-like protrusions reaching outward into the surrounding space, shown alongside a partner microbe it lives with in syntrophy.](images/07-the-fusion-fig-04.png)

*Figure 7.4 — The host, seen at last. After a twelve-year cultivation effort, the first Asgard archaeon grown in the lab proved to be a tiny, slow-growing sphere that survives only in metabolic partnership with another microbe — and that reaches out with branching arms, the kind of structure a host cell might use to embrace and engulf a passing bacterium.*

The host, it turned out, was not a simple bag waiting to get lucky. It already had reaching arms and an internal skeleton — already had the beginnings of the machinery a cell needs to grab and hold and reorganize. The capacity for complexity that we thought defined eukaryotes ran *deeper* than eukaryotes, down into the archaeal lineage that gave rise to them. The fusion did not happen to a passive cell. It happened to one that was already, in its slow cold way, reaching out.

## Two domains, or three — and what we are

This is where the great unsettled question of the last three chapters comes to its point, and it must be told honestly, because it is not settled.

Carl Woese gave us three domains — Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya — three equal trunks from one root. That is the picture most of us carry. But the endosymbiosis story does not fit three equal trunks, and the newest evidence fits it less and less. In 2023, a team led by Laura Eme and Thijs Ettema published in *Nature* a careful reconstruction, drawn from hundreds of genes across the Asgard lineages, placing the eukaryotes not as a separate trunk at all but as a branch nested deep *inside* the Asgard archaea — specifically, as the closest relative of a group they call the Hodarchaeales, within the Heimdall branch of Asgard. By that reconstruction there are not three domains. There are two — Bacteria and Archaea — and we, the eukaryotes, are a twig on the archaeal branch. A deeply modified twig, carrying a captured bacterium, but a twig on the archaeal branch all the same.

The debate is real and it is alive. Depending on which organisms you include and which genes you analyze, the trees come out two-domain or three-domain, and competent scientists disagree. This book's position — and it is a position, taken in full knowledge of the disagreement — is two domains: that the eukaryotes arose from within the Archaea, as a modified archaeal lineage that swallowed a bacterium. Not a fourth start. Not even a third trunk. A fusion.

Stand back and hold what that means about you.

You are not a single kind of thing. You are the descendant of an ancient archaeon, a cell from the lineage that learned to live in boiling vents and killing brines, a cell that reached out with branching arms in the cold mud of the deep sea. And folded inside you, in every cell of your body, generating the energy of every thought and every heartbeat, is the descendant of a bacterium your ancestor swallowed two billion years ago and never let go. You are a truce between two domains of life. A merger so old and so complete that you cannot feel the seam — but it is there, in every cell, the archaeal city built around the bacterial engine, two ancient and once-separate forms of life that became, against every expectation, a single thing that could grow large enough and complex enough to wonder where it came from.

It came from a collision. There are no new starts in biology, only edits — and the most extraordinary edit of all was the one that took two old cells and made them, permanently, one.
