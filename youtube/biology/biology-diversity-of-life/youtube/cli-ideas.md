## Candidate 01 — "Research the Virus Question: Is It Alive — and What Does That Reveal About Life?"
- Source: biology-diversity-of-life/chapters/00-frontmatter.md (book context: short 4-chapter text on foundational biology + AI tool fluency)
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: A measles virus on a table does nothing. Near a human cell, it takes over and reproduces. Most biologists say it isn't alive — but their reason is subtle and turns on what "life" actually requires. Claude can map the disagreement and show what it teaches about the definition.
- The artifact: A sourced brief covering: (a) the eight properties of life and which ones the measles virus satisfies or fails, (b) the "cellular minimum" criterion and why biologists accept it, (c) the parallel disagreement in origin-of-life research (viruses as ancient vs. recent entities), (d) a comparison matrix: virus vs. viroid vs. prion vs. bacterium vs. mycoplasma on the eight life-properties.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Research whether viruses should be classified as living. Produce: (1) the standard eight properties of life (organization, metabolism, homeostasis, growth, reproduction, sensitivity, evolution, energy use) and assess the measles virus on each; (2) the 'cellular minimum' criterion — why most biologists use it and what it excludes; (3) two opposing positions on virus origin: viruses as ancient (pre-cellular RNA world relics) vs. recent (escaped cellular genes) — evidence for each; (4) a comparison matrix: measles virus, tobacco mosaic virus, viroid, prion, Mycoplasma genitalium, E. coli — scored on each property with citations."`
- Read / check: The comparison matrix should correctly mark viroids as lacking protein entirely (RNA only) and prions as lacking nucleic acid entirely. The "escaped genes" hypothesis should be attributed to the appropriate proponents. The cellular minimum should note that even Mycoplasma (smallest known bacterium, ~500 genes) passes it by having its own ribosomes and metabolism.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Nothing — fully synthetic. The life-properties framework and all cited organisms are documented in textbook and primary literature.
- Output medium: Manim (animated matrix assembling column by column: each entity appears in a row, each life-property lights up green or red as the assessment is made, revealing the pattern — viruses consistently fail "metabolism" and "homeostasis")
- The change: Ask Claude to assess a large DNA virus (mimivirus, with 1.2 million base pairs and genes for DNA repair) on the same matrix — and show whether it changes the "not alive" verdict.
- Teardown angle: The virus question is not definitional pedantry. How you define "alive" determines what you look for when searching for life on other planets. The NASA astrobiology definition ("life is a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution") explicitly includes viruses-in-a-host and excludes them in isolation. The definition is doing real scientific work.
- Exclusions: Prion disease mechanisms, virophage ecology, RNA world chemistry.
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 02 — "Research AI as a Biology Tool: What Can Claude Verify, and What Must You Check?"
- Source: biology-diversity-of-life/chapters/00-frontmatter.md + book premise (AI + biology fluency curriculum)
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: This book was built on the premise that the easy part of intellectual work has become easier and the dangerous part — knowing which parts still require human judgment — is now harder to notice. Claude can research almost any biology question. But it hallucinates. The question is where.
- The artifact: A practical verification brief: (a) three categories of biology claims where Claude is reliable (well-documented mechanisms, quantitative relationships from textbooks, comparative anatomy), (b) three categories where Claude hallucinate or confabulate (recent papers post-2024, specific clinical trial results, contested empirical claims), (c) a verification workflow for each category (what to check, what databases to use), (d) five real examples of biology claims — three correct, two with specific verifiable errors — that Claude might generate, with the specific error identified.
- Prompt seed: `claude "I am building a biology course around AI-assisted research. Research and produce: (1) three categories of biology claims where large language models are generally reliable — explain why; (2) three categories where errors are common — explain the failure mode; (3) a verification workflow for each category (databases, primary sources, specific check); (4) generate five specific biology claims about cell biology, genetics, evolution, ecology, and physiology — then audit each for potential errors, flagging any claim that should be verified before teaching it. Be honest about your own limitations."`
- Read / check: The three reliable categories should include well-replicated mechanisms (DNA replication, citric acid cycle, Mendelian genetics) with the reasoning that these appear in thousands of training documents. The three unreliable categories should include: recent literature (post-training-cutoff), specific clinical data (trial numbers, survival statistics), and contested empirical claims (where the LLM may smooth over scientific disagreement). Each of the five generated claims should be independently verifiable.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Nothing — fully synthetic. The exercise is metacognitive: Claude auditing its own reliability. The value is in the explicit articulation of failure modes, which students can then apply.
- Output medium: slate (research brief formatted as a teacher's verification guide, with the five biology claims as a fact-check exercise slate the human can show students)
- The change: Ask Claude to generate one deliberately false biology claim that sounds plausible — explain why it sounds plausible, what makes it wrong, and what a student would need to look up to catch it.
- Teardown angle: Speed without judgment accelerates error. The AI tool is most dangerous not when it's obviously wrong but when it's confidently and plausibly wrong about a detail that students have no prior knowledge to catch. The verification workflow is the curriculum.
- Exclusions: Prompt engineering techniques, LLM technical architecture, AI ethics debates.
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 03 — "Research Organization and Emergence: What Does a Cell Have That Its Parts Don't?"
- Source: biology-diversity-of-life/chapters/00-frontmatter.md and context (book covers eight properties of life)
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: A cell can respond to its environment, regulate its temperature, and reproduce. None of its individual molecules can. The property appears at the cell level and not below. This is emergence — and biology is full of it at every scale.
- The artifact: A sourced brief: (a) emergence defined — a property appearing at one level of organization that is absent at the level below, with three non-biological examples for clarity, (b) four biological emergence examples across scales (cell physiology from molecular interactions, tissue behavior from cell interactions, population dynamics from individual behaviors, ecosystem function from species interactions), (c) why emergence is not mysticism — reductionism still works, you just have to look at the right level, (d) AI as an emergent system — does the framing apply?
- Prompt seed: `claude "Research the concept of emergence as it applies to biological organization. Produce: (1) a clear definition of emergence with three non-biological examples (ant colony behavior, water properties from H2O, market prices from individual trades); (2) four biological emergence examples at different scales: cell function from molecular interactions, tissue behavior from cell signaling, flocking from individual bird rules, nutrient cycling from species interactions; (3) why emergence does not require vitalism or mysticism — how reductionist analysis works at each level even though the property only appears at the higher level; (4) whether large language model behavior (fluency from next-token prediction) is a useful analogy. Cite primary literature for the biological examples."`
- Read / check: The flocking example should cite Reynolds 1987 (the Boids simulation) or empirical work on starling murmurations (Cavagna et al. 2010 PNAS). The tissue behavior example should include gap junctions or cell-cell signaling documented in primary literature. The AI analogy section should be analytically careful — not use "emergence" as a free pass for explaining LLM capabilities.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Nothing — fully synthetic. All cited examples are published.
- Output medium: Manim (a four-level hierarchy animating: molecule → cell → tissue → organism, with each level's emergent property appearing as a glow or label when the level is assembled, showing what is absent at the level below)
- The change: Ask Claude to find a case where emergence failed or broke down unexpectedly — where the whole turned out to be less than the sum of its parts (e.g., a disrupted ecosystem that lost function after a keystone species removal, despite all other species persisting).
- Teardown angle: Emergence is not magic — it is the consequence of specific interactions at the level below. Knowing it is emergent doesn't explain it; it just tells you which level to look at. The cell is not mysterious because it has properties its molecules don't. The mystery is why those specific molecular interactions produce those specific cellular properties.
- Exclusions: Consciousness and the "hard problem," quantum biology, systems biology modeling in depth.
- Score: 7/10

## Candidate 04 — "Research Entropy and the Cell: How Does Life Stay Ordered Without Breaking the Second Law?"
- Source: biology-diversity-of-life/chapters/00-frontmatter.md and book context
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: The second law of thermodynamics says disorder increases. A living cell is the most organized object in the known universe per unit volume. These facts are not contradictory — but the resolution requires understanding what "closed system" means, and most people never learn it.
- The artifact: A sourced brief: (a) the second law stated precisely — disorder increases in a closed system, (b) why a cell is an open system and how that resolves the apparent contradiction, (c) the actual entropy accounting — how cells export disorder through heat and waste products while maintaining local order, (d) two quantitative examples of the energy cost of cellular order maintenance (Na/K pump, protein folding quality control), (e) the Erwin Schrödinger "What Is Life?" argument and whether it holds up.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Research the thermodynamics of living cells and the second law of thermodynamics. Produce: (1) the second law stated precisely — disorder increases in isolated/closed systems, not in open systems; (2) the cell as an open system — what enters (free energy from food) and exits (heat, waste, entropy); (3) two quantitative examples: the ATP cost of Na/K pump operation per cell per day in a human; the energy cost of protein quality control (proteasome) as a fraction of cellular ATP budget; (4) Schrödinger's 'What Is Life?' (1944) argument that life feeds on negative entropy — how it holds up in modern terms; (5) a clear statement of where the 'life violates thermodynamics' misconception breaks down."`
- Read / check: The Na/K pump energy cost should cite published estimates (~20-30% of basal metabolic rate for brain neurons). Schrödinger's book should be cited (Cambridge University Press, 1944) with the "negentropy" argument. The resolution of the misconception must clearly state: entropy decreases locally in the cell; entropy increases globally in the universe, more than compensating. This is the second law satisfied.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Nothing — fully synthetic. All cited quantities are in published literature.
- Output medium: Manim (animated thermodynamic diagram: closed-system box with entropy inevitably rising vs. open-system cell with food energy flowing in and heat/entropy flowing out; the local entropy shown declining while a "universe entropy" counter rises faster)
- The change: Ask Claude to compute the approximate total entropy production of a human per day (from basal metabolic rate and body temperature) and compare it to the entropy associated with the ordered structures that a human maintains. The ratio is the real answer to "how much does it cost to be alive?"
- Teardown angle: Life doesn't cheat entropy — it outsources it. The price of cellular order is making the surroundings more disordered. You are, thermodynamically, a disorder engine that keeps its interior tidy by making the rest of the universe messier. The second law is satisfied every second you're alive.
- Exclusions: Non-equilibrium thermodynamics in detail, Maxwell's demon, Boltzmann statistics.
- Score: 7/10

## Candidate 05 — "Research Reproduction: Why Do Some Organisms Reproduce Once and Die?"
- Source: biology-diversity-of-life context (covers properties of life including reproduction strategies)
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: Pacific salmon swim hundreds of miles upstream, spawn with the entire reserves of their bodies, and die within days. Bamboo grows for a century and then flowers simultaneously across an entire forest and dies. Semelparity — one catastrophic reproductive event — sounds like a fitness disaster. It's actually evolutionarily optimal under specific conditions. Claude can show the math.
- The artifact: A sourced brief: (a) semelparity vs. iteroparity defined with the trade-off logic, (b) the mathematical condition under which semelparity is favored (when adult survival probability is low, or when a single reproductive bout can dramatically outcompete iteroparity), (c) four documented examples across taxa (Pacific salmon, bamboo, periodic cicadas, mayflies), (d) the specific ecological pressure driving semelparity in each, (e) one exception — an organism that switched from iteroparity to semelparity or vice versa under experimental conditions.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Research the evolution of semelparity (big-bang reproduction) vs. iteroparity (repeated reproduction). Produce: (1) the life-history trade-off framework — what energy spent on reproduction costs in terms of survival; (2) the mathematical condition for semelparity to be optimal — when r_semelparous > r_iteroparous (Cole's paradox and its resolution); (3) four documented examples: Pacific salmon, bamboo, periodic cicadas, and mayflies — with the specific ecological pressure driving semelparity in each; (4) one experimental or evolutionary case where the strategy shifted; (5) the salmon's post-spawning hormone surge (cortisol, sex steroids) as the proximate mechanism of senescence. Cite primary literature."`
- Read / check: Cole's paradox (1954 Ecology) should be cited. The resolution (by Charnov & Schaffer 1973 or equivalent) should be explained correctly — Cole incorrectly concluded semelparity is almost always favored; the resolution adds adult survival back correctly. The salmon cortisol surge should be cited to Robertson et al. or Carruth et al.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Nothing — fully synthetic. All cited mechanisms are in primary literature.
- Output medium: Manim (animated life-history diagram: bar charts of reproductive output per year for semelparous vs. iteroparous organisms at different adult survival probabilities, showing the crossover point where semelparity wins; salmon spawning-run timeline overlay)
- The change: Ask Claude to find an organism where semelparity evolved independently multiple times within the same genus or family — and explain what that says about the predictability of life-history evolution.
- Teardown angle: Salmon don't die after spawning because they've "given everything" in a poetic sense. They die because selection favored the allocation of every last physiological resource to the offspring over adult survival — the math works out that way when you will likely never survive a second spawning season anyway. The death is as selected for as the reproduction.
- Exclusions: Senescence and aging theory in detail, r/K selection (oversimplified), kin selection and cooperative breeding.
- Score: 7/10
