It is 2:14 a.m. in a small university hospital. A 19-year-old comes in with her roommate's insistence, harder to rouse than when they left the dorm, neck stiff, photophobic, a scatter of small dark non-blanching spots on her shins.

The resident does not need a test. Fever, stiff neck, petechial rash, college student — that is meningococcemia until the culture says otherwise. Blood cultures. Lumbar puncture. Broad-spectrum antibiotics within the hour. The lab runs the cerebrospinal fluid to the bench. Twenty minutes later the Gram stain comes back: Gram-negative diplococci, intracellular, inside neutrophils.

Ceftriaxone goes in within fifty minutes of arrival. Dexamethasone before the antibiotic. The patient survives with her fingers and toes. She loses two months of college.

That patient is alive because someone read a slide. The slide was useful because of a workflow built out of every technique this book has described. The antibiotic worked because of a chain of decisions reaching back through Pasteur and Koch and Domagk — and because resistance pressure had already moved the empirical regimen from penicillin to ceftriaxone, which is why the chapter ends with the three forces that are now reshaping the field your career will inhabit.

But first: what happens when a pathogen reaches the bloodstream, the cerebrospinal fluid, or the heart valve — the body's most consequential real estate.

## When microbes reach the bloodstream

The circulatory system moves five liters of blood through every organ once per minute. Neither it nor the lymphatic system running parallel to it has a normal microbiota. Transient bacteremia from brushing your teeth is real and is cleared within minutes by complement, the spleen, and the liver's Kupffer cells. Sustained bacteremia is always a problem.

The terminology has been refined. The current standard is Sepsis-3, published in *JAMA* in 2016.

**Bacteremia** is bacteria in the blood, demonstrated by a positive culture. It is a finding, not a syndrome.

**Sepsis** is life-threatening organ dysfunction from a dysregulated host response to infection. Note what is in that definition and what is not. The bacteria do not have to be in the blood — the infection can be anywhere, a pneumonia, a urinary tract infection, an abscess. What makes it sepsis is that the host's response has started damaging its own organs. The quick clinical screen is **qSOFA**: respiratory rate ≥ 22, altered mental status, systolic blood pressure ≤ 100. Two points out of three in a patient with suspected infection means think sepsis.

The older SIRS criteria — temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, white cell count — have been retired from the definition because they were too nonspecific. A college student with a hangover meets SIRS criteria. The new definition is sharper: the test is organ damage, not inflammation.

**Septic shock** is a subset of sepsis: persistent hypotension requiring vasopressors plus serum lactate above 2 mmol/L despite adequate fluid resuscitation. Mortality runs 30–40 percent. [^1] The lactate is doing specific work in this definition — it is the cellular signal that tissues are not getting enough oxygen, which is the proximate cause of death.

### The 1-hour bundle and the math behind it

The Surviving Sepsis Campaign's 2018 update distilled the treatment into a 1-hour bundle: measure lactate, get two sets of blood cultures from separate sites before antibiotics if possible, administer broad-spectrum antibiotics, begin 30 mL/kg crystalloid for hypotension or lactate ≥ 4, apply vasopressors if hypotension persists.

Why an hour? Because Kumar and colleagues published a retrospective study of 2,154 patients with septic shock in *Critical Care Medicine* in 2006 and found that for every hour of delay in effective antibiotic administration from the onset of hypotension, in-hospital mortality increased by approximately 7.6 percent. [^2] The first hour after hypotension is the highest-value clinical hour in all of medicine. After six hours of delay, mortality climbs above 50 percent in that dataset.

Run the calculation. If your baseline mortality for septic shock is 30 percent and you delay antibiotics by three hours, your expected mortality is on the order of 30 + (3 × 7) = 51 percent. Twenty-one extra deaths per hundred patients. From a three-hour wait.

That is what the bundle is doing. It is the arithmetic of source control plus early antibiotics plus supportive care translated into a clinical order set.

### Endocarditis — bacteremia with a home

Endocarditis is bloodstream infection that has found a surface to adhere to. A heart valve damaged by rheumatic disease, a congenital anomaly, or prosthetic material provides that surface. Bacteria grow a vegetation — a biofilm of organisms, fibrin, and platelets — that is hard to penetrate with antibiotics and impossible for the immune system to clear through normal phagocytosis. The result is sustained bacteremia from a protected nidus.

Two patterns: acute, on previously normal valves, most often *Staphylococcus aureus*, common in people who inject drugs, moves fast; and subacute, on damaged or prosthetic valves, most often the viridans streptococci — *S. mutans*, *S. sanguinis*, *S. mitis* — the oral flora seeded during dental procedures, moves slowly over weeks.

The peripheral signs — Janeway lesions on the palms, Osler nodes on the fingers, splinter hemorrhages, Roth spots on the retina — are embolic phenomena: bits of vegetation breaking off into the arterial circulation. They are pathognomonic when present and absent in most patients. Diagnosis runs on the Duke criteria: multiple blood culture sets (to distinguish continuous bacteremia from contamination) plus echocardiography to visualize the vegetation. Treatment is four to six weeks of IV antibiotics, sometimes combined with valve surgery.

### Malaria — the parasite that shaped human genetics

*Plasmodium falciparum* is the most dangerous of the five *Plasmodium* species infecting humans, the most common cause of malaria deaths, and the reason certain hemoglobin variants (sickle trait, G6PD deficiency, thalassemia minor) have been positively selected in sub-Saharan Africa over millennia — they confer partial protection against the parasite, and so heterozygote carriers survived better in high-transmission regions.

The life cycle, briefly: the female *Anopheles* mosquito injects sporozoites during a blood meal → sporozoites infect hepatocytes → merozoites emerge → red blood cells are invaded → schizonts form → more merozoites burst out in synchronous waves. The synchronous RBC rupture is what produces the cyclical fevers: every 48 hours for *P. vivax* and *P. ovale*, every 72 for *P. malariae*. *P. falciparum* is less synchronized but more dangerous — it sequesters in cerebral capillaries (cerebral malaria), causes severe anemia, and triggers multi-organ failure.

![Plasmodium life cycle ](images/14-infections-systemic-capstone-fig-03.png)
*Figure 14.3 — Plasmodium life cycle *

The global burden is enormous: roughly 250 million cases and 600,000 deaths per year, mostly children under five in sub-Saharan Africa. [^3] Treatment is artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT). Artemisinin resistance has emerged in Southeast Asia and is being tracked in Africa. Two new vaccines — RTS,S/AS01 and R21/Matrix-M — are partially effective and being rolled out in high-transmission regions. None of this is the end of malaria as a public health problem. The combination of better vaccines, combination therapies, and surveillance is what might eventually make it manageable.

## When microbes reach the central nervous system

The brain is the most carefully defended organ in the body. Three layers of meninges, bone, and a blood-brain barrier of tight-junction endothelium plus astrocyte foot processes, all engineered to isolate the brain from the body's biochemical noise. Cerebrospinal fluid is normally sterile, a few cells, 150 mL circulating through the ventricles and subarachnoid space.

When these defenses fail, you have hours — not days.

Normal CSF has 0–5 lymphocytes, glucose about two-thirds of serum glucose, protein under 45 mg/dL, and is clear. Bacterial meningitis: cloudy (the cloudiness is neutrophils), white cell count in the thousands with PMN predominance, glucose low (the bacteria are eating it), protein very high (the inflamed blood-brain barrier is leaking), and often a positive Gram stain. Viral meningitis: clear CSF, lymphocytic pleocytosis of 10–500, normal glucose, modestly elevated protein. Fungal and tuberculous meningitis: lymphocytic, low glucose, high protein — mimics viral but with slower onset and the patient looking worse over time.

The patterns are useful but not absolute. Early bacterial meningitis can show lymphocytes. Partially treated bacterial meningitis looks aseptic. The Gram stain plus culture plus PCR resolve the rest.

### Bacterial meningitis

Without treatment, mortality approaches 100 percent. With treatment, 10–25 percent, depending on age, organism, and time to first antibiotic. The same per-hour mortality math applies here as in sepsis — perhaps more acutely.

The pathogen distribution follows age. Neonates: group B *Streptococcus*, *E. coli*, *Listeria monocytogenes*. Infants and children: *S. pneumoniae*, *N. meningitidis*, *Haemophilus influenzae* type b — now rare thanks to the Hib conjugate vaccine; its virtual disappearance from the pediatric differential is one of the most dramatic demonstrations of vaccine impact in the past 40 years. Adolescents and young adults: *N. meningitidis* (dormitories, barracks), *S. pneumoniae*. Older adults: *S. pneumoniae*, *L. monocytogenes*, aerobic Gram-negative bacilli.

The clinical triad is fever, neck stiffness, and altered mental status. Add headache, photophobia, vomiting, the petechial rash when *Neisseria* is the cause. Brudzinski and Kernig signs are classic but have limited sensitivity. The decision tree, compressed: febrile patient with stiff neck and altered mental status → blood cultures → empirical antibiotics within an hour → lumbar puncture → CSF guides the next step. Empirical regimen for an immunocompetent adult: vancomycin plus ceftriaxone, add ampicillin for *Listeria* coverage in neonates and adults over 50. Dexamethasone before or with the first antibiotic dose reduces sensorineural hearing loss in pneumococcal meningitis.

### Viral meningitis and encephalitis

Viral meningitis is more common than bacterial and less severe. Enteroviruses cause most cases, with summer and fall seasonality. Treatment is supportive. Acyclovir for HSV.

Encephalitis — inflammation of the brain parenchyma — is a different beast. It produces altered consciousness, seizures, focal deficits. The most common cause of sporadic encephalitis in the United States is **herpes simplex virus 1**, with characteristic temporal lobe involvement on MRI. Without treatment, mortality is around 70 percent. With early acyclovir, it drops to about 30 percent — but survivors often have significant neurological sequelae. Any patient with encephalopathy, seizures, or focal deficits gets empirical acyclovir while the workup proceeds, because the cost of missing HSV encephalitis is catastrophic.

Arboviruses cause seasonal encephalitis: West Nile virus (mosquito-borne flavivirus, common in US summers), Eastern equine encephalitis (rare but severe), Japanese encephalitis (the leading cause of vaccine-preventable encephalitis in Asia). Diagnosis is PCR on CSF plus serology.

### Prion diseases

These are a category unto themselves. No nucleic acid. The infectious agent is a misfolded conformation of a host protein — the prion protein PrP, encoded by the PRNP gene — that recruits normally folded PrP into the misfolded state by contact. The result is spongiform encephalopathy: holes in the brain at autopsy, rapidly progressive dementia and movement disorder during life, invariably fatal. Nothing in the standard killing arsenal applies: no nucleic acid to target with radiation, no metabolic machinery to hit with antibiotics. The protein is the pathogen.

Forms: sporadic CJD (about 1 per million per year, cause unknown), familial CJD and fatal familial insomnia (PRNP mutations), iatrogenic CJD (contaminated neurosurgical instruments, dura mater grafts, cadaver growth hormone), variant CJD (acquired from BSE-infected cattle — about 230 cases worldwide), and kuru (ritual cannibalism among the Fore people of Papua New Guinea; Gajdusek's discovery that it was transmissible earned the 1976 Nobel Prize). No treatment alters the course. RT-QuIC assay on CSF or nasal brushings has transformed diagnosis; the clinical picture and prion-specific tests are now sufficient for diagnosis in most cases without brain biopsy.

## The laboratory underneath every clinical decision

Every chapter of this book has implicitly trusted that someone can determine which microbe is in a patient sample. The clinical microbiology laboratory is where that trust is justified or broken. The basic toolkit is small. The trade-offs are sharp.

**Culture** is the gold standard for most bacterial infections. Sample onto agar, incubate, grow visible colonies in 12–48 hours, subculture, run biochemical testing and antibiotic sensitivity testing. High specificity — a pure isolate is the organism. The antibiogram tells you exactly which drugs work. Cost is low. Most clinical microbiology labs run on culture.

The limits: speed. Forty-eight hours is a long time in septic shock. *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* takes weeks on standard media. Some organisms cannot be cultured on standard media at all — *Treponema pallidum*, *Mycobacterium leprae*, *Chlamydia* species without specialized systems. Antibiotics drawn before the culture can suppress growth and produce false negatives, which is why the bundle says "cultures before antibiotics if possible" — not cultures instead of antibiotics.

**Gram stain** is the first orientation — twenty minutes from sample to result. The stain tells you cell-wall architecture and morphology: Gram-positive cocci in clusters (*Staphylococcus*), Gram-positive cocci in chains (*Streptococcus*, *Enterococcus*), Gram-negative diplococci in CSF intracellular (*N. meningitidis*), Gram-negative rods in urine (*E. coli* until proven otherwise). Sensitivity is moderate — the CSF Gram stain catches roughly 60–90 percent of bacterial meningitis cases; [^4] the rest require culture or PCR. The Gram stain is the cheapest and fastest meaningful diagnostic test in microbiology.

**Serology** asks the host's immune system rather than the organism itself. Two formats: antibody detection (ELISA for patient antibodies against a pathogen antigen — HIV, hepatitis B and C, syphilis, Lyme disease) and antigen detection (sandwich ELISA for pathogen molecules — the cryptococcal antigen test on CSF, the influenza rapid test, the COVID-19 antigen test). The strengths: speed, high throughput, ability to diagnose infections where culture is impossible or slow. The limits: the window period between infection and seroconversion (anti-HIV antibodies take two to six weeks), cross-reactivity, and the IgM/IgG interpretation challenge (IgM suggests recent infection; IgG suggests past or ongoing).

**PCR** amplifies specific pathogen DNA or RNA sequences exponentially. A single copy in a sample can be detected within hours. Real-time PCR quantifies as well as detects. The strengths: speed (hours, not days), exquisite sensitivity, ability to detect organisms that cannot be cultured (HIV and CMV viral loads, *M. tuberculosis* by GeneXpert, hepatitis C RNA). Multiplex panels test for dozens of pathogens from a single swab — respiratory pathogen panels can identify influenza A and B, RSV, parainfluenza, adenovirus, rhinovirus, and several bacteria simultaneously. The limits: PCR detects nucleic acid, not viability. A patient who cleared an infection may test positive for residual DNA. Contamination can produce false positives. And PCR requires knowing which pathogen to look for — it does not survey the unknown.

**Next-generation sequencing** can sequence everything in a sample. Metagenomic sequencing on CSF or plasma can identify pathogens no targeted test would have caught. The clinical use is expanding. The barriers are cost (around $1,000 per sample), turnaround time (24–48 hours, falling), and interpretation: NGS finds many organisms; deciding which one is causing the disease requires clinical judgment that no algorithm fully supplies. Within a decade, NGS may replace blood culture as the primary diagnostic method for bloodstream infections. The evidence that it changes outcomes is not yet in.

## The three forces shaping the next half-century

Three things are reshaping clinical microbiology in ways that will define your career, whether or not you ever take another microbiology course. They run through every chapter of this book. None of them is resolved.

### Antimicrobial resistance

The first golden age of antibiotics ran from sulfonamides in the 1930s through the development of the major drug classes by the 1980s. We are now in a long pause. New antibiotic classes are scarce. Resistance has spread.

The most consequential resistant pathogens have an acronym — **ESKAPE** — named by Brad Spellberg and the IDSA: vancomycin-resistant *Enterococcus faecium*, methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus*, carbapenem-resistant *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, multidrug-resistant *Acinetobacter baumannii*, multidrug-resistant *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, extended-spectrum beta-lactamase-producing *Enterobacter* species. These organisms are the principal causes of healthcare-associated infections that no longer respond to first-line therapy. To treat them, clinicians reach for older drugs (colistin, polymyxin B) with significant toxicity, or newer expensive agents with their own resistance trajectories.

The 2022 GBD AMR collaborators estimated approximately 1.27 million deaths directly attributable to bacterial antimicrobial resistance worldwide in 2019 — and surveillance is incomplete, so this is probably an underestimate. [^5]

Why has the pipeline emptied? An antibiotic is used for one to two weeks, often becomes resistant within a few years of approval, and competes with cheap generics. The return on investment is poor compared to drugs for chronic conditions. The science is hard: easy targets have been hit. The regulatory environment is difficult: clinical trials against resistant organisms require small, heterogeneous patient populations. The result is a market failure at precisely the moment the biology demands investment.

What is trying to change it: bacteriophage therapy (a handful of compassionate use cases have shown striking results where everything else failed), antimicrobial peptides engineered from host defense molecules, AI-assisted drug discovery (Stokes et al. in *Cell* in 2020 used a graph neural network to identify halicin, a novel antibiotic with a new mechanism), and economic interventions — the CARB-X consortium, the US GAIN Act, the AMR Action Fund — trying to repair the market failure.

We are not in a post-antibiotic era yet. We are in the era of needing to act so we never reach one.

### The microbiome as a new framework

Until roughly 2005, the microbiome was a curiosity. The Human Microbiome Project (NIH, 2007–2016) changed that. Sender, Fuchs, and Milo recalibrated the numbers in *Cell* in 2016: a 70 kg adult carries approximately 3.8 × 10¹³ microbial cells, mostly in the colon — a ratio to human cells of roughly 1:1, not the 10:1 that textbooks had stated for decades. [^6] The microbiome contributes roughly 100-fold more genes than the human genome.

What this population does: digests complex carbohydrates the human intestinal enzymes cannot handle; produces short-chain fatty acids that feed the colon epithelium; educates the developing immune system (germ-free mice have small, dysfunctional immune systems); excludes pathogens through colonization resistance; metabolizes drugs and xenobiotics. *C. difficile* infection, as discussed in several previous chapters, is largely the story of antibiotic disruption of this exclusion.

The clinical implications are still being worked out. Dysbiosis — disruption of normal microbial community structure — is associated with inflammatory bowel disease, type 2 diabetes, obesity, allergic disease, and a spectrum of other conditions. Many associations are not yet causal. The intervention that has most convincingly demonstrated causality is **fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT)** for recurrent *C. difficile*: cure rates above 85 percent, through restoration of colonization resistance by reintroducing a competent community. For everything else FMT has been tried for — ulcerative colitis, metabolic syndrome, depression — results are more variable, mechanisms are less clear, and the field is still sorting out which associations will become treatments.

The microbiome is the new framework not because it has solved diseases but because it has forced a reframing. The human body is not a sterile chassis interrupted by pathogens. It is an ecosystem, and most of this book has been about what happens when one organism's growth in that ecosystem exceeds the body's tolerance.

### Emerging infectious diseases and the pipeline of future challenges

Most novel human pathogens come from animals. Jones and colleagues analyzed 335 emerging infectious disease events from 1940 to 2004 in *Nature* in 2008 and found roughly 60 percent were zoonotic — originating in animals. Of those, 71 percent originated in wildlife. The pace of emergence is increasing. [^7]

The mechanism is spillover: a pathogen circulating in an animal reservoir crosses into humans at an interface where the species mix — a farm, a market, a forest edge, a slaughterhouse. Most spillovers fail. Some succeed. HIV from chimpanzees. SARS-CoV-1 from bats via civets. MERS from bats via camels. Ebola from bats. Nipah from bats via pigs. SARS-CoV-2 from bats, probably via an intermediate host, late 2019.

The forces driving increasing spillover are not mysterious: land-use change pushing humans into wildlife habitats; climate change shifting vector ranges (*Aedes aegypti* is now established further north than fifty years ago, tick seasons are longer); industrial animal agriculture concentrating susceptible hosts; global travel moving a spillover from a remote village to an international hub in 24 hours.

What you will see in your career: more spillover events, faster pathogen identification (metagenomic sequencing of unusual clusters is now standard at major reference centers), increasingly rapid vaccine development (the mRNA platforms can in principle move from sequence to clinical trial in weeks — a capability validated during COVID-19), and sustained political conflict about every step of that process. Public health is partly a science and partly a society's collective decision-making capacity. The science has been advancing faster than the decisions.

### One Health

The three forces above share a feature: they cannot be contained within any single discipline. Antimicrobial use in agriculture — about two-thirds of global antibiotic consumption — selects for resistant organisms that find their way into human infections. Habitat destruction increases spillover risk. Climate change shifts vector ranges. Human and animal microbiomes are connected through diet and contact.

**One Health** is the framework that names this. The term was formalized in the 2000s and adopted by WHO, WOAH, and FAO as a tripartite collaboration: human health, animal health, and environmental health are interconnected, and surveillance and intervention must integrate across all three. It is not a slogan that solves anything by itself. It is the recognition that the boundaries we drew around medicine, veterinary medicine, and ecology in the nineteenth century were administrative conveniences, not features of biology. The microbes do not respect them. The resistance genes do not respect them. The viruses carrying novel surface proteins do not respect them.

## Three misconceptions worth dismantling

**"Blood culture positivity defines sepsis."** No. Sepsis is defined by organ dysfunction in response to suspected infection. About half of sepsis cases are culture-negative — the infection may be localized, the cultures may have been drawn after antibiotics, or the organism may grow poorly in standard media. Sepsis is a clinical syndrome. Bacteremia is a laboratory finding.

**"Antibiotic-resistant infections are a future problem."** Already a current one. The 2022 GBD study estimated 1.27 million deaths directly attributable to bacterial AMR in 2019. Both the numerator and the denominator of that estimate involve significant uncertainty — but the direction is clear.

**"A negative rapid antigen test means you are not contagious."** Rapid antigen tests have moderate sensitivity. A negative test in someone with symptoms or a known exposure should be repeated; a single negative does not rule out infection. The tests are most reliable when positive and least reliable when used to exclude infection in someone who already has clinical reasons to suspect it.

## What the book has built

We started in a textile shop in Delft. A linen draper ground a glass bead, looked at rainwater, and saw something move. Two centuries of argument — about whether microbes existed, whether they caused disease, whether they could be cultured, whether they could be killed — had to happen before the question in this chapter (which antibiotic, in what dose, within what window) could even be asked.

Three hundred and fifty years later, you can read a Gram stain, interpret a CSF profile, apply a sepsis bundle, explain why next-generation sequencing might replace blood culture within a decade, and articulate why antimicrobial resistance and emerging pathogens and the microbiome are not three separate problems but three angles on the same problem: the invisible world is still there, it is still evolving, and the tools for seeing it are still being built.

The pathogen names will fade. The syndromes will be reorganized by the next textbook. The method — state the question, demonstrate the evidence, work the mechanism, hand the judgment back to the reader — will not fade. The framework Pasteur and Koch built is still the right one. The problems it needs to solve are just more interesting now.

## What would change my mind

If a randomized controlled trial showed that AI-guided early sepsis recognition reduced mortality more than the current Surviving Sepsis bundle plus clinician judgment, I would revise the claim that the first sixty minutes are still made by a human reading a constellation rather than a number.

If metagenomic NGS is shown in a well-controlled trial to reduce mortality in suspected sepsis compared to blood culture plus empirical broad-spectrum antibiotics, blood culture is no longer the gold standard. The technology already detects organisms that culture misses. The clinical evidence that the additional information changes outcomes is not yet in.

## Still puzzling

I still cannot predict which patient with a positive blood culture will progress to septic shock and which will clear the infection on antibiotics alone. The pathogen factors and host factors contribute, but predicting in advance who will progress remains imprecise — which is why the bundle is a population-level intervention applied to individuals whose individual risks we cannot reliably estimate.

I do not understand why fecal microbiota transplantation works so reliably for recurrent *C. difficile* and so unreliably for everything else it has been tried for. The microbiome is a complex ecosystem we have learned to perturb without learning to predict the perturbations.

And I do not yet know whether artemisinin resistance in *P. falciparum* will become the next chloroquine failure — a single point of failure in malaria control — or whether combination therapies plus new vaccines will hold the line long enough for the next generation of antimalarials.
