# Chapter 23 — Urogenital System Infections

*The urinary tract is supposed to be sterile. The reproductive tract is supposed to have an active microbiome. Most urogenital infections are about that distinction breaking down.*

## The question before the answer

A healthy bladder is, classically, sterile. Urine is filtered out of blood, accumulates in the bladder, and exits the body through the urethra. Bacteria entering this system meet several defenses: the flushing action of urination, antimicrobial peptides in urinary tract epithelium, the acidic and high-urea environment of urine, and (in women) the resident microbiota of the vagina that competes with potential pathogens.

A healthy vagina, by contrast, has an active microbiota — dominated by *Lactobacillus* species that produce lactic acid and hydrogen peroxide, keeping the pH around 4.5 and inhibiting most other organisms. This is colonization resistance at its most successful: the lactobacilli outcompete potential pathogens for niche space.

When the balance fails — bacteria ascend into the bladder, or the vaginal community shifts away from lactobacilli, or sexually transmitted pathogens arrive — you get urogenital infection. The point of this chapter is to map those failures: the urinary tract infections from gut bacteria, the bacterial vaginosis from microbiota disruption, the STIs from external transmission.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

1. Describe the anatomy and normal microbiota of the male and female urogenital systems.
2. Identify the most common bacterial pathogens causing UTIs and predict appropriate empirical therapy.
3. Distinguish bacterial vaginosis from candidiasis and trichomoniasis by clinical presentation and laboratory findings.
4. Identify the major bacterial and viral STIs and describe their characteristic clinical pictures.
5. Recognize the stages of syphilis and their respective serologic patterns.

## Anatomy and microbiomes

**Female urogenital tract**: kidneys → ureters → bladder → urethra (short, 3–4 cm) → vagina → cervix → uterus → fallopian tubes. The short urethra and proximity to the anus make ascending UTIs much more common in women than in men.

**Male urogenital tract**: kidneys → ureters → bladder → urethra (longer, 15–20 cm) → external opening. The longer urethra is a partial barrier. Prostate and seminal vesicles connect to the urethra.

**Normal vaginal microbiota** (premenopausal): dominated by *Lactobacillus* species (*L. crispatus*, *L. iners*, *L. gasseri*, *L. jensenii*). Lactobacilli ferment glycogen (produced by vaginal epithelium under estrogen) to lactic acid, maintaining pH around 4.5. The acidic environment inhibits most pathogens. Some women have non-lactobacillus-dominated vaginal communities with similar clinical health.

**Normal urinary microbiota**: long considered absent, but high-sensitivity sequencing now shows a low-abundance microbial community in healthy bladders. The clinical significance is still being worked out.

## Bacterial urinary tract infections

UTIs are among the most common bacterial infections, affecting more than half of women at least once in their lives.

The major bacterial cause is **uropathogenic *Escherichia coli* (UPEC)** — strains carrying virulence factors that allow them to colonize the bladder. UPEC accounts for 70–90% of community-acquired UTIs. The key virulence factor is **P fimbriae** (or **type 1 fimbriae**), which bind specific receptors on bladder epithelium and prevent washout by urine flow.

Other common uropathogens:

- **Staphylococcus saprophyticus**: second most common cause of UTI in sexually active young women.
- **Klebsiella pneumoniae**: hospital-acquired and community UTIs.
- **Proteus mirabilis**: produces urease, splitting urea to ammonia and raising urine pH. The alkaline urine favors calcium phosphate precipitation, contributing to struvite kidney stones.
- **Pseudomonas aeruginosa**: typically hospital-acquired or in immunocompromised patients.
- **Enterococcus** species: increasingly common, often nosocomial.
- **Candida**: catheter-associated, in immunocompromised or diabetic patients.

### Clinical syndromes

**Asymptomatic bacteriuria**: bacteria in urine without symptoms. Common in older adults; usually does not require treatment except in specific populations (pregnant women, before urologic surgery).

**Cystitis**: bladder infection. Frequency, urgency, dysuria (painful urination), suprapubic discomfort. Urinalysis shows pyuria (white cells in urine). Urine culture grows the offending organism. Treat with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, nitrofurantoin, fosfomycin (single dose), or a fluoroquinolone for uncomplicated cases. Cipher's resistance is increasing, particularly to TMP-SMX.

**Pyelonephritis**: kidney infection. Flank pain, fever, nausea, vomiting in addition to UTI symptoms. More serious. Requires longer treatment course (7–14 days) and often hospitalization.

**Catheter-associated UTI**: nearly inevitable in patients with long-term indwelling urinary catheters, because biofilms form on the catheter surface (Chapter 9). Often polymicrobial. The biofilm reservoir continuously seeds urine with bacteria.

### Diagnosis

Urinalysis: leukocyte esterase positive (indicates pyuria), nitrite positive (indicates bacterial conversion of urinary nitrate to nitrite — mostly Gram-negative bacteria). Microscopy shows white cells and bacteria.

Urine culture: standard threshold for clinically significant UTI is ≥10⁵ CFU/mL of a single pathogen. Lower counts can be significant in symptomatic patients or with certain organisms.

### Leptospirosis

*Leptospira* species, spirochetes acquired from water contaminated by animal urine (especially rats). Initial influenza-like illness can progress to severe disease with kidney failure, liver involvement, hemorrhage (Weil disease). Treat with doxycycline or penicillin.

## Bacterial reproductive tract infections (non-STI)

### Bacterial vaginosis (BV)

The most common cause of vaginal symptoms in women of reproductive age. Not a true infection — it is a shift in vaginal microbiota from *Lactobacillus*-dominant to a mixed community of *Gardnerella vaginalis*, *Prevotella*, *Mobiluncus*, *Atopobium*, and other anaerobes. The vaginal pH rises above 4.5.

Clinical features: thin, white-gray vaginal discharge with a fishy odor (especially after intercourse, due to the alkaline pH releasing volatile amines).

Diagnosis by Amsel criteria (three of four: thin homogeneous discharge, pH >4.5, positive whiff test with KOH, clue cells on microscopy) or by Gram stain (Nugent score).

Treatment: oral or vaginal metronidazole or clindamycin. Recurrence rates are high; the microbiota does not always re-establish itself robustly.

BV is associated with increased risk of pelvic inflammatory disease, post-procedural infection, preterm labor, and acquisition of STIs.

### Toxic shock syndrome

We met it in Chapter 15. The menstrual TSS cases of the 1980s, associated with high-absorbency tampons, were the original cases — *S. aureus* growing in or near the tampon produced TSST-1.

## Sexually transmitted infections

### Gonorrhea

*Neisseria gonorrhoeae*. Gram-negative diplococci. The clinical picture varies by sex and site:

- **Men**: urethritis with purulent discharge and dysuria. Usually symptomatic.
- **Women**: cervicitis with discharge or mucopurulent discharge from the cervix. About 50% of cases are asymptomatic.
- **Pharyngeal gonorrhea**: often asymptomatic.
- **Rectal gonorrhea**: variable symptoms.
- **Disseminated gonococcal infection**: bacteremia with skin lesions, septic arthritis. Rare but serious.

Complications: pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) in women, with risk of infertility and ectopic pregnancy; epididymitis in men; gonococcal conjunctivitis in newborns (ophthalmia neonatorum, Chapter 21).

Diagnosis: nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT) on urine or genital swabs. Replaces culture for most diagnostic purposes.

Treatment: ceftriaxone is the current first-line. Resistance is a major concern. *N. gonorrhoeae* has serially lost susceptibility to most previously effective antibiotics (penicillin in the 1970s, fluoroquinolones in the 2000s, oral cephalosporins in the 2010s). Some isolates are now resistant to ceftriaxone, raising the spectre of untreatable gonorrhea.

### Chlamydia

*Chlamydia trachomatis*. Obligate intracellular bacterium with a complex two-stage life cycle (extracellular elementary body, intracellular reticulate body). Causes:

- **Urogenital chlamydia**: the most common bacterial STI in the US. Often asymptomatic — particularly in women (about 75% asymptomatic). When symptomatic: cervicitis, urethritis, dysuria, discharge. Complications similar to gonorrhea — PID, infertility, ectopic pregnancy in women; epididymitis in men.
- **Lymphogranuloma venereum (LGV)**: caused by certain *C. trachomatis* serotypes (L1, L2, L3). Genital ulcer followed by tender inguinal lymphadenopathy.
- **Trachoma**: covered in Chapter 21.

Diagnosis: NAAT.

Treatment: azithromycin (single dose) or doxycycline (7 days).

Routine screening of sexually active women under 25 is recommended because of the high asymptomatic rate.

### Syphilis

*Treponema pallidum*. Spirochete. Cannot be cultured in vitro. Diagnosis by serology and microscopy of lesions.

Syphilis progresses through stages over months to years if untreated:

**Primary syphilis**: a chancre at the site of inoculation 10–90 days after exposure. The chancre is typically painless, indurated, and ulcerated. It heals spontaneously in 3–6 weeks.

**Secondary syphilis**: 4–10 weeks after the chancre, the bacteria have disseminated. Clinical features include a generalized rash (often including palms and soles — a classic clue), mucous membrane lesions (mucous patches, condylomata lata), fever, malaise, lymphadenopathy. Also resolves spontaneously over weeks.

**Latent syphilis**: no clinical signs, but serology is positive. Early latent (within 1 year of infection) is contagious; late latent is generally not.

**Tertiary syphilis**: years to decades later. Affects various organ systems:
- **Cardiovascular syphilis**: aortic aneurysm, aortic regurgitation.
- **Gummatous syphilis**: granulomatous lesions in skin, bone, organs.
- **Neurosyphilis**: meningitis, dementia (general paresis), tabes dorsalis (sensory ataxia), Argyll-Robertson pupil (accommodates but does not react to light).

**Congenital syphilis**: transplacental transmission. Causes stillbirth, neonatal death, or characteristic features of late congenital syphilis (saddle nose, Hutchinson teeth, interstitial keratitis).

Diagnosis by two-test serology:

- **Non-treponemal tests** (VDRL, RPR): detect anti-cardiolipin antibodies. Sensitive but not specific. Titers decline with treatment.
- **Treponemal tests** (FTA-ABS, TP-PA, EIA): detect antibodies to *T. pallidum* specifically. More specific but remain positive lifelong even after treatment.

Treatment: penicillin G. *T. pallidum* remains uniformly penicillin-sensitive — another striking exception to resistance trends. The Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction (acute fever and inflammation in the hours after treatment, from massive release of antigen by killed spirochetes) is common in early treatment.

### Other bacterial STIs

- **Chancroid**: *Haemophilus ducreyi*. Painful genital ulcers (unlike the painless chancre of syphilis). Uncommon in developed countries.
- **Granuloma inguinale (donovanosis)**: *Klebsiella granulomatis*. Chronic genital ulcers. Tropical/subtropical regions.
- **Mycoplasma genitalium**: increasingly recognized cause of urethritis, cervicitis, and PID. Often resistant to common antibiotics.

## Viral reproductive tract infections

### Genital herpes

HSV-1 and HSV-2. We covered the general biology in Chapter 21. Clinical features of genital herpes: vesicular lesions on a red base, in the genital area, often with severe pain. Primary infection often more severe, with fever and lymphadenopathy. Recurrent episodes typically less severe and shorter.

Importantly, viral shedding can occur without visible lesions (asymptomatic shedding), and this accounts for much HSV transmission.

Treatment: acyclovir, valacyclovir, famciclovir. Daily suppressive therapy reduces recurrences and transmission.

### Human papillomavirus (HPV)

DNA virus. Over 200 types; about 40 affect the genital area.

- **Low-risk types (6, 11)**: cause genital warts (condyloma acuminatum).
- **High-risk types (16, 18, 31, 33, 45, etc.)**: cause cervical cancer and other anogenital cancers (anal, penile, vulvar, vaginal, oropharyngeal). HPV 16 and 18 alone account for ~70% of cervical cancers.

HPV vaccines (Gardasil, Cervarix, Gardasil 9) prevent infection with the most common high-risk types and the wart-causing types. Recommended for adolescents before sexual debut. Real-world data show substantial reductions in HPV infection, genital warts, precancerous cervical lesions, and (with longer follow-up) cervical cancer. The vaccine is one of the major public health interventions of the past 20 years.

### HIV

*Human immunodeficiency virus*. Retrovirus. Transmitted by sexual contact, blood, and from mother to child during pregnancy, birth, or breastfeeding.

HIV biology, immune effects, and clinical course are covered in Chapters 19 and 25. The sexual transmission aspect involves the genital tract: HIV crosses mucosal surfaces (cervix, anal mucosa, urethra). Coexisting STIs that produce ulcers (syphilis, herpes, chancroid) substantially increase HIV transmission risk.

## Fungal reproductive tract infections

### Vaginal candidiasis

*Candida albicans* (most common) and other *Candida* species. Common — affects most women at some point.

Clinical features: thick white "cottage cheese" discharge, vaginal itching, vulvar erythema. Vaginal pH is typically normal (4.0–4.5) — unlike BV, where pH is elevated.

Risk factors: antibiotic use (disrupting normal flora), diabetes, pregnancy, immunosuppression.

Treatment: topical or oral azole antifungals (fluconazole, clotrimazole, miconazole). Recurrent infection can be treated with longer-term suppressive therapy.

## Parasitic reproductive tract infection

### Trichomoniasis

*Trichomonas vaginalis*, a flagellated protozoan. The most common nonviral STI globally.

Clinical features: in women, frothy yellow-green discharge with vaginal irritation. In men, often asymptomatic but can cause urethritis. Cervical "strawberry" appearance with punctate hemorrhages is a classic finding on speculum exam (uncommon to actually see).

Diagnosis: wet mount microscopy (motile trichomonads visible). NAAT is more sensitive and increasingly standard.

Treatment: metronidazole or tinidazole. Sexual partners must also be treated.

## Schistosomiasis

*Schistosoma haematobium* causes urinary schistosomiasis in endemic regions of Africa and the Middle East. The parasite enters through the skin from contaminated freshwater, migrates through the body, and eventually lives in the venules around the bladder. Female worms produce eggs that lodge in bladder tissue, causing chronic inflammation, hematuria, and (long-term) increased risk of bladder cancer.

We will discuss the broader schistosome biology in Chapter 25.

## What the chapter is really about

The urogenital tract is a region where the body's distinction between "sterile" (urinary tract) and "intentionally colonized" (vagina) is normally maintained by careful biology — flushing of urine, anatomical barriers, lactobacillus-dominant microbiota. Infections happen when these systems are disrupted, or when sexually transmitted pathogens arrive with specific tools to bypass them.

For clinical practice, two patterns dominate:

**The UTI workup**. Patient with dysuria, frequency, urgency. Urinalysis confirms pyuria. Empirical antibiotic for uncomplicated cystitis in a healthy young woman; culture if complicated or recurrent. The pathogen is almost always *E. coli* from the patient's own gut.

**The STI workup**. Sexually active patient, particularly under 25, particularly with multiple partners or new partner. Screening for chlamydia and gonorrhea by NAAT. Testing for syphilis, HIV, and HSV as appropriate to the history. Treatment of partners is essential to break the transmission chain.

Both patterns rely heavily on rapid molecular testing (NAAT) that has transformed STI diagnosis over the past 20 years. The same techniques (PCR-based detection of specific pathogen sequences) work across most urogenital pathogens.

## Still puzzling

I do not fully understand why some women have recurrent UTIs and others do not, even with similar exposures. There are clearly some host factors — receptor genetics on bladder epithelium, vaginal microbiota composition, anatomical features — but the specific contributions of each remain unclear. Why a single woman might have three UTIs in a year and then none for a decade is genuinely puzzling. The biology of UTI recurrence is one of those problems where the major drivers may not yet be identified.

## What would change my mind

The traditional dichotomy of "sterile bladder" versus "colonized vagina" is being modified by metagenomic data. The bladder is not sterile, even in health; it has a low-abundance community. Some women with recurrent UTIs may have urinary dysbiosis rather than acute bacterial infection. If treatments aimed at restoring healthy urinary microbiota replace antibiotic prophylaxis as standard care, the framework in this chapter will need revision.
