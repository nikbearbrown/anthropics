# Chapter 22 — Respiratory System Infections

*You breathe in roughly 10,000 microbes a day. The lung's defenses keep almost all of them out.*

## The question before the answer

You take about 12 breaths a minute, 17,000 a day, half a billion in a lifetime. Each breath contains microbes — bacteria, viruses, fungal spores. Most of them never reach the lower respiratory tract because the upper airway is built to filter them out: turbulent flow in the nose deposits large particles on mucous surfaces; ciliated cells drag them upward; the mucociliary escalator continuously removes trapped material; alveolar macrophages handle anything that gets to the lung.

The defenses are remarkable, but they are not perfect. Some pathogens are too small to filter. Some have evolved to evade the mucus. Some take advantage of damage (smoking, viral infection, ciliary dysfunction) to colonize the lung. The result is that respiratory infection is one of the most common reasons people get sick — and respiratory infection of the lower tract is one of the leading causes of death worldwide.

The point of this chapter is to map respiratory infections by anatomy (upper vs lower) and by pathogen class (bacterial, viral, fungal), and to give you a working framework for thinking about how respiratory pathogens get past defenses and what they do once they're in.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

1. Describe the major anatomy of the upper and lower respiratory tract and the normal microbiota of each.
2. Identify the most common bacterial respiratory pathogens by clinical presentation.
3. Identify the major viral respiratory pathogens and the seasonal patterns of each.
4. Describe the major fungal pneumonias and their geographic distributions.
5. Distinguish typical from atypical pneumonia and predict which organisms cause each.

Prerequisites: Chapters 1–21.

## Respiratory anatomy

The **upper respiratory tract** includes the nose, sinuses, mouth, pharynx, and larynx. It is open to the outside and has a complex microbiota.

The **lower respiratory tract** includes the trachea, bronchi, bronchioles, and alveoli. Historically thought to be sterile in health; microbiome sequencing now shows a low-abundance microbial community that is similar to but distinct from the upper tract.

The interface between them — the larynx and trachea — is protected by the epiglottis (which closes during swallowing) and the cough reflex. The bronchi divide into progressively smaller bronchioles, ending in the alveoli where gas exchange happens. Total alveolar surface area in adult lungs is about 70 square meters — a roughly tennis-court-sized surface in continuous contact with air.

Defenses include:

- **Nasal turbinates**: bony structures that create turbulent flow, trapping particles on mucous surfaces.
- **Mucus**: produced by goblet cells throughout the airway.
- **Mucociliary escalator**: ciliated cells beat upward, moving mucus and trapped material toward the throat.
- **Cough reflex**: clears the throat of large debris.
- **Alveolar macrophages**: resident phagocytes that handle particles reaching the alveoli.
- **IgA in mucous secretions**: blocks attachment of pathogens.
- **Lysozyme, defensins, and antimicrobial peptides**: in mucus.

Compromise of any of these defenses (smoking damages cilia; influenza destroys epithelial cells; cystic fibrosis thickens mucus so cilia cannot move it; intubation bypasses the upper defenses) predisposes to infection.

## Bacterial upper respiratory infections

### Strep throat

*Streptococcus pyogenes* (Group A streptococcus, GAS) causes pharyngitis: sore throat, fever, enlarged cervical lymph nodes, and tonsillar exudate. The diagnosis is important because:

- Rapid antigen tests (immunoassay for streptococcal antigen on a throat swab) can give answers in minutes.
- Untreated streptococcal pharyngitis can lead to **rheumatic fever** — a delayed autoimmune complication where antibodies cross-react with heart, joint, and brain tissue. Rheumatic fever is now rare in developed countries (antibiotics interrupt the cascade) but remains a leading cause of acquired heart disease globally.
- It can also lead to **post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis** — immune complexes deposit in the kidney 1–2 weeks after infection.
- **Scarlet fever** (covered in Chapter 21) results from the erythrogenic toxin produced by some GAS strains.

Treatment: penicillin or amoxicillin. The bacterium remains uniformly penicillin-sensitive — a striking exception to the general antibiotic resistance trend.

### Diphtheria

*Corynebacterium diphtheriae* produces diphtheria toxin (encoded by a lysogenic phage, Chapter 6), which inhibits protein synthesis in host cells. Clinical disease: severe pharyngitis with a gray, leathery membrane on the tonsils that can extend to obstruct the airway, and systemic toxin effects on heart and nerves. Diphtheria is rare in immunized populations (the DTaP vaccine) but reappears in conflict zones and undervaccinated communities.

### Pertussis (whooping cough)

*Bordetella pertussis*. The "whoop" is the characteristic high-pitched intake of breath after a paroxysmal cough. The disease has three phases: catarrhal (cold-like, 1–2 weeks), paroxysmal (severe coughing fits, 2–6 weeks), and convalescent (gradual recovery, weeks to months). The cough is so severe that it can cause rib fractures and post-tussive vomiting in adults. In infants, pertussis can be fatal — apnea, pneumonia, encephalopathy.

The DTaP (acellular pertussis component) vaccine has dramatically reduced pertussis, but immunity wanes over years, so adolescents and adults can develop pertussis and transmit it to vulnerable infants. The Tdap booster for adolescents and pregnant women has been implemented to address this.

### Other bacterial upper respiratory infections

- **Otitis media** (middle ear infection): typically *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Haemophilus influenzae*, or *Moraxella catarrhalis* in children. Common in early childhood, often after a viral upper respiratory infection.
- **Sinusitis**: usually viral; bacterial sinusitis (mostly *S. pneumoniae*, *H. influenzae*) requires antibiotics. Most "sinus infections" are viral and self-limiting.
- **Epiglottitis**: classically caused by *Haemophilus influenzae* type b (Hib); a medical emergency because the swollen epiglottis can obstruct the airway. Nearly eliminated by the Hib vaccine in immunized populations.

## Bacterial lower respiratory infections

### Pneumonia

Pneumonia is infection of the lung parenchyma, leading to inflammation and accumulation of fluid in the alveoli. It is classified by clinical setting:

- **Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP)**: in patients not recently hospitalized.
- **Hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP)**: develops more than 48 hours after admission.
- **Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP)**: a subset of HAP in patients on mechanical ventilation.
- **Healthcare-associated pneumonia (HCAP)**: in patients with recent healthcare contact (nursing homes, dialysis, recent hospitalization).

Different settings have different likely pathogens.

#### Typical pneumonia

"Typical" pneumonia presents acutely with high fever, productive cough with purulent sputum, pleuritic chest pain, and consolidation visible on chest X-ray.

Major typical pathogens:

- **Streptococcus pneumoniae**: the most common bacterial cause of community-acquired pneumonia. Lobar consolidation, rust-colored sputum classically. Gram-positive diplococci on Gram stain.
- **Haemophilus influenzae**: nontypeable strains cause pneumonia in adults, particularly smokers and COPD patients.
- **Moraxella catarrhalis**: similar profile.
- **Staphylococcus aureus**: pneumonia is uncommon in healthy adults but occurs after influenza (post-influenzal pneumonia) and in nosocomial settings.
- **Klebsiella pneumoniae**: causes severe pneumonia in alcoholics and diabetics; "currant jelly" sputum.

Treatment of typical CAP: amoxicillin, doxycycline, or a macrolide for outpatients; respiratory fluoroquinolone or a beta-lactam plus macrolide for inpatients.

#### Atypical pneumonia

"Atypical" pneumonia presents more insidiously, with low-grade fever, dry cough, headache, and chest X-ray findings less impressive than the patient looks like they should have. The classic causes are organisms that do not Gram stain well and do not grow on standard media:

- **Mycoplasma pneumoniae**: the most common cause of atypical pneumonia, especially in young adults. Lacks a cell wall — beta-lactams do not work. Treat with macrolides, doxycycline, or fluoroquinolones.
- **Chlamydia pneumoniae**: similar profile.
- **Legionella pneumophila**: from contaminated water sources (Chapter 16). More severe, often with high fever, neurological symptoms, and GI symptoms. Treat with azithromycin or a fluoroquinolone.

"Atypical" is a clinical pattern. The pathogens can overlap with typical pneumonia. Empirical therapy in community-acquired pneumonia usually covers both.

#### "Walking pneumonia"

Often a synonym for atypical pneumonia. The patient is sick enough to know they are sick but well enough to keep functioning. *M. pneumoniae* is the classic cause.

### Tuberculosis

*Mycobacterium tuberculosis*. Approximately one-quarter of the world's population is estimated to carry latent TB infection; about 10 million develop active TB each year; about 1.3 million die. TB is one of the top infectious causes of death worldwide. [^1]

**Latent TB**: infection without active disease. The bacterium is contained within granulomas (Chapter 17). The patient has no symptoms and is not contagious. Detected by tuberculin skin test (PPD) or interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA).

**Active TB**: clinical disease with fever, night sweats, weight loss, chronic cough, sometimes hemoptysis. Most pulmonary TB; can also affect lymph nodes, bones, urogenital tract, CNS.

**Risk of progression** from latent to active TB is about 5–10% over a lifetime in healthy individuals, much higher (10% per year) in HIV-positive patients.

**Diagnosis**: acid-fast bacilli on sputum smear (rapid but insensitive), culture (slow — weeks to grow), nucleic acid amplification (rapid, sensitive). The GeneXpert test, which detects *M. tuberculosis* DNA and rifampin resistance simultaneously, has improved TB diagnosis in resource-limited settings.

**Treatment**: at least four drugs for the first two months (isoniazid, rifampin, pyrazinamide, ethambutol), then two drugs for four more months. The duration is long because mycobacteria grow slowly and are protected within granulomas. Directly observed therapy (DOT) is standard for at least the initial phase, to ensure compliance and prevent resistance.

**Multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB)**: resistant to at least isoniazid and rifampin. Treatment is more complex, longer, more toxic, and less successful. **Extensively drug-resistant TB (XDR-TB)** adds resistance to fluoroquinolones and one injectable second-line drug. Some cases now described as **totally drug-resistant**.

The TB story has gotten worse since the 1980s in several places (former Soviet Union, parts of Africa) due to HIV co-infection driving reactivation and to inadequate treatment regimens producing resistance. The drug pipeline has been thin; bedaquiline (approved 2012), delamanid, and pretomanid are the recent new drugs.

## Viral respiratory infections

The most common cause of respiratory infection overall. Many of them have no specific treatment beyond supportive care.

### The common cold

Mostly rhinoviruses (over 100 serotypes). Also coronaviruses (the seasonal ones, not SARS-CoV-2), adenoviruses, parainfluenza, RSV. Limited to upper respiratory tract in most cases. Highly contagious. Resolves in 1–2 weeks.

### Influenza

Influenza A and B viruses cause seasonal epidemics in temperate climates. Severe complications include viral pneumonia, secondary bacterial pneumonia (often *S. aureus* or *S. pneumoniae*), and acute respiratory distress syndrome. Influenza kills roughly 290,000–650,000 people worldwide each year. [^2]

Influenza A subtypes are named by their surface glycoproteins: H (hemagglutinin, 18 types) and N (neuraminidase, 11 types). The combination (H1N1, H3N2, H5N1, H7N9, etc.) describes the strain.

**Antigenic drift**: gradual accumulation of point mutations in H and N. Why the seasonal flu vaccine has to be updated every year — the strain changes enough that previous immunity no longer fully protects.

**Antigenic shift**: dramatic reassortment when two different influenza strains co-infect the same cell and exchange genome segments (influenza has a segmented genome). Produces a strain very different from anything previously circulating. The result is a strain to which most people have no immunity — the recipe for a pandemic.

Major influenza pandemics: 1918 (H1N1, "Spanish flu" — perhaps 50 million deaths), 1957 (H2N2), 1968 (H3N2), 2009 (H1N1pdm09).

Annual influenza vaccination is recommended; protection is partial because of antigenic drift. The vaccine targets four strains (two A, two B) updated each year based on surveillance.

Antivirals: oseltamivir (Tamiflu), zanamivir — neuraminidase inhibitors. Effective if given within 48 hours of symptom onset; reduce duration of illness by about a day; possibly reduce complications.

### Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV)

Major cause of pneumonia and bronchiolitis in infants. Nearly all children are infected by age 2. Severe disease primarily in infants under 6 months, premature infants, and immunocompromised adults. The new RSV vaccine and monoclonal antibody (nirsevimab) for infants have substantially reduced severe RSV disease since their approval in 2023.

### Parainfluenza virus

Causes croup in children (laryngotracheobronchitis with the characteristic barking cough) and pneumonia in young children.

### Adenovirus

Various respiratory illnesses, conjunctivitis, gastroenteritis. Outbreaks in close-quarters populations (military recruits, college students).

### Coronaviruses

Several seasonal coronaviruses cause mild common-cold illness. The novel coronavirus outbreaks have included SARS-CoV (2003), MERS-CoV (2012), and SARS-CoV-2 (2019–present).

SARS-CoV-2 emerged in late 2019 and caused the COVID-19 pandemic. The virus is a positive-sense RNA coronavirus, spread primarily by respiratory droplets and aerosols. Clinical disease ranges from asymptomatic infection to severe pneumonia with acute respiratory distress syndrome. The mRNA vaccines (Pfizer-BioNTech, Moderna) developed and deployed within a year of the virus's identification are the fastest vaccine development in history. Variants of concern (Alpha, Delta, Omicron and descendants) have continued to evolve, requiring updated vaccines.

The long-term consequences of COVID-19 — "long COVID" or "post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection" (PASC) — affect a substantial fraction of patients with persistent symptoms. The mechanisms are still being worked out. As of this writing, our understanding is incomplete. [^3]

## Fungal respiratory infections

Most fungal respiratory infections are acquired by inhalation of spores from environmental sources.

### Endemic mycoses

Dimorphic fungi (yeast in tissue, mold in environment) endemic to specific geographic regions:

- **Histoplasmosis** (*Histoplasma capsulatum*): endemic to the Ohio and Mississippi River valleys in the US, parts of Central and South America, parts of Africa. Acquired by inhaling spores from soil enriched with bird or bat droppings. Most infections asymptomatic; some cause acute pneumonia, chronic pulmonary histoplasmosis, or disseminated disease in immunocompromised patients.
- **Blastomycosis** (*Blastomyces dermatitidis*): endemic in the Mississippi and Ohio River valleys, Great Lakes region. Pulmonary infection that can disseminate.
- **Coccidioidomycosis ("Valley fever")** (*Coccidioides immitis*, *C. posadasii*): endemic in the southwestern US (especially California, Arizona) and parts of Mexico and South America. Acquired by inhaling spores from soil. Often self-limiting; severe disseminated disease in immunocompromised patients and certain ethnic groups (Filipinos, African Americans have higher rates of dissemination).
- **Paracoccidioidomycosis** (*Paracoccidioides brasiliensis*): endemic to Central and South America.

### Opportunistic mycoses

Fungi that cause disease primarily in immunocompromised hosts.

- **Pneumocystis pneumonia (PCP/PJP)**: *Pneumocystis jirovecii*. Now classified as a fungus (was thought to be a protozoan). Pneumonia in HIV/AIDS patients with low CD4 counts, transplant recipients on immunosuppression, and other severely immunocompromised hosts. Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole is both treatment and prophylaxis.
- **Aspergillosis**: *Aspergillus fumigatus* and related species. Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA) in asthma and cystic fibrosis patients. Invasive aspergillosis in neutropenic patients (often after chemotherapy). Aspergilloma — a fungal ball in pre-existing lung cavities (TB scars, sarcoidosis).
- **Mucormycosis** ("zygomycosis"): *Rhizopus*, *Mucor* species. Rhinocerebral form in diabetic ketoacidosis patients (the high glucose and acidic environment favor the fungi). Pulmonary form in neutropenic patients. Often fatal despite treatment.

## What the chapter is really about

The respiratory tract is the body's largest mucosal interface with the outside world. Infection of this surface is one of the most common medical conditions and one of the leading causes of death. The pathogens range from rhinoviruses that produce a few days of inconvenience to *M. tuberculosis* that kills more than a million people a year.

The clinical reasoning for respiratory infection follows a stereotyped pattern: what part of the airway (upper vs lower), what kind of clinical picture (acute vs chronic, typical vs atypical), what host factors (immunocompetent vs not, smoker vs not, age, exposures). The answers narrow the differential to a small number of organisms, which targeted testing can confirm.

For practice in the chapters ahead — and for clinical reasoning in general — the trick is to memorize less and reason more. There are too many respiratory pathogens to know them all individually. What you need to know is which ones are common, which ones are dangerous, which ones have specific treatments, and how to recognize them from clinical pattern plus a few key tests.

## Still puzzling

I do not fully understand why *Streptococcus pneumoniae* remains uniformly susceptible to penicillin in some regions and develops significant resistance in others. The selection pressures (antibiotic use) are present everywhere. The resistance mechanism (altered PBP) is known. Why some populations carry highly resistant strains and others remain dominated by sensitive strains is not fully accounted for by antibiotic use patterns. Possibly it relates to the global circulation patterns of specific clonal complexes that happen to be resistant or sensitive.

## What would change my mind

The COVID-19 pandemic has revealed gaps in our understanding of respiratory transmission. The earlier assumption that respiratory infections spread by droplets (large particles falling within 1–2 meters) vs aerosols (small particles staying suspended in air) is more spectrum than dichotomy. SARS-CoV-2 transmission has substantial aerosol component. Influenza may also have more aerosol component than previously appreciated. If the framework shifts to viewing all respiratory transmission as occurring across a continuum of particle sizes, with different pathogens dominant at different points, the infection control implications (ventilation, mask type, distance) will be substantial. `[verify: 2024–25 consensus on respiratory pathogen aerosol transmission]`
