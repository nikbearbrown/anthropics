## The bag that should have been saline

A first-year resident is paged at 3 a.m. A trauma patient has lost blood. The nurse asks what to hang. The resident, half asleep, says "D5W, wide open." D5W is 5% dextrose in water — a glucose solution that lives on the same shelf as saline. The bag goes up.

Twenty minutes later the patient seizes.

Here is what happened in molecules. The dextrose dissolves into the bloodstream; cells absorb the glucose almost instantly; what remains in the vascular space is water — essentially distilled water — diluting the plasma. Osmotic pressure falls. Water moves from the diluted plasma into every cell in the body, because water follows solutes, always from where they are scarce to where they are concentrated. Red blood cells swell. Neurons swell. The brain, locked inside a skull that cannot expand, begins to herniate. Seizures follow.

The correct fluid is 0.9% sodium chloride — normal saline — chosen because its osmotic concentration nearly matches plasma. No net water movement. No swelling. The patient lives.

The difference between those two bags is not pharmacology. It is four grams of solute per liter. The same physics that killed a neuron in that ICU runs every nerve impulse in this book, every heartbeat, every milliliter of urine. The chemistry is the medicine. This chapter is the chemistry.

## Why atoms bond

An atom is a nucleus — protons and neutrons packed together — with electrons arranged in shells around it. The first shell holds two electrons. The second and third hold eight. Electrons fill from the inside out, and here is the key thing: a full outer shell is a lower-energy state than an incomplete one. Atoms with incomplete outer shells will combine with other atoms to fill them, because lower energy is where matter tends to go. This is not chemistry. It is physics.

Helium has two electrons. First shell full. It does nothing. Neon has ten (two plus eight). Both shells full. Also does nothing. These elements are chemically inert.

Carbon has six electrons — two in the first shell, four in the second. The second shell wants eight. Carbon needs four more, and because it needs exactly four, it can form four bonds at once. With hydrogen. With oxygen. With nitrogen. With phosphorus. With other carbon atoms. It can form chains, rings, branches. A carbon backbone can be three atoms long or three billion. Every protein you contain, every strand of your DNA, every sugar you burn — all of it is built on carbon's four open slots.

When atoms come together, they combine in a few distinct ways. Three run all of biology.

A **covalent bond** is two atoms sharing electrons. Water is the example: oxygen has six valence electrons and needs two more; hydrogen has one and needs one more; two hydrogens sharing with one oxygen, and each outer shell feels complete. Covalent bonds are strong. They hold proteins and nucleic acids in shapes that don't casually fall apart at body temperature.

An **ionic bond** is one atom donating an electron to another. Sodium has one valence electron; chlorine has seven and needs one. Sodium donates; chlorine accepts. Sodium is now Na⁺, chlorine is now Cl⁻, and opposites attract. In a crystal, they are locked together. In water, polar water molecules pry them apart — and now you have mobile ions in solution that can carry charge across membranes and be pumped in and out of cells. The crystal can do none of that. Dissolving NaCl in water is not just a convenience; it is what makes ions usable by biology.

A **hydrogen bond** is not a bond in the same sense. It is an attraction between molecules. When a hydrogen atom bonds covalently to an electronegative atom — oxygen or nitrogen, which pull electrons hard — the shared electrons spend more time near the electronegative atom, leaving the hydrogen slightly positive. That slightly positive hydrogen is attracted to the slightly negative oxygen or nitrogen on a *different* molecule nearby. Each hydrogen bond is about 5% the strength of a covalent bond. Each one breaks and re-forms in picoseconds. In aggregate, though, they hold the two strands of DNA together. They hold every protein fold in place. And they give water the properties that make your life possible.

The hierarchy matters at the bedside. Covalent bonds are permanent architecture — not undone by body-temperature changes. Ionic and hydrogen interactions are reversible — and that reversibility is what allows enzymes to grab a substrate, transform it, and let go. An irreversible enzyme would do its job once and stop. Biology needs the permanent for structure and the reversible for control. Both are made of the same atomic physics.

## Water, and why you can't ignore it

The oxygen in a water molecule pulls the shared electrons harder than the hydrogens do. Eight protons in oxygen's nucleus versus one in hydrogen's — more protons pull harder. So the electrons in each O–H bond crowd toward oxygen. The molecule ends up with a slightly negative oxygen end and two slightly positive hydrogen ends. Bent geometry keeps the charges from canceling. Water is polar.

That polarity does a remarkable amount of work.

It makes water a near-universal solvent for charged and polar substances. When NaCl enters water, the negative oxygens of water orient around each Na⁺, the positive hydrogens around each Cl⁻, and the crystal dissolves into a sea of mobile ions. Anything polar or charged — hydrophilic — dissolves. Anything nonpolar — hydrophobic — does not. This turns out to be the most important sorting principle in cell biology.

Water also has an unusually high heat capacity. It takes a lot of energy to raise its temperature, because much of the input goes into disrupting hydrogen bonds rather than speeding molecules up. You are about 60% water by mass. You generate enormous metabolic heat. Without water's capacity to absorb it, you would cook in minutes.

And there is pH. A water molecule occasionally splits into H⁺ and OH⁻. In pure water the H⁺ concentration is 10⁻⁷ mol/L. The negative log of that is 7. Acids release H⁺ and lower the pH. Bases absorb H⁺ or release OH⁻ and raise it. Your blood is held between pH 7.35 and 7.45 by buffers — paired molecules that absorb or donate H⁺ as needed. The main one is the bicarbonate system:

$$\text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_2\text{CO}_3 \rightleftharpoons \text{H}^+ + \text{HCO}_3^-$$

Below pH 6.8 or above pH 7.8, proteins denature and the patient dies. The buffer is the distance between living and not.

The other consequence of polarity is the hydrophobic effect. When a nonpolar molecule enters water, the surrounding water molecules cannot form their usual hydrogen-bonded network with it. They form a more ordered cage instead. That cage is energetically costly, so the system minimizes total contact between water and the nonpolar material — the nonpolar molecules cluster together. Oil droplets form. Cell membranes form. Proteins fold so their nonpolar side chains hide inside and their polar ones face the water. No instructions are required. No energy is consumed. Only water.

## The four families

Almost everything in a cell that isn't water belongs to one of four molecular families. Each one is built on a carbon skeleton with characteristic clusters of atoms — *functional groups* — that determine what the molecule does.

The large molecules are usually polymers: long chains assembled by linking smaller units called monomers. The linking reaction is **dehydration synthesis** — two monomers join, releasing a water molecule, and are now covalently bonded. Reverse the reaction by adding water — **hydrolysis** — and the polymer comes apart. Your digestive system is a hydrolysis engine: it takes the polymers you eat and recovers the monomers.

**Carbohydrates** follow the formula (CH₂O)ₙ — one carbon, one water's worth of hydrogen and oxygen, repeated. The monomer is a simple sugar: glucose, a six-carbon ring, is the primary fuel. Two sugars linked make a disaccharide (sucrose, lactose). Many linked make a polysaccharide. Glycogen is a branched polysaccharide of glucose stored in liver and muscle — your quick-access energy reserve. Starch is the plant version, same monomers, similar geometry. Cellulose is also a glucose polymer, but the bonds between units are flipped 180° — and that single geometric difference means your digestive enzymes cannot break it. You can eat wood and extract no calories. Enzymes recognize specific bond shapes. This principle — shape is function — will run through every system in this book.

**Lipids** are united by one feature: they are largely nonpolar and don't dissolve in water. Three subtypes matter.

Triglycerides are three fatty acid chains on a glycerol backbone — long-term energy storage. A gram of fat yields about 9 kilocalories when burned; carbohydrate yields about 4. Fat is also anhydrous: it stores without the water that glycogen requires. This is why your body uses fat for long-term storage and glycogen for quick access.

Phospholipids have two fatty acid tails and one phosphate head where the third tail would be. The phosphate end is polar; the tails are nonpolar. One molecule, two personalities — and that split is exactly what builds a cell membrane. We'll come back to this.

Steroids share a four-ring carbon skeleton. Cholesterol is the parent compound — structural component of every animal cell membrane and precursor for every steroid hormone: cortisol, aldosterone, estrogen, testosterone. The same cholesterol that deposits in artery walls is the molecule that keeps your cell membranes working. Cholesterol is not a contaminant in your cells. It belongs there. What goes wrong is where it ends up.

<!-- → [INFOGRAPHIC: Four biomolecule families — visual summary showing monomer, polymer, example, primary function, and distinctive chemical feature for each of carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids.] -->

**Proteins** are polymers of amino acids. Twenty appear in biology, each with an amino group (–NH₂), a carboxyl group (–COOH), and a variable side chain that sets its character. Glycine's side chain is a single hydrogen — tiny, flexible. Leucine's is a branched hydrocarbon — bulky, nonpolar, tends to hide inside folded proteins. Lysine's ends in a positive amino group — water-loving, capable of ionic bonds with nearby negative side chains.

Amino acids link by peptide bonds; a long folded chain is a protein. The sequence of amino acids — the **primary structure** — is encoded in DNA. But sequence is not function. Function is shape, and shape is set by how the chain folds.

The folding happens at four levels. Primary structure is the sequence. Secondary structure is local pattern — the backbone's own hydrogen bonds produce alpha-helices (the chain spirals) and beta-sheets (the chain zigzags back on itself). Tertiary structure is the full three-dimensional shape of one folded chain, set by interactions between side chains: hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide bridges between two cysteines, and the hydrophobic effect driving nonpolar side chains to the protein's interior. Quaternary structure is multiple folded chains assembling into a working complex — hemoglobin's four subunits, each carrying one oxygen molecule, are the canonical case.

The shape is the function. An enzyme's active site is a pocket shaped to bind a specific substrate. A receptor's binding site is shaped to bind a specific hormone. One amino acid in the wrong place and the shape changes. The function changes with it.

Sickle cell disease makes this concrete. The β-chain of hemoglobin is 146 amino acids long. In sickle cell hemoglobin, position 6 carries valine — nonpolar, hydrophobic — where glutamate, charged and hydrophilic, should be. Under low-oxygen conditions, this new hydrophobic patch on the protein's surface sticks to a complementary patch on another hemoglobin molecule. The protein polymerizes into fibers. The red blood cell, normally a flexible disc, deforms into a rigid sickle. Sickled cells clog small vessels. Tissues starve for oxygen. One amino acid. The entire chain from chemistry to cell to tissue to patient runs through a single mutation.

**Nucleic acids** are polymers of nucleotides, each nucleotide consisting of a phosphate group, a five-carbon sugar, and a nitrogenous base. In DNA the bases are adenine (A), guanine (G), cytosine (C), and thymine (T); the sugar is deoxyribose. In RNA, thymine is replaced by uracil and the sugar is ribose. DNA is the archive. RNA is the working copy.

DNA forms the double helix: two antiparallel strands held together by hydrogen bonds between bases. A pairs with T (two hydrogen bonds); G pairs with C (three). The pairing is specific, and the specificity is the point. When the cell copies its DNA, the strands separate and each acts as template — an A on the old strand demands a T on the new one. The information passes essentially intact. G-C pairs require more energy to separate than A-T pairs — that's why DNA with higher G-C content is harder to denature. This will matter the first time you run a PCR.

One nucleotide deserves its own sentence: **ATP**, adenosine triphosphate. Three phosphate groups in a chain. The bond between the second and third phosphate is high-energy — hydrolyze it and about 7.3 kcal/mol is released, leaving ADP and an inorganic phosphate. The cell captures that energy and spends it on whatever work needs doing: pumping ions, contracting muscle, building proteins. Then it reattaches the phosphate to ADP using energy from glucose or fat breakdown and does it again. ATP is not energy. ATP is a molecule whose hydrolysis releases energy in a usable amount — the cell's standard unit of currency, sized for single transactions, always on hand.

## The membrane, which solved the boundary problem

Strip a living system to its essentials. The first thing it needs is a boundary — something that separates inside from outside. Without that, there is no inside, only chemistry happening at uniform concentration in open water with no way to accumulate the molecules life requires.

But the boundary cannot be a wall. A wall keeps everything out, including oxygen and nutrients. It keeps everything in, including carbon dioxide and waste. A wall is a coffin. The boundary has to *choose* — let some things pass, block others, pump some uphill against their gradients when survival demands it.

Phospholipids solve this. Each one has a polar phosphate head and two nonpolar fatty acid tails. Drop many of them into water. The hydrophobic effect takes over: heads stay in the water, tails flee from it and cluster together. The geometry that minimizes the penalty is a sheet two molecules thick, heads facing outward on both surfaces, tails buried in the interior. The **phospholipid bilayer** assembles spontaneously. No instructions needed, no energy input — just water.

The bilayer's interior is a band of hydrocarbon chains. This is the barrier. Small nonpolar molecules — oxygen, carbon dioxide, steroid hormones — dissolve into the fatty interior and slip through. Charged ions (Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻, Ca²⁺) cannot; the hydrophobic interior repels them absolutely. Large polar molecules like glucose cannot; too big and too polar both. Water gets through slowly on its own and quickly through dedicated protein channels called aquaporins.

The membrane is not just the bilayer. Embedded throughout are proteins: channels that let specific ions through, carriers that shuttle polar molecules, pumps that move things uphill against their gradients, receptors that bind hormones and transmit signals inward. Cholesterol is scattered through the bilayer, buffering fluidity — enough to keep the membrane from stiffening, not so much that it leaks. The **fluid mosaic model**, described by Singer and Nicolson in 1972, captures this: a fluid sheet of lipids in which proteins drift like tiles in a slow current.

Three clinical anchors before we move on.

Statins lower blood cholesterol by inhibiting an enzyme in cholesterol synthesis. They occasionally cause muscle pain because muscle cell membranes are sensitive to cholesterol depletion. Same molecule: structural component of membranes; cardiovascular risk factor in artery walls; statin side effect in muscle. The chemistry does not change. Only where it is.

Bacterial cell walls contain peptidoglycan; human cells do not. Penicillin blocks peptidoglycan synthesis and leaves your cells alone. Fungal membranes contain ergosterol instead of cholesterol — chemically related molecules, not identical. Antifungal drugs targeting ergosterol work, but the therapeutic window is narrower because the target is closer to your own biology. The closer the pathogen looks to you, the harder it is to kill without collateral damage. This principle will return in pharmacology and immunology.

Membrane fluidity is temperature-dependent. Cool a membrane below its lipid melting point and it stiffens. Cold-water fish compensate by building their membranes from phospholipids with extra double bonds in their tails — the kinks prevent tight packing and lower the melting point. Your cells use the same trick under cold stress, adjusting the ratio of saturated to unsaturated fatty acids in real time. The membrane is not a fixed structure. It is continuously maintained.

## The organelles, grouped by job

Inside the boundary, a eukaryotic cell is not one space but many. Internal membranes partition the cytoplasm into compartments — organelles — each specialized for a different function. The organization is not decoration. The same cell has to synthesize proteins, degrade damaged ones, generate ATP, and replicate its DNA without these processes interfering with each other. Membranes provide the separation.

**Information storage and readout: the nucleus.** The nucleus holds DNA — all of it, organized into chromosomes. The double membrane of the nuclear envelope is studded with nuclear pores, large protein complexes that export RNA and import selected proteins. DNA does not leave. When the cell needs a protein, the relevant gene is transcribed into messenger RNA inside the nucleus; the mRNA exits through a pore; the DNA stays behind. The nucleolus, a dense region within, is where ribosomes are partially assembled before export.

**Protein production: ribosomes, rough ER, Golgi.** Ribosomes read mRNA three bases at a time — each codon specifies one amino acid — and link amino acids into chains. Free ribosomes in the cytoplasm make proteins destined for use inside the cell. Ribosomes bound to the rough endoplasmic reticulum thread their growing chains directly into the ER's interior; these proteins are destined for secretion, membrane insertion, or delivery to other compartments. The smooth ER — no ribosomes — synthesizes lipids and, in liver cells, handles much of the detoxification of drugs. Smooth ER also stores calcium; in muscle cells, where it is called the sarcoplasmic reticulum, that calcium store is what triggers contraction.

The Golgi apparatus receives proteins from the rough ER in small vesicles. It modifies them — trimming and adding sugar groups, attaching phosphate tags, applying address labels — then dispatches them in new vesicles to the plasma membrane, to lysosomes, or to secretory vesicles that will fuse with the membrane and release their contents outside the cell.

<!-- → [INFOGRAPHIC: Protein secretion pathway — gene in nucleus → mRNA → ribosome on rough ER → vesicle budding from ER → Golgi stack with modification steps labeled → vesicle exiting Golgi → fusion with plasma membrane. Each step labeled with location and process name.] -->

**Energy: mitochondria.** Each mitochondrion has two membranes. The inner membrane folds into shelves — **cristae** — that dramatically increase its surface area. The electron transport chain sits on those cristae: a series of protein complexes that receive electrons from the breakdown products of glucose and fat, pass them down the chain, and at each step use the released energy to pump protons (H⁺) across the inner membrane. The proton gradient drives ATP synthase — protons flowing back through the enzyme rotate a molecular turbine, and the rotation drives the phosphorylation of ADP to ATP. It is mechanical chemistry: ion flow directly converted to a covalent bond.

One glucose fully oxidized in mitochondria yields approximately 30–32 ATP. The same glucose broken down only by glycolysis in the cytoplasm — no oxygen, no mitochondria — yields 2. This is why muscles burn in an anaerobic sprint and why mitochondrial density tracks with cell workload. A cardiac muscle cell may devote a third of its volume to mitochondria. A red blood cell has none: it ejects its mitochondria during maturation to pack more space with hemoglobin, accepts that it can only run glycolysis, and lives approximately 120 days before the spleen recycles it.

**Degradation: lysosomes.** Lysosomes are membrane-bound vesicles packed with digestive enzymes — proteases, lipases, nucleases — kept at around pH 4.5, because the enzymes only work in acid. They break down worn organelles, engulfed bacteria, and obsolete cell components. The acidic membrane-bound compartment matters: if those enzymes spilled into the cytoplasm, the cell would digest itself. Lysosomal storage diseases — Tay-Sachs, Gaucher, Hurler, and others — result from inherited mutations in lysosomal enzymes. Without the enzyme, the substrate accumulates inside swelling lysosomes and gradually kills the cell. The disease names differ. The mechanism is one defective enzyme at the same address.

**Structure: the cytoskeleton.** Three classes of protein filament scaffold the cell's interior. Microfilaments (actin) handle shape changes, crawling movement, and the contractile ring that pinches dividing cells in two. Intermediate filaments (keratins, neurofilaments) handle tensile strength. Microtubules (tubulin) serve as highways — tracks for motor proteins dragging cargo across the cell — and form the mitotic spindle that separates chromosomes during division. Vincristine and paclitaxel, both chemotherapy drugs, work by disrupting microtubule dynamics. They preferentially kill dividing cells, which is why they hit cancer. They also hit hair follicles, gut lining, and bone marrow, which is why the side effects are what they are.

## How molecules cross the boundary

Transport divides into two categories: *passive* (no ATP required; molecules move down their gradients) and *active* (ATP required; molecules move against their gradients).

**Passive transport** takes three forms.

*Simple diffusion.* Small nonpolar molecules — O₂, CO₂, steroid hormones — cross the bilayer directly. They move down a concentration gradient, from where they are more concentrated to where they are less, by random thermal motion. No protein needed. It looks directional because more molecules on the high-concentration side are bouncing into the membrane and crossing than on the other side. Nothing is being pulled. The net movement is a statistical consequence of where the starting molecules are. Forgetting this is one of the most common confusions in cell biology.

*Facilitated diffusion.* Polar or charged molecules that cannot cross the bilayer on their own use protein channels or carriers — but still move down their gradients, still without ATP. Glucose enters most cells through GLUT transporters (a family with different members in different tissues). Water uses aquaporins. Ions use ion-specific channels. The protein provides the path; the gradient pays the fare.

*Osmosis.* The diffusion of water through aquaporins or directly through the bilayer, driven by differences in solute concentration across the membrane. Water moves from where solutes are scarce to where they are concentrated — equivalently, from where water itself is more concentrated to where it is less. The working rule: water follows solutes. We will use this rule to do the bedside math in the next section.

**Active transport** moves molecules uphill, against their gradients, using ATP.

The most important active transporter in your body is the **Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase**, discovered by Jens Skou in the late 1950s. On the cytoplasmic side, the pump grabs three Na⁺ ions and one ATP. It hydrolyzes the ATP; the released energy drives a conformational change that opens the pump to the *outside* of the cell, releasing the three Na⁺. The pump then grabs two K⁺ from outside, changes shape again, and releases them into the cytoplasm. Three Na⁺ out, two K⁺ in, per ATP spent — running millions of cycles per second across every cell in your body.

The result: roughly ten times more Na⁺ outside the cell than inside; roughly thirty times more K⁺ inside than outside. Three positives exported for two positives imported leaves the interior slightly negative — the resting membrane potential. And the Na⁺ gradient that builds up is a stored resource, a battery that the cell spends on secondary tasks.

Here is what secondary active transport looks like in practice. Glucose cannot diffuse into intestinal cells against the high concentration inside. Instead, a sodium-glucose co-transporter binds both Na⁺ and glucose and lets Na⁺ flow downhill — back into the cell where it is scarce — dragging glucose uphill with it. The ATP was spent one step back, building the Na⁺ gradient. The co-transporter spends the gradient to do work on glucose. This is why SGLT2 inhibitors — a class of diabetes drugs — work by blocking this transporter in kidney cells, preventing glucose reabsorption and letting it spill into urine. The drug's target is a co-transporter. The co-transporter's power source is the Na⁺ gradient. The gradient's source is the Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase. Follow the chain.

About 20–25% of your basal metabolic energy goes to running the Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase across all cells. The brain alone, at roughly 2% of body mass, consumes about 20% of resting energy — mostly maintaining ion gradients in neurons. Every nerve impulse in this book begins with the Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase having already done its work.

**Bulk transport** handles oversized cargo. Endocytosis wraps large items — proteins, bacteria, LDL particles — in membrane and pulls the resulting vesicle inward. Exocytosis fuses internal vesicles with the plasma membrane and dumps contents outside — this is how neurotransmitters are released at a synapse. Familial hypercholesterolemia is a disease of defective LDL receptors: without them, cells cannot take up circulating LDL by receptor-mediated endocytosis; blood cholesterol climbs; atherosclerosis follows. The chemistry of one membrane protein determines whether a patient gets heart disease in their forties.

## The math that runs IV fluids

**Osmolarity** is the total concentration of all dissolved particles, in milliosmoles per liter (mOsm/L). Every dissolved particle contributes equally, regardless of charge or identity. When NaCl dissolves, each formula unit produces two particles (Na⁺ and Cl⁻), so osmolarity is roughly twice the molar concentration.

Plasma osmolarity is approximately 290 mOsm/L. A solution with the same osmolarity is *isotonic* — no net water movement across a cell membrane. Higher osmolarity outside means *hypertonic* — water leaves the cell, the cell shrinks. Lower osmolarity outside means *hypotonic* — water enters the cell, the cell swells, and eventually the membrane ruptures.

<!-- → [TABLE: Tonicity summary — columns: term, osmolarity relative to plasma, direction of water movement, effect on red blood cell, clinical example. Rows: hypertonic, isotonic, hypotonic.] -->

Three fluids, worked out:

**0.9% NaCl (normal saline).** 0.9 g NaCl per 100 mL = 9 g per liter. Molecular weight of NaCl = 58.5 g/mol.

$$\text{Molar concentration} = \frac{9 \text{ g/L}}{58.5 \text{ g/mol}} \approx 0.154 \text{ mol/L} = 154 \text{ mmol/L}$$

$$\text{Osmolarity} \approx 2 \times 154 = 308 \text{ mOsm/L}$$

Close to plasma osmolarity. Isotonic. A red blood cell placed in this solution stays the size it is.

**3% NaCl (hypertonic saline).** 30 g per liter:

$$\frac{30}{58.5} \approx 0.513 \text{ mol/L} \implies \text{osmolarity} \approx 1026 \text{ mOsm/L}$$

More than three times plasma osmolarity. Water exits cells rapidly; red blood cells shrink and develop the spiky surface called crenation. 3% NaCl is used clinically in small, controlled doses to treat cerebral edema — the logic being that pulling water out of swollen brain tissue is sometimes worth the risk.

**D5W (5% dextrose in water).** 50 g glucose per liter. Molecular weight of glucose = 180 g/mol. One particle per molecule.

$$\frac{50}{180} \approx 0.278 \text{ mol/L} = 278 \text{ mOsm/L}$$

Nearly isotonic when it goes in. But cells absorb the glucose within minutes. What remains is water. Effective osmolarity drops rapidly toward zero. D5W is, in effect, a delayed-release distilled water infusion. That is the mechanism behind the opening case.

The simple model has limits worth naming. Real cells in real tissues regulate their internal osmolyte composition over hours to days. Brain cells in chronic hyponatremia (low blood sodium) gradually export organic osmolytes — small molecules like taurine and myo-inositol — to match the diluted external environment. This is why correcting chronic hyponatremia *too fast* causes central pontine myelinolysis: the brain, having adapted to a low-sodium environment, is suddenly confronted with a much more concentrated plasma when sodium is brought back up rapidly. The cells that had exported their osmolytes now face a hypertonic environment and shrink catastrophically. The simple "water follows solutes" rule is right for seconds to minutes; over days, the cell is adjusting what is on the inside. The kidney chapter will return to this in detail.

## Protein synthesis, and why it matters for drugs

A gene is transcribed by RNA polymerase into a messenger RNA copy. The mRNA is processed, exits the nucleus through a pore, and in the cytoplasm a ribosome reads it three bases at a time, calling in the matching transfer RNA and linking the corresponding amino acid onto a growing chain. If the protein is destined for secretion, the ribosome binds to the rough ER and threads the chain in as it grows. The chain folds, gets modified, is vesicle-shipped to the Golgi, gets further modified, and is dispatched to its destination.

Three things to hold onto.

The genetic code is redundant but not ambiguous. Sixty-four codons, twenty amino acids plus three stop signals — most amino acids are specified by more than one codon, but each codon specifies exactly one thing. An mRNA read in the correct frame produces exactly one protein sequence.

Translation errors occur at roughly one per 10,000 amino acids added — much higher than DNA replication's one per billion, but most translation errors produce proteins that are degraded by quality control before causing harm.

The ribosome is a drug target. Bacterial ribosomes (70S) are structurally distinct from yours (80S). Aminoglycosides, macrolides, tetracyclines, and chloramphenicol all hit the 70S ribosome specifically enough to kill the bacterium without stopping your protein synthesis. The closer two organisms' ribosomes look, the harder selective toxicity becomes. Eukaryotic pathogens — fungi, parasites — are harder to hit this way.

## The cell cycle and what goes wrong in cancer

Cells divide on a schedule enforced by checkpoints. The cell cycle has four phases: **G1** (growth, protein synthesis), **S** (DNA replication), **G2** (growth, replication verification), and **M** (mitosis — the actual division). G1, S, and G2 together are interphase; mitosis is fast, often under an hour.

Mitosis proceeds through PMAT: **Prophase** (chromosomes condense; nuclear envelope breaks down; spindle forms), **Metaphase** (chromosomes align at the cell's equator, each attached to spindle fibers from both poles), **Anaphase** (spindle fibers pull the two copies of each chromosome to opposite poles), **Telophase** (new nuclear envelopes form; chromosomes decondense). **Cytokinesis** then pinches the cell in two with a contractile actin ring. Each daughter receives a complete genome.

Three checkpoints enforce quality:

- **G1/S:** Is the cell large enough? DNA undamaged? Nutrients adequate? All yes — enter replication. Any no — pause, or enter G0 (a long-term resting state).
- **G2/M:** Was replication complete and accurate? Any unrepaired double-strand breaks? Clean — enter mitosis. Not clean — pause for repair, or trigger apoptosis.
- **Spindle checkpoint:** Are all chromosomes properly attached to both spindle poles? Yes — proceed with anaphase. No — pause, because pulling an unattached chromosome scrambles the genome.

Cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) drive the cycle forward. Tumor suppressor proteins — p53, retinoblastoma protein (Rb), others — can halt it. When the system works, the cell divides when it should and cells with damaged DNA either repair it or die.

When the system fails, cancer begins. The hallmarks of cancer — sustained growth signaling, evasion of growth suppressors, resistance to apoptosis, replicative immortality, angiogenesis, invasion and metastasis — arise from a series of mutations accumulating over years that disable the checkpoints one by one. p53, mutated in over half of human cancers, normally triggers apoptosis in cells with unrepairable DNA damage. Without it, a cell with a broken genome divides. Its daughters inherit both the damage and the disabled checkpoint. Each generation accumulates more mutations. Eventually you have a clone that grows without permission, ignores stop signals, refuses to die, and recruits its own blood supply.

This is why cell biology is not abstract. The checkpoints are protein-protein networks built from the same bonds, shapes, and gradients we have been tracing all chapter. Break the chemistry in the right place and the cell forgets when to stop.

## Common misconceptions

**"Diffusion requires energy."** Passive diffusion, facilitated diffusion, and osmosis are all free — they run on thermal energy that is already there. Active transport is the only form that consumes ATP. The line between passive and active is exactly the line between "the gradient pays" and "the cell pays."

**"ATP is energy."** ATP is a molecule. Hydrolyzing the bond between its second and third phosphate releases about 7.3 kcal/mol in a form the cell can capture and spend. When you read "the cell uses ATP," translate it: *the cell hydrolyzes ATP and captures the released energy to do work*. Energy is not stored in ATP like water in a bottle. It is released when a specific bond breaks.

**"Mitochondria produce energy."** Mitochondria transduce energy — they convert chemical energy already stored in glucose and fat into the more usable form of ATP. They are converters, not sources.

**"DNA is the same in every cell, so cells should all be the same."** DNA is essentially identical across all nucleated cells of your body. What makes a liver cell a liver cell and a neuron a neuron is *which genes are expressed* — transcribed and translated — and which are silenced. Development sets the expression pattern; the pattern is then maintained. Two hundred-plus cell types. One genome. Gene regulation is the difference.

## What would change my mind

I would revise the central claim of this chapter — that physiology reduces, more or less cleanly, to chemistry and cell biology — if a well-replicated study identified a physiological behavior in human cells that could not be accounted for by the chemistry of bonds, gradients, and folded proteins, even in principle. Vague appeals to "emergence" do not count; the test is whether some specific, measurable, reproducible behavior resists mechanistic explanation. The current evidence is that the levels of the body are explanatorily continuous. "Continuous in principle" is not the same as "fully worked out in practice" — there is a great deal we do not yet understand about how molecular events scale into tissue and organ behavior — and I am open to surprises.

## Still puzzling

I do not fully understand why some lysosomal storage diseases cause devastating early neurological damage — Tay-Sachs is the clearest case — while others in the same biochemical family progress over years or decades. The underlying mechanism looks structurally identical: a missing enzyme, substrate accumulating in lysosomes, cells swelling and dying. But the clinical timing is wildly different, and the explanation seems to involve cell-type-specific vulnerabilities I would need to do more reading on before I felt I could teach it honestly.
