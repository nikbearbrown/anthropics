# Chapter 12 — The Digestive System and Metabolism
*From mouth to mitochondrion — one pipeline, one switch.*

---

In June 1984, a physician in Perth, Australia, drank a Petri dish of bacteria.

Barry Marshall had spent two years arguing that most peptic ulcers were caused by a bacterium — *Helicobacter pylori* — that colonized the stomach lining. The gastroenterology establishment was unimpressed. The conventional wisdom was that the stomach was too acidic for any organism to survive there, and that ulcers were caused by stress and acid hypersecretion. Journals rejected his papers. He ran out of patience. So he had himself scoped, confirmed his stomach was healthy, and drank the culture.

By day five he had developed gastritis. By day ten his wife had noticed he was vomiting in the mornings. A repeat endoscopy showed bacterial colonization and extensive mucosal inflammation. He treated himself with antibiotics, the gastritis resolved, and he and his colleague Robin Warren had finally produced direct evidence that a bacterium could colonize the human stomach and cause disease. Twenty-one years later, the Nobel Prize.

The story does two things for us. First, it makes the gastric environment concrete: the stomach is at pH 1.5 to 3.5, the acidity of battery acid, and the conventional wisdom was correct that almost nothing biological survives there. Marshall's bacterium is the exception. It survives by burrowing under the mucus layer and secreting urease, an enzyme that converts urea into ammonia and bicarbonate, creating a locally neutralized microenvironment. The bacterium does not refute the acid environment — it solves it.

Second, Marshall's experiment is a model of how to think about digestive disease: not as a label but as a mechanism. "Peptic ulcer disease" sounds like an entity. It is not. It is the consequence of a specific failure: the mucus layer that protects the stomach wall from its own acid and enzymes has been breached, and the same hydrochloric acid and pepsin that are supposed to digest food are now digesting tissue. The breach can come from bacterial infection, from chronic NSAID use that depletes mucus production, or from a tumor that drives acid hypersecretion autonomously. Three first causes, one downstream pathology. To diagnose anything in gastroenterology you have to know what each part of the system is supposed to do. That is what this chapter is about.

---

## The problem digestion solves

Your cells do not eat food. They eat *molecules* — glucose, amino acids, fatty acids, glycerol, ions. What arrives at your mouth is starch, protein, fat, and fiber — polymers and aggregates, none of which can cross a cell membrane.

The constraint is the membrane. The intestinal epithelium is a single layer of cells whose apical surface is a lipid bilayer about five nanometers thick. A whole protein — dozens to thousands of amino acids strung together — cannot dissolve through a lipid bilayer and cannot fit through any transporter. A triglyceride — three long fatty acid chains esterified to glycerol — cannot fit through a transporter and is too hydrophobic to stay in water long enough to diffuse anywhere useful in its intact form. A starch molecule — hundreds to thousands of glucose units linked end to end — cannot fit through anything.

Digestion is a size-reduction problem. Every polymer has to be cleaved to its monomers. Starch to glucose. Protein to amino acids. Triglycerides to fatty acids and monoglycerides. And those monomers have to be liberated in the right segment of the gut, with the right transporters waiting on the other side of the wall, with the right blood and lymph supply to carry them away.

Why not just absorb intact proteins? Two reasons. *Selectivity*: by reducing proteins to free amino acids, the body decouples itself from whatever organism it just ate. It does not need to recognize a cow's albumin or a peanut's storage protein — it absorbs the twenty standard amino acids and builds its own proteins from them. *Immunity*: an intact foreign protein, absorbed whole, would be recognized by the immune system as something to attack. Reducing food to monomers strips its molecular identity at the border.

The design is clean. It is not always realized cleanly — celiac disease is what happens when a fragment of wheat gluten survives digestion partially intact and the immune system finds it on the other side of the wall. We will come back to celiac. But the intent is clear: reduce food to a generic monomer mixture before anything crosses the epithelium.

The reduction is done by **hydrolases** — enzymes that break specific chemical bonds by inserting a water molecule across the bond. Amylase cleaves the bond between adjacent glucose units in starch. Proteases cleave peptide bonds between amino acids. Lipases cleave the ester bonds linking fatty acids to glycerol. Each enzyme is specific to one kind of bond. Each works best at a specific pH. Different enzymes are released into different segments of the tube at different stages of a meal. The tube's job is to stage the chemistry correctly.

## The tube, modified at every step

The gastrointestinal tract is one continuous tube, mouth to anus, roughly eight meters in a living adult. The same four-layer wall — mucosa, submucosa, muscularis, serosa — runs the entire length. But the details of each layer change at every segment to match what that segment does.

The **mouth** begins with mechanical work. Teeth crush food, multiplying its surface area perhaps a hundredfold — every subsequent chemical step depends on that surface area. Salivary glands deliver about 1.5 liters of saliva per day, containing salivary amylase, which begins hydrolyzing starch. By the time you swallow, perhaps five percent of the carbohydrate has been converted to shorter chains.

The **esophagus** is transport only — peristalsis moves the bolus to the stomach in about ten seconds. The lower esophageal sphincter keeps gastric contents from refluxing back up. When it fails, acid reaches the esophageal lining, which has no protective mucus, and the burning sensation is chemical injury to the epithelium.

The **stomach** stores, churns, and begins protein digestion. Its glands contain three specialized cell types. **Parietal cells** secrete hydrochloric acid — pH 1.5 to 3.5 — using an H⁺/K⁺ ATPase, the same pump that proton pump inhibitors block when treating ulcers. Parietal cells also secrete **intrinsic factor**, required for vitamin B12 absorption downstream; lose the stomach and you eventually lose B12 absorption. **Chief cells** secrete **pepsinogen**, an inactive zymogen that the acid cleaves into the active protease **pepsin** — an enzyme unusual in that it functions at the stomach's low pH. **Mucous cells** secrete the thick, bicarbonate-rich mucus layer that coats the gastric wall and prevents it from digesting itself. This is the barrier Marshall's bacterium burrowed under. This is the layer chronic NSAID use depletes.

The stomach barely absorbs anything — water, alcohol, some drugs. Salivary amylase is inactivated almost immediately by the acid. Fat sits untouched in large globules. Only protein is meaningfully digested in the stomach, and even then only to short peptides, not free amino acids. Everything else is staged for downstream.

The stomach churns and mixes for two to four hours, depending on meal composition. Fatty meals take longest, because hormonal feedback from the small intestine slows gastric emptying when fat is present — the intestine needs time to process it. The **pyloric sphincter** opens in small pulses, releasing about 3 mL of **chyme** — the soupy, acidic, partially digested mixture — into the duodenum at a time. Drip-feeding, not flooding.

---

## The small intestine: where everything happens

Six meters of tube — **duodenum** (25 cm, where pancreatic and biliary secretions enter), **jejunum** (2.5 m, where most absorption occurs), **ileum** (3.5 m, where vitamin B12 and bile salts are reclaimed). Essentially every macronutrient you absorb enters your blood here.

The first thing to understand about the small intestine is its surface area. The inner wall is folded at three nested scales.

**Circular folds** (plicae circulares) — visible ridges of the entire wall projecting into the lumen, amplifying surface area roughly threefold. **Villi** — finger-shaped mucosal projections 0.5 to 1.5 mm tall, amplifying surface another tenfold. Each villus has a core of blood capillaries and a single central lymphatic vessel called a **lacteal** (from *lac*, milk — after a fatty meal, the lacteals fill with lipid-rich lymph and turn white). **Microvilli** — submicroscopic projections packed so densely on each absorptive cell's apical surface they form a visible "brush border" under the electron microscope — amplifying surface another twenty- to thirtyfold.

Multiply: roughly 3 × 10 × 25 ≈ 750-fold amplification. A bare tube would have about 0.3 m². The folded intestine has somewhere between 30 and 400 m² — estimates vary by measurement method, but every method agrees the amplification is enormous. Something on the order of a tennis court of absorptive surface, packed into a tube three centimeters across.

This is the engineering answer to a time constraint. You have a few hours to absorb the nutrients from a meal before they pass out of the small intestine. The only way to do it fast enough is enormous contact surface. The only way to fit that much surface inside a body is to fold it three times over.

Celiac disease makes this concrete. The autoimmune reaction to gluten flattens the villi — they "blunt" from finger-like projections to low ridges. Total absorptive surface can fall 80 to 90 percent. The patient eats normally and starves anyway, because the dining table has been disassembled.

### The enzyme cocktail: pancreas and bile

When chyme arrives in the duodenum, two organs upstream deliver their secretions through ducts opening at the same point on the duodenal wall.

**Secretin**, released by enteroendocrine cells when they detect acidic chyme, travels in the blood to the pancreas and triggers bicarbonate release. Within minutes, the duodenal pH rises from 2 to 7. Without this neutralization, no pancreatic enzyme would function — they all need near-neutral pH.

**CCK** (cholecystokinin), released when fat and protein arrive, does two things simultaneously: contracts the gallbladder to deliver bile, and triggers the pancreas to release its enzyme arsenal.

The pancreatic enzyme arsenal is specific to the problem. **Pancreatic amylase** resumes starch digestion. **Pancreatic lipase** attacks triglycerides, cleaving off two of three fatty acid chains and leaving a monoglyceride — essentially *all* dietary fat absorption depends on this one enzyme. Its absence in cystic fibrosis produces steatorrhea: fat in the stool. **Pancreatic proteases** — trypsin, chymotrypsin, elastase, carboxypeptidases — attack protein from multiple angles.

The proteases are released as **zymogens** — inactive precursors. Trypsinogen is converted to trypsin by brush-border enterokinase; trypsin then activates the other zymogens in cascade. This is a safety mechanism: if the proteases were active inside the cells that made them, they would digest the pancreas itself. **Pancreatitis** is exactly that failure — premature zymogen activation inside pancreatic tissue, the organ digesting itself. Exquisitely painful. Potentially fatal.

**Bile**, produced continuously by the liver, is stored and concentrated in the gallbladder and released by CCK signaling. Bile salts are amphipathic — molecules with a hydrophobic end and a hydrophilic end, synthesized from cholesterol. In the duodenum they do something specific and necessary: they **emulsify** fat.

Dietary fat enters the duodenum as large globules. Pancreatic lipase is water-soluble; it can only attack the surface of a fat globule. A few large globules have very little surface area. The bile salts coat the globules, and mechanical mixing breaks them into droplets micrometers across, which the bile salts prevent from coalescing back. Surface area increases enormously. Lipase can now attack from every direction simultaneously.

This is not chemical digestion — the triglyceride bonds have not yet been cleaved. Emulsification is a surface-area solution to a kinetics problem.

After lipase has acted, the fatty acids and monoglycerides are still hydrophobic. The bile salts then form **mixed micelles** with them — spherical aggregates about 5 nm across, hydrophobic cargo inside, bile salt heads facing the watery lumen — that are stable in solution and small enough to diffuse to the brush border for absorption. The bile salts are not absorbed with the fat. They pass through the small intestine and are almost completely reclaimed in the terminal ileum, returning via portal blood to the liver. This **enterohepatic circulation** recycles the bile salt pool five to ten times per meal. Remove the terminal ileum and this recycling fails — fat malabsorption follows, because the liver cannot synthesize bile salts fast enough to compensate.

### The brush border: final cleavage and absorption

Pancreatic enzymes leave their work incomplete by design. Amylase produces disaccharides — pairs of sugars — but cannot cleave disaccharide bonds. Proteases produce short peptides but not always free amino acids. The final step happens at the brush border, in enzymes embedded directly in the apical membrane of each enterocyte.

**Maltase** cleaves maltose to two glucoses. **Sucrase** cleaves sucrose to glucose and fructose. **Lactase** cleaves lactose to glucose and galactose. **Peptidases** finish the peptides to free amino acids. Final cleavage happens *at* the absorptive surface — monomers are released directly adjacent to the transporters that will pull them in. There is no diffusion gap in which monomers might be lost.

Lactase is the famous one because most adult humans lose it. **Lactose intolerance** affects roughly two-thirds of adult humans globally — higher fractions in East Asian and many African populations. Undigested lactose reaches the colon, where bacteria ferment it, producing gas and pulling water osmotically into the lumen. Gas, cramping, diarrhea, two hours after a glass of milk. The entire syndrome is a brush-border enzyme deficiency.

### Two routes, two destinations

Monomers cross the wall by two completely different routes, ending up in different parts of the circulation.

**Glucose** crosses the apical membrane via **SGLT1** — the sodium-glucose cotransporter, which binds one glucose and two sodium ions and shuttles all three into the cell simultaneously. The driving force is the sodium gradient: the Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase on the basolateral membrane pumps sodium out continuously, keeping intracellular sodium low, so sodium's tendency to flow back in drags glucose along even when glucose is more concentrated inside than outside. Secondary active transport — the energy comes from ATP, but indirectly, via the gradient the pump maintains. Glucose exits the basolateral membrane via GLUT2, a passive transporter, into the villus capillary network. Amino acids use the same logic with Na⁺-coupled amino acid transporters.

The villus capillaries drain into the **hepatic portal vein** — a vein that runs from the gut directly to the liver, bypassing the systemic circulation. Everything absorbed as glucose or amino acids goes to the liver first. The liver inspects, processes, stores, or passes on every absorbed glucose molecule and every amino acid before they reach the rest of the body. This is the **first-pass effect**, and it is the reason the liver is the master metabolic organ.

**Fat** takes a different route entirely. Mixed micelles deliver fatty acids and monoglycerides to the brush border. The lipid products diffuse across the apical membrane directly through the lipid bilayer — they are hydrophobic enough to dissolve into it. Inside the enterocyte, they are reassembled into triglycerides, combined with cholesterol, phospholipids, and apolipoprotein B-48, and packaged into **chylomicrons** — large lipoprotein particles 75 to 1200 nm across. Chylomicrons are too large to enter blood capillaries. They are exported across the basolateral membrane and enter the **lacteal**, the lymphatic vessel in the villus core. They travel through the lymphatic system, up the thoracic duct, and drain into the systemic circulation at the left subclavian vein — bypassing the liver entirely on the first pass.

This routing difference matters. A high-fat meal raises blood lipid levels visibly within hours — serum turns milky from circulating chylomicrons — because the fat is in systemic blood before the liver can clear it. A high-carbohydrate meal does not produce the same visible effect because the liver intercepts the glucose and regulates its release. It also matters in disease: a cirrhotic patient with severe liver damage has impaired first-pass processing of glucose and amino acids, but dietary fat reaches their systemic circulation relatively intact, at least initially.

## The fed-fasted switch

Once absorbed monomers reach their destination, a second system decides what to do with them. This is where Chapter 8 left off.

The pancreatic islets secrete two hormones with opposite effects. **Insulin** says abundance: store everything. **Glucagon** says scarcity: release everything. What controls their relative levels is blood glucose — which depends on whether you have eaten recently. The **ratio of insulin to glucagon** — not the absolute level of either — is what the liver reads. A ratio of 30:1 means deep fed state, full anabolism. A ratio of 0.5:1 means starvation physiology. Both hormones are always present; what changes is their balance.

### The fed state — insulin dominates

Blood glucose rises as absorbed glucose pours in through the portal vein. Beta cells sense this and release insulin — often fivefold to tenfold above baseline. Glucagon falls.

In the **liver**: activate glycogen synthase, inhibit glycogen phosphorylase, suppress gluconeogenesis. The liver was a net glucose *producer* during the preceding fast; it now switches to a net glucose *consumer*, polymerizing absorbed glucose into glycogen. Excess glucose beyond glycogen capacity is converted to fatty acids and packaged as VLDL for export.

In **muscle**: insulin triggers the translocation of **GLUT4** glucose transporters from intracellular vesicles to the plasma membrane. Without insulin, muscle is nearly impermeable to glucose. With insulin, glucose floods in — some burned immediately, most stored as glycogen.

In **adipose tissue**: GLUT4 transporters mobilize. Adipocytes take up glucose and use it to make glycerol (the backbone for triglyceride storage). Lipoprotein lipase on adipose capillary endothelium cleaves triglycerides from chylomicrons; the fatty acids enter and are stored.

In the **brain**: no change. The brain uses GLUT1 and GLUT3 transporters that are insulin-independent. The brain takes up glucose whenever blood glucose is available, full stop, regardless of insulin status.

Insulin also stimulates amino acid uptake and protein synthesis throughout the body. The absorptive state is net anabolism.

### The fasted state — glucagon dominates

After four to six hours without food, blood glucose would fall if the body did not defend it. Glucagon rises. Insulin falls. The liver that was storing everything is now releasing everything.

Glucagon activates **glycogen phosphorylase** — liver glycogen is broken down, glucose released into blood. This **glycogenolysis** sustains blood glucose for the first six to eight hours. Glucagon also activates **gluconeogenesis** — production of new glucose from non-carbohydrate precursors: amino acids from muscle protein breakdown, glycerol from adipose triglyceride lipolysis, lactate from anaerobic glycolysis. In adipose tissue, falling insulin and rising glucagon activate hormone-sensitive lipase, releasing free fatty acids into the blood. Muscle and other tissues switch from burning glucose to burning fatty acids, preserving glucose for the brain.

The brain is the constraint the whole system is organized around. The brain requires about 120 grams of glucose per day and cannot use fatty acids directly. So the body's strategy during fasting is to *preserve glucose for the brain* — let everything else burn fat, while the liver keeps synthesizing glucose.

After roughly 24 hours, liver glycogen is depleted. Gluconeogenesis is doing all glucose production, and it requires amino acid substrate — which means breaking down muscle protein, which cannot continue indefinitely. So the liver converts excess acetyl CoA from high-rate fat oxidation into **ketone bodies** — acetoacetate and beta-hydroxybutyrate. The brain, over a few days, adapts to use ketone bodies as primary fuel, reducing its glucose demand by about 70 percent. Muscle protein breakdown slows dramatically. The body can now survive for weeks on fat reserves.

The priority hierarchy: glucose for the brain, fatty acids for everything else, muscle protein only as a last resort and only as slowly as possible.

## One meal, end to end

Run a single concrete meal through the whole pipeline to make the chapter cohere.

A patient eats a meal of 50 g carbohydrate, 50 g protein, 22 g fat. Call it about 600 kcal.

**Mouth.** Teeth grind. Salivary amylase converts perhaps 5% of the starch to shorter chains. The bolus is swallowed.

**Stomach.** HCl drops the pH to 2. Salivary amylase is denatured — carb digestion pauses. Pepsinogen is activated to pepsin and begins cleaving proteins into short peptides. Fat is untouched. The mass churns for 2 to 3 hours, then the pyloric sphincter releases it as chyme in 3 mL pulses.

**Duodenum.** Secretin detects the acidity and calls for bicarbonate — pH rises to 7 within minutes. CCK detects fat and protein and calls for bile and pancreatic enzymes. Bile salts emulsify the fat globules. Pancreatic amylase resumes starch digestion. Pancreatic lipase cleaves the triglycerides. Trypsin and the other proteases attack the peptides from multiple angles.

**Jejunum and ileum.** Brush border enzymes finish the job. Maltase, sucrase, lactase cleave the disaccharides to monosaccharides. Peptidases cleave the short peptides to free amino acids.

Glucose crosses the apical membrane via SGLT1 (driven by the sodium gradient), exits basolaterally via GLUT2, enters villus capillaries, and flows through the portal vein to the liver. The 50 g of carbohydrate — now roughly 280 mmol of monosaccharides — arrives at the liver first.

Amino acids cross via Na⁺-coupled transporters, exit basolaterally, enter portal blood, arrive at the liver. The 50 g of protein — now roughly 440 mmol of amino acids — also arrives at the liver first.

Fatty acids and monoglycerides diffuse across the apical membrane, are reassembled as triglycerides inside the enterocyte, packaged as chylomicrons, expelled into the lacteals, carried through the thoracic duct, and enter the systemic circulation at the left subclavian vein. The 22 g of fat arrives in systemic blood *bypassing the liver*.

**One to three hours post-meal.** Blood glucose rises. Insulin rises 8-fold above baseline. Glucagon falls. The liver activates glycogen synthase and begins storing glucose as glycogen. Muscle GLUT4 transporters translocate to the cell surface. Muscle takes up glucose and amino acids. Adipocytes take up glucose and fatty acids from circulating chylomicrons. Brain takes up glucose as always.

**By hour four.** The gut has delivered its load. Blood glucose returns to baseline. Insulin falls. Glucagon begins to rise. Liver glycogen phosphorylase begins activating. The liver transitions from storage to release. The absorptive state ends.

**Hours four through twelve.** The liver sustains blood glucose through glycogenolysis. Muscle and adipose switch toward fat oxidation. The brain continues consuming glucose, now supplied by the liver rather than by absorbed dietary glucose.

**After hour twelve.** Glycogen is becoming depleted. Gluconeogenesis ramps up — using glycerol from adipose lipolysis and amino acids from a slow trickle of muscle protein breakdown. Blood glucose stays stable, because that is what the system is designed to do.

The lesson: digestion and metabolism are not two separate systems. They are one continuous pipeline from mouth to mitochondrion, regulated minute-to-minute by the insulin/glucagon balance. The meal you eat at 7:00 PM is still directing your liver's metabolic activity at 7:00 AM.

The limit: Type 1 diabetes removes insulin. Glucose absorbed from a meal stays in the blood instead of being stored, and the body behaves as if it is in permanent starvation despite abundant circulating glucose — breaking down fat and muscle simultaneously, driving glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis and ketogenesis in a patient who just ate. Diabetic ketoacidosis is a fed-state disease in which the body acts as if it is starving. Take away the insulin signal and the two halves of the pipeline cannot communicate.

### Extend — toward Chapter 13

Everything the digestive system absorbs is processed by the liver. Everything the body burns produces waste — CO₂ (exhaled, Chapter 11), water, and nitrogen-containing compounds, principally urea from amino acid catabolism. The kidneys remove the urea. That is Chapter 13.

Ask your model:

```
Trace one nitrogen atom from an amino acid in a steak: from absorption
across the duodenal wall, through the portal vein to the liver, through
the urea cycle (which converts toxic ammonia to less toxic urea), into
systemic blood, to the kidney glomerulus, through the filtration
barrier, and out in urine. Identify the single step in this path where
a clinical failure — liver failure or kidney failure — would have the
most rapid lethal consequence, and explain why.
```

The answer is your first taste of Chapter 13. The digestive system gave you the nutrients. The urinary system disposes of the waste.

Chapter 13 takes the nitrogen atom you just traced and asks how the kidney builds the filter that catches it, regulates what it keeps and what it throws away, and maintains the blood chemistry that every other organ depends on.

**What would change my mind.** The chapter is built around a two-actor framing: the digestive system delivers monomers; the insulin/glucagon balance decides what to do with them. If careful longitudinal studies showed that gut microbiome signaling — short-chain fatty acid effects on enteroendocrine cells, microbial influence on bile acid pools — was a *primary* regulator of the fed/fasted switch rather than a secondary modulator, the framing would need a third actor added, not relegated to context.

**Still puzzling.** Why does the small intestine, with its massive surface area and rich enzyme repertoire, carry far less cancer risk than the colon? What evolutionary pressure maintains the enterohepatic circulation of bile salts so precisely — the system clearly works, but the failure mode (fat malabsorption after ileal resection) is serious, and continuous de-novo synthesis would seem simpler? And across the enormous correlational literature linking gut microbiome composition to obesity, depression, immune function, and metabolic syndrome, which associations are causal in the microbiome → host direction, which are reverse-causal, and which are confounded — and how do we design human studies to distinguish them without another twenty years of correlational accumulation?
