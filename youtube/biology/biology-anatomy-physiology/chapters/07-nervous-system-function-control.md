*The body runs three control systems simultaneously. Most of the time you are aware of exactly one of them.*

---

A 34-year-old man walks into an emergency department with his face swelling, hives across his chest, and breathing that sounds like air being forced through a nearly-closed valve. He ate a granola bar twenty minutes ago. It contained peanut. His blood pressure is 78/40. His pupils are wide. He is in anaphylaxis, and without intervention in the next several minutes he will die.

The nurse injects 0.3 milligrams of epinephrine into the muscle of his outer thigh. Within ninety seconds — you can watch this if you have ever seen it, and it is one of the most striking things in medicine — three things change at once. Blood pressure climbs. The wheeze softens. The face begins to look like a face again.

Here is the puzzle. Epinephrine is a single molecule. It just reversed three separate problems in three separate organ systems: the skin is depuffing, the lungs are opening, the blood pressure is recovering. How does one drug fix three different things simultaneously?

The answer is that they were never three different things. They were one problem — runaway signaling in the wrong direction — and epinephrine fixed it by activating the opposing team. The skin's blood vessels have alpha receptors that constrict when epinephrine arrives. The lung's smooth muscle has beta-2 receptors that relax when epinephrine arrives. The heart has beta-1 receptors that pump harder when epinephrine arrives. Same molecule, three different receptor types, three different tissues, each responding with exactly what the patient needed.

This is the autonomic nervous system in one syringe.

But the autonomic system is only one of three control systems this chapter is about. The other two — voluntary motor control and the sensory pathways that feed both — are less dramatic to watch, but just as elegantly designed. And all three turn out to share a single architectural principle: the arrangement of who crosses the midline, and where, is what makes the pattern of any injury readable.

## Voluntary motion: two neurons, one crossing

When you decide to lift your coffee cup, something specific happens in the brain. The decision begins in the prefrontal cortex and propagates back into the **primary motor cortex** — a strip of tissue running across the top of the brain just in front of the central sulcus, containing the cell bodies of the **upper motor neurons** (UMN). These are among the largest neurons in the body. Their axons descend through the cerebrum, through the brainstem, and reach the bottom of the medulla, where most of them cross the midline at a structure called the **pyramidal decussation**. The word *decussation* just means crossing — from the Latin for X — and it is the moment when left becomes right.

The crossed axons continue down the spinal cord in the **corticospinal tract**. At the appropriate level — cervical for the arms, lumbar for the legs — they synapse onto the **lower motor neuron** (LMN), whose cell body sits in the ventral horn of the spinal cord gray matter. The LMN's axon exits through the ventral root, joins a peripheral nerve, travels to skeletal muscle, and releases acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction. The muscle contracts. The cup lifts.

Two neurons. One crossing. That is the whole voluntary motor system in structural summary.

But the two-tier design is doing more than it looks. The LMN is what engineers would call the final common pathway — every motor command from anywhere in the nervous system, from cortex or cerebellum or spinal reflex, eventually has to go through the LMN to reach the muscle. There is no other route. Kill the LMN and the muscle cannot be moved by any means, ever again. The LMN also sustains the muscle: it continuously releases growth factors that keep muscle fibers alive. A muscle whose LMN has been severed begins wasting within days, whether it is exercised or not.

The UMN, by contrast, does not just command — it also *inhibits*. Much of what the upper motor neuron sends down the cord is a suppressive signal telling the LMN to restrain its reflex responses. Tap your patellar tendon and your quadriceps kicks — but it kicks once, briefly, because the UMN is whispering "ease up." Take the UMN away, and the LMN's reflexes run unchecked.

This is the key to reading motor lesions. Two kinds of damage, opposite signatures.

An **upper motor neuron lesion** — a stroke in the motor cortex, a tumor compressing the corticospinal tract, a demyelinating plaque in the cord — produces spastic paralysis. The muscles are weak because the command is gone. But they are stiff because reflex inhibition is gone. Deep tendon reflexes are exaggerated — *hyperreflexia*. Muscle bulk is preserved, because the LMN is still alive and still releasing trophic factors. The weakness is on the *opposite* side from the lesion, because the corticospinal tract crossed in the medulla.

A **lower motor neuron lesion** — polio destroying ventral horn cells, a nerve cut by trauma, ALS killing motor neurons — produces flaccid paralysis. Weak, floppy, wasting. Deep tendon reflexes are absent — *areflexia* — because the LMN is part of the reflex arc and it is gone. The weakness is on the *same* side as the lesion, because the LMN never crossed.

One more finding completes the picture. Brush the lateral edge of an adult's sole from heel to toe. Normally the toes curl downward. In someone with a UMN lesion, the big toe extends upward and the other toes fan out — the *positive Babinski*, or extensor plantar response. The mechanism is worth sitting with. Infants are born with this response. As the corticospinal tract myelinates over the first eighteen months of life, descending inhibition establishes itself and the toes start curling down. When a UMN lesion destroys the corticospinal tract in an adult, the inhibition is lost and the primitive infantile reflex re-emerges. The exam does not just show damage — it shows a developmental layer being peeled back by the lesion.

Two systems sculpt voluntary movement without initiating it. The **cerebellum** is the error-correction engine. It compares the motor command (sent down from the cortex) with sensory feedback (proprioception, vision, vestibular input) and computes the discrepancy, sending corrections back through the brainstem in milliseconds. Cerebellar damage leaves the muscles strong and the commands reaching them, but movement becomes clumsy: overshoot (*dysmetria*), wide-based staggering gait (*ataxia*), tremor that worsens as the limb approaches its target (*intention tremor*). The cerebellum does not initiate. It edits.

The **basal ganglia** — substantia nigra, striatum, globus pallidus — are a gatekeeper for movement initiation. They hold back unwanted movement and release wanted movement. The gate is dopamine-operated: neurons in the substantia nigra release dopamine onto the striatum, which lets movement proceed. When those neurons die — which is what happens slowly in **Parkinson's disease** — movement initiation becomes a struggle. Tremor at rest, rigidity, slowness of movement, a shuffling gait that takes many steps to start. Give these patients **levodopa**, which the brain converts to dopamine, and often they can stand and walk within fifteen minutes of a dose. The first time a clinician sees this, it does not feel like medicine. It feels like turning a switch back on — which, mechanically, is exactly what it is. Dopamine concentration restored, gatekeeper allows movement, motor system resumes.

Three subsystems: corticospinal for execution, cerebellar for correction, basal ganglia for initiation. Three failure modes with three distinct signatures. The architecture makes the diagnosis.

## Two roads for sensation

The sensory system has two parallel ascending pathways carrying different modalities, and they cross the midline at *different levels*. That is the entire secret of sensory localization.

Start at the periphery. Sensory receptors are transducers — structures that convert a physical or chemical stimulus into an electrical signal. Mechanoreceptors for pressure and vibration, thermoreceptors for temperature, nociceptors for tissue damage, proprioceptors for limb position. Each receptor type is tuned to one modality and largely deaf to others, which is what allows the brain to distinguish a sharp pin from a hot surface from a heavy load.

From the receptor, the signal travels up a peripheral nerve to the dorsal root ganglion, enters the cord through the dorsal root, and then takes one of two paths.

**Fine touch and proprioception** enter the cord and do not synapse. The axon turns directly upward and travels in the white matter of the **dorsal column** — the posterior cord — all the way to the medulla. There, at last, it synapses. The second neuron crosses the midline in the medulla and ascends as the **medial lemniscus** to the thalamus, then to the somatosensory cortex. This is the **dorsal column-medial lemniscal (DCML) pathway**. It crosses *in the medulla*.

**Pain and temperature** enter the cord and synapse *immediately* in the dorsal horn gray matter. The second neuron crosses the midline right there — at the level where the signal entered — and ascends on the opposite side as the **spinothalamic tract** to the thalamus, then to cortex. This is the anterolateral pathway. It crosses *in the cord*.

![Spinal cord cross-section showing both pathways ](../images/07-nervous-system-function-control-fig-02.png)
*Figure 7.2 — Spinal cord cross-section showing both pathways *

The diagnostic consequence of two pathways with two different crossing levels is remarkable. Consider a **hemisection** of the cord at T6 — the right half is severed (Brown-Séquard syndrome). Below T6:

- **Right side loses fine touch and proprioception** — the right dorsal column is cut, and those fibers hadn't crossed yet.
- **Right side retains pain and temperature** — those fibers from the right body crossed at entry and are now ascending on the *left* cord, which is intact.
- **Left side retains fine touch and proprioception** — the left dorsal column is intact.
- **Left side loses pain and temperature** — those fibers from the left body crossed at entry to the right cord, which is cut.

Same patient, same lesion, crossed pattern: one side loses one modality, the other side loses the other. You can reconstruct this every time from the single rule: DCML crosses in the medulla, spinothalamic crosses in the cord.

The sensory signals arrive ultimately at the **primary somatosensory cortex**, mapped somatotopically across the parietal lobe. But the map is not proportional to body surface area — it is proportional to receptor density and behavioral importance. The hands and lips are enormous. The trunk and back are tiny. This distorted map, the *somatosensory homunculus*, is the brain's allocation of cortical real estate according to what matters most, not how large it is.

## The autonomic system: two teams in continuous competition

Now the part of the chapter where almost every drug a physician prescribes and almost every visceral symptom a patient feels actually lives.

The autonomic nervous system controls everything you do not control. Heart rate, blood pressure, pupil diameter, bronchial muscle tone, gut peristalsis, glandular secretion, sweating, bladder contraction, the diameter of blood vessels. You are not running any of these from the cortex. You cannot even decide to run most of them.

The architectural fact that organizes everything else: the ANS is built on **explicit competition**. Two divisions, and they fight. The **sympathetic** division says go. The **parasympathetic** division says wait. Almost every major organ receives input from both — dual innervation — and the organ's behavior at any moment is the *net* outcome of the two, not a setpoint decided from above.

Your resting heart rate is not a number the brain picks. It is what comes out when the parasympathetic brake and the sympathetic accelerator are both pressing on the sinoatrial node simultaneously, one winning by a margin. When you start running, the sympathetic accelerates while the parasympathetic simultaneously withdraws. Both teams contribute to the change. This is why the heart can shift from 60 to 180 beats per minute in seconds — not because one signal is going from low to high, but because one is going up while the other is going down.

### The two-neuron chain

Both autonomic divisions share a structural feature that distinguishes them from the somatic motor system. The somatic system has one neuron from spinal cord to muscle. The autonomic system uses *two* neurons in series: a **preganglionic neuron** from the CNS to a **ganglion** (a cluster of cell bodies outside the CNS), and a **postganglionic neuron** from the ganglion to the target organ. The location of the ganglion, and the neurotransmitter used at each step, differ between the divisions.

![Structural comparison of the two autonomic divisions ](../images/07-nervous-system-function-control-fig-04.png)
*Figure 7.4 — Structural comparison of the two autonomic divisions *

### Sympathetic: broad, divergent, norepinephrine

The sympathetic division — its preganglionic neurons originating from the thoracic and upper lumbar cord — is built for broad activation. Its ganglia form a **paravertebral chain** running alongside the vertebral column. A single preganglionic fiber entering the chain can branch and synapse on multiple ganglionic neurons at multiple levels, each sending a postganglionic fiber to a different target. This is the anatomy of divergence: one input, body-wide coordination.

At the ganglion, the preganglionic fiber releases **acetylcholine** onto **nicotinic receptors** — ion channels that open directly, producing fast depolarization. From the ganglion, the long postganglionic fiber releases **norepinephrine (NE)** at the target organ, binding to **adrenergic receptors**.

The receptor subtypes are what make pharmacology predictable:

- **α1**: on vascular smooth muscle in skin and gut → constriction; on the iris radial muscle → dilation; on bladder sphincter → contraction.
- **β1**: predominantly on the heart → faster rate, stronger contraction, faster conduction.
- **β2**: on bronchial smooth muscle → dilation; on vascular smooth muscle in skeletal muscle and coronaries → dilation; on the liver → glycogen breakdown.

One dose of norepinephrine produces a profile of effects, not a single effect — because norepinephrine lands on different receptor types on different tissues simultaneously.

The beautiful exception: the **adrenal medulla** is innervated *directly* by sympathetic preganglionic fibers, with no postganglionic neuron. The cells of the medulla (which are themselves modified postganglionic neurons that lost their axons during development) dump **epinephrine** and norepinephrine directly into the bloodstream. The signal is now hormonal — traveling to every tissue with adrenergic receptors, lasting minutes rather than seconds. The local synapse is fast and targeted. The adrenal release is slower and global. The body uses both, in sequence, calibrated to how serious the threat is.

### Parasympathetic: narrow, local, acetylcholine

The parasympathetic division — its preganglionic neurons from the brainstem (cranial nerves III, VII, IX, X) and the sacral cord (S2–S4) — is built for precision. Its ganglia are **terminal ganglia**, located inside or immediately adjacent to the target organs. The preganglionic fiber is long; the postganglionic fiber is short. This gives fine control: the parasympathetics can tell the stomach to increase peristalsis without simultaneously telling the heart to slow.

The chemistry is simpler throughout: **acetylcholine at every synapse**. At the ganglion, ACh binds to nicotinic receptors (same as the sympathetic). At the target organ, ACh binds to **muscarinic receptors** — G-protein-coupled, slower than nicotinic, with five subtypes (M1–M5) distributed differently across tissues.

The single most important parasympathetic nerve is the **vagus** (CN X), which delivers parasympathetic input to the heart, lungs, and most of the gut. Vagal tone is what keeps resting heart rate below the sinoatrial node's intrinsic firing rate — about 100 beats per minute without any input. The average adult resting rate is 60–80 bpm. The 20–40 bpm difference is the **vagal brake**: continuous parasympathetic input slowing the SA node. Your resting heart rate is your heart's natural rate held back.

### The organ map

With the receptor framework in place, the effects of each division on every organ are predictable rather than memorized.

- **Heart**: Sympathetic (β1) → HR up, contractility up. Parasympathetic (M2, via vagus) → HR down, conduction slowed.
- **Bronchi**: Sympathetic (β2) → dilation. Parasympathetic (M3) → constriction.
- **Pupils**: Sympathetic (α1 on radial muscle) → dilation (*mydriasis*). Parasympathetic (M3 on circular muscle) → constriction (*miosis*).
- **GI tract**: Sympathetic → motility down, sphincters contracted. Parasympathetic (M3) → motility up, secretion up, sphincters relaxed.
- **Bladder**: Sympathetic (α1 on sphincter, β3 on detrusor) → retention. Parasympathetic (M3 on detrusor) → voiding.
- **Sweat glands**: Sympathetic only — but using **acetylcholine** at muscarinic receptors, not norepinephrine. This is the exception worth memorizing: sympathetic cholinergic. There is no parasympathetic innervation to sweat glands.
- **Vasculature**: Mostly sympathetic. α1 dominates in skin and gut (vasoconstriction); β2 dominates in skeletal muscle and coronaries (vasodilation). Net effect of sympathetic activation: blood redirected from gut and skin toward muscle and heart.

The eye makes the competition visible in real time. Walk into bright light: parasympathetic activation through the oculomotor nerve releases ACh on M3 receptors of the iris sphincter, circular fibers contract, pupil constricts. Walk into darkness: parasympathetic drive falls, sympathetic norepinephrine acts on α1 receptors of the iris dilator, radial fibers contract, pupil dilates. Same organ, opposite muscles, opposite neurotransmitters.

### Higher control

The autonomic divisions are orchestrated by the **hypothalamus**, which receives information about body temperature, blood osmolarity, blood pressure, blood glucose, and emotional state, and decides the sympathetic-parasympathetic balance from moment to moment. The amygdala sends inputs to the hypothalamus. A predator triggers amygdala activation, which triggers hypothalamic sympathetic output. A deadline does the same. The hypothalamus cannot distinguish a predator from a deadline, which is why the sympathetic system gets activated chronically by stressors that no fight-or-flight response can resolve. The cardiovascular cost over years — damaged arterial endothelium, hypertension, accelerated atherosclerosis — is real and mechanistic. This is not a metaphor for "stress causes heart disease." It is the anatomical pathway.

## Predicting drug effects from the receptor map

The receptor map is not a table to memorize. It is a tool that generates predictions.

**Epinephrine** — a non-selective adrenergic agonist binding α1, α2, β1, and β2 — produces a profile that is exactly what the anaphylactic patient needed:

- α1 on skin and gut vessels: vasoconstriction, blood pressure rises.
- β1 on the heart: cardiac output increases.
- β2 on bronchi: dilation, airway resistance falls.
- β2 on mast cells: histamine release suppressed, inflammatory cascade slows.

One molecule, four receptor types, four simultaneous effects. The drug is not magic. It speaks every dialect the body uses to mobilize — which is exactly what you need when the body is mobilizing in the wrong direction.

**Atropine** — a muscarinic receptor antagonist — blocks ACh at all muscarinic sites, leaving sympathetic tone unopposed. You can predict its effects by asking, for each organ: what does the parasympathetic system normally do here, and what happens when you remove it?

- Heart (M2 blocks vagal brake): heart rate rises. This is why atropine is given for symptomatic bradycardia.
- Pupils (M3 on sphincter blocked): dilation, loss of accommodation, blurred near vision.
- GI tract (M3 blocked): motility down, secretion down, dry mouth.
- Bladder (M3 blocked): urinary retention.
- Sweat glands (muscarinic, sympathetic cholinergic — remember the exception): decreased sweating, dry hot skin.
- Bronchi (M3 blocked): bronchodilation.

Atropine toxicity is this list made clinical: tachycardia, dilated pupils, blurred vision, dry mouth, urinary retention, hot dry skin, decreased bowel sounds. The classical mnemonic — "dry as a bone, red as a beet, hot as a hare, blind as a bat, mad as a hatter" — is just the receptor map in verse.

**Organophosphate poisoning** — from agricultural pesticides or nerve agents — runs the map in the opposite direction. The drug inhibits acetylcholinesterase, the enzyme that breaks down ACh in the synaptic cleft. Without the enzyme, ACh accumulates everywhere: at every muscarinic synapse and every nicotinic synapse. Massive parasympathetic over-activation: profuse salivation, lacrimation, urination, defecation, GI distress, emesis, bradycardia, bronchoconstriction, miosis. The antidote? Atropine — block the muscarinic receptors, stop the runaway stimulation. The pharmacology is the receptor map read in reverse.

The general lesson: the autonomic system is a balance, and drugs that perturb one side produce predictable imbalance on the other side. If you know the receptor map, you can derive the clinical syndrome before the textbook describes it.

## Localization: the neurological exam as an address

The nervous system is anatomically organized — different functions live in different places — and damage in one place produces a recognizable pattern of deficits. The neurological exam exploits this. It has five sections, and the pattern of what is intact versus what is impaired is the address of the lesion.

1. **Mental status**: orientation, memory, language, attention. Tests the cerebral cortex. Language deficits point to left frontal or temporal cortex; spatial neglect to right parietal.
2. **Cranial nerves**: the twelve nerves from the brain itself, each emerging at a specific brainstem level. Tests the brainstem level by level.
3. **Motor**: strength, tone, bulk, reflexes, Babinski. Tests the corticospinal tract and the LMNs.
4. **Sensory**: light touch, pinprick, vibration, proprioception. Tests the two ascending pathways.
5. **Coordination**: finger-to-nose, heel-to-shin, gait. Tests the cerebellum.

The brainstem is small — roughly the size of a thumb — and densely packed. Each cranial nerve exits at a specific level: midbrain (CN III, IV), pons (CN V, VI, VII, VIII), medulla (CN IX, X, XI, XII). A lesion damages the corticospinal tract *and* the cranial nerve nuclei at the same level. Which nerves are affected tells you where along the brainstem the lesion lives.

The tongue makes this exact. The **hypoglossal nerve** (CN XII) controls the tongue muscles and exits from the medulla. Stick your tongue out: in a healthy patient it goes straight. In a patient with a right medullary lesion damaging the right hypoglossal nucleus, the tongue deviates *to the right*. Why toward the side of the damage? Because the cranial nerve nuclei are LMNs — they have not crossed. The right hypoglossal nucleus drives the right tongue muscles. Damage the nucleus, and the right side is weak; the intact left side pushes harder, and the tongue deviates right.

Now put it together. A patient presents with right arm and leg weakness (UMN pattern — contralateral, so the lesion is left-sided), a positive Babinski on the right, and a tongue that deviates to the right. Right tongue deviation means right hypoglossal nucleus is damaged, which is in the right medulla. But the arm and leg weakness is contralateral to the lesion — the corticospinal tract has crossed or is crossing at the medulla level, and the crossing point places the lesion in the right medulla. This is a medial medullary syndrome. The five-minute exam localized it. The MRI confirms.

![Cross-section of the medulla showing the medial medullary](../images/07-nervous-system-function-control-fig-06.png)
*Figure 7.6 — Cross-section of the medulla showing the medial medullary*

Language deserves a paragraph because it is the best example of cortical localization and the one most tested on the wards. **Broca's area** in the left inferior frontal gyrus generates speech. Damage produces *expressive aphasia*: the patient understands but cannot produce fluent speech — halting, telegraphic, effortful. **Wernicke's area** in the left superior temporal gyrus comprehends language. Damage produces *receptive aphasia*: the patient speaks fluently but with nonsensical content ("word salad") and cannot understand what others say. The two regions connect via a white-matter bundle, the arcuate fasciculus; damage to the bundle alone produces *conduction aphasia* — speech and comprehension intact, but the patient cannot repeat what was just said. Three aphasia syndromes, three anatomical locations, predictable from the function of each area.

## What this chapter is really arguing

Three control systems, one underlying logic.

Voluntary motor control uses two neurons in series, crossing in the medulla. The crossing level is why a left cortical stroke paralyzes the right body. The two-tier design (UMN suppressing LMN) is why the two kinds of motor lesion have opposite signatures. The anatomy generates the diagnosis.

Sensory encoding uses two parallel pathways crossing at different levels — one in the medulla, one in the cord. The different crossing levels are why a spinal cord hemisection produces a crossed pattern of deficits rather than simple ipsilateral or contralateral loss. The anatomy generates the diagnosis.

Autonomic control uses two opposing teams in continuous competition. The receptor types are specific and consistent across the body. This is why a single drug that alters the balance — epinephrine, atropine, pilocarpine, propranolol — produces predictable effects across multiple organs simultaneously. The pharmacology is readable from the anatomy.

In every case, the organizing principle is the same: *where* the signal crosses, and *what* it crosses to, is what makes the result legible. The nervous system is not arbitrary. It is structured. And structure means that every deficit is an address, every drug effect is a prediction, and every exam finding is the system talking.

### Constrain

The simulator must encode the *exceptions* correctly. Sweat glands fire to sympathetic cholinergic stimulation — atropine should decrease sweating even though sweating is sympathetic, because the postganglionic neurotransmitter there is ACh on muscarinic receptors. The adrenal medulla effect (epinephrine release) should appear as a body-wide amplification of the sympathetic bars, not a local target. Vascular smooth muscle in skin should respond to α1 (constriction); in muscle and coronaries to β2 (dilation). The numbers do not have to be perfectly clinical, but the *direction* of every effect must be correct.

Tell the LLM: "Generate at least three test cases I can run by hand to verify the model. For each, name the slider position, drug, and organ, and tell me what the bars and response panel should show."

### Verify

Once you have the file, open it in a browser and run the test cases. Then run several scenarios through your simulator — a patient on propranolol given epinephrine; a patient with organophosphate poisoning receiving atropine; a patient with glaucoma given pilocarpine eye drops. If the simulator's answers do not match your predictions from the receptor map, something is wrong: either in the simulator or in your prediction. Find which one. If the code says atropine increases sweating, that's a bug — and tracking down the bug forces you to articulate the receptor logic more precisely than you did before.

### Explore

With the simulator working, explore. What happens to the heart rate of a patient on propranolol when stress rises from 0 to 100? What happens to the pupil of a patient on atropine in a dark room? What happens to the GI tract in extreme stress without medication?

Extend: modify the simulator to add a second drug option (beta-blocker plus epinephrine — what happens?). Or add a baseline pathology toggle — make the patient diabetic with autonomic neuropathy, and see what the orthostatic response looks like.

### Extension to Chapter 8

The nervous system you have just modeled operates on millisecond timescales. Heart rate adjusts within seconds. Pupil constricts within a second of light hitting the retina. Chapter 8 examines the **endocrine system** — hormones, the slow control architecture operating on minutes to hours to days. The adrenal medulla you met here is one bridge between the two: it receives a neural signal and converts it into a hormonal one. The adrenal *cortex* — sitting just outside the medulla, producing cortisol and aldosterone — is purely endocrine, and its tempo is entirely different.

Ask your LLM: "Sympathetic activation through nerves and activation through the adrenal medulla both increase heart rate. Why does the body have both? What does adrenal medullary epinephrine do that direct sympathetic neural input cannot do?" The answer points at duration (hormonal lasts minutes; neural lasts seconds), amplitude (medullary release reaches every tissue simultaneously), and the scale of situation each is suited for. The bridge to Chapter 8 is right there.

## What would change my mind

If the sympathetic-parasympathetic two-team model turned out to be a substantial oversimplification — if, for instance, the enteric nervous system or some third visceral subsystem were doing more independent regulatory work than the textbook duality allows for, in ways that change predictions about common drugs — I would need to revise the chapter's central framework. The pharmacology would still work, because the receptors are real, but the conceptual map would need redrawing.

## Still puzzling

Why does the brain not provide more conscious control over autonomic function? Yogis and biofeedback subjects can demonstrably modulate heart rate and blood pressure with training. The capacity is there. The access is restricted, for reasons I do not fully understand. And a different puzzle: why does the cerebellum — which contains roughly half of all the neurons in the brain — contribute so little to conscious experience? You can lose substantial cerebellar tissue and notice only clumsier movement. A structure that massive producing no apparent subjective signal of its own is genuinely strange.
