# Introduction

A worm a millimetre long is crossing a dish of agar in a darkened room, and it is about to make a decision.

You cannot see the decision. You can see the worm — a pale comma under the stereo microscope, transparent enough that you could count its cells if you had the patience, which someone once did: nine hundred and fifty-nine of them, three hundred and two of which are neurons. You can see the thin stripe of copper sulfate painted across the agar ahead of it, a chemical the worm reads as poison, every sensor it owns lighting up in alarm. And on the far side of the copper you can see what the worm cannot yet reach: food, a smear of bacteria, the only thing in this small flat universe worth crossing for.

The worm approaches the copper. It recoils. It tries again, a little to the left. Recoils again. And then — if it is hungry enough, if the pull of the bacteria has grown loud enough to outshout the alarm — it lowers its head and goes through the poison to the other side.

That is a decision. A bad outcome weighed against a good one, a cost set against a reward, a choice made and committed to by an animal with three hundred and two neurons and no brain in any sense you would recognise. Nothing about it is grand. The worm is not aware that it chose. It will be dead in a few days, having produced several hundred offspring and crossed a few more copper lines. But sit with it for a moment, because the thing the worm just did is the thing this book is about, all the way up.

The thing is *intelligence* — and the first surprise is that nobody can tell you what that word means.

This is not a failure of effort. In 1986 two psychologists asked roughly two dozen of the leading experts on intelligence to write down, plainly, what they meant by the word, and got back two dozen definitions that did not fit together. Eight years later a different group of fifty-two researchers signed an open letter declaring the matter settled — and could not get a majority of the experts they mailed it to even to add their names. A hundred years of careful work, and the field cannot agree on its central term. You could read that as a scandal. I read it as a clue. The word *intelligence* has been doing different jobs for different people, and each job is legitimate, and the path forward is not to crown a winner but to figure out which job you need the word to do and then use it for that job without pretending the others have vanished.

So here is the argument this book makes, stated once, plainly, and then earned across fourteen chapters.

Intelligence is not one thing. It is not a single quantity that accumulates along a ladder with bacteria at the bottom and humanity at the top, each rung a little more of the same stuff. It is a *family* of distinct capacities — steering, learning, memory, emotion, perception, navigation, prediction, planning, reading other minds, knowing what you do not know, and a handful more — and each member of that family has its own evolutionary history, its own mechanism, its own ecological problem it was shaped to solve. They do not all rise together. The desert ant that walks home across a featureless salt pan in a dead-straight line runs a navigational computation more precise than anything in your head, and it is not a failed human; it is a superb ant. A fish passes a mirror test that a gorilla fails. A scrub-jay plans for a hunger it does not yet feel. On no single measure do humans win every comparison, because there is no single measure — there is a map, and every animal, including us, occupies a region of it defined by which capacities it has, at what level, in what combination.

To walk that map without fooling ourselves, we need an instrument, and I will tell you which one I have chosen and what it costs. There is a definition — distilled by two computer scientists from more than seventy others — that says intelligence is *an agent's ability to achieve goals across a wide range of environments.* It never mentions neurons or language or carbon, which means a bacterium and a machine can be judged on the same criterion. It is graded rather than pass-fail, so the worm and the human are both in the conversation and the question becomes the *shape* of the intelligence, not whether it counts. And it is operational: hand me an agent and an environment and I can, in principle, run the test. What it costs is everything that is not a goal — moods, attachments, the inner weather of an animal that a reward signal does not capture. We will feel that cost most in the chapters on emotion and on other minds, and when we do, I will reach for sharper tools. A working instrument is allowed to be imperfect. It is not allowed to be hidden.

There is one more thread, and I am going to seed it here and then mostly keep quiet about it, because it works best if you feel it click a beat before I name it.

Watch what we human beings do with each of these capacities once we have them. We build a thing that does the same trick outside the body. The worm steers; we build a robot vacuum that steers and knows nothing the worm does not. The synapse remembers; we invent writing, and the page remembers for the group. The eye perceives; we grind a lens and see the bacterium. The ant integrates a path; we orbit satellites and call it GPS. The brain predicts and plans; we build models and forecasts. And lately we have built a thing — there is probably one open on a screen near you — that extends pattern-matching and language across a breadth no prior tool approached. Each chapter of this book will end by quietly naming the tool that extends the capacity it just described. I will not comment on the pattern until the last chapter. But the pattern is the second argument, the one underneath the first: the boundary of a mind has never been the edge of the skull, and the newest tool on the shelf belongs in the same story as the worm in the dish — not because the machine is alive, but because *we* have always been the kind of animal that pushes its thinking out into the world. The catalogue of evolved minds will turn out to be written by a mind doing the very thing it catalogues.

That is the whole journey, compressed: from the brainless decision on the agar, up through the family of capacities one at a time, each found first in the simplest creature that demonstrably has it and then followed as it elaborates, to the machine run through the identical test, and finally to us, holding the question mark we started with and refusing to put a full stop where one has not been earned.

The worm is still on the dish. It is hungry, and the copper is still in the way, and it is about to decide. Let us start where intelligence is hardest to define and easiest to underestimate — with the question of what the word even means.

The worm in the dish is waiting.
