# Chapter 9 — Social Cognition and Theory of Mind

Atlanta, Georgia. James Burkett, in Larry Young's laboratory at Emory, has a pair of prairie voles in adjacent chambers. They are bonded — prairie voles form lifelong pair bonds, which is exactly why Young's lab works with them rather than their cousins. The montane vole, a near relative, does not pair-bond; the difference between the two species comes down to where in the brain they carry receptors for oxytocin and vasopressin, the two small peptides that do so much of mammalian attachment. The prairie vole is the animal that taught us the chemistry of bonding. Here it is being asked a harder question: not whether it bonds, but whether it consoles.

One of the voles is removed for a brief stressor — mild restraint, a small unfamiliar disturbance — then returned to its partner. The partner approaches. The partner grooms — not the brief, idle grooming prairie voles do all the time, but extended, directed grooming, focused on the returned animal, scaling in duration with how stressed the returned animal appears to be. The unstressed partner is *consoling* the stressed one. And it does this for a familiar partner, not for a stranger.

Now the move that makes this more than a touching anecdote.

Burkett injects a drug that blocks oxytocin receptors directly into the anterior cingulate cortex — the ACC, a strip of cortex folded along the midline that, across many mammals, lights up when an animal observes distress in another. The injection is local and specific: it shuts down oxytocin signaling at this one cortical target, before the social situation begins. Then the partner is stressed and returned, as before. And the consoler does not console.

It is not sedated. It is not unable to make affiliative contact — it grooms an unstressed partner normally. What has dropped out, specifically, is the directed helping: approaching the distressed partner and grooming it longer than usual in response to that distress. Block oxytocin in the anterior cingulate, and the helping disappears.

That is the whole chapter in one pharmacological cut. Social cognition is *not* one capacity that comes in larger and smaller amounts. It is a stack of distinct operations that can be pulled apart — neurally, behaviorally, evolutionarily — and the pulling-apart is the work.

---

There are three layers, and the boundaries between them matter more than people tend to notice.

**Emotional contagion** is automatic affect-alignment. The observer's emotional state shifts to match the target's, with no explicit model of who or what is producing the shift. A chick startles when its neighbor startles, before there is time to evaluate the threat independently. A person in a theater laughs because the room is laughing, often without knowing what was funny. A rat freezes when it sees another rat freeze. Contagion is ancient and widespread across vertebrates, and its adaptive logic is transparent: a flock in which every alarm is instantly triggered by one neighbor's alarm is far harder to surprise than a flock of independent evaluators.

**Empathy**, in the comparative literature's narrow sense, is contagion plus *self–other differentiation*. The empathic animal has a real affective response to the target's state — the affect is genuine — but represents the target as a separate self rather than simply mirroring the state inside its own body. The behavioral signature that most cleanly separates this from raw contagion is consolation: an uninvolved bystander going to a victim of distress and offering affiliative contact — grooming, touching, a soft vocalization — with no personal stake in the outcome.

Consolation has been documented in great apes, canids, elephants, corvids, and the prairie vole. It is notably absent in macaques, even though emotional contagion in macaques is robust: they startle at a conspecific's distress, they show physiological arousal, but they do not afterward go to the victim and groom it. The difference is not the presence of affect. It is the self–other boundary that makes *directed* help possible at all. A system that mirrors a state without locating its source outside itself cannot aim help at an external source, because from inside that system, the distress simply *is* its own.

The Burkett result is what makes this taxonomy molecular rather than merely descriptive. The ACC oxytocin block removes consolation. What it does *not* remove is the consoler's tendency to be affected by the partner at all — the affiliative repertoire is intact, the animal is not numb. The cleanest way to put it is that the consolation layer is mechanistically separable from the contagion layer beneath it: you can knock out the directed-helping behavior with a targeted manipulation while the animal remains a functioning, socially responsive vole. Two layers, dissociable, different mechanisms.

**Theory of mind** is the third layer: the cognitive representation of another agent's mental states — beliefs, intentions, knowledge, perspectives. It need not involve sharing the agent's affect at all. The subordinate chimpanzee of the next section is not feeling what the dominant feels. It is modeling what the dominant *can see*, and using that model to make a strategic choice.

---

There is a standard experiment on rats that shows why these distinctions matter operationally — and why behavior alone can never cleanly diagnose them.

In a chamber, behind a transparent wall, a rat is receiving mild electric shocks. An observer rat can press a lever that stops the shocks. It does nothing for the observer. The observer presses the lever.

This result has been cited as evidence for rat empathy, rat emotional contagion, and rat altruism. Those are three different claims, and the experiment cannot tell them apart. The observer might be feeling what the shocked rat feels — affect-matching — and pressing the lever to switch off its *own* aversive state, in which case this is self-regulation, not empathy. Or it might represent the other rat as a separate self in distress and act to relieve *that* rat's distress — directed help, empathy proper. Or it might simply be suppressing an unpleasant pattern of squeaking and thrashing, with no model of the other rat's experience at all.

Each reading has a different neural architecture and a different evolutionary story. The same lever-press is consistent with all three. A behavior consistent with empathy is not the same as a behavior that *requires* empathy. Most of the work of comparative cognition lives in that gap.

---

Now to the case the rat does not establish: mental-state attribution in primates.

Two chimpanzees in a research enclosure. Between them, a researcher sets out two pieces of food. One is visible to the dominant animal. The other is hidden from the dominant's view behind an opaque divider — only the subordinate can see around it. The barrier opens; both chimps move. The subordinate goes for the hidden food. Every time. Reverse the configuration so that both pieces are visible to the dominant, and the subordinate retreats entirely, taking nothing.

Brian Hare, Josep Call, and Michael Tomasello published this in 2000. The subordinate is not simply running for the nearest food. It is modeling what the dominant *can see*, and acting on the gap between the dominant's information and its own. Follow-up work showed chimpanzees track not only what a competitor currently sees but what it has recently seen, and that they read gaze direction as a sign of informational state.

What this establishes is *perceptual perspective-taking*: representing what another agent can and cannot see from where it stands, and predicting its behavior from that representation. It is one genuine component of theory of mind. It is not the whole of it.

The hard test is the *false-belief* task — the case where an agent's visual access and its knowledge come apart. The Hare paradigm cannot probe this, because in it what the dominant can see and what it knows are the same thing. The subordinate tracks current perception. Whether it can represent the dominant as an agent who holds a *false* belief about the world is a different question, and needs a different design.

The philosopher Daniel Dennett identified the gap in 1978, reading Premack and Woodruff's original paper — the one that asked, in its title, "Does the chimpanzee have a theory of mind?" and gave the field its phrase. Dennett argued that to test for belief attribution you need a design in which the subject's knowledge of the world *differs* from the actor's, and you ask whether the subject predicts the actor's behavior from the actor's *false* belief rather than from the truth.

Heinz Wimmer and Josef Perner provided the design in 1983. Simon Baron-Cohen, Alan Leslie, and Uta Frith made it canonical in 1985 as the Sally–Anne task.

Sally puts a marble in a basket. Sally leaves the room. Anne moves the marble to a box. Sally returns. Where will Sally look for her marble?

Four-year-old neurotypical children answer correctly: the basket, where Sally left it and where Sally *believes* it still is. Three-year-olds answer wrong: the box, where the marble actually is. The three-year-old's error is not a memory failure — ask where the marble *is* and the three-year-old knows. Ask where Sally will *look* and the three-year-old says the box, because their own accurate knowledge of the marble's location overrides any inference about Sally's belief. What changes between three and four is the capacity to hold a representation of a belief *as a representation* — a mental state that lives in a mind and can be wrong about the world.

For forty years after Sally–Anne, great apes failed every version of it, and the consensus hardened: apes could attribute goals and perhaps desires, but not beliefs. They were perspective-takers, not false-belief reasoners. Then in 2016 the consensus broke.

---

In a primate research center in Japan, a chimpanzee sits in a quiet room. An infrared eye-tracker watches her eyes. On a screen, a video plays.

A human in an ape-like "King Kong" costume hides in one of two haystacks. An actor watches it hide, then leaves. While the actor is gone, King Kong moves to the other haystack — and then, in some versions, leaves the scene entirely. The actor returns and will search. Where?

A fraction of a second before the actor begins to move, the chimpanzee's eyes shift to the *first* haystack. The place King Kong used to be. The place where the actor falsely believes King Kong still is — not where King Kong actually went. The ape is looking at the spot the actor *thinks* matters.

She is anticipating a mistake. She is predicting the actor will go to the wrong place, based on a belief she knows to be false, and she is doing it before the actor makes any movement she could simply follow. Christopher Krupenye, Fumihiro Kano, and colleagues published it in *Science* in 2016, and the effect held across chimpanzees, bonobos, and orangutans: the anticipatory gaze went to the false-belief location, significantly above chance. The designers used a confrontational, King-Kong-versus-human scenario on purpose — apes attend hardest to social conflict, and the implicit method needed their full attention to work where explicit tasks had failed.

It was the first time any non-human animal passed a version of the false-belief task that forty years of behavioral testing had ruled out. The ape looked at the wrong place. The wrong place was the right answer.

What changed between 2016 and the previous four decades was not the species. It was the measure. Every earlier false-belief test demanded an explicit behavioral response — select a photograph, press a button, search a location. Human children show the anticipatory-looking pattern on *implicit* false-belief tasks at around fifteen months, more than two years before they can pass the explicit version. Krupenye and colleagues applied that same implicit method to apes, and found the implicit competence the explicit tasks had been missing.

The critic's response was immediate, and it has a name. Cecilia Heyes argued the apes might be *submentalizing*: running simple learned associations about where objects last appeared in an actor's view, with no representation of belief as a mental state at all. Apes have enormous experience watching objects get moved around; perhaps they had simply learned the statistical regularity that actors tend to look where they last saw a thing.

The barrier-experience control answered this, and it is the linchpin of the whole case.

---

Fumihiro Kano and colleagues, in a 2019 study, pre-exposed different apes to barriers of the type used in the false-belief videos. One group experienced an *opaque* barrier — they pressed their faces to it and learned, from their own perception, that it blocked vision. A second group experienced a *transparent* barrier — similar in its surface features, but see-through.

Later, both groups watched the same video, in which an actor stood behind a barrier of the type the ape had personally experienced while events unfolded on the far side. And the apes' anticipatory gaze depended on their *own* past experience with that specific barrier.

Apes with opaque-barrier experience looked to the false-belief location: the actor stood behind something opaque, therefore the actor did not see the move, therefore the actor holds a false belief. Apes with transparent-barrier experience looked to the *true* location: the actor stood behind something see-through, therefore the actor *did* see the move, therefore the actor knows where King Kong went.

This is the dissociation submentalizing cannot explain. The two groups saw identical on-screen events. The only difference between them was their own first-person experience of what that kind of barrier permits or blocks. The apes were projecting their own perceptual experience onto the actor — using what they knew the barrier allows *them* to perceive in order to infer what the actor had been able to see. The mechanism, in a word, is self-projection: the ape runs its own perceptual machinery as a model of the other mind.

Self-projection shows up, in a different key, in corvids. Nathan Emery and Nicola Clayton found that western scrub-jays that had themselves been thieves — birds that had raided other birds' caches — would re-cache their own food after being watched, while naive birds that had never stolen did not. The experienced thief knows what *it* does when it watches another bird cache, and projects that tendency onto the observer. It takes a thief to know what a thief will do. This is not false belief. But it requires taking one's own mental states as a model for another agent's — the core move that self-projection demands.

---

A computational frame makes the comparative hierarchy clear and builds the bridge to the machine.

In ordinary reinforcement learning — the previous chapter — an agent is handed a reward function and learns a policy that maximizes it. In *inverse* reinforcement learning, the situation runs backward. An observer watches another agent behave and infers the reward function — and the world model — that would explain the behavior. Watch someone walk toward the kitchen and you do not merely register a trajectory. You infer that they are hungry and believe food is in the kitchen. From observed behavior you back-compute a compact set of latent variables — desires, beliefs, intentions — that explains everything the person is doing and predicts what they will do in situations you have not yet seen.

Each layer of social cognition is adding a richer model of the target agent. Goal attribution infers only the target state of the agent's policy: *he is walking toward the kitchen*. Perspective-taking adds the agent's information state: *he is walking toward the kitchen because he can see food on the counter*. False-belief reasoning adds the possibility that the agent's world model is *wrong*: *he is walking toward the empty kitchen because he believes there is food there, though I know there is not*. Each level generalizes differently, and the richest one handles all the cases below it plus the divergence between an agent's information and the truth.

The inverse-reinforcement-learning frame is what makes the AI test sharp. A system genuinely modeling an agent's mental states should *generalize* when the scenario is modified, because its model is built on the agent's information state rather than on the surface form of the story. A system that pattern-matches on narrative templates will fail to generalize when the surface form changes, even if the underlying information-state logic is untouched.

---

In 2023, Michal Kosinski reported that GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 passed a large battery of standard false-belief tasks — Sally–Anne and variants — with accuracy approaching that of human adults, and titled the work to suggest theory of mind may have *spontaneously emerged* in large language models as a byproduct of scale. The speculation traveled fast.

Tomer Ullman answered it the same year by making small modifications to the same tasks. Replace the opaque container in the Sally–Anne story with a transparent one, so Sally could have seen the marble move even while she was present. Add a sentence explicitly granting the actor knowledge of the object's new location. Make the agent distracted. Negate the question. Each change preserves the *logical structure* of the false-belief situation while altering the surface features of the canonical story.

On the modified versions, GPT-4 kept predicting that Sally would look in the basket — the answer correct for the *canonical* version — even when the modification made plain that Sally had seen the marble move and therefore knew where it was. The model was applying a learned template: *actor-absent-during-move → actor-has-false-belief*. It applied the template even when the story's explicit content contradicted it.

Humans do not do this. A human reading the transparent-container version updates immediately: Sally saw it happen, so Sally knows. The inference generalizes because the human is running an actual model of Sally's information state — taking the story's content as input and computing a belief-state for Sally, something like the inverse-reinforcement-learning computation applied to that content.

The pattern across Ullman's variants — pass on the canonical, fail on the structurally-modified perturbations — is the same diagnostic this book has met in every domain. Preserve the logical structure; alter the surface features. Genuine competence generalizes; surface template-matching does not. And the diagnostic is asymmetric. Failing the perturbations diagnoses template-matching cleanly — you know what the system is doing. Passing them does *not* establish genuine modeling of mental states, because a more sophisticated template-matcher operating at higher abstraction might also pass. The test is sharp on one side and soft on the other, and the honest stance is to say so: fail this and you are doing template-matching; pass it and you are doing something better, but not necessarily a model of another mind.

---

Let me close with the convergence, because the comparative claim of this book lands here as hard as anywhere.

Bottlenose dolphins in Shark Bay, Western Australia, run a three-tier alliance system. First-tier pairs or trios of males herd single females for mating access. Second-tier teams of four to fourteen males compete against other teams, and those teams stay stable for decades, across lifetimes. Third-tier coalitions-of-coalitions form for particular contests. A male's reproductive success depends on knowing not just who his immediate allies are but which groups his allies' groups are currently aligned with — and on updating that knowledge as the alliances shift. The bookkeeping, in sheer number of relationships and their higher-order dependencies, is comparable to chimpanzee politics in de Waal's Arnhem colony.

The dolphin lineage split from the primate lineage roughly ninety-five million years ago. Their brains are anatomically distinct — a heavily convoluted cortex, but with different cytoarchitecture, different layering, different columnar organization. Two lineages arrived at comparable political complexity from entirely different starting materials.

At the cellular level, what converges across primates, cetaceans, and elephants is the presence of Von Economo neurons — large spindle-shaped cells in the anterior cingulate and frontoinsular cortex, found in socially complex mammals and rare or absent in species without complex sociality. They appear independently in three lineages facing the same computational demand. The convergent *presence* is well-supported. The specific functional hypothesis — that their sparse, high-speed projections allow rapid social decisions that cannot wait for full cortical processing — is consistent with their morphology and still awaits direct confirmation. But the independent arrival at the same cellular solution, in the same cortical regions, across three distantly related lineages, is exactly what you would predict if the *problem*, not the substrate, determines the form of the solution.

That is the book's thesis in its most socially elaborate form: what the environment demands is the determining variable. The substrate is the medium. The function is the thing.

What remains genuinely contested is whether non-human animals do explicit, second-order, recursive mentalizing — and the evidence suggests they do not, in the way humans plainly do. The apes pass the implicit false-belief task. They have not been shown to pass the explicit version the way a four-year-old does. The implicit–explicit gap is large and informative — though the field should be honest that the implicit paradigms themselves, in apes and in human infants alike, are under active debate about how robustly they replicate. Human children pass implicit at about fifteen months and explicit at about four years: a developmental gap of two and a half years. Apes appear to live in something like that gap — implicit yes, explicit not yet shown.

What closes the gap in human development is still debated, but language is almost certainly involved. The explicit Sally–Anne test requires holding a representation of a belief *as a representation* — keeping the marble's true location and Sally's false belief in mind at once, and suppressing your own accurate knowledge to compute Sally's. That is a recursive, second-order operation, and whether it *requires* language or whether language is merely its most visible expression is open.

The worm at the copper line — the animal this book began with — was making a decision. A genuine, state-dependent, experience-informed decision, in 302 neurons. Somewhere between that worm and a four-year-old answering a question about Sally's marble, the nervous system acquired the capacity to hold a model of another mind, and then to hold a model of a model that was *wrong*, and to reason from the wrongness rather than from the truth.

That last capacity is now being built, deliberately, into the tools on our desks. A recommendation system maintains a model of you — what you want, what you know, what you will do next — and acts on it. A tutoring system tracks what a student has and has not yet understood, and chooses its next move from that model of the student's knowledge. A chatbot, mid-conversation, runs a representation of what you already know so it can decide what to tell you and what to leave out — simulating, in effect, your information state, the way the ape simulated the actor's behind the barrier. The capacity the book has been climbing toward, the modeling of another mind, has become a thing we manufacture. And the manufactured version fails exactly where Ullman caught it failing: it passes the canonical case and breaks on the perturbation, holding a model of your mind that is fluent and confident and sometimes simply wrong about what you can see. The capacity is real. Its phylogenetic and mechanistic boundaries are still being drawn. And the diagnostic for whether any system — vole, ape, child, or machine — actually has it is not whether it passes the canonical test. It is whether it passes the perturbations.
