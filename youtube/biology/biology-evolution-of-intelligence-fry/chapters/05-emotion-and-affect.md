# Chapter 5 — Emotion and Affect

A bumblebee in a laboratory in London has spent several days learning two associations. One color of plastic cylinder holds a sweet sucrose solution, rewarding. A different color holds quinine, bitter and aversive. By the time the experiment proper begins, the bee approaches one reliably and turns away from the other.

Then, just before the test, the experimenter does something the bee has no reason to expect. A single bead of sucrose — stronger than anything it met during training — appears on the staging platform. The bee drinks it. No cue announced it. Nothing predicted it. It is, in the exact sense of the phrase, a pleasant surprise.

A few seconds later the bee is released into an arena holding cylinders of a color it has never seen — not the trained-good color, not the trained-bad one, but something in between. Ambiguous. Could be food. Could be the bitter thing. Could be nothing at all.

The bee that just had the sucrose surprise reaches that ambiguous cylinder noticeably faster than control bees who got only water. It dithers less on the way. A pleasant surprise has tilted its reading of a stimulus it has never encountered.

Now the part that matters. Another group of bees received a tiny topical dose of fluphenazine — a drug that blocks dopamine from binding its receptors — before the surprise. The fluphenazine did not stop them drinking the sucrose. It did not slow their legs. But it erased the bias completely. Bees given sucrose-plus-fluphenazine behaved exactly like bees given only water. The sensory event was intact. The thing that should have followed it was not.

In a human, that pattern has a name. The lightness after good news, the way the world looks a shade more benign once something unexpectedly pleasant has happened — that is a mood shift. In a bumblebee, with a brain the size of a sesame seed and around a million neurons against your eighty-six billion, we have to decide what to call it. And that decision turns out to be more interesting than it sounds, because it forces us to be precise about what the word *emotion* is even being used to mean.

The word is doing three different jobs in ordinary speech, and prying them apart is the only way forward.

The first job is emotion as feeling — the inner texture of the experience, the what-it-is-like. This is the one you have direct access to when you are frightened or delighted, and, as the philosopher Thomas Nagel argued in 1974, it is accessible *only* from the inside. There is something it is like to be a bat, if bats have any inner life — but no amount of recording from bat neurons tells you whether that condition is met. Call this the phenomenal sense.

The second job is emotion as behavior — the flinch, the freeze, the cry, the arrangement of a face. Easy to record. But behavior is a sign of an underlying state, not the state itself. A skilled actor produces grief without grieving. A reflex looks like fear in a body that holds no fear at all.

The third job is emotion as functional state — a description of what a nervous system is *doing*, making no claim about feeling. You define it by operational properties: how long it lasts, what it biases, what brings it on, what shuts it off. This is the one you can actually investigate in a bee.

Most arguments about animal emotion are really two people using two of these definitions and not noticing. One says "bees don't have emotions" and means they don't have feelings. The other says "bees do have emotions" and means they have functional states with the right properties. Both can be true at once. What the bee experiment shows — and what it does not — depends entirely on which question you are asking. This chapter commits to the functional definition, not because feeling is unimportant (it is the thing nearly everyone actually cares about) but because the functional definition is the one where evidence can decide anything.

In 2014, David Anderson and Ralph Adolphs proposed a set of criteria — emotion primitives — that a nervous-system state must satisfy before we are entitled to call it emotional in this functional sense. The core set is four, and each is independently testable.

*Scalability*: the intensity of the state tracks the intensity of the trigger. Small threat, mild state; large threat, strong state.

*Persistence*: the state outlasts the stimulus that set it off. A pure reflex ends when the stimulus ends. A functional emotional state runs on.

*Valence*: the state is intrinsically positive or negative — something the system acts as though it wants more of, or wants to escape. This is what separates emotion from bare arousal. Arousal tells you something is happening. Valence tells you which way to lean.

*Generalization*: the state biases responses across later situations, including ones unrelated to the original trigger. A genuinely emotional state acts as a prior on the world — not a reply to one stimulus, but an expectation about what stimuli in general are likely to mean.

(A fifth property, coordination — the state moving physiology, behavior, and cognition together in one direction — is sometimes added, and it is real, but it sits alongside the four primitives rather than among them.) Notice what the list does not include: consciousness, feeling, anything you have to be told. The criteria describe functional architecture, and that architecture can be tested in an animal that cannot say a word.

The technique that makes the testing possible is the cognitive-bias paradigm, and its logic is elegant. If a valenced state is functionally a prior on the world, then an animal in a negative state expects danger and loss, and an animal in a positive state expects reward and safety. So the state should show up in how the animal reads genuinely ambiguous information. Hand it a stimulus halfway between a trained-good and a trained-bad exemplar — something it has no experience with — and watch which way it leans. The animal cannot tell you how it feels. But when you ask it, in effect, *what is this new thing*, its answer betrays the prior.

In 2011, Melissa Bateson and colleagues ran exactly this on honeybees. The bees were trained — one odor meant sugar, another meant quinine — until they reliably extended their proboscis toward the rewarded odor and held it back from the aversive one. Then half the bees were sealed in a plastic tube and shaken hard for sixty seconds, a rough approximation of what a bee feels in a predator's grip: the world has gone violently wrong, the outcome is uncertain, and there is nothing to be done about it just now. The other half waited in their cage.

Afterward both groups met the trained odors and a range of intermediate ones spanning the gap between good and bad. The unshaken bees responded in a sensible gradient — the closer a test odor sat to the rewarded one, the more often they extended. The shaken bees were different. They were markedly less likely to extend toward the ambiguous odors. The shaking had not changed what the odors smelled like, or damaged the bees' sense of taste, or erased their memory of which odor was safe. What it had changed was the direction in which they resolved uncertainty. In a human, biased interpretation of ambiguous information persisting after a stressful event is one signature of anxiety. In the bee, it is a functional state doing to cognition what anxiety does to ours.

Then Perry, Baciadonna, and Chittka ran the positive version in 2016, and added what Bateson's result had lacked: a mechanism. The bee with the sucrose surprise approached the ambiguous cylinder faster. Block dopamine with fluphenazine and the surprise goes behaviorally invisible — the bee still drinks, still navigates, but the affective shift that should follow never forms. That is what a mechanism looks like: not a behavioral correlation but a pharmacological dissociation. And the molecule doing it is no exotic insect chemical. It is dopamine — the same neuromodulator the worm uses, the same molecule the previous chapter watched carry the prediction-error signal in Schultz's recordings from monkey midbrain, blocked here by the same class of drug used to quiet human psychosis. The architecture is ancient, and the function runs across half a billion years of nervous systems.

Run the Anderson–Adolphs criteria across the Perry result one at a time, because this is the move you should be able to make for any candidate animal. *Scalability*: stronger surprises produce larger biases, and very small ones produce none — the relationship is graded. *Persistence*: the surprise lasted seconds, yet the bee, moved to a different arena and shown a novel color, still carried the bias minutes later — the state outlived the event. *Valence*: the bias was toward faster approach, and the trigger, sucrose, was positive — the state has a sign, in the direction the trigger predicts. *Generalization*: here is the edge, and here is the most interesting live disagreement in the field.

The Perry test stimulus — an in-between-colored cylinder — sat inside the same sensory channel and the same task as the training. The bias generalized from "sucrose surprise" to "ambiguous cylinder," which is *some* generalization. Whether it would reach a stimulus with nothing to do with food — an ambiguous shelter entrance, say — has not been cleanly tested. This is not a minor methodological gap. It is the crux of the disagreement between Lars Chittka, who reads the bee as having something like mood — a global state that spreads broadly — and Martin Giurfa, who reads it as appetitive motivation — a state specific to food-seeking that spreads only within the food domain. Think of the difference between being in a good mood and being hungry. Both make you readier to approach food. But a good mood also colors how you hear an unrelated piece of music; hunger does not. The experiment that would settle it is specifiable — an ambiguous, non-food stimulus, under the same sucrose surprise, with and without fluphenazine — and to my reading it has not been done cleanly. The chapter's central question is sitting, right now, in one unrun experiment.

Except that Perry's 2016 paper had a second experiment, and it leans the question toward mood. After the optimism test, the same group asked a different question: how fast does a bee resume foraging after a fright? They simulated a predator attack — a robotic spider that briefly trapped the bee at a feeder — and then measured how long each bee waited before going back out to forage. Bees that had been given a sucrose pre-reward got back to work faster than bees that had not. A drop of sugar shortened the recovery from a scare. That matters for the generalization question, because the predator-recovery task is *not* the optimism task. The same positive manipulation moved behavior in two different domains — how an ambiguous color is read, and how quickly fear is shaken off. A state that bridges two unrelated tasks is behaving more like mood than like a narrow food-seeking drive. It does not close the Chittka–Giurfa debate, but it tilts it, and it does so with the most vivid scene the work produced: a bumblebee, freshly menaced by a mechanical spider, going back out to forage sooner because it had a sip of something sweet.

Two other cases push the question to its edges, and the second one is genuinely strange. Robert Elwood's hermit crabs: a crab in a high-quality shell tolerates more electric shock before abandoning it than a crab in a poor shell. The crab is weighing the badness of the shock against the goodness of what it stands to lose — trading them off on some shared currency of value that a pure reflex account, which predicts a fixed withdrawal threshold, cannot produce. That shared currency is exactly what valence is. In 2022 the United Kingdom recognized decapod crustaceans — and cephalopod molluscs — as sentient in the Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act, the first time invertebrates were granted formal sentience in UK law.

Now the strange case. In 1962, George Adrian Horridge showed that a *decapitated* cockroach — thoracic ganglion intact, brain removed — can learn to hold its leg up to avoid a shock. The leg dangles above a saline bath; whenever it drops into the bath it completes a circuit and the body receives a shock. After enough exposures the headless animal keeps the leg raised. A yoked control, wired to receive the same shocks at the same times but with no relation to its own leg position, never learns. The contingency was what mattered, and a cluster of neurons with no brain above it learned it. Under the functional definition, that thoracic ganglion holds a state that meets the operational criteria. Under the phenomenal definition, it almost certainly feels nothing, because there is no central integration that could be the what-it-is-like of anything. The two definitions diverge here about as sharply as they can. What I find most telling is what the cockroach implies about where functional states can live: not necessarily in the kind of centralized brain we associate with feeling. The framework strains at this edge — and a definition doing real work *should* make you flinch at its edges. (One caution: claims that the same headless preparation shows learned helplessness — reduced escape after inescapable shock — circulate in the literature, but I have not found a clean primary source for them. Horridge's result is leg-position learning. I would not lean on more than that.)

Two recent declarations about animal consciousness are worth knowing precisely, because both have been cited more confidently than their wording supports. The Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness, signed in 2012, stated that "humans are not unique in possessing the neurological substrates that generate consciousness." The press read that as a declaration that animals are conscious. What it actually claimed was that animals possess the structural prerequisites — a statement about anatomy, not about feeling. The New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness, in 2024, was more careful still, asserting that "there is at least a realistic possibility of conscious experience" not only in all vertebrates but in many invertebrates, including, at minimum, cephalopod molluscs, decapod crustaceans, and insects. *Realistic possibility* is doing real work in that phrase. It is not the claim that a bee feels anxious the way you do. It is the claim that the probability is high enough that ignoring it would be a moral error — a calibrated probabilistic statement, which is the right standard for what we can and cannot currently know.

The gap between the functional and the phenomenal may never close — not because science will fail to improve, but because the phenomenal question may be one that third-person methods are structurally unable to answer. There may be no observation from outside a nervous system that settles whether there is anything it is like to be inside it. This is the hard problem of consciousness, hard in the specific sense that no quantity of functional evidence resolves it. What the evidence *can* do is make the denial progressively less comfortable. One neuromodulatory architecture conserved from worms to vertebrates. Functional states meeting the criteria. Cognitive-bias results replicating across animals separated by half a billion years. The pattern is consistent with something running through the whole continuum of nervous systems. The competing claim — that there is a hard cutoff, experience above it and nothing below — has no mechanism behind it. It is a stipulation, not a theory. Anyone is entitled to hold that only humans feel. But they then owe us the rung of the ladder where feeling appears, and the mechanism that produces it there and not on the rung below — and the ladder has no rung that is not connected to the one beneath it.

I do not know whether the shaken bee felt anxious. I know it had a state that did to its cognition what anxiety does to ours. That may not be the same thing. But it is not nothing. The shaken bee did not choose her pessimism. She did not deliberate, did not decide that recent events made the world look more dangerous. The state arose from chemistry, and the chemistry changed what everything else meant. This is true of her. It is also true of you.

And it is the reason a particular human tool now exists. We are the one animal that learned to read its own affective state from the outside — to do to ourselves what the cognitive-bias paradigm does to the bee. The mood diary, the phone that pings you to rate the day on a scale, the wearable counting your heart-rate variability as a proxy for stress, the software now built to detect valence in a voice or a face: affective computing, the field calls it. We could not get at the bee's inner state, so we built a behavioral assay for it. We cannot get reliably at our own from the inside either — feeling is a poor instrument for measuring feeling — so we are building the same kind of assay for ourselves, externalizing the read-out onto a device that logs what the chemistry is doing and hands it back to us as a chart. The bee betrays its prior in how fast it approaches an ambiguous cylinder. We betray ours to a sensor, and read the answer off a screen.
