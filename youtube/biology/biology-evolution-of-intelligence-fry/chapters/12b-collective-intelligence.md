# Chapter 12b — Collective Intelligence

It is 1906. A country livestock fair in Plymouth, in the west of England. Among the stalls there is a competition: a fat ox, dressed and ready for slaughter, stands on display, and for sixpence anyone may write their guess of its weight on a numbered ticket. Butchers enter. Farmers enter. So do clerks and schoolteachers and people who have never touched a side of beef in their lives. At the end of the day the organizers have a sack of tickets and they award the prize, and ordinarily that would be the end of it.

But a particular eighty-four-year-old man asks for the tickets afterward. His name is Francis Galton, and he is interested in the wisdom of crowds for the opposite of the usual reason — he expects to find them foolish. He believes the average voter is incompetent and he means to prove it with the ox. He collects roughly eight hundred tickets, discards thirteen as illegible or defective, and is left with **787 valid estimates**. He lines them up and takes the median — the middle value, the guess that splits the crowd in half.

The median of those 787 guesses is 1,207 pounds. The mean is 1,197. The ox actually weighs 1,198 pounds.

The crowd, taken as a body, is off by less than one pound out of twelve hundred — more accurate than any single expert in it. Galton, to his credit, published the result honestly in *Nature* the next year, even though it cut against what he had set out to show.

*Figure 12.3 — The 787 independent guesses spread wide, but their errors cancel: the red true-weight line (1198 lb) sits all but on top of the median and mean — the crowd off by less than a pound.*

That result is real, and the temptation is to read it as a general endorsement of collective wisdom. It is not. It is the cleanest demonstration of exactly one of three quite different things people lump together under "collective intelligence" — and getting the three apart is the whole of this chapter. The pointing toddler and the schoolyard grammar of the companion chapter were about how two minds share a single thought. This chapter is about what happens when you have not two minds but thousands, and across not one moment but generations.

---

The first mechanism is the one the ox illustrates: **aggregation**.

The 787 errors cancelled because they were *independent*. No guesser had been told what any other guessed. No single bias pushed all 787 estimates the same way — some ran high, some low, and with enough independent draws the high errors and the low errors annihilate each other around the truth. This is the entire mechanism, and it is worth being precise about what it requires and does not require. It does not require any individual to be accurate. It does not require communication between the estimators — in fact it requires the *opposite*. It requires only that the errors not all point in the same direction.

Which tells you exactly when aggregation fails. It fails when errors are *correlated* — when everyone has read the same misleading article and absorbed the same bias in the same direction. It fails when the crowd is *herding* — when later guessers can see earlier guesses and adjust toward them, so the estimates are no longer independent draws but echoes of each other. The "wisdom of crowds," correctly stated, is the wisdom of *independent* judgments about *factual* questions where errors are uncorrelated. Outside those conditions the same arithmetic that nailed the ox's weight produces a stampede off a cliff. Aggregation is not a property of crowds. It is a property of independence, and independence is fragile.

The second mechanism is **coordination**, and it looks like intelligence but often is not.

A starling murmuration — fifty thousand birds wheeling as one dark body across an evening sky, folding and splitting in response to a hunting peregrine — is the canonical case. For a long time it seemed impossible that birds could track the whole flock fast enough to move like that. In 2008 Andrea Ballerini and colleagues worked out how: each bird does not track the flock at all. It tracks its six or seven nearest neighbors — and, crucially, its six or seven nearest *by rank*, not by distance, so the rule holds whether the flock is packed tight or spread thin. The coupling is scale-free. Local rules, followed by every individual, produce global coherence with no leader and no plan.

But coordination is not the same as collective intelligence, and the distinction is sharp. The flock is not *deciding* anything. It is *aligning* behavior. Alignment can be a precondition for some kinds of collective intelligence, but it is not itself intelligence. A financial market that coordinates millions of buyers and sellers through price is doing coordination; whether it deserves the word "intelligent" depends entirely on whether the coordinated output solves a problem better than any participant could alone — and sometimes it does, and sometimes it produces a bubble. Coordination organizes a crowd. It does not, by itself, make the crowd smart.

The third mechanism is the one that separates us from everything else alive, and it is called **cumulative culture**.

Here each generation does not start over. It begins from the modified output of the generation before — a ratchet, in the mechanical sense: a wheel with a pawl that lets it turn forward but catches it from slipping back. The flint tools of 800,000 years ago became, across scores of millennia, the Levallois prepared-core technique, then pressure-flaked microliths, then bronze, then iron, then the transistor. No individual dropped naked into a forest re-derives any of this in a single lifetime. The knowledge is not in any one head. It accumulates across the chain of heads, each starting where the last left off.

This is the line between human collective cognition and the bee colony's. The bee swarm, as we are about to see, performs a genuinely brilliant collective computation. But it performs the *same* brilliant computation this year that its ancestors performed a million years ago. The human ratchet found the transistor, then the microprocessor, then asked what comes next. Three mechanisms, three failure modes, three sets of requirements. Take them one at a time, starting with the swarm, because its mechanism is beautiful and it teaches something the rest of the chapter needs.

---

In June, on a warm afternoon in upstate New York, a honeybee colony grows too crowded for its hive. The old queen leaves with roughly half the workers. The swarm — ten thousand bees, the mass of a basketball — settles on a tree branch and hangs there. Now the colony has to find a new home. Choose a poor cavity and it dies over the coming winter. Choose well and the lineage continues. And nobody is in charge. There is no architect bee, no surveyor, no vote-counter. The decision has to assemble itself out of ten thousand individuals, none of whom can see more than its own small part of the problem.

In 1997, Thomas Seeley was watching such a swarm near Ithaca when a student with a vibration detector caught something Seeley had been missing for years. Scouts dancing on the cluster to advertise candidate sites were sometimes being interrupted. Another bee would approach a dancer, butt her with its head, and emit a brief buzz at about 380 hertz. The dancer would fall silent. Seeley had found the **stop signal**, and with it the last piece of the mechanism.

Here is how the swarm decides. A scout that has surveyed a candidate cavity flies back to the cluster and performs a waggle dance encoding the site's direction, distance, and — through the vigor and duration of the dance — its quality. Each scout scores its site independently against a checklist evolution has tuned: a cavity above roughly forty liters in volume, an entrance around fifteen square centimeters, dry, defensible, high off the ground. The dance compresses all of that into a single intensity signal. Other scouts watching a vigorous dance are more likely to fly out and inspect that site themselves; if they agree, they return and add their own dances. Positive feedback amplifies real quality differences — a site twice as good recruits roughly twice as fast.

But positive feedback alone has a fatal flaw: left unchecked, it locks onto whichever site happened to recruit a lead first, regardless of quality, because early random luck snowballs. The stop signal is the cure. Scouts committed to one site actively *inhibit* the dancers advertising rival sites — and they do it in proportion to their own numbers, so the larger a site's support grows, the harder it suppresses its competitors. Sites accumulate support in proportion to quality and simultaneously drain support from rivals in proportion to rival strength. The swarm is not merely biased toward the best site; it actively starves the alternatives.

When the number of scouts converging on a single site crosses roughly fifteen — the **quorum threshold** — those scouts return and begin to pipe, a high-frequency vibration that warms the flight muscles of the whole mass. Above a temperature threshold the swarm lifts off as one body and flies to the chosen home. The quorum requirement keeps the swarm from committing before the consensus is genuine.

Seeley and his colleagues showed in 2012 that this four-part architecture — quality-weighted positive feedback, cross-inhibition between rival populations, a quorum threshold, and a gating threshold for liftoff — is mathematically the same structure as the *leaky competing accumulator*, the model Joshua Gold and Michael Shadlen had used to describe how individual neurons in a primate's parietal cortex make a perceptual decision. In the brain version, two populations of neurons — one voting "leftward," one "rightward" — each take in evidence and each inhibit the other, and the decision is made when one crosses threshold. The bee swarm is that exact algorithm at a different scale: two populations of dancers, each suppressing the other, evidence piling up, decision at quorum. The colony and the cortex did not inherit this from a common ancestor. They arrived at it independently, because it is the right answer to the same problem — deciding fast but not *too* fast, under noise and time pressure.

And there is a final twist that reaches back to the previous chapter on metacognition. The swarm's quorum is not fixed. A cluster hanging in a dangerous, exposed spot — high over open water, say — accepts a *lower* quorum and decides faster; a cluster in a sheltered spot waits longer for higher confidence. The threshold tracks the cost of being wrong. The swarm monitors its own decision process and adjusts how much certainty it demands before acting. That is not just collective intelligence. It is collective *metacognition* — a crowd that knows, in effect, how sure it needs to be.

The stop signal is precisely the part the standard "wisdom of crowds" story leaves out. Galton's ox was aggregation: independent estimates, errors cancel. The swarm is something else — estimates that *interact*, in a way that beats both pure aggregation (which can never actually decide) and pure positive feedback (which locks onto the first mover). Cross-inhibition is what makes dissent productive. The swarm finds the best cavity *because* dissenting scouts can dampen rival enthusiasm — because the mechanism uses disagreement rather than averaging it away. The lesson for any committee that has watched the first proposal on the table swallow the whole meeting is not "agree faster." It is "build cross-inhibition into the room, so the first mover cannot dominate before the alternatives have been weighed."

---

Now the ratchet.

Somewhere in central Africa, roughly 800,000 years ago, a hominin kneels by a rock outcrop and knaps flint against a harder stone. The technique in her hands is not hers. Someone older taught it to her, and someone older taught them. The tool she strikes off is slightly better than her teacher's, because she noticed something her teacher did not. She will pass her version to the next pair of hands. No one designs this. The improvement in any single generation is too small to see. But across a hundred thousand years it produces the Levallois technique; across a million, metallurgy.

Michael Tomasello's argument, in *The Cultural Origins of Human Cognition*, is that an individual human is not dramatically smarter than an individual chimpanzee at any single cognitive task. Nothing in the preceding chapters of this book found a lone cognitive operation a human performs so far above a chimpanzee that it could explain the gulf between a flint flake and the International Space Station. What explains the gulf is not individual brilliance. It is **transmission fidelity** — how accurately one generation copies the last.

The ratchet requires three things, and the missing one is always the same. First, individuals who can *innovate* on inherited practice. Second, *faithful transmission* of those innovations — copying accurate enough that the next generation inherits the improvement rather than a degraded smear of it. Third, *each generation starting where the last left off* rather than reinventing from scratch.

Other species have the first and third but fail on the second. Chimpanzee culture is real — tool styles differ between populations in ways only social learning can explain. But chimpanzee copying *degrades*: introduce a novel technique into a group and the copies are noisier than the original, and across generations the technique drifts away from its best form faster than improvements accumulate. The chimpanzee ratchet, if it exists at all, slips back faster than it clicks forward.

Human transmission fidelity sits above that threshold, and it sits there because of exactly the machinery the companion chapter on language laid out: joint attention, declarative pointing, imitation of *means* and not just ends, and language itself. Each of these raises the fidelity of the copy. Together they push human cultural transmission over the line where the ratchet catches and holds. This is why the two halves of this story are one story. The social-attentional machinery that makes language possible — two minds locking onto a shared object — is the same machinery that makes the ratchet possible, because cumulative culture is just that same act of sharing, run across generations instead of across a park bench: making one mind's content available to another as a shareable, modifiable, transmissible thing.

How much does population matter? Joseph Henrich argued that the ratchet depends on the size and connectivity of the network carrying it — that below a critical population a community loses skills faster than it generates them, and the ratchet runs in reverse. His most cited illustration is the Tasmanian toolkit: after rising seas cut Tasmania off from mainland Australia roughly ten thousand years ago, the isolated population's material culture, on his reading, simplified over the millennia. It is a vivid case and worth treating carefully, because it is genuinely **contested**. Dwight Read and others have argued the Tasmanian simplification may reflect ecological change or sampling artifacts rather than a population-size effect, and a sharp exchange over the case ran in *PNAS* in 2016. The underlying principle — that transmission fidelity and population connectivity govern whether culture accumulates or decays — rests on firmer ground in controlled experiments. Maxime Derex and colleagues showed in 2013 that when people learn a multi-step skill in chains of varying size, larger groups maintain and improve complex skills that smaller groups progressively lose, with the loss tracking group size as the model predicts. The principle holds. The Tasmania case is one disputed piece of evidence for it, not the load-bearing proof.

And the speed at which the ratchet can run, given the right conditions, is the Nicaraguan schoolyard from the companion chapter. First-cohort children pooled home-signs into a rough shared system. Second-cohort children, arriving young enough to take that system as their primary input, restructured it — added agreement, morphology, recursion. The third cohort inherited a fully grammaticized language and used it as the platform for whatever came next. Twenty years. Three cohorts. A language built from nothing, because the social-attentional machinery was in place and the fidelity between cohorts was high. The ratchet does not need centuries to be visible. It needs only the conditions.

---

There is one distinction left, and it is the one that decides whether the systems now compressing the human record are participating in the ratchet or merely reading it.

In 1958, the chemist-turned-philosopher Michael Polanyi drew the line between the **explicit** dimension of a practice — what can be written down, put in a book, transmitted in text — and its **tacit** dimension — what cannot be fully articulated but does most of the real work. A master violin-maker knows in his hands how much pressure to apply when shaving the spruce top. He cannot write it down in a form a novice could follow from the page. The novice has to stand at the bench and make a thousand attempts while the master's corrections reshape his *hands*, not just his beliefs about what to do. *We know more than we can tell*, was Polanyi's phrase. The tacit is transmitted in person, in practice, in the same physical space where the work happens.

This is not a footnote to the explicit record. It is what makes the explicit record usable. A medical student who has read every paper on surgical technique and never held a scalpel cannot operate. A scientist who has read every paper in her field but never run an experiment cannot look at a result and feel that it is too clean to trust — a judgment experienced researchers hold and rarely articulate, because it was handed to them at the bench, not in the literature.

The ratchet runs on both layers. The explicit record speeds the transmission of what can be written down; the click is faster when the record is better and more widely shared. But the tacit layer is what lets the next generation actually *do* the experiments rather than just read about them, notice the thing that is not in any paper, and pass *that* to the generation after. The institution that has most accelerated the human ratchet — the research university, the modern laboratory — is the one that keeps both layers under the same roof, the record-keeping and the practice-transmission enriching each other in the same building.

A large language model trained on the corpus of published human knowledge has compressed an unprecedented fraction of the **explicit record** into a queryable form. The compression is real, and the amplification it gives the aggregation layer is real: for literature synthesis, first drafts, transposing knowledge across domains, surveying what is known, a capable model is a genuine accelerant. What it has not been shown to do is *participate in the practice that generated the record*. It has read the papers. It has not run the experiments. No one has stood with a hand on its wrist and said *this is the result we don't publish, and here is why we don't trust it*. So it will produce outputs consistent with the record and will not reliably notice when the record itself is wrong — when a decade's consensus rested on a cascade of correlated errors that a replication study later collapsed. It has the clicks. Whether it can contribute the next one is the open question.

And the question has genuinely shifted since Polanyi, which is worth admitting plainly. Polanyi wrote before high-frame-rate motion capture of skilled practice, before reinforcement learning from human demonstration. Recent vision-language-action models — Google DeepMind's RT-2 line and its successors, from 2023 onward — were trained on large corpora of robot-manipulation demonstrations annotated with language, and acquired behaviors roboticists had called tacit: how much grip force to apply, how to adjust when a grasp slips, how to recover from a near-miss without restarting. None of that was written as rules. It was lifted from data about expert behavior. Some of the tacit, in this narrow domain, compressed. The strong reading is that Polanyi was wrong — tacit knowledge is not uncapturable in principle, just expensive, and the cost has fallen. The weak reading is that what these models acquire is a functional approximation that behaves like tacit knowledge inside the trained distribution but lacks whatever made the master's hands transfer to a problem he had never seen. Imitation-learning researchers have a name for the relevant failure: *cascading errors*. The demonstrator's data shows the system what to do near the demonstrator's good trajectories and almost nothing about what to do once the agent drifts somewhere the demonstrator never went; one small error puts it slightly off-distribution, the next input resembles the training data less, and errors compound. The master's hands were trained not only on his successes but on a lifetime of his recoveries from near-misses. The model has not had that lifetime. The evidence does not yet settle which reading is right. The honest framing for 2026 is that whether tacit knowledge is capturable in principle has moved from a settled matter to a live empirical question, and the next decade will tell us which forms compress and which do not.

Where I come down, cautiously: AI systems are unambiguous participants in the aggregation layer. They are participants in the explicit-record-extension layer in domains where the record is rich. Whether they are participants in the *practice* — the part that generates new evidence by doing experiments and interpreting failures — is unsettled, and the rate at which they are being deployed into practice-adjacent roles is itself the experiment running in real time.

---

Step back, and the through-line of this chapter is also the answer to a question the whole book has been circling.

For most of human history the ratchet ran on memory and the spoken word, and it was correspondingly fragile — a generation lost to famine or war could take a body of knowledge with it. The archive changed that. Writing externalized the explicit record so that it no longer had to be carried in living heads; a library is a population's memory stored outside any individual, surviving the deaths of everyone who wrote into it. The network — the printing press, then the journal, then the internet — drove the cost of distributing and revising that record toward zero, and in doing so it raised the effective population size of the ratchet far past anything Henrich's Tasmania could reach. A researcher today inherits the work of strangers on other continents and other centuries, clicks the ratchet one notch further, and deposits the result back into the archive for strangers not yet born. The starling murmuration coordinates across fifty thousand birds in one sky. The human archive coordinates across billions of minds and thousands of years, and it does so by the same trick the toddler used to point at the dog — making one mind's content available to another as a shareable object — extended now past breath, past distance, past death. The systems we have built to read that entire archive at once are the newest instrument plugged into it, and what they can and cannot do with it is the subject the rest of this book turns to. The knowledge was never in the individuals. It is in the network. We have just built a new kind of node.
