# Chapter 6 — Pattern Recognition and Perception

In 2016, Cait Newport and colleagues lowered two photographs of human faces over a tank of fish.

The fish was an archerfish, a small reef and mangrove species best known for an entirely different trick. It hangs just below the surface, fixes on an insect resting on overhanging leaves, corrects for the way light bends as it crosses from air into water, and fires a precise jet of water that knocks the insect down into the tank to be eaten. Its brain is the size of a pea. It has no neocortex — none of the six-layered sheet of tissue mammalian neuroscientists are fond of calling the seat of intelligence. What it has is the older three-layered pallium that vertebrates have run for at least half a billion years.

The fish learned which of the two faces meant food. That is not the surprising part; telling apart two images is a low bar. The surprising part came next. Newport's group dropped the trained face into a lineup of forty-four photographs — the familiar one plus forty-three faces the fish had never seen — and asked which it would spit at.

It picked the right face about eighty-one percent of the time. Then they made it harder. In a second experiment they stripped away the easy cues, standardizing the images for color, for brightness, for the overall outline of the head, so that nothing remained to go on but the arrangement of the facial features themselves. Accuracy did not fall. It rose, to roughly eighty-six percent. The fish was picking one specific human face out of a crowd of human faces, using only the geometry of the face, on hardware that evolution assembled before mammals existed.

The surprise is not that the fish is clever. The surprise is that we ever assumed face recognition required anything the fish lacks. The architecture that does this is not a specialization of primates, not of mammals, not even of vertebrates with a neocortex. It is the foundational vertebrate answer to a computational problem that arose the moment the first animal with eyes had to tell objects apart in a visual world — and the fish has been solving it, on three layers of pallium, for five hundred million years.

This chapter is about how that machine works. And about why a neural network trained on a million images can match the fish on a benchmark and still be doing something different underneath.

Any perceptual system that acts intelligently has to do two things that pull against each other.

The first is discrimination. Two stimuli that are similar but not the same must be kept apart. The predator and the harmless decoy have similar silhouettes. The toxic berry and the edible one look nearly alike. A system that cannot separate similar inputs cannot act correctly.

The second is generalization. A thing met in a new form must be recognized as the same kind of thing met before. The predator seen from above and the predator seen from below cast different shapes on the retina. A face in dim light carries different brightness values than the same face in sun. A system that cannot bridge these variations recognizes only what it has already seen in precisely the form it already saw it.

These pull in opposite directions. Discrimination wants representations far apart for inputs that are nearly alike — small differences in, large differences out. Generalization wants representations close together for different instances of one category — the predator from above and the predator from below collapsing onto the same response. And a third demand compounds both: invariance, recognition that holds steady across changes in viewpoint, scale, rotation, lighting, and partial hiding. The frog is the frog whether seen from the side, from above, or upside down.

The vertebrate pallium solves all three at once, with the same neurons and the same circuit, and it has done so for at least half a billion years — which is the strongest evidence available that this is not one design among many but close to *the* solution, near enough that nothing in the lineage has felt pressure to redesign it. The trick comes in three operations, and the cleanest place to watch all three is the sense of smell, because the olfactory version of the problem is the oldest and the most transparent. Most of the pallium's deep history is olfactory; vision arrived later and was plugged into hardware first built for smell.

The blueprint is a sheet of excitatory neurons — pyramidal cells — laced through with inhibitory interneurons. Inputs arrive from the senses; outputs leave toward muscle. And the excitatory cells talk sideways to each other through horizontal axons, the recurrent collaterals, that run across the sheet and synapse on their neighbors. Three elements: excitatory projection cells, inhibitory interneurons, recurrent connections. Three operations follow, one from each.

The first operation is dimensionality expansion. A fish has several hundred kinds of olfactory receptor in its nose, each responding to a broad range of molecules, each molecule lighting up a broad range of receptors at different strengths. The signal arriving at the brain is a high-dimensional pattern smeared across hundreds of channels. And the first thing the pallium does is throw fibers from the olfactory bulb *diffusely* onto its pyramidal neurons — each bulb fiber touching many cortical cells, each cortical cell sampling many bulb fibers, no two cortical cells drawing the same combination. The hundreds of input channels are projected onto hundreds of thousands of cortical cells: a higher-dimensional space than the input arrived in.

Why would spreading a signal into more dimensions help? Picture two points sitting almost on top of each other on a line — nearly indistinguishable. Now give each a second coordinate, drawn from some other feature of the input. If that second coordinate is at all sensitive to whatever separates them, the points pull apart in the plane. Add a third dimension, a thousandth, each picking up some further aspect of the difference, and two points that were a hair apart on the line become reliably distinct in the expanded space. This is the geometric content of Cover's theorem, proved by Thomas Cover in 1965: a set of points that cannot be cleanly separated in a low-dimensional space can almost always be separated in a high enough one, provided the projection introduces nonlinear variation. The same insight underlies the kernel trick in machine learning. The pallium does not need to be clever about pulling similar inputs apart. The expansion does it geometrically — as long as each cortical cell samples a *different* random subset of inputs, so that each new cell is a genuinely new dimension rather than a redundant copy. (The cerebellum runs the most dramatic version of this same trick anywhere in biology, fanning a few thousand inputs out onto the order of fifty billion granule cells — in the human cerebellum, the single largest population of neurons in the brain.)

Expansion alone is not enough, though. If the expanded representation is *dense* — if many cortical cells fire for any given odor — the gain is mostly thrown away, because two similar inputs still produce strongly overlapping patterns, just spread across more cells. So the second operation sparsifies. When an excitatory cell fires, it drives nearby inhibitory interneurons, and those interneurons clamp down on the surrounding excitatory cells — especially the ones that were only weakly driven. Only the cells receiving the strongest input survive. Out of a thousand cortical neurons that could respond to an odor, perhaps twenty to forty actually fire; the rest go silent, and each odor lights a different small subset. This is sparse coding, and it sharpens discrimination hard: two odors with twenty percent overlap in their inputs might end up with only one or two percent overlap in their cortical patterns, because the inhibitory competition forces the winners to diverge. What was similar becomes distinct. And sparseness has a second payoff worth holding onto for later — if only twenty of a thousand cells carry any given pattern, two patterns rarely collide by accident, and the connections strengthened for one pattern are mostly different from those strengthened for another. The brain's resistance to overwriting old memories with new ones comes partly from the simple fact that its codes are too sparse to bump into each other.

The first two operations handle discrimination. The third handles generalization, and it is the one that earns a closer look. The pyramidal cells are wired to each other through those recurrent collaterals, and those connections are modifiable by the same Hebbian plasticity the previous chapter traced in the slug and the hippocampus: when a set of cells fires together, repeatedly, the connections among them strengthen. After that strengthening, something new becomes possible. Suppose a fish has learned that Odor A means food, and Odor A lights a pattern of forty cells out of a thousand, their mutual connections now strong. Later the fish meets a dilute whiff of Odor A — enough molecules to drive only twenty-five of the forty directly. Those twenty-five fire. But each of them is strongly wired to the other fifteen, the ones too weakly stimulated to fire on their own. Each of those fifteen now receives recurrent input from twenty-five strong partners at once, summing far above threshold. The fifteen fire. The pattern *completes*. From a partial input the network has reconstructed the full template, and the downstream evaluator receives the same signal it would have for the full odor. The fish approaches the food.

Without the recurrent connections, the dilute odor stays twenty-five cells — a weaker, different pattern — and the evaluator fails to recognize it. This is pattern completion, and it is the generalization operation: the partial or noisy input flows into the nearest stored template by the dynamics of the recurrent network. It is what makes learned knowledge useful in a real world where inputs are always partial, smudged, and variable. It is also what separates this architecture from a simple feedforward feature detector. A feedforward system maps input to output once, through a fixed transform. The recurrent system has *dynamics* — its output at one moment feeds back as input at the next, and over a few tens of milliseconds it settles into the stored pattern that best matches what came in. The formal name is auto-associative memory: the network's stable states are the stored patterns, and any partial input slides into the nearest one. This is the Hopfield network, written down in mathematical form by John Hopfield in 1982 — and run by the vertebrate pallium for half a billion years before the paper.

Now trace a face through the whole machine, in the primate visual system where it has been mapped neuron by neuron. Light falls on the retina, two million photoreceptors firing in proportion to local brightness: high resolution, but raw, no features yet. The signal climbs to V1, the primary visual cortex, where each neuron is tuned to a small oriented edge at one spot on the retinal map — vertical, horizontal, every angle between. This was David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel's discovery in 1962, the work for which they shared the Nobel Prize nineteen years later, in 1981; the face has now been broken into thousands of local edge fragments. That is dimensionality expansion in vision — one feature per pixel at the retina, many features per location at V1, and similar faces beginning to pull apart because their edge structure differs in ways raw brightness did not expose. Onward to V2 and V4, where neurons combine V1's edges into corners, junctions, curves, color and texture patches, the dimensionality still climbing and lateral inhibition culling at every stage. Then to inferior temporal cortex, IT, where neurons respond to whole-object features — eyes, mouths, the spatial layout of a face's parts — and a small subset respond to faces and only faces. These are the cells whose loss produces prosopagnosia, the inability to recognize a familiar face while ordinary object recognition stays intact. The IT representation is sparse: any given face lights only a small subset, and different faces light different subsets.

And then the stage the standard models leave out, the stage that does the recognizing. The IT neurons that fired for your mother's face on past occasions have, through Hebbian plasticity, formed strong reciprocal connections. When you see her again — partly hidden, in poor light, from an angle you have never seen — some of those neurons fire from the feedforward input, their recurrent connections pull in the rest of the learned ensemble, and the pattern completes. The full *mother's-face* ensemble is active. Downstream regions read it out; the amygdala attaches feeling; the hippocampus retrieves the memories tied to this person; motor systems begin a smile. For Newport's archerfish, the same sequence runs on three layers of pallium instead of six layers of cortex. Retinal input enters the optic tectum and the dorsal pallium; the pallium expands the representation, sparsifies it through lateral inhibition, and completes it through recurrent collaterals; the output drives the muscles — the spit. Three layers or six, sheets or nuclei: it matters far less than whether you have the three operations. Have dimensionality expansion, sparse coding, and recurrent completion, and you have pattern recognition.

I want to name one thing I cannot explain, because the chapter's honesty requires it. Caroline DeLong and colleagues trained goldfish to discriminate photographs of a toy turtle from a toy frog, learned at zero rotation, then tested at ninety, one hundred eighty, and two hundred seventy degrees. The fish stayed well above chance at every angle — genuine viewpoint invariance. But they were *faster* on the upside-down, one-hundred-eighty-degree case than on the ninety-degree one, and that asymmetry is not what pure viewpoint-invariant recognition predicts; a fully viewpoint-independent representation should treat all rotations alike. The asymmetry hints that invariant recognition is built on top of a viewpoint-*sensitive* substrate — perhaps something like mental rotation, cheaper for a one-hundred-eighty-degree flip, which preserves left–right symmetry relations, than for a ninety-degree turn, which does not. I do not fully understand it. (I should also flag that the precise reaction-time asymmetry here is one detail I have not been able to confirm against the original figures, so treat the specific anomaly as provisional.) But its existence, real or not, points at something true: invariant recognition rests on a viewpoint-sensitive base, and the two interact in ways our models have not captured. The three-operation story is the main structure. This is a loose thread inside it, and I would rather hand it to you than hide it.

That brings us to the machines. Hubel and Wiesel's edge detectors were the direct inspiration for the convolutional neural network: if the brain recognizes by building a hierarchy of ever more abstract features, an artificial system with the same hierarchy should manage the same feats. By 2012, when AlexNet won the ImageNet competition by a wide margin, that architecture had taken over computer vision. And in one sense the inspiration paid off cleanly. Daniel Yamins and James DiCarlo at MIT, in 2014, trained a hierarchical network on object recognition and then asked whether its internal activity predicted the firing of real neurons in monkey IT cortex. The network's top layer was the best predictor of IT responses the field had ever produced. Match the architecture and the task closely enough, and you get matching representations. That is a real and important result.

In another sense the analogy breaks, and a 2019 experiment made the break unmistakable. Robert Geirhos and colleagues at Tübingen pulled apart two cues that normally travel together: shape and texture. A photograph of a cat has the shape of a cat *and* the texture of cat fur; a human classifying it uses the shape, and the texture is incidental. So Geirhos built cue-conflict images — a cat-shaped silhouette filled with elephant skin, a bear filled with the texture of a soda can — and asked humans and ImageNet-trained networks to label them. Humans followed the shape, calling the cat-shaped image a cat about ninety-five percent of the time. The networks followed the texture, calling it an elephant. The systems that had reached superhuman benchmark scores had been right for the wrong reason: they were not building shape-based representations of objects, they were classifying textures, and the two strategies agree on most natural images only because in nature elephants reliably come wrapped in elephant skin. Break the correlation and the difference stands exposed. And the texture bias was not one model's quirk; Geirhos found it across every major ImageNet architecture he tested.

This is the operational meaning of *right answer, wrong mechanism*. A benchmark score does not tell you whether the system solved the problem the way you think. Look at what was copied from biology and what was left out, and the gap explains itself. The feature hierarchy was copied — local detectors at the bottom, abstraction rising. Sparse coding was copied only loosely; the nonlinearities in these networks produce some sparsity, but not the lateral-inhibition, winner-take-most competition the cortex runs, so the representations come out less cleanly separated and less robust under occlusion. Recurrent auto-associative completion was mostly absent — until the attention mechanism in transformer architectures brought back something resembling recurrent dynamics — and without it a network must recognize a degraded input directly from one feedforward pass; if the degraded input does not closely match the training distribution, it fails. The engineers took the feedforward recognition path and left out the recurrent stabilization path. The result is a system superb at the specific discriminations it was trained on and brittle on much else. The fish has run the full architecture for five hundred million years. The networks have run two-thirds of it for ten.

Two honest limits before closing. The completion story is clearest in the olfactory pallium and in hippocampal CA3, where the recurrent wiring is dense and well-characterized; in IT cortex it is supported but not established at the same level of evidence, and I have described it there with more confidence than the data strictly license. And I do not fully understand what makes the recurrent dynamics *stop* at the right moment rather than running away into oscillation or blowup. In practice the network settles in tens of milliseconds. The mechanisms that govern the settling — thalamic gating, cortical feedback, slow inhibition — are described in textbooks more cleanly than the evidence warrants. We have models. We do not have confidence that the models are right.

Everything in this chapter is a way of pulling signal out of a flood of input — expanding it so similar things separate, thinning it so the separation survives, completing it so a fragment recovers the whole. The retina caps the flood at what two million photoreceptors can carry. And here is the quiet thing humans did about that ceiling. We could not make our eyes admit more, so we built instruments to widen the input before it ever reached the retina. The microscope took the world below the limit of the eye and threw it onto the same three-operation machinery the archerfish uses to find a face. The telescope did the same for the world above the limit, hauling a galaxy's worth of separated points into a representation a pallium could complete. And when even the trained eye could no longer keep up with the flood — millions of medical scans, billions of photographs — we built the image classifier, the convolutional network itself, to run the front two-thirds of the architecture for us at a scale no nervous system could reach. The fish does discrimination, generalization, and invariance inside one pea-sized brain. We do the same operations by pushing them out into glass and silicon — extending the front end of perception past the eye, so that the ancient pallium, unchanged, can keep doing what it has always done with what we feed it.
