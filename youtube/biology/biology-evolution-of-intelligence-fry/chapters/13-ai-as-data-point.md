# Chapter 13 — AI as Data Point

It is the afternoon of May 11, 1997, on the thirty-fifth floor of the Equitable Building in midtown Manhattan, and Garry Kasparov is staring at a chessboard he has already lost. He is thirty-four years old, the highest-rated chess player who has ever lived, and he has been world champion for twelve years. Across the board sits nothing — a human operator relaying moves from a machine in another room. The machine is an IBM cabinet the size of two refrigerators, six and a half feet tall, with two hundred and fifty-six custom processors evaluating roughly two hundred million chess positions every second. Its name is Deep Blue. Nineteen moves into the sixth and deciding game of the rematch, Kasparov pushes back from the table, holds his head in his hands, and resigns. He has lost the match three and a half to two and a half. It is the first time a reigning world champion has been beaten in a full match, under tournament time controls, by a machine.

The newspapers said the machine had become intelligent. They were wrong, and the way they were wrong is the subject of this chapter.

Deep Blue did not understand chess. It did not know it was playing chess. It did not know it was playing. It searched — it took the position on the board, generated every legal reply, and every reply to those replies, and every reply to those, building a tree of possible futures millions of branches wide, scoring each leaf with a hand-tuned function that counted material and king safety and pawn structure, and choosing the move that led toward the best-scoring branch under the assumption that Kasparov would also play his best. It was a search algorithm running on specialized silicon. What it had that Kasparov did not was speed: two hundred million positions a second against a human's two or three. It won the way a calculator wins an arithmetic contest. The result was real. The chess was real. The intelligence the headlines announced was a category error.

Nineteen years later, the error got harder to make, and more interesting.

It is March 10, 2016, in the Four Seasons Hotel in Seoul, and Lee Sedol — one of the greatest Go players of his generation, holder of eighteen world titles — is playing the second game of a five-game match against a program called AlphaGo. Go is not chess. The board is nineteen by nineteen, the number of legal positions exceeds the number of atoms in the observable universe by a margin that makes the comparison almost meaningless, and brute-force search of the kind Deep Blue used is hopeless — the tree is too wide to enumerate. For decades, Go was the game the machines could not play, because playing it well seemed to require something the experts called *intuition*: a sense, not reducible to calculation, of where the stones wanted to go.

On the thirty-seventh move of that second game, AlphaGo placed a stone on the fifth line from the edge — a shoulder hit, deep in the board, in a region where received Go wisdom said no strong player would play this early. The commentators, themselves professional players, assumed it was a mistake, a bug, a misclick. Lee Sedol stood up and left the room for fifteen minutes. When he came back he sat and studied the board for a long time. The move was not a mistake. AlphaGo's own policy network — the part of the system trained to predict where a human would play — had assigned that move a probability of roughly one in ten thousand. A human would almost never play it. AlphaGo played it because a different part of the system, trained by playing millions of games against versions of itself, had calculated that it was good. It won the game. It won the match, four to one. The next year, in Wuzhen, a stronger version beat Ke Jie, the world's top-ranked human, three games to none.

Move 37 is the thing worth holding onto, because it is genuinely different from Deep Blue. AlphaGo was not searching a tree it could exhaust. It had learned a function — a deep neural network, trained first on human games and then on games against itself — that took a board position and returned an estimate of which moves were good and who was winning. That function had discovered, somewhere in the billions of parameters adjusted by self-play, a piece of Go knowledge that thousands of years of human masters had not found. This is not a calculator winning an arithmetic contest. This is something that learned, from exposure to the game, a regularity its teachers did not possess.

So: is it intelligent?

That is the question this book has spent twelve chapters teaching you not to ask in that form. *Is it intelligent* is not one question. It is at least three questions sharing a sentence, and almost all the public confusion about artificial intelligence comes from answering one of them while sounding as though all three have been settled.

---

The first is the height question. *Is the machine more or less intelligent than a human, a chimpanzee, a crow?* The question assumes intelligence is a single quantity that admits of a ranking — the *scala naturae*, the ladder, the thing Chapter 1 spent its whole length arguing against and the eleven chapters since have been the empirical case against. A Clark's nutcracker caches on the order of tens of thousands of pine seeds across a mountainside in autumn and relocates them through the winter under snow, months later, from spatial memory. It cannot produce a sentence. The octopus integrates color, polarization, and texture across eight independently mobile arms into a real-time model of its surroundings of a richness no primate's somatic sense approaches. It has almost no social cognition and dies a few months after it breeds, never having met its young. No organism is uniformly high on every axis. *More intelligent* only means something once you have specified which axis, and once you have specified the axis, the global ranking dissolves into the comparative map this book has been drawing.

The second question conflates skill with the efficiency of acquiring it. In 2019 the engineer François Chollet wrote a paper, *On the Measure of Intelligence*, that drew a line the comparative literature had been drawing for decades without a clean name for it. Skill is what a system does on a task. Skill-acquisition efficiency is how much it needed — how much data, how many examples, how much built-in prior structure — to get there. Hold the two apart and a familiar confusion comes unstuck. In 2017 a team at Stanford led by Andre Esteva trained a convolutional neural network on 129,450 clinical images spanning two thousand skin conditions and showed it matching twenty-one board-certified dermatologists on the sensitivity and specificity of cancer classification. The result is sometimes read as a machine that has *learned dermatology*. But the dermatologist acquired her skill over twenty years of biological development, medical training, and patients seen — some fraction of them with mortality on the line for a misread. The network reached comparable benchmark performance from a hundred and thirty thousand labeled examples and a loss function it was minimizing. Skill: present in both. Skill-acquisition efficiency: lower in the network by orders of magnitude. That gap is not a footnote to what the two systems are. It is the answer to what the two systems are.

The third question is the one this book has been quietly preparing you to ask, and the deepest. It is not *can the machine produce the behavior*. It is whether the cognitive function is running, or only the language that describes it.

A system trained on text learns that text. Among the things it learns is the language humans use to describe their own minds: *I'm not sure about that. Let me think about what she believes. The evidence suggests but does not prove.* In a human, those phrases are produced by cognitive operations — uncertainty monitoring, belief attribution, perspective-taking, calibrated reasoning, the very capacities this book diagnosed in dolphins and apes and crows. In a language model, those same phrases are produced by predicting the next token over a training distribution that contained vast quantities of such language. The question is whether, underneath the linguistic markers, the operation has run — or whether the model has learned to emit the *output* of a cognitive operation without performing the operation, the way it learned to emit the output of any other linguistic regularity.

This is not a question about consciousness. It is a question about mechanism, and mechanism is testable. The test is the one this book has used on every animal in it: preserve the logical structure of a task and change the surface features that a mere pattern-matcher would lean on. A system running the real operation generalizes across the change. A system matching templates does not.

So let me do to a frontier language model exactly what the comparative psychologists did to the macaque and the scrub-jay and the cleaner wrasse. Run the same paradigms. Change the surface. Watch the seam.

---

Start with theory of mind, the capacity Chapter 9 traced from prairie-vole consolation to the false-belief tests in apes. The canonical instrument is the Sally-Anne task: Sally puts a marble in a basket, leaves the room, Anne moves it to a box, Sally comes back. Where will Sally look? A child who has passed the four-year-old threshold says the basket — Sally believes, falsely, that the marble is where she left it. In 2023 the psychologist Michal Kosinski reported that frontier language models pass the canonical version at near-ceiling rates, and the result drew serious speculation that theory of mind had emerged spontaneously inside the model.

Then the perturbation. The same year, Tomer Ullman published a short, surgical paper — *Large Language Models Fail on Trivial Alterations to Theory-of-Mind Tasks* — that did to the model what a good experimentalist does to any too-clean result. He preserved the logical structure of the false-belief task and altered the physical circumstances in ways that should change the answer if the system is actually reasoning about Sally's information state. Make the container transparent. Now Sally is in the room, watching the marble through the glass the whole time. There is no false belief — she has seen everything. The model, asked where Sally will look, applies the template anyway: *the basket, because that is where she left it and she does not know it was moved*. It has matched the narrative shape of a false-belief story and produced the false-belief answer to a situation that contains no false belief. Right answer on the original. Wrong mechanism under perturbation. The model is doing template-matching on the structure of the story, not inverse reasoning over Sally's access to information.

Now perception, the capacity Chapter 6 found in an archerfish picking one human face out of forty-four. A modern vision model identifies objects in ordinary photographs with a confidence a radiologist would respect; on standard image benchmarks it reaches accuracy no biological system matches for breadth and throughput. The benchmark scores are real, and inside the distribution the benchmark measures, the systems are extraordinary. Then the perturbation, and this one was found before anyone went looking for it in a chatbot. In 2018 Robert Geirhos and colleagues built images of *cue conflict* — a silhouette in the shape of a cat, filled with the texture of elephant skin. Shape says cat. Texture says elephant. Humans, asked what they see, follow shape about ninety-five percent of the time; we are shape creatures, the outline is the object. The ImageNet-trained networks followed texture, at high rates, and called the cat an elephant. Superhuman benchmark accuracy, reached by a computation that was not the one the humans were running — because in every natural photograph shape and texture arrive together and agree, the network had been free to take whichever was statistically easier to learn, and it took texture. When I ran the same cue-conflict design against a current multimodal model for this chapter, the failure was the same: cat-shaped, elephant-skinned, the model said elephant. Right answer in-distribution. Wrong mechanism under cue-conflict. (I should be honest that this last run is my own diagnostic, not a published replication; the Geirhos result on the ImageNet networks is the established one.)

Now metacognition, the capacity Chapter 11 found in the dolphin Natua reaching for the *uncertain* paddle and in Hampton's macaque declining the trials it would have failed. Here the verdict has to be more careful, because the honest answer is that the test has not been cleanly run. The model produces calibrated-sounding confidence language — *I'm fairly confident; this may be outside what I know well* — in roughly the right places. And there is real signal here: in 2022 Stephanie Lin and colleagues showed that a model can be trained to express its uncertainty in words, *ninety percent confidence*, calibrated against its own accuracy, without reading out any internal probability — the verbalized confidence is a learned linguistic surface, taught from human confidence expressions paired with whether they turned out right. That is more than nothing; the paper found the calibration leans on internal representations that do track something about the model's epistemic state. But it is not yet what Hampton measured. Hampton's signature was an *accuracy advantage on the trials the macaque chose to take* — proof the animal was reading the trial-specific state of its own memory, not the experimental category "hard." The equivalent test on a frozen language model — does it do better on the questions it opts into than on the ones it is forced to answer, and does that advantage survive Ullman-style perturbation of the surface? — has not, to my knowledge, been run cleanly. So the placement is *equivocal*, and equivocal is the honest word. The fluent language is established. The underlying monitoring is not yet measured.

Now navigation, the capacity Chapter 7 found in a desert ant crossing a salt pan in a dead-straight line to a one-millimeter hole. Give a multimodal model high-resolution imagery of that Tunisian pan. It will describe the terrain in four languages, recount *Cataglyphis* biology, and discuss the path-integration literature in more detail than any ant carries or could carry. It cannot reliably tell you which way the ant should walk to get home. This is not a knowledge gap — the model holds more propositional knowledge about desert navigation than every ant that has ever lived, combined. It is a structural absence. The ant's navigation is implemented in a body: a vestibular system, proprioception in six legs counting strides, a sky-compass reading polarized light, a home vector updated continuously, and death at the end of a failed return. The model's "navigation" is token sequences about navigation. There is no closing feedback loop on physical consequence. A larger model trained on more of the literature would describe the journey better. It would not make the journey, because there is no it that journeys. Near-zero on this axis is not a gap you patch by adding parameters. It is a fact about a system that has never had a body, never returned to a nest, never failed to return and ended.

---

Step back and look at what those probes have in common, because the convergence is the point.

Five different capacities. Five different domains — social cognition, perception, metacognition, navigation. Run the same diagnostic move in each: hold the logic, change the surface, watch whether the answer tracks the logic or the surface. And the same shape of result keeps coming back. Canonical pass. Perturbation fail. The system reaches the right output by a computation that is not the computation a biological agent with stakes would be running to reach it.

This is the trade-off, named plainly, and it is the trade-off the whole chapter turns on: **fluency against grounding.** The model is a system that passes the language test while failing the test a toddler passes. It will discuss the false-belief task with more erudition than the four-year-old who solves it, and then misapply it the moment the marble is behind glass. It will describe the cat-shaped elephant in clean prose and call it an elephant. The capacity to produce the *language of* a cognitive function has been pushed to a height no organism reaches; the capacity to run the function under conditions the language did not memorize has stayed near the floor. Those two things, in a human, are welded together. In the model they have come apart. And the place where they come apart is exactly where the surface stops matching the structure.

Now assemble the readings into a shape. Plot the capacities of this book on the spokes of a wheel and mark where the machine sits on each. There is a frontier zone where the profile pushes to the extreme high: pattern recognition in-distribution, retrieval and synthesis of explicit written knowledge, in-context language operations, the sheer breadth of associative reach. There is a near-zero zone where it collapses: embodied navigation, theory of mind under perturbation, perception under cue-conflict, the consequence-bearing core of every capacity that lives in a body. And there is an equivocal band between — metacognitive language without demonstrated metacognitive monitoring, emotion words without affective behavior, plans that hold on canonical templates and break under structural variation.

The shape is not a smooth curve. It is jagged — sharp spurs outward, sharp collapses inward, on adjacent spokes. And no biological profile looks like this.

The *C. elegans* profile, from Chapter 3, is a small, tight kernel at the cognitive floor: state-dependent steering, bare-essentials learning and memory, and absence — not low scores, absence — on everything else, because most of the spokes do not apply to a worm. The corvid profile is mid-sized and asymmetric: strong on flexible problem-solving, tool use, episodic-like memory, planning, perspective-taking in caching, flat on language. The chimpanzee profile is broad and roughly continuous all the way around its body, with a visible flat side only on declarative language and recursive mentalizing. Overlay the machine on any of these and it matches none of them. It is not the worm with extras, not the corvid rescaled, not the chimpanzee with the language spoke filled in. It is extreme in both directions on neighboring spokes — at the frontier on in-distribution pattern recognition and near the floor on embodied navigation at the same time, two values no animal holds together, because no animal's pattern-recognition system was ever built without a body to look around with. The shape is not a rung on a ladder. It is a region of cognitive space biology never populated.

---

That shape is not a coincidence. It follows from how the system was made, and naming the mechanism is the deepest move in the chapter.

Every cognitive profile in this book was carved by stakes — by mortality, by reproductive failure, by social rank lost or held. The chickadee's hippocampus enlarges in autumn because birds with smaller autumn hippocampi cached worse and starved at higher rates through the winter; the ones with the larger organ are the ones whose lineages survived to be measured. The honeybee colony's decision rules are tuned to the real survival parameters of overwintering in a temperate climate because colonies running miscalibrated rules — choosing a nest cavity too small, too exposed, too damp — froze and left no daughters. The chimpanzee tracks coalition dynamics with precision because individuals who misread an alliance lost reproductive access or were driven from the group. Every spoke on every animal in this book was shaped, over evolutionary time, by the cost of getting that spoke wrong, paid in the only currency selection accepts.

The machine's profile was carved by gradient descent on a training loss, applied to a corpus written by biological organisms whose lives were full of stakes. The model inherits the *output* of stakes-driven cognition wherever that output was written down. It does not inherit the parts of cognition that were never written down — the parts that live in the body, the reflex, the procedural memory laid down by iterations of actually getting it wrong with the consequence attached.

That is why the shape is what it is. The spokes where the machine scores at the frontier are the spokes with abundant training data, because they correspond to cognitive products humans recorded in enormous volume: language form, propositional knowledge, labeled images, reasoning expressed in text. The spokes where it collapses are the ones where the operation lives in a body and leaves little written trace: embodied navigation, real-time error correction with mortality on the line, the stakes-bearing core of judgment. The stakes axis is not one more spoke to add. It is the explanatory variable for the shape of the whole wheel. Biological profiles are roughly continuous because one body has to handle every spoke at once, and a body that is superb at one thing and absent at the neighboring thing tends not to survive to reproduce. The machine's profile is jagged because the training distribution is uneven and there is no body forcing the spokes to cohere.

---

Let me close on a distinction the chapter has been circling, because it is the hinge into the final chapter.

Every spoke can be split into two questions. Does the system have the capacity — and who points the capacity at what? A pH meter has the capacity to measure hydrogen-ion concentration precisely. It does not choose which solution to dip into; the chemist does. A GPS receiver computes position from satellite timing to within a few meters. It does not choose the destination; the driver does. This is the pattern every chapter of this book has closed on without naming it — the worm and the Roomba, the desert ant and the GPS, written memory, the microscope, the recommender trained on the same prediction-error term as the dopamine neuron. A cognitive instrument extends a capacity, faster or at greater scale, while the *direction* — what to aim it at, how to read what comes back, when to trust it — stays with the user.

The machine is the next entry in that catalog, and the pattern holds with a twist. The twist is that it extends some capacities so far past the biological baseline that the user is tempted to confuse capacity with direction. A model that produces fluent prose can be mistaken for an author. A model that produces calibrated-sounding analysis can be mistaken for an analyst. A model that produces metacognitive-sounding sentences can be mistaken for something that monitors itself. In each case the surface output of the capacity resembles the surface output of the directing function, because in us the two are welded together — and the model, as we have just watched across five probes, is precisely the system in which they have come apart. The diagnostic I ran in this chapter is exactly that decoupling check. Right answer on the canonical case: the capacity is present. Wrong mechanism under perturbation: the directing function is not. Across all five probes the signature is the same — extraordinary reach on the capacity, and the direction still sitting with the human.

This is not a verdict on whether the machine is intelligent. The radar shape is the answer to a better-posed question — not *how high* but *what kind of thing, and where in the distribution that kind of thing lives*. It is a region no animal occupies, produced by an optimization no lineage faced, with the capacities pushed outward and the consequence-bearing direction left near the floor. The headline in 1997 said the machine had become intelligent. The better sentence, the one this book has spent thirteen chapters earning, is that the machine is the newest and strangest data point on a map of mind that was never a ladder — built by us, shaped by no stakes of its own, and holding the direction, still, in human hands. What that position implies — what stays human, why, and what the user has to keep doing to keep the whole toolkit working — is the question the last chapter asks.
