# Chapter 12a — Language

A fourteen-month-old is in the park with her mother. A basset hound comes waddling past, all ears and loose skin and rolling gait, and the child's whole body reorients toward it. Her arm comes up. Her index finger extends, aimed straight at the dog. And then she does the thing that matters: she turns her head and looks at her mother's face.

She is not asking for the dog. She does not want her mother to fetch it, feed it, or do anything at all with it. She wants her mother to *see* it. She holds the point and watches her mother's eyes swing toward the animal, then checks her mother's face for the flicker of shared recognition — *yes, I see it too* — and only then, satisfied, does she lower her arm.

That gesture has a name. It is called **declarative pointing**, from the Latin *declarare*, to make clear or make known — pointing not to get something but to make something known, to align two minds on the same object in the world. By eighteen months she will do it dozens of times a day: at airplanes, at the moon, at pictures in a book, at a truck going by that holds no possible use for her whatsoever. The function is pure. To share attention. To make sure two minds are fixed on the same thing at the same time, and to *know* that they are.

In more than thirty years of careful observation by researchers specifically watching for it, no chimpanzee has been reliably documented to point at a thing because the thing is interesting and someone else might like to see it too.

That asymmetry — a human toddler does something effortlessly, dozens of times a day, that our nearest living relatives essentially never do — is the doorway into the hardest question in comparative cognition. This chapter is about language: what it actually is, why communication is not the same thing, what the most heavily trained apes can and cannot do, and what a schoolyard full of deaf children in Nicaragua built out of nothing.

---

Start by separating two words that everyday speech welds together: communication and language.

By any reasonable definition, the animal kingdom is drowning in communication. Plants release defense chemicals through fungal networks in the soil and neighboring plants respond. Vervet monkeys give three acoustically distinct alarm calls — one for leopard, one for eagle, one for snake — and each sends the troop into a different escape. Honeybees waggle in figure-eights on the comb to direct nestmates to flowers kilometers away. Communication is ancient and everywhere. The question of whether any non-human species has *language* is not the question of whether it communicates. It is far narrower.

In 1960, the linguist Charles Hockett laid out thirteen design features to mark the boundary, in an article in *Scientific American*. Most of the thirteen are widely shared across animal signals. Four of them carry the weight for the hard cases. **Displacement** is the ability to talk about things not present in time or space — yesterday, tomorrow, the next valley. **Productivity** is the ability to generate genuinely novel utterances from a finite stock of parts. **Cultural transmission** means the system is learned rather than hardwired. And **duality of patterning** means meaningless units (sounds) combine into meaningful ones (words), which combine again into utterances — a two-layered combinatorial system. The claim, sharpened over fifty years of research, is that human language is the only system in which all of these co-occur.

In 2002, Marc Hauser, Noam Chomsky, and Tecumseh Fitch pushed the line further, arguing that the one feature uniquely and essentially human is **recursion** — embedding structures inside structures of the same kind, with no built-in ceiling. *The dog that the cat that the mouse startled chased ran away.* You can nest those clauses indefinitely; the rule does not run out. No non-human communication system does this. (Their claim is itself contested — some linguists argue recursion is not the lone dividing line — but the structural observation stands: no animal signal embeds without limit.)

The second distinction is the one the basset hound illustrated. **Imperative** communication uses a signal to control another's behavior: *give me that, come here, go away*. **Declarative** communication uses a signal to align another's attention with your own: *look at that, isn't that something*. Apes do imperative robustly. The honest question is whether they do declarative reference at all — and the answer is more interesting than a flat no.

In the wild, great-ape declarative gestures are rare to absent. Decades of patient observational work — Michael Tomasello and his colleagues, Katie Slocombe and hers — find that wild apes gesture overwhelmingly to *control* what another ape does, not to share attention for its own sake. There are scattered exceptions worth naming honestly. Cat Hobaiter and colleagues documented a wild chimpanzee mother in Uganda's Budongo Forest scratching a leaf in front of her offspring in what looked like a referential signal directing attention to a body part — a single documented case, not a pattern. Captive apes complicate things further. David Leavens and William Hopkins showed, in a long program beginning in the 1990s, that captive chimpanzees raised among humans point at unreachable food at high rates — roughly half their largest sample produced clear pointing gestures, none of them trained. But Tomasello's reading is that even this spontaneous pointing is imperative: the ape points to *get* the food, treating the human as a tool for retrieval, not as a mind to inform. That reading fits the data; it is also harder to test cleanly than the raw pointing count suggests.

What the comparison establishes is not absence but rarity and contested motive. Great-ape declarative reference is rare in the wild, present at low rates in captivity, and disputed in its purpose when it appears. The human fourteen-month-old, by contrast, points to share attention dozens of times a day, with no payoff, no training, and in every culture ever studied. The difference is in frequency, spontaneity, and universality — not in absolute presence or absence.

The third distinction undoes a story that sounds intuitive but is wrong. We have not one vocal system but two, and they are anatomically separate. The first is ancient — a subcortical emotional-expression system, shared across mammals, that produces laughter, crying, screaming, the involuntary cry of pain, the grunt of effort. It is largely automatic and hard to suppress. The second is newer — a cortical language system, running on loops between cortex and the structures beneath it, that produces symbolic speech.

We know these are separate systems because of a clean clinical **double dissociation** — two patients, each with the opposite half broken. A patient with **Broca's aphasia**, from damage to the left inferior frontal gyrus, loses the ability to produce grammatical speech. He cannot assemble the sentence *the boy walked to school*. But he can still laugh at a joke, weep with grief, and — this detail is the one that lands — swear, fluently and reflexively, when he drops a hammer on his foot. The emotional vocal system is untouched. The mirror-image patient has **pseudobulbar affect**, from damage to the pathways carrying voluntary control down to the brainstem. He laughs and cries uncontrollably, at the wrong moments, regardless of how he feels — but his language is perfectly intact. One man keeps emotion and loses grammar; the other keeps grammar and loses control of emotion.

The implication runs directly against the comfortable story that human language evolved out of primate calls by slow semantic enrichment — that the alarm call gradually became the word. It did not. The call system and the language system are *parallel*, not serial. Looking at vervet alarm calls for the ancestors of human syntax is looking in the wrong organ. The roots of syntax are in the cortex, in the white-matter tracts that link the language regions, and in the social developmental program we turn to next — not in the ancient subcortical machinery that vervets and chimpanzees and humans all share.

---

Here is the mechanism most accounts of language leave out entirely. Children do not download language as a module. They run a developmental program — a staged sequence in which each step builds the scaffold for the next, and in which the child is an active builder, not a passive recipient.

At two to four months, before any word exists, there is the turn. Infant and caregiver fall into proto-conversations: the baby coos, the mother answers, the baby coos again, the mother answers again — a rhythmic, alternating exchange that carries no meaning at all. Eye contact gates it; gaze is how each knows whose turn it is. What the infant is learning, before learning any word, is the bare structure of symbolic exchange itself: that communication has a producer and a receiver, that roles alternate, that you watch the other's face to track the rhythm. Chimpanzee mothers do not do this with their infants. The comparative literature has found no systematic equivalent in any non-human primate.

At nine to twelve months the dyad becomes a triad. Now the child and the caregiver both attend to a third thing — an object, an event, a picture — while each monitors the other's attention to it. The child follows the caregiver's gaze; the child points and looks back to confirm the caregiver looked. This is **joint attention**, and it is the cognitive ground on which words are eventually laid. The order matters and is easy to get backwards. Joint attention comes *first*. Words are then dropped into a social frame that already exists. The child does not learn words and then begin to share attention. She shares attention, and then uses words to fill that shared frame with labels. In the same window she begins the declarative pointing the basset hound illustrated — the gesture aimed not at getting but at sharing.

By eighteen to twenty-four months the child does something with no parallel anywhere in the animal kingdom: she asks questions. Not learned request-forms, but genuine information-seeking — *what's that? why? where did it go?* The question is the active form of joint attention. It is not merely sharing the adult's attention; it is reaching into the adult's *mind* to fill a gap in the child's own. To ask "why," the child must grasp that the adult knows something she does not, that the knowledge can be transferred, and that transferring information is itself a legitimate purpose of communication — an end, not just a means.

The most-trained apes do not produce questions at any reliable rate. The single most-cited exception is Alex, the African grey parrot whom Irene Pepperberg worked with for thirty years, who once looked into a mirror and asked *what color?* — and was told, *grey*. One individual. One trainer. One documented instance across decades. That such a case exists at all is striking. That it remains essentially unique after half a century of comparative language research tells you how rare this particular operation is outside our species.

Underneath this social curriculum runs a statistical engine. Jenny Saffran, Richard Aslin, and Elissa Newport played eight-month-old infants two minutes of a continuous stream of nonsense syllables — no pauses, no stress, no intonation, just flat sound. The only cue to where one "word" ended and the next began was the transitional probability between adjacent syllables: some pairs occurred together far more often than others. After two minutes of merely listening, the infants could tell high-probability strings from low-probability ones. Eight-month-olds, doing distributional statistics in the background, automatically, before they can sit up unsupported.

But raw statistical capacity is not what the curriculum supplies — that is present from birth. What the curriculum supplies is the social scaffolding that orients the child toward linguistic input and motivates her to parse it. The statistical engine runs on the fuel the social program provides. Strip out the curriculum and the engine spins without traction.

---

The ape-language program is one of the most ethically demanding and empirically scrutinized enterprises in the history of comparative psychology, and what the data show, read honestly, is worth stating plainly.

Apes can master symbol-referent pairing for hundreds of items. The bonobo Kanzi, raised at the Language Research Center in Atlanta, learned to use roughly 350 lexigrams — abstract symbols on a keyboard — and came to understand a great deal of spoken English directed at him, following novel instructions and responding to sentences he had not heard before. He was not drilled on the lexigrams the way earlier apes had been; he picked them up by watching his mother's training sessions and using the keyboard on his own. The comprehension is real, and the methodology is among the most carefully examined in the field.

What the best-trained apes have *not* produced, across every subject and every study, is the cluster that defines human language: recursive syntax, spontaneous question-asking, reliable displacement beyond the here-and-now, and the frequent declarative reference a fourteen-month-old produces without anyone teaching her.

Herbert Terrace's Project Nim is the clearest evidence, and it is also a cautionary tale about method. Nim Chimpsky was raised in a signing environment for nearly four years and acquired around 125 signs, producing close to 20,000 multi-sign utterances. When Terrace and his colleagues analyzed the recordings frame by frame, the picture collapsed. Most of Nim's utterances were prompted by the trainer's immediately preceding sign. Longer strings did not carry more meaning; they just repeated high-frequency signs in shuffled order — *give orange me give eat orange me eat*. No reliable word-order regularity that could be called grammar ever emerged. And, crucially, the frame-by-frame analysis revealed how much the trainers had been unconsciously cueing the ape — the same Clever Hans problem that has haunted animal-cognition claims for a century. Part of why the field now reads Nim as it does is that the methodological scandal forced everyone to look harder at what the apes were actually generating versus what eager humans were reading into them.

The comparison to the human child is exact and a little brutal. The eighteen-month-old has a smaller vocabulary than Nim had and produces shorter utterances. But her utterances carry more information, they are not trainer-prompted at anything like Nim's rate, she asks questions, and she is on a trajectory that arrives, by three years, at fully recursive grammar. Nim's trajectory arrived at a plateau.

And the popular story about *why* — that apes lack the brain hardware — is wrong too. Broca's area exists in chimpanzees, bonobos, and gorillas. Claudio Cantalupo and William Hopkins showed in 2001 that the chimpanzee homolog of Brodmann's area 44 is left-hemisphere asymmetric, exactly as it is in humans. The machine part is there, and already lateralized. What differs in the human brain is not the presence of the region but its integration into a larger network — in particular the dramatic expansion of the arcuate fasciculus, the white-matter cable connecting the temporal and frontal language areas. Language is not a module sitting in a box. It is a property of a network, and the network is assembled by the developmental program. Remove the proto-conversations, the joint attention, the declarative pointing, the questions — and the network the program would have built does not assemble.

---

Which brings us to the cleanest natural experiment in the emergence of language ever observed.

Managua, Nicaragua, 1979. The country's first school for deaf children opens. The children who arrive share no common language. Each grew up in a hearing family, inventing an idiosyncratic system of home-signs that got them through the day but could not be understood across individuals. The teachers tried to teach oral Spanish through lipreading. As a method for teaching language to children who cannot hear, it failed almost completely.

What happened off the lesson plan is the thing.

The children pooled their home-signs in the schoolyard and on the buses. A shared system began to take shape — recognizable across individuals, still rough, still missing grammar. Then the second cohort arrived. The youngest of them — young enough that this emerging community of signers was their *primary* linguistic input rather than a second language layered onto an existing home-sign — did not merely learn what their older peers had assembled. They restructured it. They introduced spatial agreement, pointing to locations in signing space to mark which verb belonged to which argument. They built verb morphology that none of their teachers could produce. They built recursion. They built, in short, a language.

Ann Senghas and Marie Coppola documented the result across two decades of fieldwork. Nicaraguan Sign Language emerged from a community of children who had no model and every other cognitive capacity intact. By the second cohort the system had the full structural complexity of any natural human language — and it had gotten there in roughly twenty years, with no teacher and no external grammar to copy.

*Figure 12.2 — Structural complexity climbing across three cohorts of signers with no model to copy; the red second cohort is the generation that built the grammar — the notch where the ratchet caught.*

Notice what that rules out. It is not the case that children acquire language by passive exposure to a grammar that already exists in the adults around them — the first cohort had no such grammar to absorb. And it is not the case that grammaticizing a symbolic system requires slow cultural accumulation across many generations — the youngest children did the grammaticizing themselves, inside a single developmental window.

The reading I am left with: human children build language because they cannot *not* build it. The drive to organize symbolic communication into a recursive, generative system is, on the evidence of Nicaragua, as robust a biological imperative as walking. What those children needed was not a grammar. It was each other — a community of other children with shared intentions and joint attention. Provide the social materials and a single generation will assemble the structural machinery from scratch. Strip the materials away and the machinery does not assemble, no matter how much raw statistical capacity is sitting there waiting.

The chimpanzee can point at the banana, when pointing gets it the banana. It cannot, as far as anyone has been able to show, point at the moon simply because the moon is there and someone else ought to see it. That single difference — sharing attention with another mind for the sake of sharing — is the social substrate that everything else in human language is built on top of. Take it away and the most extensive training in the world produces Nim's plateau. Supply it, even with no language to copy, and you get a schoolyard inventing grammar.

---

There is a quieter extension of all this, and it sits on the desk most readers of this book are working at. For most of human history, language died with the breath that carried it — every utterance gone the moment it was spoken, the shared attention dissolving as soon as the two minds turned away. Writing changed the terms. A mark scratched into clay or pressed into a page is declarative pointing made permanent: *look at this* addressed to a reader who is not present, who may not be born yet, who shares no moment with the writer at all. The joint-attention frame, which in the toddler lasts only as long as the dog is in view, is frozen and made portable. Print drove the cost of copying that frozen frame toward zero, so that one mind's content could be aligned with millions of others across centuries. These are the same trick the fourteen-month-old performs in the park — making one mind's experience available to another as a shareable object — run through technologies that defeat the limits of breath and distance and time. The pointing finger, the frozen page, the printed edition: different scales of the one capacity. And the systems we now train on the entire frozen record inherit something extraordinary from it, and lack something too — but that is a thread for the chapter on collective intelligence, where the archive and the network take the story up.
