# Chapter 12 — Infections I: Skin, Eye, and Respiratory Tract

*From a single inhaled bacillus to six months of four-drug therapy — and why every step of that journey follows from the cell wall.*

---

In October 1918, a Boston resident going to work on a Monday had reasonable odds of dying by Friday.

The second wave of the influenza pandemic hit American cities that fall. A surgeon at Camp Devens, thirty miles from Boston, wrote to a colleague about soldiers brought in with what looked like ordinary flu, blue in the face within hours, dead the next morning. He had been a physician for years. He wrote: *"I have never seen anything to compare with it."*

Two months of an upper respiratory illness killed more people than four years of trench warfare. The agent: a virus 80 to 120 nanometers across, eight RNA segments, two surface proteins.

That gap — between the scale of the agent and the scale of its effect — is the question this chapter is organized around. *S. aureus* is a sphere a micron across. *M. tuberculosis* divides once a day, which is glacial by bacterial standards. Influenza is a bag of RNA with two spikes. None of these looks impressive. All of them, together, account for a sobering fraction of human mortality.

The way from "tiny thing" to "leading cause of death" is mechanism. Not the name of the pathogen — the thing it actually does to you, and why what it does follows from what it is.

---

## The skin: a negotiated truce

Your skin is already covered in bacteria. *Staphylococcus epidermidis*, *Cutibacterium acnes*, *Corynebacterium*, hundreds of thousands of organisms per square centimeter — doing no harm and arguably some good. Skin infection happens when a transient organism reaches deeper tissue through broken skin, or when a resident organism harmless on the surface gains access because the surface failed. The list of organisms equipped to exploit either route is short.

### *Staphylococcus aureus*

Gram-positive cocci in clusters. Coagulase-positive — the test that distinguishes it from harmless *S. epidermidis* in a clinical lab. Beta-hemolytic. Golden colonies. About 30% of healthy people carry it in their nostrils.

Three features explain why it causes so much disease when most staphylococci are harmless.

**Protein A** binds antibodies by their constant (Fc) region — the wrong end. An antibody coating a bacterium should present its variable end to the phagocyte for recognition. Protein A flips the antibody around so the recognizable end is buried against the bacterial surface. Opsonization fails before it starts.

**Toxins.** Exfoliative toxin cleaves desmoglein-1, the molecular fastener between adjacent upper-epidermal cells. Sheets of skin slide off — staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome. TSST-1 is a superantigen: it crosslinks the T-cell receptor to MHC class II molecules non-specifically, activating roughly 20% of T cells simultaneously instead of the one-in-a-million that a normal antigen would activate. The resulting cytokine storm is toxic shock syndrome. Panton-Valentine leukocidin kills neutrophils specifically; PVL-carrying strains cause more aggressive abscesses and a rare necrotizing pneumonia.

**The wall.** *S. aureus* has a thick peptidoglycan wall studded with teichoic acids — rigid, mechanically tough, and resistant to desiccation. It survives on doorknobs, stethoscopes, and gym towels for days. *Neisseria gonorrhoeae*, a Gram-negative with a thin wall and no outer protection, dies on a dry surface in minutes. The contrast is entirely explained by cell-wall architecture.

Walk up the depth of skin invasion and you get the clinical syndromes in order: folliculitis (one hair follicle infected), furuncle (deeper abscess from one follicle), carbuncle (connected furuncles), cellulitis (diffuse infection of dermis and subcutaneous tissue), abscess (walled-off pus). SSSS and TSS are toxin-mediated, not invasion-mediated — they can happen far from any local wound.

The MRSA problem follows from the cell-wall drugs. Beta-lactams work by binding penicillin-binding proteins and blocking peptidoglycan cross-linking. MRSA carries *mecA*, which encodes PBP2a — a modified penicillin-binding protein with an active site too narrow for most beta-lactams to enter. When the normal PBPs are blocked, PBP2a builds the wall instead. The drug never reaches a target it can disable. Community-acquired MRSA (CA-MRSA) emerged in the 1990s, often carries PVL, and now dominates skin MRSA in the United States. Oral TMP-SMX or doxycycline for outpatient disease; IV vancomycin or daptomycin for severe infection. Abscesses need drainage — antibiotics alone into an abscess fail because penetration is poor, bacterial density is enormous, and the environment is anaerobic.

### *Streptococcus pyogenes*

Gram-positive cocci in chains. Catalase-negative (five seconds to distinguish from staphylococci). Beta-hemolytic, Lancefield Group A.

Where *S. aureus* specializes in toxin and antibody-blockade, *S. pyogenes* specializes in *spreading*. Streptokinase dissolves fibrin clots. Hyaluronidase digests connective-tissue matrix. DNase liquefies pus. Streptolysins lyse red cells, neutrophils, platelets. Together these enzymes dissolve the tissue planes the bacterium needs to traverse. The clinical signature is rapid spread with a sharply advancing edge.

The most dangerous expression of this: **necrotizing fasciitis**, which spreads along the fascial plane between subcutaneous fat and muscle faster than the surface inflammation suggests. By the time the skin looks alarming, the deep destruction is already extensive. Pain out of proportion to visible findings is the warning sign. Mortality without immediate surgical debridement is high.

Two post-infectious consequences are the reason we treat strep throat with antibiotics at all. **Rheumatic fever** develops two to four weeks after pharyngitis when antibodies against streptococcal M protein cross-react with cardiac valve tissue — molecular mimicry causing valvular damage long after the bacterium is gone. **Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis** deposits immune complexes in glomerular capillaries. Neither complication is the bacterium still present; both are the immune response still running. The antibiotic eliminates the antigenic stimulus. The sore throat would get better on its own. The heart valve might not.

*S. pyogenes* has remained uniformly susceptible to penicillin for more than seventy years of routine use. The reason is not settled — more on this in *Still puzzling*. First-line treatment: oral penicillin V or amoxicillin.

### *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*

Gram-negative rod. Oxidase-positive. Blue-green pigment, grape-like odor, low-permeability outer membrane, constitutive efflux pumps, chromosomal beta-lactamase. *Pseudomonas* does not cause disease in healthy skin. It causes disease in damaged or compromised tissue — burn wounds, hot-tub folliculitis (under-chlorinated water, brief exposure, rapidly spreading pustules), contact-lens keratitis, cystic fibrosis lungs. The intrinsic resistance determines treatment: antipseudomonal beta-lactams (piperacillin-tazobactam, ceftazidime, meropenem), aminoglycosides, or ciprofloxacin, often in combination. Resistance develops during treatment.

## The eye: small list, large stakes

The cornea has no blood vessels. That is why it is transparent. It is also why the immune system has limited access, and why the few organisms that establish a corneal infection can do permanent damage before help arrives.

**Conjunctivitis** is usually viral (adenovirus — watery discharge, self-limiting) or bacterial (*S. aureus*, *S. pneumoniae*, *H. influenzae* — purulent discharge). Neonatal conjunctivitis in the first month of life carries different stakes: *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* acquired from the birth canal can perforate the cornea within days. Erythromycin ointment at birth is prophylaxis against this.

**Keratitis** — infection of the cornea itself — is vision-threatening. *Pseudomonas* dominates contact-lens-associated bacterial keratitis; overnight wear and tap water on the lens case are the risks. HSV-1 produces dendritic branching corneal ulcers on reactivation, visible under fluorescein stain, with progressive scarring through repeated episodes. *Acanthamoeba* keratitis — a free-living amoeba from tap water — is associated almost exclusively with poor contact-lens hygiene and is notoriously painful and difficult to treat.

The clinical lesson: a contact-lens wearer with eye pain needs ophthalmology evaluation, not watchful waiting. The cost-benefit calculation on the cornea is not the same as on the skin.

## Upper respiratory infections: the filter and its failures

You breathe roughly 12 times a minute, each breath carrying thousands of microbes. Most never reach the lower tract. The upper airway is a filter: turbulent airflow deposits large particles on mucous surfaces; ciliated epithelium beats them upward continuously; cough clears the rest. Upper respiratory disease happens when an organism colonizes and replicates in the upper-airway epithelium, or exploits the stripped-cilia aftermath of a preceding viral infection.

**Streptococcal pharyngitis.** Group A strep causes 5–15% of adult sore throats and perhaps 25–30% in school-age children. Sudden sore throat, fever, tonsillar exudate, swollen anterior cervical nodes — usually no cough, no rhinorrhea, which distinguishes it from viral pharyngitis. Rapid antigen test gives an answer in ten minutes: high specificity, about 85% sensitivity, so negative tests in high-suspicion patients get confirmed by culture.

We treat with penicillin not to shorten the sore throat — that resolves in a week regardless — but to prevent rheumatic fever and its cardiac sequelae. The absolute benefit is small in populations where rheumatic fever is now rare. In endemic settings, it is substantial. The antibiotic eliminates the antigen driving the cross-reactive immune response.

**Otitis media.** A viral upper respiratory infection swells the eustachian tube, fluid accumulates in the middle ear, bacteria ascend from the nasopharynx. The organisms: *S. pneumoniae*, nontypeable *H. influenzae*, *M. catarrhalis*. One of the most common reasons children receive antibiotics — and one of the areas where stewardship has most firmly shifted toward watchful waiting in mild cases, because many resolve without antibiotics.

**The common cold.** Rhinoviruses — over 100 serotypes, which is why immunity to "the cold" never fully develops. Seasonal coronaviruses, adenoviruses, parainfluenza, RSV. Self-limiting, resolves in one to two weeks. Antibiotics do nothing. They kill bacteria. The cold is viral. The reason antibiotics still get prescribed for viral URIs — patient expectation, the small chance of bacterial complication — does not change the underlying biology.

## Pneumonia: the lower airway overrun

**Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP)** — in a person not recently hospitalized — is dominated by *Streptococcus pneumoniae* (still the single most common bacterial cause), the atypicals (*Mycoplasma*, *Chlamydia pneumoniae*, *Legionella*), and respiratory viruses (influenza, RSV, SARS-CoV-2). Hospital-acquired pneumonia shifts toward Gram-negative rods (*Pseudomonas*, *Klebsiella*, *E. coli*, *Acinetobacter*) and MRSA — organisms that thrive in healthcare environments.

Within CAP, two clinical patterns:

**Typical pneumonia.** Sudden onset, high fever, productive rust-colored sputum, pleuritic chest pain, lobar consolidation on X-ray. The classical organism is *S. pneumoniae* — Gram-positive lancet-shaped diplococci with a thick polysaccharide capsule that inhibits phagocytosis and is simultaneously the basis for every pneumococcal vaccine. The capsule is the virulence factor and the vaccine target.

**Atypical pneumonia.** Gradual onset, low-grade fever, dry cough, headache and malaise, X-ray worse than the patient looks. The classical organism is *Mycoplasma pneumoniae* — a bacterium with no cell wall at all. Beta-lactams do nothing to it because there is no peptidoglycan to attack. Macrolide, doxycycline, or fluoroquinolone. *Chlamydia pneumoniae* fits the same picture. *Legionella* is the severe end — high fever, GI symptoms, neurologic signs, often in patients exposed to contaminated water systems.

## Influenza: the moving target

Influenza A is a negative-sense, segmented RNA virus. Two surface glycoproteins define each strain. **Hemagglutinin (HA)** binds sialic acid on respiratory epithelial cells and mediates viral entry — 18 subtypes. **Neuraminidase (NA)** cleaves sialic acid to release progeny virions from infected cells — 11 subtypes. The combination names the strain: H1N1, H3N2, H5N1.

Influenza changes in two ways that are mechanistically distinct and have completely different public-health consequences.

**Antigenic drift** is the accumulation of point mutations in HA and NA. Influenza's RNA polymerase has no proofreading — error rate roughly $10^{-4}$ per base per replication, which produces approximately one mutation per genome per replication cycle on a 14-kilobase genome. A handful of these mutations land in the epitopes that antibodies recognize. Over a season, drift produces enough antigenic change that prior-year antibodies fit this year's strain less well. The seasonal vaccine is reformulated annually based on WHO global surveillance, with variable success depending on prediction accuracy. This is a solvable, recurring problem.

**Antigenic shift** is not accumulation of mutations. It is reassortment. If two strains co-infect the same cell — an avian H5N1 and a human H3N2 meeting in a pig permissive for both — their eight RNA segments can redistribute. A hybrid virus emerges carrying a hemagglutinin subtype that circulating human antibodies have essentially never seen. 1918 (H1N1). 1957 (H2N2). 1968 (H3N2). 2009 (H1N1pdm09). All shift events. Annual reformulation of the seasonal vaccine cannot prepare for a shift event because the novel HA does not exist in circulation until the moment it emerges. A universal influenza vaccine targeting conserved internal proteins or the HA stalk is the goal; the candidates are progressing in trials. Diagnosis: rapid antigen tests (fast, modestly sensitive); PCR (sensitive and specific, increasingly point-of-care). Treatment: neuraminidase inhibitors (oseltamivir, zanamivir) reduce progeny release and modestly shorten illness if given within 48 hours. Baloxavir inhibits viral cap-snatching. Annual inactivated trivalent or quadrivalent vaccine.

## *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*: from one inhaled bacillus to six months of therapy

This is the deep-dive, because TB is the cleanest example in clinical microbiology of how cell-wall structure, growth rate, and intracellular survival all conspire to determine management. Every clinical decision in TB therapy is a response to a biological feature of the organism.

### The cell wall

*M. tuberculosis* is a rod with a cell envelope unlike anything else in clinical microbiology. Standard peptidoglycan, then an enormous covalent layer of **mycolic acids** — branched fatty acids 60 to 90 carbons long, linked via arabinogalactan to the peptidoglycan. The result is a waxy, hydrophobic, nearly impermeable barrier. The Gram stain fails because the crystal violet cannot penetrate it. We use the **acid-fast stain** (Ziehl-Neelsen): hot carbol fuchsin forced in by heat, then acid-alcohol decolorization that strips dye from normal bacteria but not from mycobacteria, which hold it through mycolic-acid binding.

The waxy wall is the central fact. Everything downstream follows from it.

### What happens after you inhale one bacillus

An infected person coughs. A droplet nucleus — a small dried droplet carrying a few bacilli — drifts. You inhale it. The bacillus reaches an alveolus and is engulfed by an alveolar macrophage.

Normally the phagosome would mature: fuse with lysosomes, drop its pH, expose the contents to digestive enzymes and reactive oxygen species. *M. tuberculosis* prevents this. It secretes proteins (ESAT-6, CFP-10) that block phagosome-lysosome fusion. The pH stays survivable. The bacillus replicates inside the macrophage that was supposed to destroy it.

The macrophage signals for help. Other macrophages, dendritic cells, and T cells arrive, surround the infected cells, and form a **granuloma** — a walled-off structure with infected macrophages in the center, some fused into giant multinucleated cells, lymphocytes around them, a fibrous capsule outside. The granuloma contains the infection. In about 90% of immunocompetent infected people, it stays contained — **latent TB**. No symptoms, not contagious, chest X-ray clear. PPD or IGRA detects the immune sensitization.

In 5–10% over a lifetime (much higher with HIV, or with TNF-alpha inhibitor therapy, which explains its package-insert warning), the granuloma fails. The center undergoes **caseous necrosis** — the dead tissue with the consistency of cheese that named the lesion — liquefies, ruptures into the airways, and spills bacteria. The patient now has active TB: chronic cough, fever, drenching night sweats, weight loss. The chronic cough disperses bacilli. The infection propagates.

### Why the therapy is six months

Standard treatment for drug-susceptible active TB: four drugs for two months (isoniazid + rifampin + pyrazinamide + ethambutol), then two drugs for four more months (isoniazid + rifampin). Six months. Each design feature is a response to a biology problem.

**Slow growth.** *M. tuberculosis* divides every 18–24 hours. The population inside a patient includes actively growing organisms, slowly growing organisms in granulomas, and nearly dormant persisters. Bactericidal antibiotics work best on actively dividing cells. The slow-growing and dormant populations die slowly. A short course leaves persisters to reactivate.

**Intracellular survival.** Most of the bacterial population is inside macrophages and granulomas. Pyrazinamide is active specifically in the acidic intracellular environment — it is essentially inactive outside cells. Rifampin and isoniazid penetrate broadly. The regimen is designed to hit bacteria in different physiological states and locations simultaneously.

**Resistance prevention.** Spontaneous mutation to isoniazid resistance occurs at roughly $10^{-7}$ per replication; to rifampin at roughly $10^{-8}$. A patient with active TB harbors $10^8$ to $10^{10}$ organisms. A pre-existing isoniazid-resistant mutant is essentially certain to be present; a rifampin-resistant mutant, very likely. But a mutant resistant to *both* arises at roughly $10^{-15}$ — which means zero in any one patient. Two effective drugs simultaneously make resistance evolution during treatment effectively impossible. Four drugs in the initial intensive phase is belt-and-suspenders.

The clinical implementation is **DOTS** — Directly Observed Treatment, Short-course — in which a health worker watches the patient swallow each dose. Unsupervised treatment fails at unacceptable rates, and failure produces the resistance categories: **MDR-TB** (resistant to at least isoniazid and rifampin), **XDR-TB** (MDR plus fluoroquinolones and an injectable second-line drug). The 2019 BPaL regimen (bedaquiline + pretomanid + linezolid) for highly resistant TB shortened treatment and improved cure rates — the largest TB therapeutics advance in decades.

### The limit

What TB teaches: slow growth + intracellular persistence + a nearly impermeable wall + resistance evolution under monotherapy = an organism that demands long, combination, directly observed therapy. Each feature of the treatment is a response to a feature of the organism. The six months is not arbitrary.

What we still cannot do: detect actively replicating but symptom-free disease in real time. Roughly a quarter of the world's population is estimated to carry latent TB. We can detect prior exposure. We cannot predict which latent infections will reactivate this year. Until we can, the latent pool will continue to feed new transmission.

## Three misconceptions worth naming

**"Antibiotics shorten a cold or the flu."** They do not. Antibiotics target bacterial machinery — cell-wall synthesis, the 70S ribosome, bacterial gyrase. Rhinoviruses, coronaviruses, and influenza have none of these. The cost of prescribing antibiotics for a viral URI is resistance selection and a few percent rate of antibiotic-associated diarrhea. The average benefit is zero.

**"TB always causes symptoms."** No. Latent TB — the dominant form — produces no symptoms, is not contagious, and leaves the chest X-ray clear. A PPD or IGRA detects immune sensitization. Roughly a quarter of the world's population is estimated to carry it. Screening of high-risk asymptomatic populations and treatment of latent infection is a public-health strategy specifically because active disease is the minority presentation.

**"Influenza drift and shift are the same kind of problem."** They are not. Drift is gradual accumulation of mutations in the same subtype, managed by annual vaccine reformulation based on surveillance. Shift is sudden emergence of a novel HA subtype through gene-segment reassortment between strains of different origins, producing a virus to which human populations have no prior immunity. Annual vaccination cannot prepare for shift. The two mechanisms require different public-health responses and are not more and less of the same phenomenon.
