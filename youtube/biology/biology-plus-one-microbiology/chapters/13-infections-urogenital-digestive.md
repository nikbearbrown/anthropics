# Chapter 13 — Infections II: Urogenital and Digestive Systems

*Two tracts that meet the outside world more often than the body would prefer. The infections that exploit that meeting are the most common bacterial illnesses on Earth.*

---

A 74-year-old woman is admitted with community-acquired pneumonia. A sensible antibiotic regimen is started. By day three her cough is better. By day five her bowels are not — ten or twelve stools a day, abdominal pain, a fever that had seemed resolved. The stool toxin test comes back positive for *Clostridioides difficile*. She is started on oral vancomycin — a second antibiotic, to treat a disease caused by the first. She improves. Three weeks after discharge she relapses. Then again. Then again. By the third recurrence her gastroenterologist offers fecal microbiota transplantation: donor stool, screened, suspended, delivered by colonoscopy into her colon. Within 48 hours the diarrhea stops. Six months later she remains well.

Sit with the shape of that story. An antibiotic that was probably the correct choice for her pneumonia produced a worse illness than the one it cured. The durable cure was not a drug at all — it was someone else's intestinal microbial community, replacing the one the antibiotics had destroyed.

This is the question every infection in this chapter is really asking: *what is the normal microbial state at this site, and what disturbed it?* The urinary tract is supposed to be sterile. The vagina is supposed to be lactobacillus-dominated. The gut is supposed to contain a hundred trillion bacteria in a working ecology that resists invaders. The infections that follow are what happens when one of those three statements stops being true — sometimes because a pathogen arrived from outside, sometimes because a treatment we prescribed opened the door.

---

## The urinary tract: a short defense with a short urethra

A healthy urinary tract is sterile from the bladder up. Urine is bactericidal in several ways — high urea, low pH, and the simple mechanics of flushing every few hours. Antimicrobial peptides line the urothelium. In women, the lactobacillus-dominated vaginal microbiota produces lactic acid that chemically suppresses nearby pathogens.

The defense has an anatomical weakness. The female urethra is three to four centimeters long. It opens close to the anal region. The bacteria most likely to arrive at the urethral opening are the bacteria that live in the gut — and of those, the one that arrives most often is the one best equipped for what comes next.

About 80% of community-acquired uncomplicated urinary tract infections are caused by **uropathogenic *Escherichia coli*** (UPEC). These are not random *E. coli*. They are strains carrying specific machinery for surviving in urine: most importantly, **type 1 pili** with a tip protein called FimH that binds mannose residues on the surface of bladder epithelium. Without that grip, the next urination flushes the bacterium away. With it, the bacterium stays long enough to multiply, invade epithelial cells, and form intracellular bacterial communities that act as a reservoir for relapse — which is why some women who clear one UTI have another a few weeks later from the same strain.

The remaining 20% of community UTIs come from a small cast: *Staphylococcus saprophyticus* (especially in sexually active young women), *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, *Proteus mirabilis* (whose urease splits urea into ammonia, raising pH and predisposing to struvite kidney stones), and enterococci. In hospital-acquired or complicated settings, the cast widens: *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, *Candida*, and resistant Gram-negatives.

The clinical spectrum runs from simple to severe. **Cystitis** — bladder infection — gives dysuria, frequency, urgency, sometimes suprapubic discomfort. Urinalysis shows pyuria and a positive nitrite. In a young woman with classic symptoms, empirical therapy without culture is standard: nitrofurantoin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole where resistance allows, or single-dose fosfomycin. **Pyelonephritis** — kidney infection — adds flank pain, fever, nausea. This requires longer therapy, broader antibiotics, and consideration of IV treatment. The reason the simple regimens for cystitis fail here: nitrofurantoin and fosfomycin reach concentrations in the bladder that are insufficient in kidney parenchyma. The organ matters.

**Catheter-associated UTI** deserves a separate sentence because it operates by different rules. A catheter is a plastic surface bathed in urine, and bacteria are very good at coating plastic with biofilm. The biofilm protects the organisms from antibiotics and provides continuous seeding of the urinary tract. The single most effective intervention is removing the catheter. Every day it stays in is another day the bacteria get a fresh start. This is not a clinical subtlety — it is the primary management principle, and it is often the one thing that is slowest to happen in real hospital care.

Why some women have three UTIs a year and others have none in their lifetimes with equivalent anatomy and exposures is not solved. Probable contributors include host-receptor genetics, vaginal microbiota composition, and the intracellular reservoir mentioned above. The honest answer is that we do not fully understand recurrence, and the chapter should say so rather than invent confidence.

## Sexually transmitted infections: the pattern across organisms

STIs are among the most common infections on Earth. The WHO estimates more than a million new sexually transmitted infections every day globally. I will run through the major bacterial and viral agents, but I want to focus on two pathogens that deserve mechanistic attention: *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* for its resistance trajectory, and *Chlamydia trachomatis* for its peculiar biology.

The pattern across organisms, before the details: *transmission route and asymptomatic carriage drive epidemiology*. Chlamydia spreads so successfully because most infected women have no symptoms. HSV spreads so successfully because asymptomatic shedding occurs between visible outbreaks. HPV is nearly universal among sexually active adults because it transmits through skin contact and most infections clear without symptoms. The pathogen that announces itself loudly gives the host a reason to seek care and break the chain. The pathogen that whispers passes unnoticed.

### *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* — a resistance trajectory in real time

*N. gonorrhoeae* is a Gram-negative diplococcus that infects mucosal surfaces. Men with urethral infection usually know it — dysuria and discharge. Women are asymptomatic about half the time, which is the problem: undiagnosed cervical infection ascends to cause pelvic inflammatory disease, with fallopian tube scarring, infertility, and ectopic pregnancy. Newborns of infected mothers can develop ophthalmia neonatorum. A small fraction develop disseminated infection — bacteremia with pustular skin lesions and septic arthritis.

What makes gonorrhea a priority in the antibiotic-stewardship conversation is not these clinical consequences alone — it is the resistance arc, which is worth knowing in chronological order because it reveals how fast a bacterium can shed effective therapies.

Sulfonamides: useful in the 1930s, resistance widespread by the 1940s. Penicillin: first-line for decades, then resistance via plasmid-borne β-lactamases through the 1970s and 1980s. Tetracyclines: gone by the 1980s. Fluoroquinolones: gone by the mid-2000s. Oral cephalosporins: gone by the 2010s. Injectable ceftriaxone — still first-line, but isolates with reduced susceptibility and outright resistance are now reported globally. The CDC currently recommends a single high-dose injection of ceftriaxone (500 mg). That is the last reliable single-drug option. New agents (gepotidacin, zoliflodacin) are in late development, but the pattern of the past 80 years is clear: resistance has followed every gonorrhea therapy eventually. `[verify: 2025 CDC gonorrhea treatment guidelines]`

### *Chlamydia trachomatis* — the obligate intracellular bacterium

*Chlamydia trachomatis* is the most common bacterial STI in the United States, and the most biologically strange. It is a bacterium — Gram-negative cell wall, ribosomes, its own DNA — but it cannot replicate outside a host cell. It is an obligate intracellular pathogen, like a virus in lifestyle if not in chemistry.

The life cycle has two forms with confusing historical names. The **elementary body (EB)** is the small, dense, metabolically inactive infectious particle — the travel form. It is durable enough to survive in the extracellular environment and equipped to bind host cell receptors. Think of it as the spore-equivalent: it gets in, but it does not replicate. Once inside a host cell, the EB converts to the **reticulate body (RB)** inside a host-derived vacuole called an inclusion. The RB is the replicating form. It divides by binary fission, fills the inclusion, then re-condenses back into EBs, the cell ruptures, and the new EBs spread to adjacent cells.

The clinical consequence of living inside host cells: antibody cannot reach it. The body's humoral immune response is poorly positioned to clear a pathogen that spends most of its life cycle inside a vacuole. This is part of why chlamydial infection persists and why the inflammation it eventually causes — when the immune response mounts a cell-mediated response — tends to produce scarring. The same cell-mediated response that finally clears the bacterium is responsible for much of the fallopian tube scarring that leads to infertility.

Clinically, urogenital chlamydia presents like a milder gonorrhea — cervicitis, urethritis — and roughly three-quarters of infected women have no symptoms at all. Diagnosis: NAAT on urine or swab. Treatment: doxycycline for seven days, or single-dose azithromycin for situations where adherence is uncertain.

### Syphilis, herpes, HIV, HPV — the essential pattern

*Treponema pallidum* (syphilis) moves through four stages: a painless ulcer at the inoculation site (primary), a disseminated rash including palms and soles (secondary — one of the most useful clinical clues in medicine), latent silence (serologically positive, clinically quiet), and years-to-decades later, cardiovascular and neurological destruction (tertiary). The diagnosis relies on paired serology: a non-treponemal test (RPR or VDRL) for sensitivity and disease-activity tracking, plus a treponemal test (FTA-ABS, TP-PA) for specificity. Treatment remains penicillin G — remarkably, after 80 years, *T. pallidum* has not developed penicillin resistance. The slow division rate and narrow penicillin-binding protein target probably contribute, though the full biology of this exception is not understood.

Herpes simplex (HSV-2, sometimes HSV-1) establishes lifelong latency in sensory ganglia. Reactivation produces recurrent lesions in roughly the same skin region. Asymptomatic viral shedding between visible outbreaks accounts for most transmission. No cure: the latent virus in the dorsal root ganglion is unreachable by antivirals. Management is suppression.

HIV is a retrovirus. Its genome is RNA, reverse-transcribed into DNA, integrated permanently. It infects CD4-bearing cells — the immune cells whose loss defines the clinical progression to AIDS. Antiretroviral therapy has transformed a fatal disease into a chronic manageable one. Patients on effective ART with undetectable viral loads live near-normal lifespans and do not sexually transmit the virus (U=U: Undetectable = Untransmittable). The latent provirus integrated into host genomes is not reached by any current drug — which is why cure remains elusive while suppression is achievable.

HPV: low-risk types (6, 11) cause genital warts. High-risk types (16, 18) produce E6 and E7 proteins that inactivate p53 and Rb, driving cervical and oropharyngeal cancer over years to decades. The HPV vaccine — a recombinant virus-like particle — is one of the clearest public health achievements of the past quarter century. Where adolescent vaccination uptake has been high, reductions in HPV infection, precancerous lesions, and cervical cancer rates are now measurable in national registry data.

## The gut: ecology more than infection

If the urinary tract is a sterile site that gets invaded, the gut is opposite: a hundred trillion bacteria living in working ecology that normally resists invaders. Digestive infections happen when that ecology fails — by overwhelming dose, by special tricks that defeat colonization resistance, or by antibiotic-induced collapse of the resident community.

### Timing as the first diagnostic tool

The most useful first question for an acute diarrheal illness is the interval from exposure to symptoms.

Symptoms in one to six hours mean the damage was done before you ate — the bacteria grew in the food, secreted toxin, and the toxin is what made you sick. The bacterium does not need to still be alive. Reheating does not help because the toxin is heat-stable. This is **intoxication**: *Staphylococcus aureus* enterotoxin, *Bacillus cereus* emetic form.

Symptoms in 12 to 72 hours mean the organism itself is the problem — it got in, multiplied, and either invaded tissue or produced toxin in situ. This is **infection**: *Salmonella*, *Campylobacter*, *Shigella*. Fever usually comes with it, because live bacteria in or near the mucosa trigger an inflammatory response.

Symptoms after 3–8 days, with bloody diarrhea and low or absent fever, in a child or older adult, should immediately raise Shiga toxin–producing *E. coli* (STEC). The timing is longer because STEC has to colonize, produce toxin, and for the systemic consequences to develop.

Symptoms two to six weeks after a course of antibiotics: *Clostridioides difficile*.

### The *E. coli* that are not commensal

Most *E. coli* are harmless. A handful of pathotypes are not, and they cause distinct syndromes worth keeping separate.

ETEC (enterotoxigenic *E. coli*) is traveler's diarrhea: watery, non-bloody, usually self-limiting. Its heat-labile toxin is structurally similar to cholera toxin — same mechanism, same result, smaller scale.

STEC (Shiga toxin–producing *E. coli*) is a different matter. The toxin gene arrived by horizontal gene transfer from a phage — as we traced in Chapter 10, the O157:H7 story is the lysogenic conversion story. The Shiga toxin enters the bloodstream, finds its receptor Gb3 on renal and cerebral endothelium, kills endothelial cells, and triggers the thrombotic microangiopathy of hemolytic uremic syndrome: hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, acute kidney injury. Treatment is supportive. Antibiotics are generally avoided because lysis of the bacteria releases more toxin. The 1993 Jack in the Box outbreak — more than 700 cases, four pediatric deaths, all traced to undercooked hamburgers — turned this pathotype into a food-safety landmark. `[verify: Jack-in-the-Box 1993 case-count and outcome data]`

### Cholera toxin and Shiga toxin: why the same word produces different diseases

Both are AB toxins. Both produce diarrheal disease. The clinical pictures could not be more different, and the difference is entirely in the target.

Cholera toxin's B subunit binds GM1 ganglioside on small intestinal enterocytes. The A subunit ADP-ribosylates Gαs, locking it in its active state. Adenylate cyclase runs continuously. cAMP rises. CFTR chloride channels open and stay open. Chloride, sodium, and water flood into the gut lumen — liters per day, non-stop. The enterocyte is alive. There is no inflammation. No fever. No blood in the stool. The stool is rice-water: clear, isotonic fluid. The patient dies of dehydration if untreated.

The enterocyte being alive is what makes oral rehydration therapy work. Cholera toxin attacks cAMP-driven chloride secretion through CFTR. It does not touch SGLT1, the sodium-glucose cotransporter on the same cell. Give glucose and sodium by mouth, and SGLT1 pulls sodium across the brush border — water follows — at the same rate that CFTR is losing it from the other channel. Intake matches output. That is the entire principle. A packet of salt and sugar dissolved in clean water turns a disease with 50% mortality into one with roughly 1% mortality. The therapy follows directly from knowing which transporter the toxin did not break.[^4]

Shiga toxin's B subunit binds Gb3 — a different receptor, found not just on intestinal epithelium but on endothelial cells throughout the vasculature, in high density in the kidney glomerular capillaries and the brain. The A subunit is an N-glycosidase: it cleaves a single adenine from the 28S ribosomal RNA. The ribosome stops. The cell dies. Colonic epithelial cells die, producing bloody, inflammatory diarrhea. Shiga toxin that translocates to the bloodstream reaches renal endothelium, kills it, exposes basement membrane, triggers platelet aggregation and red cell destruction. That is HUS.

So: same modular architecture, different receptor, different molecular target, different cellular consequence (signaling perturbation without death versus protein synthesis arrest and death), different tissue distribution of the receptor, and therefore completely different disease. The mechanism explains the clinical picture.

### *Clostridioides difficile*: the disease antibiotics build

*C. difficile* is the leading cause of healthcare-associated diarrhea in the United States, and its biology is striking because it is fundamentally an ecological disease, not a classical infection.

*C. difficile* is an obligate anaerobe that forms hardy spores surviving on hospital surfaces. Alcohol-based hand sanitizer does not reliably kill them — soap and water is required. Many people are colonized asymptomatically. The spores germinate in the gut but cannot proliferate because the normal microbiota is occupying all the metabolic niches. This is colonization resistance.

The trigger is a broad-spectrum antibiotic — clindamycin, fluoroquinolones, and cephalosporins are particular culprits. The antibiotic disrupts the normal colonic community. The niches open. *C. difficile*, resistant to most antibiotics and already present as spores, proliferates and produces its two toxins, TcdA and TcdB. Both toxins disable Rho-family GTPases inside colonic epithelial cells, breaking the cytoskeleton, killing the cells, and producing the characteristic pseudomembranous colitis — yellowish plaques of dead epithelium visible on colonoscopy.

Symptoms: watery diarrhea with characteristic odor, abdominal pain, leukocytosis, fever. Severe disease progresses to toxic megacolon and perforation.

The apparent paradox of treatment: the first-line drugs are oral vancomycin and fidaxomicin — antibiotics, for a disease caused by antibiotics. The paradox dissolves when you understand the pharmacology. Oral vancomycin is essentially unabsorbed from the gut. It acts locally at high concentration in the colon, killing *C. difficile* but exerting much less impact on recovering microbiota elsewhere. Intravenous vancomycin — same molecule, different route — does not work for *C. difficile* because it never reaches the colon at therapeutic concentration. Fidaxomicin has narrower activity than vancomycin, spares more of the residual microbiota, and produces lower recurrence rates. The drug is not going into the bloodstream; it is going into the ecosystem.

Recurrence rates tell the story of the underlying problem. About 20% of patients relapse after a first episode, 40% after a second, 60% after a third. Antibiotics treat the active *C. difficile* population. They do not restore the colonization resistance that was destroyed. The underlying dysbiosis persists. When the antibiotic pressure is removed, *C. difficile* spores germinate again from the gut or from environmental re-exposure.

Fecal microbiota transplantation resolves this. Donor stool, screened and delivered colonoscopically (or by capsule in newer protocols), carries a complete functioning microbial community that re-establishes colonization resistance. The cure rate for recurrent CDI is above 90%. This is the patient from the opening of the chapter. The logic is ecological: you are not killing a pathogen; you are restoring the niche that excludes it.

The deeper lesson: every unnecessary antibiotic course is a chance to disrupt a patient's microbiota and open that niche. Hospital infection-control programs that reduce inappropriate antibiotic use have also reduced *C. difficile* rates. The disease is, in a precise sense, iatrogenic — produced by treatment. Calling it a stewardship illness is not rhetorical. It is mechanistically accurate.

## Tracing an outbreak: the population-level version of the same logic

An individual case is a disease. A cluster of cases at the same time and place is an outbreak. The discipline that investigates it uses the same mechanistic reasoning applied at population scale — and it is worth walking through once, because the logic is both beautiful and directly useful.

The starting move is a **case definition**: a set of criteria — symptoms, timing, geography — that determines what counts as a case. Too narrow, and you miss real cases. Too broad, and you include unrelated illnesses and lose statistical signal. The definition is not the final truth; it is the working filter.

The **epidemic curve** — onset time plotted against case count — tells you the mode of spread before you know the pathogen. A sharp single peak suggests a point-source exposure: everyone ate the same contaminated food at the same event. A long curve with secondary peaks suggests person-to-person transmission. The distance between the peak and the presumed exposure estimates the incubation period, which narrows the pathogen list immediately. A 12–72 hour peak at a wedding with fever and diarrhea points to *Salmonella*. A 1–6 hour peak after catered food with vomiting points to a preformed toxin.

Once you have a plausible vehicle, you calculate **attack rates**: the fraction of people who ate the implicated food who became ill, versus the fraction who did not eat it but became ill anyway. In a textbook outbreak: 80% attack rate among chicken eaters, 15% attack rate among non-eaters. That pattern — high in eaters, low in non-eaters — is the signature of a foodborne pathogen in that vehicle.

**Trace-back** follows the implicated food upstream: caterer → producer → farm → flock. State agriculture officials culture the environment. The isolate from the farm matches the isolate from the patients by serotype and antibiotic susceptibility. The flock is depopulated.

The limit is honest: many outbreaks are never traced. By the time cases are recognized, the food may be gone. The wedding reception is an easier problem than a multi-state cluster of sporadic cases linked to contaminated leafy greens shipped to a dozen distribution centers. The logic is the same. The evidence available to apply it differs.

## Still puzzling

Why FMT cures recurrent *C. difficile* in over 90% of patients but has yet to produce durable benefit in inflammatory bowel disease — same intervention, same general principle, vastly different clinical result — is an open question. Whether *N. gonorrhoeae* will be the first historically-treatable bacterial pathogen to become routinely untreatable in clinical practice, or whether new antibiotics and a vaccine will arrive in time, is the public health question whose answer the next decade will write. Why some women have recurrent UTIs with anatomy and exposures identical to women who never do remains unsolved.

**What would change my mind:** If a defined microbial consortium proved to prevent *C. difficile* in hospitalized patients on antibiotics as reliably as FMT cures recurrent CDI, the framing here — antibiotic-stewardship-plus-rescue — would shift toward ecological-restoration-first. The case for ecology is already strong for *C. difficile*. It is not yet strong enough across the rest of the chapter to demand a rewrite.

**Still puzzling:** Why FMT cures recurrent *C. difficile* at over 90% and has yet to produce durable benefit in inflammatory bowel disease. Whether *N. gonorrhoeae* will become routinely untreatable before new antibiotics arrive. Why some women have recurrent UTIs while others with identical anatomy and exposures do not.
