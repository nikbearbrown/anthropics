# Chapter 1 — An Invisible World

*The history of microbiology is the history of being surprised by what is in a drop of water.*

---

Here is something I want you to sit with before we do anything else.

For most of recorded history — thousands of years — people were being killed by things they could not see. They were also eating food made by those same invisible things: bread, wine, beer, cheese, yogurt, kimchi, kefir. The crops they grew were partly made possible by invisible things fixing nitrogen in the soil. The bodies that did all this living and dying were *full* of invisible things, most of them helpful.

None of these people knew any of this. Not a single person on Earth, for most of human history, had ever seen a bacterium. The creatures were just there, doing their work, invisible.

Then, in 1675, in a textile shop in Delft, the Netherlands, a man who spent his days inspecting fabric — counting threads, judging weave quality, the ordinary work of a cloth merchant — ground a glass bead into a lens, held a drop of rainwater in front of it, and saw things *moving*.

That is the moment I want to start with. Not because it's a dramatic opening — though it is — but because it poses the question this entire chapter is really about: **how do you build a science around things you cannot see?**

It turns out there are four moves. Each one was forced by the limits of the previous one. And together, they gave us what counts as evidence in all of biology today.

## Before the lens: a long time of not knowing

Let's be careful about what "not knowing" means here, because it's not the same as "not thinking about it."

The Romans had Varro. Marcus Terentius Varro, writing in 36 BC, put this in an agricultural manual — advice about where to build a farmhouse:

> *"Precautions must also be taken in the neighborhood of swamps… because certain minute creatures grow there which cannot be seen by the eye, which float in the air and enter the body through the mouth and nose and there cause serious diseases."*[^3]

That is a correct hypothesis. Invisible creatures. Airborne. Entering through respiratory routes. Causing disease. Written two thousand years before the germ theory of disease was experimentally confirmed.

Varro could not prove it. He had noticed that swampy areas correlated with illness — which is a real correlation, because the danger in a swamp is mosquitoes carrying *Plasmodium*, not bad air — and he reasoned backward to a cause he could not observe. The shape of the reasoning was right. The evidence for it was essentially zero.

This is the first lesson: having the right idea and being able to *demonstrate* the right idea are completely different things. Varro sat with a correct hypothesis for two thousand years before anyone could show it was true.

The Greeks had their own framework. They called it *miasma* — bad air, the exhalations of rotting matter and stagnant water. The word *malaria* still carries this theory inside it: *mal'aria*, bad air. Wrong about the mechanism, right about the correlation. If you followed miasma theory and avoided swamps, you were less likely to get malaria — not because the air was bad, but because you were avoiding the mosquitoes. A wrong theory that produces correct predictions is still useful. It's just not science yet.

And then there's Thucydides, writing about the plague that devastated Athens between 430 and 410 BC. A third of the city died. Thucydides survived it and recorded, among other things, a single observation that matters enormously:

*Those who had recovered from the disease did not catch it again, even when nursing the actively sick.*[^2]

That sentence is the first written record of what we now call acquired immunity. He had no idea what was happening inside those bodies. He did not know about antibodies, or T cells, or immunological memory. He had a pattern, observed carefully, written down honestly. The mechanism took another 2,400 years to work out. The observation came first.

This is worth dwelling on: some of the most important ideas in microbiology arrived not from microscopes or cultures or postulates, but from careful attention to what was happening in front of people's eyes. The mechanistic explanation came later. The pattern was always there, waiting to be noticed.

## Move 1: the lens (demonstration replaces argument)

Antonie van Leeuwenhoek was not a scientist by training. He was a linen merchant in Delft who inspected fabric for the city and, as a hobby, ground lenses. He was secretive about his technique — nobody else in his era could replicate what he did, and he kept it that way deliberately. His microscopes were not the compound instruments that natural philosophers of the era used. They were simpler: a single glass bead, ground to a very small sphere, mounted in a brass plate. A small sphere of glass is more curved than a large one, and more curvature means more magnification. His best lenses got to around 270×. The compound microscopes of his contemporaries topped out around 30×.[^4]

He looked at everything. Plaque scraped from his own teeth. Rainwater that had been sitting in a covered tub on his roof. Pond water. Pepper infusions. Blood.

In the rainwater, he saw things swimming. He estimated more than a million in a single drop. He called them *animalcules* — little animals.

The Royal Society in London was skeptical. They sent a delegation to verify. The delegation confirmed it. In 1680, Leeuwenhoek was elected a Fellow without ever traveling to London.

Now here is the thing I want you to understand about what this changed. It did not change the *existence* of microbes. They had always been there. What it changed was the *kind of claim* you could make about them.

Before Leeuwenhoek: "there exist tiny creatures in water that cause disease" was an *argument*. You could agree or disagree with it. You could raise objections. You could refuse to be convinced.

After Leeuwenhoek: it was something you could *show someone*. You could put them in front of a brass plate with a glass bead and a drop of water, and they would see the creatures moving. The argument became a demonstration.

That is a qualitative shift in the nature of the evidence. And it immediately generated a new question — because as soon as you can see microbes growing in a broth, you want to know where they came from.

## Move 2: the flask (ruling out alternatives)

If you boil a broth — killing everything in it — and leave it sitting out, microbes appear. Where do they come from?

Two hypotheses competed, and they competed seriously, for about two hundred years.

The first was *spontaneous generation*: living things arise from non-living matter under the right conditions. This was not a fringe position. Aristotle held a version of it. It had serious defenders through the 18th century. The intuition behind it was not stupid: leave meat out and maggots appear. Leave broth out and microbes appear. The most natural explanation is that life is somehow latent in matter, waiting for the right conditions.

The second was *biogenesis*: living things come only from other living things. Microbes in broth came from microbes already in the air.

The problem was that experiments kept failing to settle it, because each side could explain the other's results. In 1668, Francesco Redi put meat in three conditions — open, sealed, covered with gauze — and showed that maggots appeared only on the open meat, not in the sealed or gauze-covered jars. That killed spontaneous generation for animals you could see. Proponents retreated: maybe large animals didn't arise spontaneously, but microbes did.

In 1745, John Needham boiled mutton broth and sealed the flasks. Microbial growth appeared anyway. He said: spontaneous generation. In 1768, Lazzaro Spallanzani boiled broth longer and sealed more tightly. No growth. Needham said: you boiled away the "vital force." The exchange went nowhere, because each side had an explanation for the other's results that preserved their own position.

This is what an unresolved scientific controversy looks like. Two hypotheses. Both consistent with all available evidence. No decisive experiment. The argument could go on forever.

Louis Pasteur ended it, and the way he ended it is the most important methodological lesson in this chapter.

Pasteur built a flask whose neck was drawn out into a long, sinuous S-curve. He boiled broth inside. The neck stayed open — air moved freely in and out. But any particles suspended in air, including microbes, would settle in the lowest dip of the curve and never reach the broth.

The broth stayed clear. For months.

Then Pasteur tilted the flask so the broth touched the accumulated particles in the curve. Within days, the broth clouded with microbial growth.

The decisive feature: the flask design made the two hypotheses *predict different outcomes*. Spontaneous generation predicted: broth open to air will eventually cloud, regardless of what the air contains. Biogenesis predicted: broth exposed to air but not to airborne particles will stay clear.

The flask enforced that distinction. Air in; particles out. The result was unambiguous.

<!-- → [IMAGE: Diagram of Pasteur's swan-neck flask showing the S-curve, particle deposition in the bend, and both the "clear broth" and "tilted flask with growth" states — this is the experiment in one image] -->

I want to be precise about what this experiment proved and what it didn't. It proved that microbes in broth come from microbes in the air. It proved biogenesis over spontaneous generation. It said nothing about whether those microbes caused specific diseases. That was a completely separate question, and it took a different kind of experiment to answer it.

## Move 3: the clinicians (evidence ahead of mechanism)

The germ theory of disease — the idea that specific microbes cause specific illnesses — was not invented in a laboratory. The experimental proof came from laboratories. The clinical evidence came first, from physicians who were watching patients and noticing patterns, without any ability to isolate or identify the organisms responsible.

Ignaz Semmelweis was an obstetrician at Vienna General Hospital in the 1840s. The hospital had two maternity wards: one staffed by physicians and medical students, one staffed by midwives. The physicians' ward had a maternal mortality rate around 16% from puerperal fever. The midwives' ward had a rate around 2%.[^6]

Semmelweis noticed that the physicians came directly from performing autopsies on women who had died of the same fever. He instituted handwashing in chlorinated lime. The mortality on the physicians' ward dropped to roughly match the midwives' ward.

He had no germ theory. He talked about "cadaverous particles." His colleagues rejected him — partly because the implication was that physicians were killing their own patients, which was not a comfortable conclusion. He died in an asylum in 1865, possibly from sepsis. The mechanism he was preventing — streptococcal infection carried from cadaver tissue to wound — was not identified until after his death.

In 1854, John Snow traced a cholera outbreak in London's Soho neighborhood to a single water pump on Broad Street. He did this by mapping the deaths — each death plotted to its address — and looking at where they clustered. He persuaded the local authority to remove the pump handle. The outbreak ended.[^7]

Snow had no organism. He could not see *Vibrio cholerae* in the water. He had a map, a hypothesis about water-borne transmission, and an intervention whose results confirmed the hypothesis. He was right thirty years before Koch isolated the bacterium.

The lesson is that the evidence structure mattered more than the mechanism. Snow's map was causal evidence — an intervention that changed the outcome — even though he couldn't name the cause. This turns out to be a recurring theme in biology: you can know that something works before you know why it works.

## Move 4: Koch's postulates (rules for inferring cause)

Robert Koch started doing microbiology in a home laboratory in Wollstein, in what is now Poland. He was a country doctor. His laboratory was paid for by his wife. He had a microscope, a supply of cattle blood from dying animals, and, over time, a set of techniques that he invented from scratch: pure culture on solid medium, staining with dyes, photography through the microscope.

In 1876, anthrax was killing cattle across rural Germany. Farmers knew the disease moved through herds. Microscopists had observed rod-shaped bacteria in the blood of dying animals. What nobody had established was whether those rods *caused* the disease, were a *consequence* of the disease, or were simply along for the ride.

Koch proved the rods caused it. He did it in four steps that he later generalized into a framework.

**Step one:** He examined blood from many sick cattle and found the rods consistently. He examined blood from healthy cattle and found none. The bacteria and the disease traveled together.

**Step two:** He grew the bacteria in pure culture — isolated, separated from all other organisms — in serial transfers through the aqueous humour of ox eyes. By the end, no original animal tissue could have been carried through. He had the bacterium alone.

**Step three:** He took material from that pure culture and inoculated healthy mice. The mice died of anthrax, with the same clinical signs as the cattle.

**Step four:** He examined the dead mice, found the same bacteria, and re-cultured them — closing the circle.

<!-- → [INFOGRAPHIC: Koch's four postulates as a cycle diagram — organism present in disease → isolated in pure culture → causes disease in new host → re-isolated from new host — with anthrax as the worked example and one "failure mode" annotation per step (e.g., "HIV fails Postulate 3" for the inoculation step)] -->

In 1884 he wrote these steps down as explicit postulates. They are a causal inference framework: association (Postulate 1), isolation (Postulate 2), intervention (Postulate 3), replication (Postulate 4). Each postulate closes an alternative explanation. After all four are satisfied, the inference — *this organism causes this disease* — is not guesswork. It's the only explanation consistent with the evidence.

Koch's postulates were also, almost immediately, a framework that kept breaking. And the breaking is as instructive as the framework itself.

Viruses could not be grown on agar. The second postulate, as written, had to be amended. *Mycobacterium leprae*, the cause of leprosy, has never been cultured on artificial medium — not to this day. HIV cannot be ethically introduced into healthy humans; there is no animal model that produces the same disease. Many pathogens — *Salmonella* Typhi, *Helicobacter pylori*, SARS-CoV-2 — colonize people who never get sick, failing the "absent from healthy hosts" clause.

In 1988, Stanley Falkow reformulated the postulates at the molecular level: a gene is implicated in causing disease if it appears in pathogenic strains and not in harmless ones, if disrupting it reduces virulence, and if restoring it restores virulence.[^10] That moves the unit of analysis from organism to molecule — which lets the framework handle things that resist culture, asymptomatic carriage, and multi-species infections. It also introduces its own failure modes, which we'll return to much later.

What I want you to hold onto is the shape of this progression. Each new class of pathogen broke the existing framework in a specific, diagnosable way. The response wasn't to abandon the framework — it was to extend it in the direction the break pointed. That is how science actually moves.

## The tree of life

Once you can see thousands of different organisms, you have a taxonomy problem: what are these things, and how do they relate to each other?

Carl Linnaeus solved the naming problem in 1735 with a convention so elegant it has survived unchanged: every species gets a two-part Latin name. Genus, capitalized. Species, lowercase. Both italicized. *Homo sapiens*. *Felis catus*. *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*. The abbreviation *E. coli* compresses *Escherichia coli* — genus initial, full species name. Every biology paper in every language follows this convention. That's the mark of a design that got something exactly right.

The deeper question — how are organisms related to each other, and how far back does the family tree go — waited for Carl Woese.

In the late 1960s, Woese had a problem. Taxonomy based on visible features works fine for animals. But two bacteria can both look like rods and be completely unrelated. What feature do you compare?

His answer: stop looking at the outside. Look at a molecule that every cell on Earth must have, that is so essential it changes very slowly over evolutionary time. The gene encoding the small subunit of ribosomal RNA — 16S rRNA in bacteria and archaea, 18S in eukaryotes — is that molecule. Ribosomes are the machines that make proteins. Every cell needs them. If two organisms share more of the 16S sequence, they share more recent common ancestry. The sequence is a clock that has been running for billions of years.

In 1977, Woese and George Fox published their results.[^11] A group of microorganisms — methanogens, halophiles, thermophiles, organisms living in hot springs and salt flats and anaerobic sediments — had a 16S sequence so different from other bacteria that they could not belong to the same branch. They were a completely separate **domain** of life. Woese called them Archaea.

Most microbiologists hated this. They had been calling certain cells "bacteria" for a century. Woese spent years being dismissed by the mainstream of his own field.

By the 1990s, the three-domain tree was consensus. All life on Earth falls into three deep groups: Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya. And Archaea, biochemically, look more like Eukarya than like Bacteria — which means the split between Archaea and Eukarya is *more recent* than the split that produced Bacteria. The tree is not symmetric. The earliest branch point separated Bacteria from everything else.

Everything alive — every cell you have ever seen, eaten, or been — is in one of those three domains. All of them descended from a single ancestor that lived perhaps 3.5 to 4 billion years ago. We call it LUCA: the Last Universal Common Ancestor.

Notice what is not on the tree: viruses. They are not cells. They have no ribosomes. There is no 16S rRNA to sequence. Viruses are not on the tree of life because they are not on the tree of life — they are a different kind of biological entity entirely, and fitting them into the same framework requires thinking about what "life" even means. We will return to this.

## What to do with the misconceptions

Let me be direct about two things this chapter should permanently dislodge.

The first: the idea that microbes are things to be killed. This is understandable — the history we've just traced focuses on pathogens because pathogens are what got people's attention. But of the bacterial species we've characterized, fewer than 100 are routine human pathogens. Your gut carries somewhere around 10^13 to 10^14 bacterial cells — roughly as many as your own cells — and the overwhelming majority are beneficial or commensal. They produce vitamin K. They ferment dietary fiber into compounds your colon uses for fuel. They train your immune system. They crowd out pathogenic species by occupying the niche. The Human Microbiome Project, running from 2007 to 2016, catalogued thousands of species living on and in healthy people, and the catalog is still expanding.[^12] Germ-free mice — raised in completely sterile conditions with no microbial exposure — have stunted immune systems, altered gut architecture, and metabolic abnormalities that persist through their lives. You need the microbes.

The second: the "Pasteur invented germ theory alone" story. Pasteur did the experimental work that proved microbes caused fermentation, spoilage, and specific diseases. He did not invent the idea. Varro had it in 36 BC. Fracastoro proposed *seminaria contagionis* — seeds of contagion — in 1546. Semmelweis acted on it in 1846 without naming it. Snow demonstrated it epidemiologically in 1854 without isolating an organism. Lister applied it to surgery in 1865. Koch made the inference rigorous in 1876. Germ theory is a collective achievement, assembled from experimentalists, clinicians, and epidemiologists over centuries. The single-discoverer story is useful shorthand and misleading history.

## Still puzzling

I do not fully understand why no archaeon has ever been confirmed as a human pathogen. Archaea live in the gut, they share the same body sites as bacterial commensals, they have the genetic flexibility to acquire virulence factors, and yet — nothing. The explanations on offer (immune sensor mismatch, slow growth, niche incompatibility) all feel partial. This seems like something the field has not yet looked at carefully enough, rather than a settled negative result.

I'm also unsettled by the microbiome's challenge to "one organism, one disease." Many syndromes — inflammatory bowel disease, periodontal disease, bacterial vaginosis — involve community-level shifts in microbial composition that don't map onto a single causal agent. Koch's framework, even in Falkow's molecular reformulation, is built for single agents. What causal language even means at the community scale is an open question, and one the field is still working out.

### Application

4. **Koch's postulates on a real pathogen.** Apply the four postulates to *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* — the case Koch himself worked out. Then apply them to *Mycobacterium leprae*, the cause of leprosy. Identify which postulate fails for *M. leprae*, explain why it fails, and describe what modern microbiology uses in place of the postulate that cannot be satisfied.

5. **Semmelweis and the evidence structure.** Semmelweis had no germ theory, no identified organism, and no mechanism. Describe what kind of evidence he *did* have, and explain why that evidence was — or wasn't — sufficient to justify changing clinical practice. Snow faced an analogous situation in 1854. Compare the two cases: what did each have, what did each lack, and what did each do with the evidence they had?

6. **The wrong theory that worked.** The chapter argues that miasma theory was wrong about mechanism but right about correlation. Give a modern example of a public health intervention that works for reasons its original proponents did not fully understand. What does this suggest about the relationship between explanation and evidence in medicine?

### Synthesis

7. **The spontaneous generation debate as a case study in unresolved science.** The debate ran for roughly two hundred years and was conducted mostly with experiments that didn't settle it. Trace the debate from Redi (1668) through Needham (1745) and Spallanzani (1768) to Pasteur (1859/1864). For each experiment, identify: what the experimenter claimed, what escape hatch the other side used, and why Pasteur's design closed all the escape hatches at once. What general principle about experimental design does the sequence demonstrate?

8. **Koch's postulates as a breaking framework.** The chapter says the postulates kept breaking almost immediately after they were written. List four pathogens that violate one or more postulates, name which postulate each violates, and explain why the violation is a genuine failure of the framework rather than a fixable procedural problem. Then evaluate: does the pattern of failures suggest the framework was wrong, or that it was right for its intended domain and wrong to apply beyond it?

9. **Woese's methodological move.** Explain why 16S rRNA sequencing solved a problem that morphology-based taxonomy could not. What property of the molecule makes it useful as a phylogenetic clock? What did the results reveal about the relationship between Archaea and Eukarya that would have been invisible to any morphology-based method?

### Challenge

10. **The microbiome challenge to Koch.** The chapter ends by noting that many syndromes — IBD, periodontal disease, bacterial vaginosis — involve community-level shifts that don't map onto a single causal agent. Koch's postulates, even in Falkow's molecular reformulation, are built for single agents acting alone. Design a framework — as a set of postulates or criteria — that could establish causation for a community-level microbial shift. Your framework should handle: (a) no single organism that is always present in disease and always absent in health, (b) the community itself as the unit of analysis, and (c) the practical impossibility of introducing an entire defined community into a healthy host. State where your framework is strong and where it would itself break.

11. **Thucydides' observation, 2,400 years later.** The chapter notes that Thucydides recorded the first evidence of acquired immunity in 430 BC — without any concept of what immunity was. The mechanism took 2,400 years to work out. Identify two or three observations in current biology or medicine where the pattern is clear and robust but the mechanism is not yet understood. For each: what is the observation, how strong is the evidence for it, and what would a mechanistic explanation need to account for? What does the Thucydides case suggest about how much weight to give a well-established pattern in the absence of mechanism?
