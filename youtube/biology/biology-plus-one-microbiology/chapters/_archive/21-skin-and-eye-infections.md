# Chapter 21 — Skin and Eye Infections

*The barrier is the organ. Compromise the barrier, and the resident microbes become the disease.*

## The question before the answer

Your skin, by surface area, is the largest organ in your body. By weight, the largest. By microbial density at the human-environment boundary, also the largest. A square centimeter of your skin contains hundreds of thousands of bacteria. Most of them — *Staphylococcus epidermidis*, *Cutibacterium acnes*, *Corynebacterium* species, various *Malassezia* yeasts — are present continuously, in stable communities, doing no harm.

Then you get a cut. Or a burn. Or a surgical incision. Or a diabetic ulcer. Or your immune system gets compromised. Now the same bacteria that have been living on you for decades have access to the deeper tissue they couldn't reach before. The skin infection that follows is, in many cases, an infection by *your own bacteria*.

This chapter is about what happens when the skin and eye barriers fail. The pathogens are sometimes outside-in (*Staphylococcus aureus* from someone else's skin; *Acanthamoeba* from contaminated contact lens solution) and sometimes inside-out (your own *Cutibacterium* in acne; your own *Streptococcus pyogenes* in necrotizing fasciitis). Identifying the pathogen and treating the infection often comes down to knowing the local microbiota and what conditions tip a commensal into a pathogen.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

1. Describe the major layers of the skin and the microbiomes of distinct body sites.
2. Identify the most common bacterial pathogens of skin and explain their characteristic infections.
3. Identify the major viral skin and eye infections by clinical presentation.
4. Distinguish superficial from subcutaneous fungal infections (cutaneous mycoses).
5. Identify the most common parasitic skin/eye infections and where they are encountered.

Prerequisites: Chapters 1–20.

## Skin anatomy, briefly

The skin has three layers:

**Epidermis**: outermost, mostly stratified squamous epithelium with keratinized dead cells at the surface. About 0.05–1.5 mm thick depending on body site. Continuously shed and replaced from the basal layer below.

**Dermis**: connective tissue with blood vessels, nerves, sweat glands, sebaceous glands, hair follicles. Much thicker than epidermis.

**Hypodermis (subcutaneous tissue)**: fat, connective tissue, large blood vessels and lymphatics. Connects skin to underlying structures.

Each layer is a different microbial environment. The epidermis surface is dry, slightly acidic, exposed to air. Hair follicles and glands are deeper, oilier, more humid. Each environment supports different microbial communities, and pathogens that succeed in one layer may not succeed in another.

## Normal skin microbiota by site

The hands have a transient population that changes with each touch. The forehead and scalp have oilier environments dominated by *Cutibacterium acnes*. The armpit is humid, dominated by various *Corynebacterium* and *Staphylococcus*. The feet are warm and moist, particularly between the toes, supporting *Staphylococcus*, *Corynebacterium*, and *Malassezia*. The back, conversely, is relatively dry with low diversity.

The eye surface is bathed in tears (with lysozyme, IgA, lactoferrin) and has a much sparser microbiota than skin — *Staphylococcus epidermidis*, occasional *Corynebacterium*, low-abundance *Cutibacterium acnes*.

The body's microbial communities have been characterized in detail by the Human Microbiome Project and related efforts. The picture is that each body site has a stable community shaped by local environment, and that disruption of the community (by antibiotics, by hygiene practices, by disease) has consequences for both colonization resistance and chronic conditions like acne and atopic dermatitis.

## Bacterial skin infections

### Staphylococcal infections

*Staphylococcus aureus* is carried in the nose by about 30% of people without symptoms. From there it can be transferred to skin and cause:

- **Folliculitis**: infection of hair follicles. Small red papules with central pus.
- **Furuncles (boils)**: deeper folliculitis, with abscess formation.
- **Carbuncles**: clusters of furuncles connected under the skin.
- **Impetigo**: highly contagious superficial infection, often in children. Honey-colored crusts on a red base, classically around the mouth and nose.
- **Scalded skin syndrome**: caused by exfoliative toxin; the toxin cleaves desmoglein in the upper epidermis, causing widespread skin peeling. Usually in young children.
- **Toxic shock syndrome (TSS)**: caused by TSST-1 superantigen. Fever, hypotension, diffuse erythroderma, multi-organ involvement. The 1980s tampon-associated outbreaks were menstrual TSS; non-menstrual TSS occurs from surgical wounds and other staphylococcal infections.

**MRSA** (methicillin-resistant *S. aureus*) is a major clinical concern. Hospital-acquired MRSA (HA-MRSA) has been around for decades; community-acquired MRSA (CA-MRSA) emerged in the 1990s and now causes most MRSA skin infections in the US. CA-MRSA strains often carry Panton-Valentine leukocidin (PVL), a toxin that kills white blood cells and is associated with severe abscesses and necrotizing pneumonia.

Treatment of *S. aureus* skin infections depends on severity. Simple impetigo: topical mupirocin or oral cephalexin. Abscesses require drainage; antibiotics alone are insufficient.

### Streptococcal infections

*Streptococcus pyogenes* (Group A streptococcus, GAS) causes:

- **Impetigo**: with or without staphylococci; "non-bullous" impetigo is often streptococcal.
- **Cellulitis**: bacterial infection of the deeper skin layers. Spreading redness, warmth, swelling. Sometimes streptococcal, sometimes staphylococcal, often clinically indistinguishable.
- **Erysipelas**: a more superficial cellulitis with a sharply demarcated raised red border. Classic on the face or legs.
- **Necrotizing fasciitis**: deep, rapidly progressing infection of the fascia between subcutaneous tissue and muscle. The bacteria spread along the fascia faster than the surface inflammation suggests. Mortality high without immediate surgical debridement. Often called "flesh-eating bacteria."
- **Scarlet fever**: a strep throat (Chapter 22) accompanied by erythrogenic toxin producing a sandpapery rash, strawberry tongue, and circumoral pallor.
- **Streptococcal toxic shock syndrome**: rare but extremely severe; mortality 30–50%.

### Acne

*Cutibacterium acnes* (previously *Propionibacterium acnes*) is a normal commensal of hair follicles. In adolescence, sebum production increases under hormonal influence, follicles become plugged, and *C. acnes* proliferates in the anaerobic environment. The combination of comedone formation, *C. acnes* proliferation, and inflammation produces acne.

Treatment is more dermatology than infectious disease: topical retinoids to reduce comedone formation, benzoyl peroxide as a non-antibiotic antimicrobial, topical and oral antibiotics for moderate cases, isotretinoin (Accutane) for severe cases.

### Other bacterial skin infections

- **Anthrax**: *Bacillus anthracis* cutaneous infection produces a painless ulcer with a black eschar (necrotic center). Bioterrorism concern; rare in clinical practice in most countries.
- **Pseudomonas folliculitis ("hot tub folliculitis")**: itchy red papules after exposure to inadequately chlorinated hot tubs or pools. Usually self-limiting.
- **Erysipeloid**: from *Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae*, often acquired from fish or meat handling.
- **Cat-scratch disease**: *Bartonella henselae* from cat scratches; usually self-limiting lymphadenopathy.
- **Lyme disease (Chapter 25)**: tick-borne *Borrelia burgdorferi* produces the characteristic erythema migrans rash at the bite site in 70–80% of cases.

## Eye infections

### Conjunctivitis ("pink eye")

Inflammation of the conjunctiva, the membrane covering the eye surface and inner eyelid. Causes:

- **Bacterial**: *Staphylococcus*, *Streptococcus*, *Haemophilus*, *Moraxella*. Purulent discharge, eyelids stuck together on waking. Treat with topical antibiotics; most cases self-resolve.
- **Viral**: adenovirus is the most common cause. Watery discharge, often bilateral. Highly contagious. Resolves without treatment in 1–2 weeks.
- **Allergic**: itching, watery discharge. Treated with antihistamines.
- **Neonatal conjunctivitis (ophthalmia neonatorum)**: occurs in the first month of life. Causes include *Chlamydia trachomatis* (most common), *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* (most dangerous — can cause corneal perforation in days), and *Staphylococcus*. Both *Chlamydia* and *N. gonorrhoeae* are sexually transmitted infections acquired during birth. Prophylactic ocular antibiotic at birth (erythromycin ointment or silver nitrate) prevents gonococcal ophthalmia.

### Trachoma

*Chlamydia trachomatis* serotypes A through C cause chronic conjunctivitis in regions with poor hygiene. Repeated infection over years leads to scarring of the cornea and inward turning of the eyelids; eyelashes scrape the cornea; chronic damage causes blindness. Trachoma is the leading cause of preventable blindness worldwide, with about 200 million people at risk. The WHO SAFE strategy (Surgery for advanced disease, Antibiotics for active disease, Facial cleanliness, Environmental improvement) has dramatically reduced trachoma in many countries.

### Keratitis

Corneal infection. More dangerous than conjunctivitis because the cornea can scar, impairing vision.

- **Bacterial keratitis**: *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, *Staphylococcus*, *Streptococcus*. Often associated with contact lens use, particularly extended-wear lenses or poor hygiene.
- **Viral keratitis**: herpes simplex (HSV-1) causes dendritic keratitis — branching corneal ulcers visible under fluorescein staining. Recurrent HSV keratitis can cause progressive corneal scarring.
- **Fungal keratitis**: *Fusarium*, *Aspergillus*, *Candida*. Often after agricultural or vegetative injury. Increased incidence with use of contaminated contact lens solutions in early 2000s outbreaks.
- **Acanthamoeba keratitis**: from *Acanthamoeba castellanii* or related amoebae. Associated with contact lens wear, particularly use of tap water or homemade saline. Painful, difficult to treat, can lead to blindness.

## Viral skin infections

### Warts

**Human papillomavirus (HPV)**: a family of DNA viruses with over 200 types. Different HPV types cause different conditions:

- **Common warts (verruca vulgaris)**: HPV types 1, 2, 4, 7. Skin papules with rough surface, usually on hands.
- **Plantar warts**: HPV 1, 4. On the soles of feet, often painful.
- **Flat warts**: HPV 3, 10. Smooth, slightly raised.
- **Genital warts (condyloma acuminatum)**: HPV 6, 11. Soft, cauliflower-like growths.
- **Cervical and other anogenital cancers**: HPV 16, 18, 31, 33, others. Will be covered in Chapter 23.

Treatment of warts: cryotherapy, topical salicylic acid, surgical removal, immunomodulators (imiquimod). Many warts resolve spontaneously over months to years.

### Herpes simplex virus

HSV-1 and HSV-2 are DNA viruses that cause primary infection followed by latent persistence in sensory ganglia, with periodic reactivation producing recurrent lesions.

**HSV-1**: classically causes oral herpes (cold sores, fever blisters) on the lips and around the mouth. Acquired in childhood through close contact. Reactivates with fever, stress, sun exposure, menstruation.

**HSV-2**: classically causes genital herpes. Acquired through sexual contact. Reactivates similarly.

The clinical distinction between HSV-1 and HSV-2 is becoming less sharp; both can cause oral or genital lesions, depending on transmission route. HSV-1 is now responsible for a significant fraction of genital herpes in the US, particularly in younger people.

**Herpes whitlow**: HSV infection of fingers, classically in healthcare workers exposed to oral lesions. Now less common with use of gloves.

**Herpes gladiatorum**: HSV-1 transmitted between wrestlers through skin contact.

**Neonatal herpes**: HSV transmitted from mother to newborn during birth. Can cause skin, eye, and mouth infection, or disseminated disease, or CNS infection. Mortality high without immediate antiviral therapy. Mothers with active genital HSV at delivery are typically delivered by cesarean section to reduce transmission.

Treatment: acyclovir, valacyclovir, famciclovir. These nucleoside analogs are selectively activated by viral thymidine kinase and inhibit viral DNA polymerase. They suppress active disease and reduce shedding but do not cure latent infection.

### Varicella-zoster virus (VZV)

A herpesvirus. Primary infection causes **chickenpox (varicella)** — fever and a widespread itchy vesicular rash, with lesions in various stages of development simultaneously. Highly contagious by respiratory droplets. Self-limiting in healthy children; more severe in adults and immunocompromised.

After primary infection, VZV remains latent in sensory ganglia. Reactivation produces **shingles (herpes zoster)** — a painful vesicular rash distributed in a dermatome (the area of skin innervated by a single spinal nerve). Shingles can affect any dermatome but is particularly painful and visible on the face (especially the trigeminal distribution, which can affect the eye) and trunk.

The varicella vaccine (live attenuated, since 1995 in US) has dramatically reduced chickenpox in children. The shingles vaccine (Shingrix, recombinant subunit, since 2017) is recommended for adults over 50 and is highly effective at preventing shingles and post-herpetic neuralgia.

### Other viral exanthems

- **Measles (rubeola)**: paramyxovirus. Fever, cough, conjunctivitis, Koplik spots (white spots inside the mouth), then a confluent maculopapular rash spreading from face down. Highly contagious. Resurgent in recent years due to declining vaccination.
- **Rubella (German measles)**: togavirus. Mild rash and lymphadenopathy. The major concern is congenital rubella syndrome — infection in early pregnancy causes severe birth defects.
- **Roseola**: HHV-6 (a herpesvirus). High fever in infants, followed by a rash as the fever resolves.
- **Fifth disease (erythema infectiosum)**: parvovirus B19. "Slapped cheek" rash in children.
- **Hand, foot, and mouth disease**: coxsackievirus and other enteroviruses. Vesicles on the hands, feet, and mouth in young children.
- **Smallpox (variola)**: eradicated in 1980. Last natural case 1977. Stored in two laboratories under WHO oversight. Major bioterrorism concern.

## Fungal skin infections

### Tineas (dermatophyte infections)

**Dermatophytes** are fungi that digest keratin and infect skin, hair, and nails. The three main genera are *Trichophyton*, *Microsporum*, and *Epidermophyton*. The clinical syndromes are named by site:

- **Tinea capitis**: scalp. Patchy hair loss, scaling. Common in children.
- **Tinea corporis**: body. Annular (ring-shaped) red, scaling rash with central clearing. "Ringworm" — no actual worm involved.
- **Tinea cruris**: groin. "Jock itch."
- **Tinea pedis**: feet. "Athlete's foot." Particularly between toes.
- **Tinea unguium (onychomycosis)**: nails. Yellow, thickened, distorted nails.
- **Tinea versicolor (pityriasis versicolor)**: not a true dermatophyte but caused by *Malassezia* yeasts. Hypo- or hyperpigmented patches on trunk.

Treatment: topical antifungals (terbinafine, clotrimazole, miconazole) for limited disease; oral terbinafine or itraconazole for extensive or nail disease.

### Subcutaneous and deeper fungal infections

Less common, often after traumatic implantation of fungal material.

- **Sporotrichosis ("rose gardener's disease")**: *Sporothrix schenckii*. Subcutaneous nodules along lymphatic vessels.
- **Chromoblastomycosis**: dark-pigmented fungi causing chronic subcutaneous lesions.
- **Mycetoma (Madura foot)**: chronic deep infection of subcutaneous tissue, fascia, and bone. Severe.

### Candidiasis

*Candida albicans* and related species. Cutaneous candidiasis presents as moist red patches with satellite lesions, classically in skin folds (diaper rash, intertrigo). More serious infections (oral thrush, vaginal candidiasis, esophagitis, candidemia) are covered elsewhere.

## Parasitic skin and eye infections

### Acanthamoeba

*Acanthamoeba* species are free-living amoebae found in soil, fresh water, and tap water. They cause Acanthamoeba keratitis (above) and, rarely, granulomatous amoebic encephalitis. The eye infection is particularly associated with contact lens wear; using tap water to rinse lenses or contact lens cases is a major risk factor.

### Loa loa (eye worm)

A filarial nematode endemic to West and Central Africa. The adult worm migrates through subcutaneous tissue and can cross the eye, where it is visible to the patient and an observer. The infection produces transient swelling (Calabar swellings) and chronic immune effects. Transmitted by deerflies.

### Onchocerciasis (river blindness)

*Onchocerca volvulus*, a filarial nematode. Adult worms live in subcutaneous nodules. Female worms produce microfilariae that migrate through skin and eyes, causing chronic inflammation and, over years, blindness. Endemic in sub-Saharan Africa, with smaller foci in Latin America and Yemen. Transmitted by blackflies that breed along rivers. Mass distribution of ivermectin has dramatically reduced onchocerciasis in many endemic regions.

### Scabies and lice

Not microbial infections in the strict sense, but mite and louse infestations are typically managed by infectious-disease principles.

**Scabies**: *Sarcoptes scabiei*, a mite that burrows in the epidermis. Intense itching, particularly at night, with characteristic linear burrows visible in finger webs and other thin-skinned sites. Highly contagious. Treated with topical permethrin or oral ivermectin.

**Head lice (pediculosis capitis)**: *Pediculus humanus capitis*. Common in school children. Itching of scalp; nits (eggs) attached to hair shafts. Treated with topical permethrin or other pediculicides.

## What the chapter is really about

The skin and eyes are the body's outward-facing surfaces. They have continuous, intimate contact with the microbial world. The defenses — physical barrier, chemical antimicrobials, normal microbiota, immune surveillance — are usually sufficient. When they fail, the infections that follow are often by organisms already present, exploiting a temporary or chronic compromise.

For clinical practice, the differential diagnosis of a skin lesion is broad — bacterial, viral, fungal, parasitic, inflammatory, neoplastic. The patient's history (exposures, travel, immunosuppression, age, trauma), the lesion's appearance, and the time course narrow the possibilities. Empirical treatment is often appropriate, but a culture, biopsy, or scraping should be considered when the diagnosis is uncertain or the response to treatment is poor.

The eye infections are a smaller list but with disproportionately serious consequences — vision-threatening complications develop rapidly with corneal infections. The contact lens user with eye pain needs prompt evaluation, not reassurance. The newborn with conjunctivitis needs immediate antibiotic eye drops and a workup for sexually transmitted pathogens. The patient with shingles affecting the eye needs ophthalmology consultation. The cost-benefit of "watchful waiting" tilts heavily toward early intervention for ocular infection.

## Still puzzling

I do not understand why the eye, despite being constantly bathed in tears and rich in antimicrobial peptides, remains so vulnerable to a small set of specific infections (HSV, Pseudomonas, Acanthamoeba). The cornea in particular is a strange tissue immunologically — it lacks blood vessels and has limited immune access. The trade-off may be that transparency requires the absence of cellular infiltration; if you let immune cells into the cornea routinely, you would lose vision. But the consequence is that the corneal infections that do happen can be devastating. The compromise seems unforgiving; I would like to understand the evolutionary logic better.

## What would change my mind

The microbiome literature is generating new picture of skin infection — that conditions like atopic dermatitis and acne are not classical infections at all but rather dysbioses (imbalances in microbial communities). If treatments aimed at restoring healthy microbial balance (live bacterial therapies, prebiotics, narrow-spectrum antimicrobials sparing commensals) replace the broad antibiotic approach, the framework in this chapter will need substantial revision. `[verify: 2025 clinical status of microbiome-targeted dermatology]`
