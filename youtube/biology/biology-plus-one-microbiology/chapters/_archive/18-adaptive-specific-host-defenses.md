# Chapter 18 — Adaptive (Specific) Host Defenses

*Your immune system contains receptors for pathogens that haven't existed yet.*

## The question before the answer

Your body, at any given moment, contains approximately a hundred billion lymphocytes. The remarkable thing about this population is the variety. Each lymphocyte expresses a unique receptor — a particular antibody or T cell receptor — that recognizes one specific molecular pattern. The total number of distinct receptors in your body is estimated to be between 10¹¹ and 10¹⁸, depending on how you count.

That number is larger than the number of pathogens you will ever encounter, larger than the number of pathogens that exist on Earth, larger than the number of pathogens that have ever existed and ever will exist. Your immune system has receptors for shapes that have never appeared in evolutionary history and never will.

How? Not by inheriting them — your genome doesn't have enough DNA to encode that many receptors. The receptors are generated by a process of genetic recombination that happens during the development of each lymphocyte. The cell randomly combines fragments of receptor genes, producing a new sequence. The vast majority of those sequences are useless; a small fraction happen to recognize something that matters. The fraction that recognizes pathogens you actually encounter gets selected and expanded; the rest never get used.

The adaptive immune system is the closest thing the body has to a programmable defense. It learns by encountering pathogens, retains memory of those encounters, and responds more quickly and effectively the next time. The point of this chapter is to understand how that system works — how it generates diversity, how it picks the right receptors, how it remembers, and how vaccines exploit the memory to provide protection without disease.

## Specificity, memory, tolerance

Adaptive immunity has three hallmarks that distinguish it from innate immunity.

**Specificity**: each adaptive immune cell has a receptor for one particular antigen. The immune response to one pathogen does not transfer to an unrelated pathogen. Antibodies against measles do not protect against mumps.

**Memory**: after a first encounter with a pathogen, the immune system retains memory cells. A second encounter triggers a faster, larger response.

**Tolerance**: the immune system does not, in healthy people, attack its own tissues. Self-reactive lymphocytes are eliminated or controlled during development. Failure of tolerance produces autoimmune disease (Chapter 19).

These properties together produce the characteristic features of adaptive immunity: slow first response (days), fast subsequent response (hours), durable protection against pathogens previously encountered, and the basis for vaccination.

## Antigens

An **antigen** is any molecule that can be recognized by an antibody or a T cell receptor. The molecular feature that is actually recognized — the binding site — is called an **epitope**. Each antigen can have many epitopes; each epitope can be bound by many different antibodies.

An **immunogen** is an antigen that actually provokes an immune response. The distinction matters because some antigens cannot trigger immunity on their own. A **hapten** is a small molecule that is antigenic (can bind antibodies once they exist) but not immunogenic (cannot trigger an immune response by itself, because it's too small to be presented by MHC). Haptens become immunogenic when conjugated to a larger carrier protein.

Most antigens are proteins or large polysaccharides. The immune system distinguishes self from non-self largely by recognizing peptides bound to MHC molecules — see below.

## The two arms of adaptive immunity

Adaptive immunity is divided into two arms based on which cells do the work.

**Humoral immunity** is mediated by **B cells** and the antibodies they produce. Antibodies circulate in blood and lymph. They are most effective against extracellular pathogens — bacteria in body fluids, toxins, viruses outside cells.

**Cellular immunity** is mediated by **T cells**. T cells directly kill infected cells or coordinate the broader response. They are most effective against intracellular pathogens — viruses inside cells, intracellular bacteria like *Mycobacterium*, parasites that have invaded cells.

The two arms cooperate. Cellular immunity is necessary for many humoral responses (helper T cells activate B cells). Humoral immunity supports cellular responses (antibodies opsonize pathogens for cytotoxic destruction).

## Antibody structure

An antibody (immunoglobulin) is a Y-shaped protein with two binding sites that recognize specific antigen epitopes. The structure has four polypeptide chains: two identical **heavy chains** and two identical **light chains**, joined by disulfide bonds.

Each chain has a **variable region** at one end and a **constant region** at the other. The variable regions of one heavy and one light chain together form the antigen-binding site. The constant regions determine the antibody's class and effector functions.

Five classes (isotypes) of antibody in humans, distinguished by their heavy chains:

**IgG** is the most abundant antibody in serum (about 75% of total immunoglobulin). Four subclasses (IgG1–IgG4). It opsonizes pathogens for phagocytosis, activates complement, and crosses the placenta — which is how newborns acquire passive immunity from their mothers. IgG dominates the secondary immune response and is the antibody class measured in most diagnostic serologic tests.

**IgM** is the first antibody class produced in a primary immune response. It is a pentamer (five Y-shaped units joined together), giving it 10 antigen-binding sites. It is highly effective at activating complement. IgM concentration peaks at about a week after exposure and then declines as IgG production ramps up.

**IgA** is the major antibody in secretions — saliva, tears, breast milk, mucosal surfaces. It is a dimer in secretions, a monomer in serum. It is the major protector of mucous membranes against pathogens. Some pathogens (*Neisseria*, *Haemophilus influenzae*, *Streptococcus pneumoniae*) have evolved IgA proteases to cleave it.

**IgE** is present in serum at very low levels. It binds to mast cells and basophils via a special receptor. When IgE on the cell surface encounters its specific antigen, the cell releases histamine and other mediators. IgE mediates allergic responses (hay fever, asthma, anaphylaxis) and helminth defense. Elevated IgE is a marker of allergic disease or parasitic infection.

**IgD** is the rarest antibody class. It appears on the surface of mature B cells before antigen exposure. Its function is incompletely understood but appears to involve B cell activation.

## How antibodies do their work

Antibodies do not kill pathogens directly. They work by binding pathogens and recruiting other components of the immune system or directly blocking pathogen function.

**Neutralization**: an antibody binding to a virus's surface protein can prevent the virus from attaching to host cells. An antibody binding to a bacterial toxin can prevent the toxin from binding its target. Neutralizing antibodies are the basis of immunity to many viruses and to bacterial toxoid vaccines.

**Opsonization**: an antibody coating a pathogen marks it for phagocytosis. Phagocytes have Fc receptors that bind the constant region of antibody, allowing them to engulf antibody-coated targets more efficiently than naked ones.

**Complement activation**: antibodies (especially IgM and some IgG subclasses) activate the classical pathway of complement, leading to direct killing of Gram-negative bacteria and recruitment of phagocytes.

**Antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC)**: an antibody bound to an infected cell can be recognized by NK cells, which kill the target.

**Agglutination**: antibodies (especially IgM, with 10 binding sites) can cross-link multiple pathogens into clumps that are easier to clear.

## MHC and antigen presentation

T cell receptors do not recognize antigens directly. They recognize peptide fragments of antigens bound to **major histocompatibility complex (MHC)** molecules on cell surfaces.

The MHC system is a clever solution to a hard problem: how can T cells survey what's inside cells without entering them? The answer: every cell continuously samples its own proteins, chops them into peptides, and displays the peptides on its surface bound to MHC. T cells circulate and check the surface displays. If the displayed peptide is from a foreign protein (a viral protein in an infected cell, a bacterial protein in an infected macrophage), the T cell recognizes the foreignness and acts.

Two classes of MHC molecules:

**MHC class I** is on almost all nucleated cells. It presents peptides from proteins synthesized inside the cell — endogenous antigens. A viral protein being made in an infected cell will be chopped, loaded onto MHC I, and displayed. Cytotoxic T cells (CD8+) recognize peptides on MHC I.

**MHC class II** is on professional antigen-presenting cells — dendritic cells, macrophages, B cells. It presents peptides from proteins the cell has engulfed from outside — exogenous antigens. Helper T cells (CD4+) recognize peptides on MHC II.

The cells that present MHC I display "what I'm making." The cells that present MHC II display "what I've eaten." This division allows the immune system to detect intracellular pathogens (MHC I) separately from extracellular ones (MHC II).

MHC molecules are highly polymorphic — each person has different MHC variants. The differences mean that different people present different peptides effectively. This is part of why some people respond strongly to a particular pathogen and others don't. It is also why organ transplantation is hard — the recipient's T cells see the donor's MHC molecules as foreign and attack them. **HLA matching** (human leukocyte antigen, the human MHC) is the process of finding donor-recipient pairs that are closely matched at MHC, reducing rejection.

## T cell development and selection

T cells originate from progenitors in the bone marrow and migrate to the **thymus** (the "T" in T cell) to mature. The thymus is a remarkable organ: it grows during childhood, peaks at puberty, and atrophies in adulthood. Most adult T cells were produced before adolescence; the supply of new T cells declines with age.

In the thymus, developing T cells rearrange their T cell receptor (TCR) genes through a process similar to antibody recombination, generating diverse receptors. They then go through two rounds of selection:

**Positive selection**: T cells whose receptors can bind self-MHC molecules with some affinity survive. T cells whose receptors cannot bind any self-MHC are useless (they would never recognize anything) and die.

**Negative selection**: T cells whose receptors bind self-peptides on self-MHC with high affinity are eliminated. These would attack self tissues if allowed to mature. Self-tolerance is built in here.

The selection is brutal — about 95% of developing T cells die in the thymus, leaving only those that can recognize foreign peptides on self-MHC with the right affinity.

The genes for the **T cell receptor** are organized in segments (V, D, J for the beta chain; V, J for the alpha chain). During development, each cell randomly chooses one V, one D, one J segment for the beta chain and one V and one J segment for the alpha chain, combining them with some imprecision at the junctions. The result is enormous diversity — estimated 10¹⁵ possible receptors from a relatively small set of gene segments. This is the same combinatorial logic used by B cells for antibody diversity.

## T cell subsets

After leaving the thymus, T cells circulate and differentiate based on antigen exposure into several functional subsets.

**Helper T cells (Th, CD4+)** recognize peptides on MHC II. Their main function is to coordinate other immune cells — activating B cells, activating cytotoxic T cells, activating macrophages, releasing cytokines that direct the response. Subtypes include Th1 (cellular immunity, intracellular pathogens), Th2 (humoral immunity, allergic response, helminths), Th17 (mucosal immunity, fungal and extracellular bacterial infections), and follicular helper T cells (germinal center B cell responses).

**Cytotoxic T cells (Tc, CD8+)** recognize peptides on MHC I. Their main function is to kill cells that display foreign peptides — virus-infected cells, tumor cells with neoantigens. They kill by inserting perforin into the target membrane and injecting granzymes that trigger apoptosis.

**Regulatory T cells (Treg)** suppress immune responses. They prevent autoimmunity and limit the magnitude of responses to prevent excessive damage. Loss of Treg function is implicated in autoimmune disease.

**Memory T cells** are long-lived cells generated after antigen exposure. They persist for decades and provide rapid response to re-exposure.

## Superantigens

A subset of bacterial toxins called **superantigens** bypass normal T cell activation. Instead of binding a specific peptide-MHC complex with a specific TCR, superantigens bind a region of MHC class II and a non-variable region of the TCR, cross-linking them. The result is activation of large numbers of T cells (up to 20% of all T cells, versus the ~0.001% activated by a typical antigen). The massive activation releases enormous amounts of cytokine, producing toxic shock.

Major superantigens: staphylococcal toxic shock syndrome toxin (TSST-1), staphylococcal enterotoxins, streptococcal pyrogenic exotoxins. We met these in Chapter 15.

## B cells

B cells originate and mature in the bone marrow (the "B" in B cell, despite the name actually deriving from Bursa of Fabricius in birds — different B's). They express surface antibody (the B cell receptor, or BCR) generated by the same kind of V(D)J recombination that produces TCRs.

B cells encounter their specific antigen, take it up, process it, present peptide fragments on MHC II, and present to helper T cells. If the helper T cell agrees that this antigen is worth responding to, it activates the B cell. The activated B cell proliferates and differentiates into **plasma cells** that secrete antibody and **memory B cells** for future encounters.

Some antigens — large polysaccharides like bacterial capsules — can activate B cells without T cell help (**T-independent antigens**). These responses are faster but produce only IgM and limited memory. T-dependent responses are slower but produce class-switched antibodies (IgG, IgA, IgE) and robust memory.

### Primary versus secondary response

A **primary immune response** — the response to a first encounter with an antigen — takes 5–10 days to develop and produces primarily IgM, with peak antibody levels modest. Plasma cells decline after the response, but memory cells persist.

A **secondary immune response** — to a subsequent encounter with the same antigen — is faster (1–2 days), larger (much higher antibody titers), and dominated by class-switched IgG (or IgA at mucosal surfaces). This is immunity in the practical sense. The secondary response is what protects vaccinated individuals.

The change between primary and secondary responses is the basis of vaccination. A vaccine triggers a primary response without causing disease, leaving memory cells. When the real pathogen arrives, the secondary response is immediate and protective.

## Classifications of adaptive immunity

Immunity comes in several practical varieties:

**Natural active immunity**: from actual infection. The immune system responds to the pathogen and develops memory. Long-lasting but requires getting sick.

**Natural passive immunity**: from antibody transfer between individuals without an active immune response. Maternal IgG crossing the placenta gives newborns protection for the first few months. Breast milk IgA protects breastfed infants' mucous membranes. Both are transient — only as long as the borrowed antibodies last.

**Artificial active immunity**: from vaccination. The immune system responds to a vaccine antigen and develops memory. Long-lasting without requiring disease.

**Artificial passive immunity**: from infusion of antibody preparations. Rabies immunoglobulin given to a person bitten by a possibly rabid animal. Monoclonal antibodies against specific pathogens or toxins. Transient.

## Vaccines

The history of vaccination begins, in the modern sense, with Edward Jenner in 1796. Jenner noticed that milkmaids who had contracted cowpox seemed immune to smallpox. He took material from a cowpox lesion and inoculated an eight-year-old boy with it. The boy developed mild cowpox. Jenner then exposed him to smallpox; the boy did not develop disease. The technique — **variolation** with cowpox material to protect against smallpox — was published in 1798. [^1]

The word "vaccine" comes from *vacca*, the Latin for cow.

The basic principle: present the immune system with a representation of a pathogen that triggers an adaptive response without causing the disease. The representation can be:

**Live attenuated vaccines** contain a weakened version of the pathogen. The pathogen still replicates and triggers a robust immune response but does not cause significant disease. Long-lasting immunity, often lifelong. Risk: rare reversion to virulence; cannot be given to immunocompromised patients. Examples: measles, mumps, rubella (MMR), oral polio (Sabin), yellow fever, varicella, BCG (tuberculosis).

**Inactivated (killed) vaccines** contain pathogen that has been killed by heat, chemicals, or radiation. Cannot replicate. Safer for immunocompromised patients but generally produces weaker immunity than live vaccines, often requiring boosters. Examples: inactivated polio (Salk), influenza inactivated, hepatitis A, rabies.

**Subunit vaccines** contain only specific antigenic components of the pathogen (proteins, polysaccharides), not the whole organism. Cannot cause disease. Examples: hepatitis B (recombinant surface antigen), HPV (recombinant capsid protein), pneumococcal polysaccharide.

**Conjugate vaccines** are polysaccharide vaccines linked to a protein carrier. The carrier converts a T-independent polysaccharide antigen into a T-dependent one, generating class-switched memory. Conjugate vaccines work in young children, who do not respond well to plain polysaccharide vaccines. Examples: *H. influenzae* type b (Hib), pneumococcal conjugate (PCV13, PCV20), meningococcal conjugate.

**Toxoid vaccines** are inactivated toxins. They produce immunity against the toxin specifically, not the bacterium. Useful for diseases caused mainly by toxin production. Examples: tetanus, diphtheria.

**mRNA vaccines** contain mRNA encoding a pathogen protein (the antigen), delivered in lipid nanoparticles. The mRNA enters host cells, which translate the antigen and present it to the immune system. The mRNA is degraded; the host cell makes the antigen briefly. Examples: COVID-19 (Pfizer-BioNTech, Moderna), some experimental influenza and cancer vaccines. The COVID-19 mRNA vaccines were developed and approved in less than a year, demonstrating the speed advantage of this platform. [^2]

### Adjuvants

Most non-live vaccines need an **adjuvant** — a component that enhances the immune response. Aluminum salts (alum) are the most common adjuvant in humans. Newer adjuvants include MF59 (an oil-in-water emulsion), AS01 (a saponin-based adjuvant), and CpG (synthetic DNA that activates innate immunity through TLR9). The adjuvant works by triggering innate inflammation at the injection site, which recruits dendritic cells and amplifies the adaptive response.

## What the chapter is really about

Adaptive immunity is a programmable defense system that learns by encounter and remembers. The mechanism — randomly generated receptors selected by their ability to bind real pathogens — is genuinely strange when you think about it. The system pre-builds a vast library of receptors in case something matches, then heavily uses the ones that turn out to match and ignores the rest.

The implications are far-reaching. Vaccination works because the adaptive system can be educated with non-pathogenic representations. Autoimmunity happens when the selection against self-reactive lymphocytes fails. Immunodeficiency happens when components are missing or impaired. Cancer immunotherapy works (when it does) by helping the adaptive system recognize tumors as foreign. Transplantation requires either close MHC matching or pharmacological suppression of the adaptive response.

The chapters that follow (19, 20) explore what happens when adaptive immunity goes wrong (hypersensitivity, autoimmunity, immunodeficiency) and how we measure it in the lab. The clinical chapters (21–26) describe specific infections, many of which are partly characterized by their adaptive immune responses. Throughout, the adaptive immune system is the second wall — the one that holds when the first wall fails, the one that learns from each encounter so the next is easier.

## Still puzzling

I do not fully understand why some pathogens (HIV, hepatitis C, malaria) successfully evade adaptive immunity over the long term while others (most vaccines work) do not. HIV's hyper-mutating envelope is part of the answer for HIV; antigenic variation is part of the answer for malaria; the host's chronic activation may exhaust T cells. But the broader picture — what determines whether the adaptive immune system "wins" — is incomplete. Why some pathogens have evolved successful evasion and others have not is a long-standing question.

## What would change my mind

The textbook treatment of adaptive immunity has been heavily focused on antibody-mediated and conventional T cell responses. Recent work on **regulatory T cells**, **innate lymphoid cells (ILCs)**, **tissue-resident memory cells**, and **trained immunity** is expanding the picture significantly. If trained immunity or ILC biology turn out to be substantially more important clinically than current data suggest, the innate-adaptive distinction itself will need to be modified. `[verify: 2025 status of trained immunity and ILC biology]`
