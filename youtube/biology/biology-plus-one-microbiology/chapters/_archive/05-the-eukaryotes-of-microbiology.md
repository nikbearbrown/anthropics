# Chapter 05 — The Eukaryotes of Microbiology

*A category is a place to start. When you have to put protists, fungi, parasitic worms, algae, and lichens in the same chapter, the category is doing more work than it should.*

## The question before the answer

Imagine you are sorting a basket of objects into bins and someone hands you these: a single-celled amoeba that engulfs prey, a yeast that ferments sugar, a tapeworm two meters long, a microscopic red alga, and a lichen, which is itself two organisms in a permanent partnership.

What do these things share? Cells with nuclei. That is essentially it. They are all eukaryotes — descendants of the original endosymbiotic event that produced eukaryotic cells (Chapter 3) — and they are unified into one chapter of a microbiology textbook because at some life stage they look microscopic, or because the techniques used to study them are the techniques of microbiology.

This is a chapter built around the messiness of biological categories. "Protist" is not a real natural group. It is the leftover drawer: eukaryotes that are not animals, plants, or fungi. The chapter is going to be honest about that. The point is not to pretend the categories are clean. It is to learn the actual organisms — what they do, what diseases they cause, what their life cycles look like — and to notice where the chapter's own structure breaks down.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

1. Describe the major life cycles of unicellular eukaryotic parasites and identify which stages cause disease.
2. Distinguish the main groups of parasitic helminths (nematodes, trematodes, cestodes) by morphology and life cycle.
3. Distinguish yeasts from molds and identify which structural features make a fungus pathogenic to humans.
4. Explain why algae are studied in microbiology, and identify the medically relevant toxin-producing species.
5. Describe what a lichen is — and why it is the textbook example that cooperation can build something with new properties.

Prerequisites: Chapters 1–4.

## Protists

The term *protist* is taxonomic shorthand for "single-celled eukaryote that doesn't fit elsewhere." It is not a phylogenetic group. Modern molecular taxonomy has fractured what used to be "protists" into several large supergroups (Excavata, Chromalveolata, Rhizaria, Archaeplastida, Amoebozoa, and others). The boundaries shift as new sequence data come in. For clinical purposes, the categorization that matters is functional: how does the organism live, and how does it make people sick?

### Protozoa

*Protozoa* — from the Greek for "first animals" — is an older subgroup of protists that includes the unicellular eukaryotes that hunt or parasitize. They are not actually one evolutionary group. The word groups them by lifestyle.

The major modes of locomotion:

- **Pseudopods**: extensions of the cell membrane and cytoplasm. *Amoeba* moves this way. *Entamoeba histolytica* causes amoebic dysentery — a serious gastrointestinal infection in regions with poor sanitation.
- **Flagella**: long whip-like extensions, usually one or two per cell. *Giardia lamblia* uses flagella to swim through the small intestine. *Trypanosoma brucei* causes African sleeping sickness; *T. cruzi* causes Chagas disease. *Trichomonas vaginalis* causes the most common nonviral sexually transmitted infection in the world.
- **Cilia**: short hair-like structures covering the cell in coordinated rows. *Paramecium* is the classic example. The medically important ciliated parasite is *Balantidium coli*, a rare cause of intestinal disease.
- **Apicomplexan**: a group of obligate intracellular parasites with no obvious locomotion in their adult stages but with a complex life cycle involving multiple hosts. *Plasmodium* is the genus that causes malaria. *Toxoplasma gondii* is one of the most successful parasites on Earth — possibly a third of all humans carry it asymptomatically. *Cryptosporidium* causes waterborne diarrheal disease.

The apicomplexan life cycle deserves attention because it is the strangest. *Plasmodium falciparum*, the malaria parasite responsible for most malaria deaths, requires *both* a mosquito and a human to complete its life cycle. In the mosquito, it undergoes sexual reproduction and develops into infectious sporozoites that live in the mosquito's salivary glands. When the mosquito bites a human, sporozoites enter the bloodstream and travel to the liver, where they invade liver cells and reproduce asexually. The progeny burst out, enter red blood cells, reproduce again asexually, burst out (causing the cyclic fevers of malaria), and the cycle continues. Some of the parasites differentiate into sexual forms that a mosquito can pick up in its next blood meal, completing the cycle.

This is a parasite that has co-evolved with mosquito and human for at least 30 million years. The seven-stage life cycle is why a malaria vaccine is so hard to design: a vaccine effective against one stage may have no effect on the others, and the parasite has many opportunities to evade the immune system across different cellular environments. The first approved malaria vaccine, RTS,S/AS01, was endorsed by the WHO in 2021 and offers about 30% efficacy against severe malaria. [^1] That is not zero. It is also not what a vaccine usually does. Malaria is hard.

### Algae among the protists

Many of the organisms historically called "algae" are protists. They have chloroplasts (descended from a secondary endosymbiotic event — a eukaryote engulfed a cyanobacterium-containing eukaryote, leaving the chloroplast embedded inside multiple membranes), they photosynthesize, and they are usually single-celled or simple multicellular.

The major algal groups: diatoms (single-celled, with intricate silica shells that fossilize beautifully), dinoflagellates (some bioluminescent, some toxic), red algae, brown algae (which includes the kelps — actually multicellular and quite large), and green algae (sister to the lineage that became land plants).

Algae are studied in microbiology because:

1. **Toxin production**. Some dinoflagellates produce neurotoxins that accumulate in shellfish — saxitoxin, brevetoxin, ciguatoxin. Eating contaminated shellfish causes paralytic shellfish poisoning, neurotoxic shellfish poisoning, or ciguatera fish poisoning, depending on the toxin. Harmful algal blooms ("red tides") in coastal waters can close fisheries and kill marine wildlife.
2. **Ecology**. Photosynthetic algae are the base of marine food webs. The carbon cycle of the oceans is largely run by them.
3. **Biotechnology**. Agar — the gelling agent that made Koch's pure cultures possible (Chapter 1) — is extracted from red algae. Alginate, carrageenan, fucoidan, and various other algal polymers are used in food, cosmetics, and biomedicine.
4. **Biofuels**. Algae can convert sunlight to lipids at higher efficiency than terrestrial crops, and several companies are working to make algal biodiesel commercially viable.

## Helminths — animals in a microbiology book

Helminths are worms. They are macroscopic, often centimeters or meters long. They are multicellular animals. They are not microorganisms in any defensible sense — until you remember that their eggs and larvae are microscopic, and that diagnosis of helminth infection nearly always involves microscopy of stool or tissue samples. Microbiologists handle them because the diagnostic pipeline overlaps with the bacterial and protozoan one.

There are three major groups of parasitic helminths.

### Nematodes (roundworms)

Cylindrical, unsegmented, with a complete digestive tract (mouth and anus). *Ascaris lumbricoides* is the most common parasitic worm worldwide — roughly a billion people are infected. *Necator americanus* and *Ancylostoma duodenale* are hookworms, which enter through the skin (usually the feet) and migrate to the small intestine where they suck blood. *Enterobius vermicularis* is the pinworm, the most common helminth in developed countries — usually in children, causing perianal itching. *Trichinella spiralis* is acquired from undercooked pork and causes trichinosis. *Wuchereria bancrofti* causes lymphatic filariasis (elephantiasis).

The diagnostic move for most intestinal nematodes is to identify the eggs in a stool sample. Each species has a distinctive egg shape. A trained microbiologist can identify the parasite from the egg under a brightfield microscope.

### Cestodes (tapeworms)

Flat, ribbon-like, segmented. They have no digestive tract — they absorb nutrients across their body wall, living attached to the intestinal lining of a host. Each segment (proglottid) contains a complete set of reproductive organs and can detach and pass in feces.

*Taenia solium* is the pork tapeworm. The adult worm in the intestine is rarely dangerous, but its larval stage can encyst in human muscle and brain tissue (neurocysticercosis), a leading cause of acquired epilepsy in many parts of the world. *Taenia saginata* is the beef tapeworm, less dangerous. *Echinococcus granulosus* is a small tapeworm whose larval cysts can grow to the size of grapefruits in human liver, lung, or brain (hydatid disease). *Diphyllobothrium latum* is the fish tapeworm, which can absorb so much vitamin B12 from its host that the host develops pernicious anemia.

### Trematodes (flukes)

Flat, unsegmented, usually leaf-shaped. They have a complex life cycle that always involves a snail intermediate host and at least one vertebrate definitive host.

The medically important trematodes are the schistosomes — three species of *Schistosoma* that cause schistosomiasis (bilharzia), one of the most prevalent parasitic diseases in the world. Adult worms live in pairs in human blood vessels; the female lays eggs that lodge in tissues, causing chronic inflammation. Long-term *S. haematobium* infection of the urinary tract is a recognized cause of bladder cancer.

*Fasciola hepatica* is the liver fluke, transmitted by eating watercress contaminated with metacercariae. *Clonorchis sinensis* and *Opisthorchis* are liver flukes acquired by eating raw or undercooked fish — they are a recognized cause of cholangiocarcinoma in endemic regions.

I want to flag one structural feature shared across helminths: the life cycle is almost always multi-host. Eggs leave one host. Larvae develop in an intermediate (often invertebrate) host. Infectious larvae enter a definitive (often vertebrate) host. The complexity of the cycle is what makes these infections hard to interrupt — you can attack the worm, the snail, the cercaria, or the egg, but breaking one stage is not enough to break the cycle. Public health interventions against helminths tend to be aimed at the most accessible stage, which is usually the egg in human feces, which is usually addressed by sanitation. The decline in helminth infections in developed countries is the result of toilets, not antiparasitic drugs.

## Fungi

Fungi are eukaryotes with chitin in their cell walls. They are not photosynthetic — they live by absorbing nutrients from their environment, often after secreting enzymes to break down nearby material. They include single-celled forms (yeasts) and multicellular filamentous forms (molds), and many species can switch between the two (dimorphism).

### Yeasts

Single-celled fungi that reproduce by budding (asymmetric cell division, producing a smaller daughter cell that pinches off from the parent) or fission. *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* is the most studied eukaryote in laboratory biology and the agent of all alcoholic fermentation, all bread leavening, and a vast amount of basic cell biology research.

The medically important yeasts:

- **Candida albicans** and related *Candida* species. Normal residents of skin, mouth, gut, and vagina. Cause yeast infections (vulvovaginal candidiasis), thrush (oral candidiasis), and invasive candidiasis in immunocompromised patients. *Candida auris*, identified in 2009, has emerged as a multi-drug-resistant nosocomial pathogen of significant concern.
- **Cryptococcus neoformans** and **C. gattii**. Cause meningitis, particularly in immunocompromised hosts. We will return to *Cryptococcus* in Chapter 26.

### Molds

Multicellular filamentous fungi. The basic filament is a *hypha* (plural: *hyphae*), a long tube of cytoplasm. A mass of hyphae is a *mycelium*. The visible part of a mushroom is the fruiting body — a reproductive structure that releases spores. The bulk of the fungus is below ground or inside its food source.

Medically important molds:

- **Aspergillus** species. Common environmental molds. Spores are inhaled constantly without consequence in healthy people. In immunocompromised patients (chemotherapy recipients, transplant patients), they can cause invasive pulmonary aspergillosis, a frequently fatal lung infection.
- **Penicillium** species. The genus that gave us penicillin. Most species are environmental decomposers; a few cause infections. *P. marneffei* is dimorphic and causes opportunistic infections in HIV patients in Southeast Asia.
- **Histoplasma**, **Blastomyces**, **Coccidioides**, **Paracoccidioides**. Dimorphic fungi — mold in the environment, yeast inside the human host. Each is endemic to a specific geographic region. They cause respiratory and disseminated infections. We will return to them in Chapter 22.
- **The dermatophytes** — *Trichophyton*, *Microsporum*, *Epidermophyton*. Filamentous fungi that infect keratinized tissue (skin, hair, nails), causing tinea infections (ringworm, athlete's foot, jock itch).

### Mycotoxins

Some molds produce toxic secondary metabolites that contaminate food. *Aspergillus flavus* produces aflatoxins on improperly stored grains and peanuts — aflatoxin B1 is one of the most potent known liver carcinogens. *Claviceps purpurea* infects rye and produces ergot alkaloids, which cause ergotism (a syndrome of vasoconstriction, gangrene, and hallucinations that may explain some episodes in medieval Europe, including possibly the Salem witch trials). [^2]

### Why fungal infections are hard to treat

Fungi are eukaryotes. We are eukaryotes. Drug selectivity is harder to achieve than it is against bacteria, because the molecular machinery a drug might target is shared between fungus and host. Most antifungals work by exploiting one significant difference: fungal cell membranes use *ergosterol* as the dominant sterol, while animal cell membranes use *cholesterol*. Azole antifungals (fluconazole, voriconazole) inhibit ergosterol synthesis. Polyene antifungals (amphotericin B, nystatin) bind ergosterol directly and disrupt the membrane. The selectivity is good but not perfect, and amphotericin B in particular has significant kidney toxicity (nicknamed "amphoterrible" by clinicians).

Echinocandins (caspofungin, micafungin) target β-glucan synthesis in the fungal cell wall — a target with no human counterpart, since we have no cell walls. They are newer and better tolerated. We will revisit antifungal mechanisms in Chapter 14.

## Lichens

A lichen is the most interesting eukaryote in this chapter, and it is not technically one organism.

A lichen is a permanent symbiotic partnership between a fungus (the *mycobiont*, usually an Ascomycete) and a photosynthetic partner (the *photobiont*, either a green alga or a cyanobacterium). The fungus provides physical structure, water retention, and protection. The photobiont provides organic carbon through photosynthesis. The combination is a composite organism with properties neither partner has on its own: it can colonize bare rock, tolerate extreme drying, survive temperatures from –50°C to +85°C, and live for thousands of years (some Arctic lichens are estimated at 9,000 years old).

Lichens are mostly classified by their fungal partner. The same fungal species, partnered with different photobionts, produces different-looking lichens. There are around 14,000 known lichen-forming fungal species. Most ascomycete fungi cannot photosynthesize, but the lineage that became lichens evolved this partnership independently at least nine times. Cooperation works.

The interest for biology is partly philosophical. A lichen is a single physiological entity built from two genomes. It is what symbiosis can become when it goes far enough. We met this idea in Chapter 3 with mitochondria; lichens are a less ancient example of the same principle. Cooperation can produce something that is not adequately described as either of its components.

Lichens are sensitive to air pollution, particularly sulfur dioxide, and have been used as biomonitors of air quality for decades. Lichens are also the basis of subsistence diets for caribou and reindeer, and the ecological foundation of much of the Arctic tundra. They are not microorganisms by any honest definition; their inclusion in microbiology is a category artifact. They are still worth understanding because they show what biology can do when it stops obeying the textbook's organism boundaries.

## What the chapter is really about

The "eukaryotic microbes" category is a teaching convenience, not a natural group. Protists, fungi, algae, helminths, and lichens have very little in common evolutionarily. They are placed together here because the techniques of microbiology — culture, microscopy, biochemical identification — are used to study them, and because at some stage of their life cycle they are microscopic.

What unifies them, instead of phylogeny, is medical and ecological relevance. The diseases they cause — malaria, schistosomiasis, candidiasis, aspergillosis, the dermatophyte infections, the helminthic infections that affect roughly a quarter of the world's population — are major causes of human morbidity. The ecological roles they play — algal photosynthesis, fungal decomposition, lichen colonization of bare rock — are foundational to terrestrial and aquatic ecosystems.

The honest lesson is that biology's categories are scaffolding. You can use them. You should not be too attached to them. When the data force a recategorization, recategorize.

## Still puzzling

I do not understand, mechanistically, why mass treatment for soil-transmitted helminths in some endemic regions seems to reduce overall human health gains less than the parasite burden alone would predict. Helminths are unambiguously bad for their hosts. Mass deworming campaigns should produce major improvements in growth and cognition. Some studies show this. Others do not. The "hygiene hypothesis" — that some childhood exposure to parasites trains the immune system in ways that prevent later autoimmune disease — is plausible but not settled. The relationship between humans and our parasitic worms is more complicated than "parasite = pathogen."

## What would change my mind

I have presented the dimorphic fungi as separate species — *Histoplasma*, *Blastomyces*, *Coccidioides*, etc. The molecular phylogeny is increasingly suggesting that the boundaries between some of these species are blurrier than the textbook treatment suggests, and that what looks like several species may be a small number with substantial geographic and morphological variation.
