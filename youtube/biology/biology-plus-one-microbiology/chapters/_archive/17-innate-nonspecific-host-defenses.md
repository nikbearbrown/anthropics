# Chapter 17 — Innate (Nonspecific) Host Defenses

*Most pathogens never get past the wall. The wall is what you don't notice.*

## The question before the answer

Right now, your skin is colonized by hundreds of bacterial species. Your gut contains 10–100 trillion bacterial cells. Your nose, your mouth, your conjunctiva, your urogenital tract — all contain microbial communities. And yet, almost certainly, you are not sick.

The reasons are mostly hidden. Your skin is a multilayered barrier shedding cells constantly. Your tear fluid contains an antibacterial enzyme that lyses Gram-positive cell walls. Your stomach acid kills most ingested microbes. The mucus in your respiratory tract is dragged upward continuously by ciliated cells. Your bladder flushes itself with urine that contains antimicrobial peptides. Even before any immune cell has recognized any pathogen, you are spending substantial metabolic resources keeping the microbial world at arm's length.

This is the innate immune system. It is the body's first response to infection — present from birth, identical in every person, ready before any specific pathogen is recognized. It does not learn. It does not improve with experience. It is the wall behind which the more elaborate adaptive immune system (Chapter 18) has the time and signals it needs to do its work.

The point of this chapter is to map the innate system: the barriers, the chemical defenses, the cells that patrol tissues, and the inflammatory response that escalates when the barriers fail. Most successful infections require some specific bacterial adaptation that lets them get past the innate defenses. The defenses are not glamorous, but they are doing essentially all of the everyday work of keeping you alive.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

6. Describe phagocytosis from recognition to killing and identify the major killing mechanisms.
7. Explain the signs of inflammation and fever in terms of underlying mechanisms.

## Physical and mechanical barriers

The first line of defense is keeping pathogens out.

**Skin** is the biggest. About 1.8 square meters of stratified squamous epithelium covered with a layer of dead, keratinized cells. The outer layer is constantly shed and replaced, taking attached microbes with it. The skin is also dry — its low water activity is hostile to most bacteria. It is slightly acidic (pH 4–6) — also hostile to many microbes. Skin breaks (cuts, burns, surgical wounds) compromise this barrier and are major routes of infection.

**Mucous membranes** line the respiratory, gastrointestinal, urogenital, and ocular tracts. They are not as tough as skin but have their own defenses:

- **Mucus** is a thick, sticky gel that traps microbes, preventing them from reaching the underlying epithelium. Mucus contains antimicrobial proteins, IgA antibodies, and various trapping factors.
- **Cilia** are short hair-like structures on epithelial cells. In the respiratory tract, cilia beat upward in coordinated waves, dragging the mucous layer (and trapped microbes) toward the throat to be swallowed or coughed out. This is the **mucociliary escalator**. Damage to ciliated cells (by smoking, by infection, by toxins) breaks this defense and predisposes to respiratory infection.
- **Tears** wash microbes out of the eye and contain lysozyme.
- **Saliva** flushes the oral cavity, contains lysozyme, IgA, and lactoferrin.

**Specialized barriers**:

- **Stomach acid** (pH 1–3) kills most ingested microbes. *Vibrio cholerae* requires about 10⁸ organisms to cause infection partly because most are killed by stomach acid; people on antacid therapy can be infected with much smaller doses.
- **Bile** in the duodenum is antimicrobial.
- **Urine flow** flushes the urinary tract; urinary stasis (from obstruction, indwelling catheters) is a major risk factor for UTI.
- **Cerumen** (earwax) in the external ear contains antimicrobial fatty acids.

The pattern: most barriers combine a physical element (epithelial layer, mucus, flow) with a chemical element (acid, enzymes, antimicrobial peptides). The combination is more effective than either alone.

## Normal microbiota as defense

The bacteria already living on you are part of your defense system.

The normal microbiota occupies ecological niches that opportunistic pathogens would otherwise colonize. They consume nutrients pathogens need. They produce metabolites (lactic acid, hydrogen peroxide, bacteriocins) that inhibit competitor growth. They train and modulate local immune responses. They form physical barriers.

Disrupt the microbiota — typically by broad-spectrum antibiotics — and you remove this layer of defense. The classic case is *Clostridioides difficile* colitis, where the gut microbiota normally keeps *C. difficile* in check. Broad-spectrum antibiotics knock back the competitor bacteria, *C. difficile* overgrows, and you get severe colitis.

The role of the microbiota in defense is one of the more recently appreciated aspects of human immunity. Until about 2005, we did not have the tools (metagenomic sequencing) to characterize the microbiota in detail. The current research consensus places microbiota-mediated colonization resistance as a major part of innate defense, on par with the chemical and cellular components. Fecal microbiota transplantation — transferring stool from a healthy donor to a patient with recurrent *C. difficile* — has cure rates over 90%, demonstrating that restoring the microbiota restores the defense. We will return to this in Chapter 24.

## Chemical defenses

Once a microbe gets past the physical barriers, it encounters chemical defenses in tissue fluids and blood.

### Lysozyme

A small enzyme that hydrolyzes peptidoglycan. Present in tears, saliva, mucus, breast milk. Effective primarily against Gram-positive bacteria, whose thick peptidoglycan is exposed. Less effective against Gram-negatives because their peptidoglycan is shielded by the outer membrane. Discovered by Alexander Fleming in 1922 (before penicillin) — he isolated it from his own nasal mucus, which he had dripped onto a bacterial culture and observed killed the bacteria. [^1]

### Defensins and other antimicrobial peptides

**Defensins** are short cationic peptides (about 30 amino acids) that disrupt microbial membranes. They are produced by neutrophils, epithelial cells, and Paneth cells in the intestine. Different defensins target different microbes. They work by binding to negatively charged microbial membranes and forming pores that depolarize and kill the cell.

### Complement

The complement system is a cascade of about 30 plasma proteins that, when activated, can directly kill bacteria (Gram-negatives, mostly), opsonize them (coat them so phagocytes can eat them more easily), and recruit immune cells.

Three activation pathways:

**Classical pathway**: triggered by antibody bound to a pathogen surface. C1 binds the antibody. The cascade proceeds.

**Lectin pathway**: triggered by mannose-binding lectin (MBL), a serum protein that recognizes mannose on bacterial surfaces (most human cells have mannose buried under other sugars, but bacteria expose it). MBL is structurally similar to C1 and activates the same downstream cascade.

**Alternative pathway**: spontaneous low-level activation that is amplified by surfaces lacking the regulatory proteins host cells have. Bacterial surfaces lack these regulators; host surfaces don't. The pathway is "always on" at low level and gets amplified specifically on foreign surfaces.

All three pathways converge on cleaving C3 and then C5. Downstream effects:

- **C3b** opsonizes the surface (coats it for phagocytosis).
- **C3a and C5a** are anaphylatoxins — they trigger inflammation, increase vascular permeability, attract phagocytes.
- **C5b–C9** assemble into the **membrane attack complex (MAC)** — a pore that perforates Gram-negative outer membranes, killing the cell.

Complement deficiencies cause specific susceptibilities. C3 deficiency leads to recurrent bacterial infections. Late-component deficiencies (C5–C9) cause susceptibility specifically to *Neisseria* infections — *N. meningitidis* and *N. gonorrhoeae* — because *Neisseria* species are unusually sensitive to MAC-mediated killing.

### Cytokines

**Cytokines** are signaling proteins secreted by immune cells. They coordinate the immune response and signal between cells. Some are interleukins (IL-1 through IL-38+), some are tumor necrosis factors (TNF-α, TNF-β), some are growth factors (GM-CSF), some are chemokines (small cytokines that direct cell movement).

Major innate cytokines:

- **IL-1** and **TNF-α**: pro-inflammatory cytokines released by macrophages in response to infection. Cause fever, mobilize neutrophils, induce acute-phase response.
- **IL-6**: triggers acute-phase protein production by the liver.
- **IL-8**: a chemokine that attracts neutrophils to infection sites.
- **IL-10**: anti-inflammatory; brakes the immune response to prevent excessive damage.

The cytokine network has a logic of escalation and dampening. Initial detection of infection triggers pro-inflammatory cytokines that bring more immune cells and resources to the site. As the infection is controlled, anti-inflammatory cytokines turn down the response. When the network breaks — too much pro-inflammatory signaling, not enough damping — the result is the "cytokine storm" of septic shock or hyperinflammatory states.

### Interferons

**Interferons** are cytokines that mediate antiviral defense. Type I interferons (IFN-α, IFN-β) are produced by virus-infected cells. They signal to neighboring cells: "I am infected; prepare for the virus." Neighboring cells respond by upregulating antiviral genes — increased proteasomal degradation of viral proteins, increased MHC class I expression (which marks them for cytotoxic T cell recognition), shutdown of protein synthesis, induction of ribonucleases that degrade RNA.

Type II interferon (IFN-γ) is produced by NK cells and activated T cells. It activates macrophages, increasing their bactericidal capacity.

Type III interferons (IFN-λ) act on epithelial cells.

Interferon's discovery in 1957 by Alick Isaacs and Jean Lindenmann was one of the first identifications of an antiviral cytokine. Recombinant interferons are used therapeutically for hepatitis C (until directly-acting antivirals replaced them), some cancers, and multiple sclerosis.

### Acute-phase proteins

When infection occurs, the liver dramatically increases production of several plasma proteins. **C-reactive protein (CRP)** binds to certain bacterial surfaces, activating complement and opsonizing the bacterium. CRP is the most commonly measured acute-phase protein clinically — elevated CRP is a marker of acute inflammation. **Serum amyloid A**, **ferritin**, **fibrinogen**, **mannose-binding lectin** are also acute-phase proteins.

The acute-phase response is part of why inflammatory conditions look the way they look — elevated CRP, elevated ESR (erythrocyte sedimentation rate, which reflects increased fibrinogen), elevated ferritin. These are not diagnostic of a specific infection but tell you the immune system is acutely engaged.

## Cellular defenses

### Blood and hematopoiesis

All blood cells (red cells, platelets, white cells) come from **hematopoietic stem cells** in the bone marrow. The stem cells differentiate through committed progenitors into the various mature blood cell types in a process called **hematopoiesis**.

The major white blood cell categories:

- **Granulocytes**: neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils. Contain granules visible by light microscopy.
- **Agranulocytes**: monocytes, lymphocytes. No visible granules.

A complete blood count (CBC) measures the major cell types. A typical adult has 4,000–11,000 white blood cells per microliter; about 60% are neutrophils.

### Neutrophils

**Neutrophils** are the most numerous white blood cells and the front line of bacterial defense. They have a characteristic multilobed nucleus and granules containing antimicrobial enzymes and peptides. Neutrophils circulate in blood for about 6 hours before either being recruited to tissues or dying.

When tissue is infected, neutrophils are the first cells to arrive — usually within hours. They migrate from blood to tissue by squeezing between endothelial cells (a process called **extravasation** or **diapedesis**), guided by chemokines from the infection site. Once in the tissue, they phagocytose pathogens, kill them with reactive oxygen species and antimicrobial peptides, and die after consuming a few microbes. Dead neutrophils plus tissue debris is what pus is. Neutrophils have a short lifespan in tissues — hours to a couple of days.

### Macrophages

**Macrophages** are larger, longer-lived phagocytes that develop from circulating monocytes once they enter tissues. They reside in tissues continuously, surveilling for pathogens. Tissue-specific macrophages have names — Kupffer cells in liver, microglia in brain, alveolar macrophages in lung — but they are all macrophages.

Macrophages do several things: phagocytose pathogens, present antigens to T cells (Chapter 18), produce cytokines, repair tissue damage. They are slower to respond than neutrophils but stay longer and do more sophisticated work.

### Dendritic cells

**Dendritic cells** are the bridge between innate and adaptive immunity. They patrol tissues, take up antigens, and migrate to lymph nodes to present those antigens to T cells. They are the primary antigen-presenting cells for initiating adaptive immune responses.

### NK cells

**Natural killer (NK) cells** are lymphocytes that kill virus-infected cells and tumor cells without prior antigen exposure. They recognize cells that have downregulated MHC class I (a strategy used by viruses to evade T cell recognition) and kill them by inducing apoptosis. NK cells provide an early antiviral defense before adaptive T cell responses are fully mobilized.

### Eosinophils, basophils, mast cells

**Eosinophils** are involved in defense against helminths and in allergic responses. They release toxic granule contents that damage parasite surfaces. Their numbers rise dramatically in parasitic infections and in many allergic conditions.

**Basophils** in blood and **mast cells** in tissues release histamine and other mediators in response to specific triggers, particularly IgE-bound antigens. They mediate allergic responses and contribute to inflammation.

## Phagocytosis in detail

Phagocytes (neutrophils, macrophages, dendritic cells) eat pathogens. The process is mechanistically interesting and the basis of much of innate defense.

The steps:

1. **Recognition**. The phagocyte detects the pathogen through receptors on its surface. Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and other pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) recognize **pathogen-associated molecular patterns** (PAMPs) — bacterial LPS, lipoteichoic acid, peptidoglycan fragments, double-stranded RNA, etc. These are molecular features that are common to broad classes of pathogens and absent in host cells. The recognition is "innate" in the sense that the receptors are encoded in the germline, not generated by recombination as adaptive receptors are.

2. **Engulfment**. The phagocyte extends pseudopods around the pathogen, fusing them on the far side to enclose it in a vacuole called a phagosome.

3. **Phagosome-lysosome fusion**. The phagosome fuses with lysosomes, which contain digestive enzymes and antimicrobial proteins. The resulting phagolysosome is now a hostile compartment.

4. **Killing**. Inside the phagolysosome, the pathogen is destroyed by multiple mechanisms:
   - **Reactive oxygen species (ROS)**: NADPH oxidase generates superoxide; subsequent enzymes produce hydrogen peroxide, hypochlorite, hydroxyl radicals. These oxidize proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids. Patients with **chronic granulomatous disease** lack functional NADPH oxidase and cannot make ROS effectively; they have recurrent serious infections with catalase-producing bacteria (which break down hydrogen peroxide).

### Opsonization

Phagocytes recognize some pathogens directly through pattern recognition receptors, but they recognize others much more efficiently if the pathogens are coated with **opsonins** — proteins that mark them for phagocytosis. The main opsonins are antibodies (from adaptive immunity) and complement fragments (C3b). Opsonized pathogens are engulfed much faster and more efficiently than naked ones.

Some bacteria have evolved to resist opsonization. Capsules (Chapter 15) are a major mechanism — the capsule physically prevents opsonins from reaching the bacterial surface. This is why encapsulated bacteria (*S. pneumoniae*, *N. meningitidis*, *H. influenzae*) are particularly difficult for phagocytes to handle, and why splenectomized patients (who lack the spleen's macrophage population that handles opsonin-resistant encapsulated bacteria) are at special risk for these infections.

## Inflammation

Inflammation is the coordinated tissue response to injury or infection. The five classical signs were described by the Roman physician Celsus in the first century CE: **rubor** (redness), **tumor** (swelling), **calor** (heat), **dolor** (pain), and **functio laesa** (loss of function).

The mechanism:

1. **Detection**. Pattern recognition receptors detect pathogens or tissue damage.
2. **Mediator release**. Macrophages, mast cells, and damaged cells release histamine, prostaglandins, leukotrienes, cytokines.
3. **Vasodilation**. Blood vessels in the area dilate (redness, heat).
4. **Vascular permeability**. The vessels become leakier, allowing plasma proteins (including complement, antibodies, and clotting factors) to enter tissue (swelling).
5. **Chemotaxis**. Chemokines attract neutrophils and other immune cells to the site.
6. **Extravasation**. Cells migrate from blood into tissue.
7. **Phagocytosis and pathogen killing**.
8. **Tissue repair**.

The pain comes from the swelling (pressure on nerve endings) and from inflammatory mediators (prostaglandins, bradykinin) directly sensitizing pain receptors. The loss of function is mechanical from swelling and chemical from inflammation interfering with normal tissue activity.

**Acute inflammation** is short-term, beneficial in most cases, and resolves when the pathogen is cleared. **Chronic inflammation** persists for weeks, months, or years. It can result from persistent infection (tuberculosis, fungal infections), autoimmune disease, or other unresolved triggers. Chronic inflammation produces tissue damage — fibrosis, granuloma formation, organ dysfunction.

## Fever

Fever is a systemic inflammatory response. Pyrogens — substances that raise body temperature — come from outside (exogenous) or inside (endogenous).

**Exogenous pyrogens** include bacterial endotoxin (LPS) and other microbial products.

**Endogenous pyrogens** are cytokines released by the immune response — IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α. They act on the hypothalamus to raise the body's temperature set point. The body then generates heat (shivering, increased metabolism, decreased perspiration) to reach the new set point. The patient feels cold and shivers (chills) until the new temperature is achieved.

Why fever exists:

- Many pathogens grow more slowly at elevated temperatures.
- Immune cells function more efficiently at higher temperatures.
- The acute-phase response operates better.
- Iron sequestration (by hepcidin and other regulators) is enhanced, starving pathogens of iron.

Fever is therefore generally beneficial, within limits. Mild to moderate fever (38–40°C) accelerates pathogen clearance. Severe fever (above 41°C) can damage host tissues — denaturing proteins, especially in the brain. Treating fever with antipyretics (acetaminophen, ibuprofen) is controversial in adults with mild infections — it relieves discomfort but may slow pathogen clearance.

## What the chapter is really about

The innate immune system is the work the body does to make adaptive immunity possible. By the time a T cell or B cell is responding to a specific pathogen, the innate system has been engaged for hours or days — detecting the infection, limiting its spread, killing many of the invaders, and signaling the adaptive system to start.

Almost every successful pathogen has evolved specific ways to evade or subvert innate defenses. Capsules to evade phagocytosis. IgA proteases to defeat mucosal antibodies. Stealth strategies to avoid pattern recognition receptors. The arms race between pathogens and innate immunity has been ongoing for hundreds of millions of years and shapes both sides.

For clinical practice, the innate immune system matters because compromise of any part of it produces specific infection susceptibilities. Splenectomy: encapsulated bacterial infections. Neutropenia (low neutrophils from chemotherapy): bacterial and fungal infections. Complement deficiency: *Neisseria*. Chronic granulomatous disease: catalase-positive bacteria. The pattern of infection often points to the immune defect.

## Still puzzling

I do not fully understand why fever evolved with the specific temperature set points it has. The hypothalamic shift to 38–40°C is consistent across mammals and many other vertebrates. The benefit (faster immune response, slower pathogen growth) is plausible. The cost (more energy expended, risk to host tissues at high fevers) is also real. The trade-off should depend on the pathogen, the host, and the environment, yet the response is roughly stereotyped. I am not sure whether the convergence is because the optimal trade-off has narrow margins or because the mechanism is hardwired in ways that prevent fine-tuning.

## What would change my mind

The picture I've given of the innate immune system as relatively fixed at birth has held for decades, but recent work on **trained immunity** suggests that innate cells (particularly macrophages and NK cells) can be "trained" by previous infections to respond more vigorously to subsequent challenges — a form of memory not previously thought to exist in the innate system. If trained immunity turns out to be more important clinically than current data suggest, the line between innate and adaptive immunity will need to be redrawn. `[verify: 2025 status of clinical trained-immunity literature]`
