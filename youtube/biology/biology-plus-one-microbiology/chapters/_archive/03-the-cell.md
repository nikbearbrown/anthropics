# Chapter 03 — The Cell

*Every cell comes from another cell. The exception that proves the rule is the cell that swallowed another and never let go.*

## The question before the answer

For most of recorded history, educated people believed that life appeared from non-life all the time. Maggots emerged from rotting meat. Mice appeared in stored grain. Flies came out of dung. The Greek atomist Anaximander and the philosopher Aristotle wrote about it as a fact of the natural world. The seventeenth-century chemist Jan Baptist van Helmont gave a recipe: put a dirty shirt and some wheat in an open jar, leave it for twenty-one days, and mice will appear.

This was not stupidity. It was the obvious explanation given the available evidence. No one had ever watched a fly lay its eggs in meat. No one knew where mice came from when they appeared overnight. The hypothesis that life emerged spontaneously from suitable matter fit observation perfectly well for two thousand years.

The reason this idea finally died — and the reason it took so long to die — is the subject of the first half of this chapter. It died because three people, across two centuries, designed experiments that closed every loophole defenders of the idea kept opening. The story is a small masterclass in how science actually rules out an alternative.

The second half of the chapter is about what came next: the recognition that every living thing on Earth is made of cells, that every cell comes from another cell, and that one of the most consequential events in evolutionary history was a cell swallowing another cell, failing to digest it, and the descendants of that captured cell becoming your mitochondria. You are, in a meaningful sense, a colony.

## The argument that took two centuries

Spontaneous generation defenders had a strong intuitive position. Maggots really do appear in meat. Bacteria really do appear in broth. To dislodge the idea, you had to design an experiment in which the conditions for spontaneous generation were preserved — air, warmth, nutrients — but in which no life appeared.

### Redi, 1668

Francesco Redi, an Italian physician, set out three jars of meat. The first he left open. The second he covered tightly with a lid. The third he covered with a fine gauze that let air through but kept flies out. Maggots appeared in the first jar (open). None appeared in the second (sealed). On top of the third jar, flies landed on the gauze and laid eggs there; the meat inside remained maggot-free.

Conclusion: maggots come from flies, not from meat. The experiment ruled out one class of spontaneous generation case — visible insects — and showed it was an artifact of unobserved egg-laying.

Spontaneous-generation defenders pivoted. They conceded that visible animals had visible origins, but argued that microbes — the *animalcules* that Leeuwenhoek had just begun describing — still must arise spontaneously, since they appeared in broth that had been heated.

### Spallanzani, 1768

Lazzaro Spallanzani, an Italian priest and natural philosopher, took Redi's experimental design and applied it to microorganisms. He boiled broth in flasks for an hour to kill any microbes inside, then sealed half the flasks and left half open. The sealed flasks remained clear. The open ones grew cloudy with microbial growth.

Defenders pivoted again. They argued that the sealed flasks failed to grow microbes because Spallanzani had killed the "vital force" in the air by boiling. Without that vital force, spontaneous generation could not occur. The defense was now an unfalsifiable claim about an invisible essence — a philosophical move that retreats from evidence-checkable territory.

### Pasteur, 1859

Louis Pasteur designed an experiment whose elegance has not aged. He took swan-neck flasks — flasks with long, curving, S-shaped necks — and filled them with broth. He boiled the broth thoroughly, sterilizing it. The flask's neck was open to the air, so any "vital force" in the air could enter freely. But dust and microbes, being heavier than air, settled in the curve of the neck and never reached the broth.

The flasks remained clear for months. Years, in some cases. (Some of Pasteur's original swan-neck flasks are still in the Pasteur Institute in Paris, still clear, more than 160 years later.)

When Pasteur tipped a flask so the broth touched the dust in the curve, the broth clouded within days. The microorganisms had been right there in the neck, but air alone had no power to generate them.

This was the end of spontaneous generation as a serious scientific position. The biogenesis principle — *omnis cellula e cellula*, "every cell from a cell," in Rudolf Virchow's later phrasing — became the cornerstone of cell biology. Life comes from life.

## Cell theory

Robert Hooke, in 1665, looked at a thin slice of cork through his compound microscope and saw a regular array of empty boxes. He called them *cells* — the Latin word for the small rooms monks lived in — because that is what they resembled. He was looking at the dead cell walls of plant tissue. He did not know they had ever been alive.

Almost two centuries later, two German biologists working separately came to a complementary insight. Matthias Schleiden, in 1838, examined plant tissues under more powerful microscopes and proposed that all plants were composed of cells. Theodor Schwann, in 1839, extended the claim to animals. Together they argued: every living thing is built from cells. [^1]

In 1855, Robert Remak (and later Rudolf Virchow, who often gets sole credit for synthesizing the idea) added a third tenet: cells arise only from other cells. *Omnis cellula e cellula.* No new cells appear by spontaneous generation; every cell traces back, in principle, through an unbroken chain of cell divisions to the first cell.

The three tenets of cell theory, then:

1. All living things are made of cells.
2. The cell is the basic unit of life.
3. Cells come only from other cells.

These propositions look almost trivial today. They were not trivial when they were made. They unified plant, animal, and microbial biology under a single framework and they ruled out an alternative — spontaneous generation — that had dominated scientific thinking since antiquity.

## Germ theory

The connection between microbes and disease was made over the same period. The history is messier than the textbook version makes it look.

**Ignaz Semmelweis** (1818–1865), a Hungarian obstetrician working at Vienna General Hospital in the 1840s, noticed that women who gave birth in the doctors' ward died of puerperal fever at rates of 10–35%, while women who gave birth in the midwives' ward died at rates around 3%. He worked out, with painful clinical detail, that the doctors were coming to deliveries directly from autopsies and were carrying something on their hands. He instituted handwashing with chlorinated lime. Maternal mortality in his ward dropped to under 2%.

The medical establishment refused to believe him. The notion that doctors might be transmitting disease was offensive. Semmelweis was driven from his position, harassed, and eventually committed to an asylum where he died of injuries inflicted by the guards. [^2] His findings were vindicated within twenty years, but the human cost of the establishment's resistance is hard to quantify.

**John Snow** (1813–1858), a London physician, mapped cholera deaths during the 1854 outbreak in Soho and traced them to a single water pump on Broad Street. He removed the pump handle. The outbreak abated. This is now considered the founding act of epidemiology — and we will return to it in Chapter 16 — but Snow's central insight was that cholera was transmitted by something in the water, not by miasma. He had no theory of the agent. He had a map.

**Joseph Lister** (1827–1912), a Scottish surgeon, applied Pasteur's principles to surgery. He used carbolic acid (phenol) to sterilize instruments and wounds. Postoperative infection rates in his ward dropped from around 50% to around 15%. His name now graces a brand of mouthwash, which is a less dignified legacy than he earned.

**Robert Koch**, whom we met in Chapter 1, supplied the formal experimental link between specific microbes and specific diseases. His postulates established the standard.

The combined work of these people produced germ theory: the claim that infectious disease is caused by specific microorganisms transmitted from one host to another. By the end of the nineteenth century, this was the consensus. The miasma theory was gone. So was the vague humoral framework that had dominated Western medicine since Galen.

I want to stress one thing about this history. None of the people in it had a microscope good enough to see the agents they were inferring. Semmelweis did not see the *Streptococcus pyogenes* on the doctors' hands. Snow did not see the *Vibrio cholerae* in the water. They reasoned from patterns of mortality. The microscopic confirmation came later. The reasoning came first.

## Endosymbiotic theory

Now I want to spend a few pages on a single idea, because it is the most important deep mechanism in this chapter and one of the most surprising results in all of biology.

Look at a mitochondrion under high-resolution microscopy. It has two membranes, an outer one and an inner one. Inside the inner membrane is a watery matrix containing DNA — circular DNA, like a bacterium's, not the linear nuclear DNA. The mitochondrion has its own ribosomes, smaller than the ribosomes in the rest of the cell, and structurally very similar to bacterial ribosomes. It reproduces by binary fission, like a bacterium, on its own schedule, separate from cell division. Antibiotics that target bacterial protein synthesis — chloramphenicol, tetracyclines — also inhibit mitochondrial protein synthesis. This is why some antibiotics have side effects in mammals.

Each of those observations, on its own, is curious. Together, they suggest that mitochondria are not just organelles. They are the descendants of a free-living bacterium that was engulfed by an ancestor of all modern eukaryotic cells, billions of years ago, and never digested.

This idea was proposed in 1967 by Lynn Margulis, then a young biologist at Boston University, in a paper that almost every journal she submitted it to had rejected. The eventual publication, in the *Journal of Theoretical Biology*, was the founding document of what is now called endosymbiotic theory. [^3]

Here is the proposed history. About 1.5 to 2 billion years ago, a large anaerobic archaeal cell — a single-celled organism that did not use oxygen — engulfed a smaller aerobic bacterium. The engulfed bacterium was not digested. Possibly it was protected from digestion by some quirk of its outer membrane. Whatever the reason, it survived inside the larger cell, and the two cells turned out to be useful to each other.

The smaller bacterium had something the host cell did not: a respiratory chain that could extract energy from oxygen. Oxygen, at this point in Earth's history, was a relatively new presence in the atmosphere — produced by cyanobacterial photosynthesis over the preceding billion years — and was actually toxic to most existing life. A cell that could metabolize oxygen safely had a tremendous energetic advantage. The host cell, by harboring such a bacterium, got access to oxygen-based metabolism without having to invent it.

Over evolutionary time, the bacterium became a permanent resident. It lost the genes it no longer needed (much of its DNA migrated to the host nucleus or was lost entirely). It became unable to live outside the host. The host became unable to live without it. The two lineages merged into one. The resulting hybrid cell — with its captured bacterial passenger doing the energy work — was the ancestor of every plant, animal, fungus, and protist on Earth. Your every breath is metabolized by descendants of a bacterium your ancestor ate two billion years ago.

A similar event, somewhat later, produced chloroplasts. A eukaryotic cell engulfed a cyanobacterium and failed to digest it. The captured cyanobacterium retained its photosynthetic apparatus. Its descendants are the chloroplasts of every plant and algal cell on Earth, doing the photosynthesis. Chloroplasts also have two membranes, circular DNA, and bacterial-style ribosomes.

The evidence for endosymbiosis is now overwhelming. Mitochondrial DNA and chloroplast DNA have been sequenced. Their ribosomal RNA places them clearly within the bacterial domain — not within the eukaryotic lineage. Mitochondria are closest to a group called the alphaproteobacteria (specifically related to *Rickettsia*). Chloroplasts are clearly cyanobacterial. Lynn Margulis was right; she just had to be right for thirty years before the field admitted it.

There is one extraordinary implication. Eukaryotic cells are not a single ancestral lineage. They are a chimera — a fusion of two prokaryotic lineages, with all the additional complexity (nucleus, endomembrane system, cytoskeleton) built on top of that fusion. The split into three domains we discussed in Chapter 1 is real, but Eukarya is the only domain that arose by combining members of the other two.

This is the most interesting fact in cell biology, and almost no introductory textbook explains it as the unifying idea it is. I want you to carry it forward.

## What's inside a prokaryotic cell

Prokaryotic cells lack a nucleus. Their DNA sits in a region called the *nucleoid*, which is not bounded by a membrane but is distinct from the surrounding cytoplasm. The DNA is a single circular chromosome, supercoiled to fit into the cell. Most bacteria carry, in addition to the chromosome, small circular extrachromosomal DNA molecules called *plasmids*. Plasmids often carry genes for antibiotic resistance, virulence, or metabolic capabilities not present in the chromosome — and they can be transferred between bacteria, which is one of the main reasons antibiotic resistance spreads as fast as it does. We will return to plasmids in Chapter 11.

Bacterial **ribosomes** are smaller than eukaryotic ribosomes (designated 70S, where S is a Svedberg unit of sedimentation in a centrifuge). The 70S vs 80S distinction is the chemistry that makes many antibiotics selective for bacteria — they bind to 70S ribosomes and not 80S, so they kill bacteria but not the human cells they live in.

Some bacteria contain **inclusions** — granules of stored material (sulfur, polyphosphate, lipids, glycogen, magnetite). Magnetotactic bacteria have inclusions of magnetite that align them with Earth's magnetic field, an evolutionary trick whose evolutionary purpose is still debated.

**Endospores** are a survival strategy. Some bacteria (*Bacillus*, *Clostridium*) can, when nutrients run out, form a dormant structure inside the parent cell — a small, dehydrated, highly resistant capsule containing a copy of the genome and a minimal set of proteins. The endospore can survive heat, radiation, chemicals, and centuries of starvation. When conditions improve, the endospore germinates back into an active cell. *Bacillus anthracis* endospores can persist in soil for decades. *Clostridium botulinum* endospores in improperly canned food are the source of botulism. *Clostridioides difficile* endospores in hospitals are the source of an infection we will return to in Chapter 24.

The **plasma membrane** is a lipid bilayer — phospholipid heads facing outward toward water on both sides, hydrophobic tails facing inward toward each other. The bilayer is selectively permeable; small uncharged molecules can cross, charged molecules need transporters. Embedded in the membrane are protein machines that pump ions, transport nutrients, sense the environment, and in some bacteria, conduct photosynthesis.

The **cell wall** sits outside the plasma membrane. In bacteria it is built from peptidoglycan — chains of sugars cross-linked by short peptide bridges. The peptidoglycan is what makes the cell wall rigid and what lets the cell resist osmotic pressure. (Bacterial cytoplasm is typically much more concentrated than the surrounding medium; without a wall, the cell would swell and burst.) Gram-positive bacteria have a thick peptidoglycan layer; Gram-negative bacteria have a thin one between two membranes, with the outer membrane containing lipopolysaccharide (LPS) — the molecule that triggers endotoxic shock in humans. We will return to LPS in Chapter 15.

Around the wall, many bacteria have a **glycocalyx** — a polysaccharide capsule or slime layer. Capsules help cells stick to surfaces, evade immune detection, and resist desiccation. They are major virulence factors for organisms like *Streptococcus pneumoniae*; vaccines against these bacteria often target the capsule.

Some bacteria have **filamentous appendages**. *Fimbriae* are short, hair-like structures that help cells adhere to surfaces. *Pili* are longer and more specialized; sex pili form bridges for conjugation (DNA transfer between cells), which we will detail in Chapter 11. *Flagella* are long whip-like filaments that rotate, driven by a motor embedded in the cell membrane, propelling the cell through liquid. The flagellar motor is one of the few rotary engines that biology has invented, and it is structurally beautiful — we will not have space to do it justice here.

## What's inside a eukaryotic cell

Eukaryotic cells are bigger and more complicated. The defining feature is the **nucleus**: a double-membraned compartment that contains the linear chromosomes, the nucleolus (where ribosomal RNA is made), and the machinery for transcription. The nuclear envelope has pores that selectively allow molecules in and out. The compartmentalization is the reason eukaryotes can run transcription and translation as separate processes — transcription happens in the nucleus, translation in the cytoplasm — while in bacteria these processes are coupled.

The **endomembrane system** is a network of membranes that synthesizes, modifies, and traffics proteins. The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) does the initial synthesis and modification. Rough ER (studded with ribosomes) makes proteins destined for membranes or for secretion. Smooth ER makes lipids and detoxifies metabolites. The Golgi apparatus, a stack of flattened membrane sacs, modifies and sorts proteins from the ER and packages them in vesicles for delivery to their final destinations. Lysosomes are membrane-bound bags of digestive enzymes that break down material the cell has taken in or material the cell wants to recycle.

**Peroxisomes** handle oxidative reactions that would be dangerous if done in the cytoplasm. They produce hydrogen peroxide as a byproduct and contain catalase to neutralize it.

The **cytoskeleton** is a network of protein filaments that gives the cell shape, organizes its contents, and powers cellular movement. Three classes of filaments: microtubules (made of tubulin, the largest, used for cargo transport and chromosome separation in mitosis), actin filaments (the smallest, used for cell shape and crawling motility), and intermediate filaments (in between, mostly structural).

**Mitochondria** — see the endosymbiosis section above — produce most of the cell's ATP through oxidative phosphorylation. We will revisit them in Chapter 8.

**Chloroplasts** — also see above — perform photosynthesis in plants and algae.

The **plasma membrane** is again a lipid bilayer, similar in principle to the bacterial one but with cholesterol (in animals) or sterols (in fungi and plants) interspersed to modulate fluidity.

Eukaryotic **cell walls** are present in plants, fungi, and algae, but their chemistry differs by group. Plant walls are mostly cellulose. Fungal walls are mostly chitin. Animal cells have no wall — only a plasma membrane — which is why animal tissues are soft.

Some eukaryotic cells have **flagella** or **cilia**. Eukaryotic flagella are structurally completely different from bacterial flagella, despite the shared name. They are bundles of microtubules in a characteristic 9+2 arrangement, powered by motor proteins that slide microtubules past each other. Sperm cells use flagella. Tracheal cilia move mucus up out of the lungs. The same basic machinery, in different deployment.

## What the chapter is really about

A cell is the smallest unit that can do all of life by itself. Smaller than a cell, you have biochemistry but no agency — a protein cannot reproduce on its own; a ribosome cannot acquire energy from the environment; a virus needs a host. A cell can do all of it. Membrane, genome, metabolism, replication, response to stimuli — packed into one self-sustaining unit between 1 μm and 100 μm across.

But the cell itself is a hybrid. Look at any eukaryotic cell and you are looking at a structure that contains the genomes of two ancient lineages — the nuclear genome from one ancestor, the mitochondrial genome from another. The animal you are looking out from this textbook is, by molecular weight, perhaps 10% bacterial descendant. The cells doing your thinking are running their energy economy on captured bacterial machinery.

That is the right way to remember cell theory. *Omnis cellula e cellula* is true. But for eukaryotes the chain of cells running back through time is two chains, briefly joined, then permanently merged. The simplest correct picture of life is not a tree of separated branches. It is a tree with a few crucial knots where lineages combined and then continued together.

## Still puzzling

I do not fully understand why eukaryotic cells, once they arose, did not arise *again*. Endosymbiosis on the scale that produced mitochondria appears to have happened essentially once in Earth's history. (Chloroplast endosymbiosis happened later, possibly more than once in the more recent past, but the original mitochondrial event seems to be unique.) The conditions seem like they should have recurred — there is no shortage of cells of different sizes meeting each other in modern oceans. Why is the major endosymbiotic merger a once-only event? It would tell us something interesting about evolution if we could answer this clearly.

## What would change my mind

The story I have told about endosymbiosis is widely accepted but rests heavily on phylogenetic placement of mitochondrial rRNA within alphaproteobacteria. If a closer bacterial ancestor were identified — or if mitochondria turned out to derive from a deeper branch — the timing and the dynamics of the original merger would need revision. The same goes for the proposal that the host cell was archaeal rather than a primitive eukaryote already in possession of a cytoskeleton.
