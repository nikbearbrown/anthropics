# Chapter 15 — Microbial Mechanisms of Pathogenicity

*Most bacteria are harmless. The ones that aren't have specific tools for being harmful.*

## The question before the answer

You carry, at any moment, somewhere between 10 and 100 trillion bacterial cells on and in your body. They are on your skin, in your mouth, in your nose, in your gut, on your eyes, in your urogenital tract. Most of them are harmless. Many of them are beneficial. A few are potentially dangerous but kept under control by the rest. Almost none, on a given day, are actually causing you a disease.

When a microbe does cause disease, the question is *why this microbe, at this moment, in this person.* The same *E. coli* that has lived peacefully in your gut for decades can become deadly if it enters your bloodstream. The same *Staphylococcus aureus* that colonizes one in three people's noses can kill if it gets into a surgical wound. The same *Streptococcus pneumoniae* that may briefly visit your throat with no symptoms can cause pneumonia, meningitis, or sepsis given the right circumstances.

Pathogenicity is not a fixed property of an organism. It is the outcome of an interaction between specific bacterial traits (virulence factors), specific host conditions (immune status, integrity of barriers, presence of medical devices), and specific events (exposure, breach, immune compromise). The point of this chapter is to map out what the bacterial traits are — the tools by which a microbe causes disease — and how they combine to produce the clinical syndromes you will meet in Chapters 21–26.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

1. Distinguish signs from symptoms and use the correct terms in case descriptions.
2. State Koch's postulates and identify three classes of pathogen for which they do not apply.
3. Define pathogenicity, virulence, ID50, and LD50 in terms of measurable quantities.
4. List the major virulence factor categories (adhesion, immune evasion, exotoxin, endotoxin, intracellular invasion) and identify which bacterial species use which.
5. Describe the structural and functional difference between exotoxins and endotoxins and predict the clinical syndromes each produces.
6. Identify the major virulence factors of fungi, protozoa, and helminths.

Prerequisites: Chapters 1–14.

## Signs versus symptoms

Worth getting right early: a **sign** is something observable by an examining clinician (rash, fever measured by a thermometer, abnormal lung sounds, swollen lymph nodes). A **symptom** is something reported by the patient (pain, nausea, fatigue, dizziness). Fever is a sign if you measured it; chills are a symptom because the patient describes them.

Diseases are characterized by their signs and symptoms, and the differential diagnosis often starts from there. A patient presenting with productive cough, fever, and pleuritic chest pain is presenting with three findings — two symptoms and one sign — that together point toward bacterial pneumonia and away from heart attack.

A **syndrome** is a constellation of signs and symptoms that occur together and have predictive value, even when the underlying cause is uncertain or variable. Septic shock is a syndrome (fever or hypothermia plus low blood pressure plus altered mental status plus laboratory evidence of organ dysfunction). The cause might be *E. coli* bacteremia, gram-positive endocarditis, or fungemia. The syndrome is the same; the etiology varies.

## Categories of infectious disease

**Communicable** diseases pass from person to person. Influenza, tuberculosis, COVID-19, gonorrhea, measles.

**Noncommunicable** infectious diseases come from an environmental source rather than another person. Tetanus from a soil-contaminated wound. Legionnaires' disease from a contaminated cooling tower. Most fungal infections.

**Contagious** is a subset of communicable — diseases that pass particularly easily. Chickenpox is contagious; HIV is not, despite being communicable.

**Iatrogenic** ("doctor-caused") diseases result from medical intervention. Catheter-associated urinary tract infections. Surgical site infections. *C. difficile* colitis induced by antibiotic therapy.

**Nosocomial** (or healthcare-associated) infections are acquired in a healthcare setting. Defined as those that develop more than 48 hours after admission. About 1 in 25 hospitalized patients in the US has a nosocomial infection at any given time.

**Zoonotic** diseases are transmitted from animals to humans. Rabies, Lyme disease, brucellosis, plague, COVID-19 (originally), Ebola.

These categories are useful because they predict transmission and risk. A hospital infection control team thinks about nosocomial infections differently from an outpatient clinic dealing with community-acquired infections, and both think differently from a public health office tracking foodborne outbreaks.

## Stages of disease

Acute infectious diseases generally progress through five stages:

1. **Incubation period**: time from exposure to onset of symptoms. The pathogen is replicating but has not yet produced enough disease to be noticeable. Can range from hours (food poisoning from preformed toxin) to decades (HIV to AIDS).

2. **Prodromal period**: early, mild symptoms before the disease is fully developed. Often vague — fatigue, low fever, headache. The patient feels something is wrong but can't pinpoint it.

3. **Period of illness** (acute phase): full clinical disease. Maximum symptoms. Pathogen often at peak numbers. Diagnostic specimens are most likely to be positive during this period.

4. **Period of decline**: symptoms diminish as the immune system controls the infection (and/or treatment takes effect). The patient is improving but may still be contagious.

5. **Period of convalescence**: recovery, with residual weakness and gradual return to baseline. Some pathogens (e.g., *Salmonella*) continue to be shed during this period, even after symptoms resolve. The patient is recovering; the public is still at risk.

For chronic infections (TB, HIV, hepatitis B and C, untreated syphilis), the pattern is different — periods of latency, reactivation, and progressive damage that can extend over decades.

## Koch's postulates, in detail and in limitation

We met Koch's postulates briefly in Chapter 1. Here they are again:

1. The microorganism must be found in abundance in all organisms suffering from the disease, and not found in healthy organisms.
2. The microorganism must be isolated from the diseased organism and grown in pure culture.
3. The cultured microorganism should cause the same disease when introduced to a healthy organism.
4. The microorganism must be recovered from the inoculated diseased experimental host.

These are an exceptionally clear statement of what it would mean to claim that a microbe causes a disease. They worked beautifully for the diseases of Koch's era — anthrax, tuberculosis, cholera — where the relationship between pathogen and disease was relatively direct.

**What to do with the output:** Hill's criteria are the workhorse of modern medical epidemiology. They are not perfect; they are the framework most public health decisions actually use.

They fail, predictably, in several common situations:

**Pathogens that cannot be cultured.** *Mycobacterium leprae* (leprosy) cannot be cultured in standard media; it has only been reliably grown in armadillos and mouse footpads. *Treponema pallidum* (syphilis) cannot be cultured in vitro. *Chlamydia* species are obligate intracellular parasites and cannot grow on agar plates. None of these organisms pass Postulate 2.

**Asymptomatic carriers.** Many pathogens are carried by healthy people who never develop disease. *Neisseria meningitidis* lives harmlessly in the throats of 5–25% of healthy people. *Streptococcus pneumoniae* colonizes the nose of about 25% of children. Postulate 1 (not found in healthy organisms) fails for nearly every pathogen that has asymptomatic carriers.

**Diseases with multiple causes.** Pneumonia is caused by several different bacteria, viruses, and fungi. The same patient may have polymicrobial infection. Postulate 1 cannot apply to a disease defined by syndrome rather than etiology.

**Pathogens that cause different diseases.** *Streptococcus pyogenes* causes pharyngitis, scarlet fever, rheumatic fever, necrotizing fasciitis, and toxic shock syndrome, depending on the strain, site, and host. The same organism, different diseases.

**Ethics**. Postulate 3 requires deliberately infecting a healthy host. This is unethical for human diseases, so animal models are used as proxies, with the obvious caveat that animal disease may not perfectly mirror human disease.

To address these limitations, **molecular Koch's postulates** (formulated by Stanley Falkow in 1988) were proposed:

1. The phenotype or property under investigation should be associated with pathogenic members of a genus or pathogenic strains of a species.
2. Specific inactivation of the gene(s) associated with the suspected virulence trait should lead to a measurable loss in pathogenicity or virulence.
3. Reversion or allelic replacement of the mutated gene should lead to restoration of pathogenicity.

These postulates apply to specific virulence genes within an organism, rather than to whole organisms causing disease. They are appropriate to modern molecular microbiology and can be applied to organisms (HIV, *M. leprae*) for which traditional Koch's postulates fail. [^1]

## Pathogenicity and virulence

**Pathogenicity** is the ability of a microbe to cause disease.

**Virulence** is the degree of pathogenicity — how severe the disease, how many cells are needed to cause it, how often infection leads to clinical disease.

A **primary pathogen** is a microbe capable of causing disease in a healthy host. *M. tuberculosis*, *Yersinia pestis*, *Bordetella pertussis*, rabies virus.

An **opportunistic pathogen** causes disease only when host defenses are compromised. *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* causes serious infections in cystic fibrosis patients, burn patients, and immunocompromised patients, but rarely causes disease in healthy people. *Candida albicans* lives in many healthy people without causing disease, but causes serious infections in immunocompromised hosts. The same organism, two completely different clinical profiles.

The distinction matters: a primary pathogen isolated from a patient is almost always significant. An opportunistic pathogen isolated from a patient may be a contaminant, normal flora, or a real pathogen, depending on context.

### Quantifying virulence

**ID50** (infectious dose 50%) is the number of organisms required to cause infection in 50% of exposed people. **LD50** (lethal dose 50%) is the number that causes death in 50%.

These vary enormously by pathogen. *Shigella dysenteriae* has an ID50 around 10 organisms — a few cells are enough to cause dysentery. *Vibrio cholerae* requires about 10⁸ organisms — the stomach acid kills most of them, so you need a large dose. *Bacillus anthracis* inhalation anthrax has an ID50 of 8,000–50,000 spores.

The clinical implication: a pathogen with a low ID50 is dangerous in small quantities. *Shigella* outbreaks can spread by hand-to-hand contact with minimal contamination. *V. cholerae* requires substantial contamination of food or water and is less efficiently transmitted person-to-person. Knowing the ID50 helps predict transmission dynamics.

## Stages of pathogenesis

For a microbe to cause disease, it has to:

1. **Enter the host** — through a portal of entry.
2. **Attach** to host cells — usually via specific adhesion factors.
3. **Invade** — penetrate tissues, possibly enter cells.
4. **Multiply** — at the site or elsewhere.
5. **Evade** host defenses — avoid being killed by the immune system.
6. **Cause damage** — through toxins, immune responses, or physical effects.
7. **Exit** — through a portal of exit, to spread to a new host.

Each step requires specific bacterial capabilities (virulence factors). We will go through them in roughly that order.

## Portals of entry and exit

The body has natural barriers — skin, mucous membranes, the stomach's acidity, normal flora — that prevent most microbes from entering. Pathogens have specific portals they can use.

**Skin**: usually a strong barrier, but can be breached by cuts, burns, surgical wounds, needle sticks, or specific microbes that can penetrate intact skin (*Schistosoma*, certain dermatophytes). Hookworm larvae enter through unbroken skin of the feet.

**Mucous membranes** of the respiratory, gastrointestinal, urogenital, and ocular tracts: easier to penetrate than skin. The mucous membranes have their own defenses (mucus, ciliated cells, antimicrobial peptides), but they are thinner and more permeable than skin.

**Parenteral**: anything that bypasses normal barriers — injection, transfusion, organ transplant, indwelling catheter, surgery.

**Vertical** transmission: mother to fetus or newborn. Across the placenta, during birth, or through breast milk. Important for some pathogens — congenital rubella, congenital toxoplasmosis, neonatal HSV, mother-to-child HIV transmission.

The portal of exit is often the same as the portal of entry, mirroring the route of transmission. Respiratory pathogens enter and exit through respiratory droplets. GI pathogens enter and exit through feces. Sexually transmitted pathogens enter and exit through urogenital secretions. The route can also differ — rabies enters through a bite (skin breach) but exits through saliva (mucous membrane / bite).

## Adhesion

Most pathogens must attach to specific host cells before they can colonize. Without attachment, the microbe gets washed away by mucus, cleared by ciliary action, or flushed out by urine flow.

Adhesion is mediated by **adhesins** — specific bacterial molecules that bind to specific host cell receptors. The receptor specificity determines tissue tropism. A pathogen that adheres to receptors found on respiratory epithelium will cause respiratory disease; the same pathogen might be unable to colonize the gut.

Common adhesin types:

- **Fimbriae and pili**: short hair-like protein structures on the bacterial surface. *E. coli* uropathogenic strains have P fimbriae that bind specific glycolipids on bladder epithelium, enabling urinary tract infection. Without P fimbriae, the bacteria are washed away by urine before they can establish.
- **Lipoteichoic acid**: in Gram-positives, this anchors to the cell wall and binds host fibronectin.
- **Capsules**: polysaccharide layers that mediate attachment to surfaces and also provide immune evasion. *Streptococcus pneumoniae*'s capsule allows attachment to respiratory epithelium.
- **Viral attachment proteins**: each virus has specific spike or envelope proteins that bind specific host cell receptors. SARS-CoV-2 spike binds ACE2; HIV gp120 binds CD4.

Antiadhesion therapy — drugs that prevent attachment without killing the microbe — is an active research area. The advantage in principle: no selective pressure for resistance, because the bacteria aren't being killed.

## Immune evasion

Once attached, a pathogen has to survive immune attack. Several common evasion mechanisms:

**Capsules**. Polysaccharide capsules resist phagocytosis. Phagocytes (macrophages, neutrophils) cannot easily engulf cells with thick capsules. The capsule of *S. pneumoniae* is the major virulence determinant; encapsulated strains cause disease, unencapsulated strains do not. Capsules are also poorly immunogenic on their own and can mimic host carbohydrates to escape immune detection.

**Antigenic variation**. Some pathogens periodically change their surface antigens, escaping the antibody response that was directed at the previous antigens. *Trypanosoma brucei*, the African sleeping sickness parasite, has hundreds of variant surface glycoprotein genes that it switches between, presenting a moving target to the immune system. *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* varies its surface pilin proteins. Influenza accumulates antigenic mutations through antigenic drift and dramatic changes through antigenic shift (reassortment).

**Intracellular hiding**. Some pathogens replicate inside host cells, where antibodies cannot reach them. *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* lives inside macrophages — the very cells designed to kill it. *Salmonella* enters intestinal cells. *Listeria* hijacks actin to move from one cell to another without ever leaving cells. *Chlamydia* is obligately intracellular.

**Cleaving antibodies**. Some pathogens produce **IgA proteases** that cleave secretory IgA in mucous membranes, preventing the antibodies from binding. *Neisseria*, *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Haemophilus influenzae*.

**Antigen mimicry**. Some pathogens produce surface molecules that resemble host molecules, escaping immune recognition. Group A *Streptococcus* has surface antigens similar to human cardiac myosin and connective tissue — when the immune system responds to the streptococcus, the antibodies can cross-react with heart tissue, causing rheumatic fever.

**Coagulase and other clotting manipulation**. *Staphylococcus aureus* coagulase converts fibrinogen to fibrin, forming a clot around the bacterium that protects it from immune cells. Staphylokinase later dissolves the clot to spread the infection.

## Exotoxins

**Exotoxins** are proteins secreted by bacteria. They are typically extremely potent — small amounts cause large effects — and highly specific in their cellular targets.

The major exotoxin categories:

**AB toxins** have two functional parts. The B subunit binds to host cells; the A subunit is the active enzyme that does the damage. Diphtheria toxin (ADP-ribosylates elongation factor 2, stopping protein synthesis), cholera toxin (ADP-ribosylates a G protein, causing massive chloride and water secretion into the gut), pertussis toxin, anthrax toxin, botulinum toxin, tetanus toxin. The A-B architecture is repeatedly evolved by different bacteria.

**Membrane-disrupting toxins** create holes in host cell membranes. *Streptococcus pyogenes* streptolysin O. *Staphylococcus aureus* alpha-toxin. *Listeria monocytogenes* listeriolysin O. Phospholipase C from *Clostridium perfringens* (causes gas gangrene). The result: cells lyse, tissues necrose.

**Superantigens** force unregulated activation of large numbers of T cells, producing massive cytokine release. The clinical result is toxic shock — fever, hypotension, organ failure. *Staphylococcus aureus* toxic shock syndrome toxin (TSST-1), staphylococcal enterotoxins, streptococcal pyrogenic exotoxins (SpeA, SpeC). The connection between superantigens and toxic shock was worked out partly through the menstrual TSS outbreaks of the early 1980s (associated with high-absorbency tampons).

Specific toxins with specific clinical effects:

- **Botulinum toxin** (BoNT) from *C. botulinum* is one of the most potent toxins known by weight — the LD50 in humans is about 1 ng per kilogram. It blocks acetylcholine release at neuromuscular junctions, causing flaccid paralysis. It is also clinically useful at extremely low doses (Botox) for various neurological and cosmetic conditions.
- **Tetanus toxin** (TeNT) from *C. tetani* blocks inhibitory neurotransmitter release in the CNS, causing spastic paralysis. Tetanus and botulism are mirror images at the molecular level: both block neurotransmitter release, but in opposite types of neurons.
- **Shiga toxin** from *Shigella dysenteriae* and **Shiga-like toxin** from *E. coli* O157:H7 inhibit protein synthesis and damage kidney endothelium, causing hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS), a leading cause of acute kidney failure in children.
- **Cholera toxin** causes a massive secretion of chloride into the intestinal lumen, with water following osmotically. The patient loses liters of fluid per day. Untreated cholera can be fatal within hours from dehydration.

## Endotoxins

**Endotoxin** is the lipopolysaccharide (LPS) of the Gram-negative outer membrane. It is **lipid A**, the inner anchor of LPS, that is the toxic component.

Endotoxin differs fundamentally from exotoxins:

- Endotoxin is part of the bacterium itself, not secreted.
- It is a lipid, not a protein.
- It is heat-stable.
- It is released when bacteria die or are killed, not actively.
- The clinical effects are general rather than specific.

When endotoxin enters the bloodstream — usually because Gram-negative bacteria are dying there — it triggers a massive inflammatory response. Macrophages release IL-1, TNF-α, and other cytokines. The cascade produces fever, drop in blood pressure, vascular leak, organ dysfunction. At high levels, it produces septic shock and disseminated intravascular coagulation.

## Genotypes of virulence

The genes encoding virulence factors are often clustered in **pathogenicity islands** — regions of the chromosome that look distinct from the rest of the genome (different GC content, flanked by mobile element sequences), suggesting they were acquired by horizontal gene transfer at some point.

Some virulence factors are encoded on plasmids. The toxin genes for *Bacillus anthracis* are on plasmids; cure the bacterium of the plasmid and it loses its lethality. Some are encoded by prophages (Chapter 6): diphtheria toxin is encoded by the corynephage; cholera toxin by the CTX phage; some Shiga toxins by Stx phage.

The implication: virulence is not a fixed property of a species. It is encoded by specific genes that can move between strains and species via horizontal transfer. A new pathogenic strain can emerge by acquiring virulence genes from an existing pathogen — which is one route for the emergence of new pathogenic *E. coli* strains, new virulent *Streptococcus*, and so on.

## Virulence in non-bacterial pathogens

### Fungi

Fungal virulence factors include:

- **Capsules**. *Cryptococcus neoformans* has a polysaccharide capsule that resists phagocytosis.
- **Cell wall components**. Beta-glucans trigger inflammatory responses.
- **Adhesins**. *Candida albicans* has adhesion factors that bind to host epithelial cells.
- **Hyphal switching**. *C. albicans* can switch between yeast and hyphal forms; the hyphal form is more invasive.
- **Melanin production**. Some fungi (*C. neoformans*, *Aspergillus fumigatus*) produce melanin that helps resist oxidative stress from phagocytes.
- **Toxins**. Aflatoxins from *Aspergillus flavus* are carcinogenic; not pathogenicity in the classical sense, but disease-causing.

### Protozoa

Major virulence factors:

- **Adherence**. *Entamoeba histolytica* uses a lectin that binds galactose residues on intestinal epithelium.
- **Tissue invasion**. *E. histolytica* secretes pore-forming proteins (amoebapores) that lyse host cells.
- **Antigenic variation**. *Trypanosoma brucei* surface glycoproteins change to evade antibodies.
- **Immune modulation**. *Toxoplasma gondii* can suppress immune responses and persist long-term.
- **Intracellular life cycles**. *Plasmodium* hides inside red blood cells; *Leishmania* inside macrophages.

### Helminths

Helminths are large; they cannot hide intracellularly. They evade immune attack through:

- **Cuticles and tegumental surfaces** that resist immune attack.
- **Immune modulation**. Many helminths secrete molecules that downregulate the host's inflammatory responses (this is partly why the hygiene hypothesis suggests that helminth exposure may protect against autoimmune disease).
- **Antigenic mimicry**.
- **Long-lived life cycles** that simply outlast aggressive immune responses.

## What the chapter is really about

Pathogenicity is an outcome of an interaction, not a fixed property. A microbe is pathogenic in a particular host, under particular conditions, deploying particular tools. The same microbe in a different host or different state may be commensal or absent.

The virulence factor framework lets you reason about pathogens systematically. When you meet a new pathogen, ask: how does it get in? How does it adhere? How does it evade the immune system? How does it cause damage? How does it spread to the next host? The answers will tell you both the clinical picture and the targets for intervention.

It is worth noticing that virulence often comes at a cost. The most virulent strains often have shorter transmission windows (they kill the host before it can spread them) and are less efficiently transmitted. *Yersinia pestis* during the Black Death killed perhaps 50% of those infected within days — efficient for short-term spread, terrible for long-term persistence. Modern variants are less lethal in part because intermediate virulence transmits better. The evolutionary equilibrium of pathogen virulence is shaped by transmission dynamics as much as by host immunity. We will come back to this in Chapter 16.

## LLM exercises

1. **Building a pathogen.** Tell the LLM: "Imagine a hypothetical bacterium that you are designing to cause a respiratory infection. List the virulence factors it would need at each stage (entry, adhesion, evasion, damage, exit) and explain how each would contribute." Then ask which existing pathogen most closely matches your design.
2. **Why Koch's postulates fail.** Pick three pathogens that fail one or more of Koch's postulates (*M. leprae*, HIV, *Helicobacter pylori* are candidates). For each, ask the LLM to identify which postulate fails and why, and what modification of the postulates would let you still argue causation. Compare to molecular Koch's postulates.
3. **AB toxin mechanisms.** Ask the LLM to describe how diphtheria toxin enters a cell, what its A subunit does inside, and how its B subunit determines tissue tropism. Then ask whether the same mechanism could be used against any target — that is, could you, in principle, replace the B subunit with one binding a different receptor and convert the toxin into a targeted drug? (This is the basis of immunotoxin research.)
4. **Endotoxin vs exotoxin clinical picture.** Give the LLM two clinical scenarios — one of a patient with septic shock from Gram-negative bacteremia, one with toxic shock syndrome from staphylococcal superantigen — and ask it to compare what the patient looks like, what laboratory values would distinguish, and what treatments differ. The mechanism (endotoxin vs exotoxin) drives differences in clinical presentation.
5. **The opportunism question.** Ask the LLM why *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* is so frequently a pathogen in cystic fibrosis patients and so rarely a pathogen in healthy adults. The answer should involve specific defects in the CF airway (thick mucus, defective mucociliary clearance, altered surface chemistry) interacting with the bacterium's virulence factors. The point is that opportunism is interaction, not the bacterium being more or less dangerous.
