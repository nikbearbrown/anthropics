# Chapter 24 — Digestive System Infections

*The gut is the largest single immune organ in the body. It contains more bacteria than the body has cells. Most of the time, it works.*

## The question before the answer

You spend roughly six hours a day digesting. The food you eat is broken down by enzymes and acid, mixed with bile, absorbed across an intestinal surface area of roughly 30 square meters, and the remainder is shaped into stool and expelled. Throughout this process, the gut is colonized by an enormous and continuously active microbial community — 100 trillion bacteria, contributing more genes to your physiology than your own genome contributes. The community is dense, diverse, and intimately involved in nutrition, immune development, and even mood.

The microbiota's daily job is enormous. It also keeps potential pathogens in check by colonization resistance. When the balance is disrupted — by antibiotics, by acid suppression, by a new pathogen that the existing community cannot exclude — the gut can become a site of severe disease. Diarrheal disease is one of the leading causes of childhood death globally. Foodborne illness affects perhaps a billion people a year. The pathogens range from viral gastroenteritis that resolves in a day to *Vibrio cholerae* that can kill within hours.

The point of this chapter is to map the spectrum of digestive system infections by site (mouth vs intestine), by mechanism (infection vs intoxication), and by pathogen class. I will spend particular attention on a few cases — *Helicobacter pylori* and ulcers, *Clostridioides difficile* and post-antibiotic colitis, *Vibrio cholerae* and the simplest miracle of public health — because they illustrate principles that apply across the chapter.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

1. Describe the anatomy and normal microbiota of the digestive system.
2. Distinguish foodborne infection from foodborne intoxication and predict symptom timing for each.
3. Identify the most common bacterial GI pathogens and their characteristic clinical pictures.
4. Identify the major viral, protozoan, and helminthic pathogens of the GI tract.
5. Describe oral cavity infections, particularly dental caries and periodontal disease.

Prerequisites: Chapters 1–23.

## Digestive system anatomy and microbiota

The digestive tract runs from mouth to anus, with characteristic microbiota at each site:

**Mouth**: anaerobes and facultative anaerobes. *Streptococcus*, *Lactobacillus*, *Actinomyces*, *Bacteroides*. The dental plaque community is dense and biofilm-organized.

**Esophagus**: transient bacteria from swallowing. Very low resident microbiota.

**Stomach**: low pH (1–3) keeps the population low. *Helicobacter pylori* is the major resident in people who carry it (a substantial fraction of humanity).

**Small intestine**: progressively more microbes from duodenum to ileum. *Lactobacillus*, *Streptococcus*, then more *Bacteroides* and anaerobes distally.

**Large intestine (colon)**: the densest microbial community in the body. 10¹¹–10¹² cells per gram of contents. *Bacteroides*, *Firmicutes* (*Faecalibacterium*, *Ruminococcus*), *Bifidobacterium*, *Akkermansia*. Most are obligate anaerobes.

The colonic microbiota is responsible for:

- **Fiber digestion**: breakdown of complex polysaccharides that human enzymes cannot handle, producing short-chain fatty acids (acetate, propionate, butyrate) that nourish colon cells.
- **Vitamin production**: vitamin K, several B vitamins.
- **Colonization resistance**: preventing pathogen establishment by competing for nutrients and niches.
- **Immune education**: the gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) develops in response to the microbiota; germ-free animals have abnormal immune systems.
- **Drug metabolism**: gut bacteria modify many drugs, sometimes altering efficacy or toxicity.

## Oral cavity infections

### Dental caries

Tooth decay. The mechanism is well understood: bacteria in dental plaque ferment dietary sugars to acid; acid demineralizes tooth enamel; over time, holes form. *Streptococcus mutans* is the dominant cariogenic species — it adheres to tooth surfaces, ferments sucrose efficiently, produces lactic acid, and tolerates the acidic microenvironment it creates.

Prevention: fluoride (strengthens enamel, inhibits bacterial enzymes), dietary sugar reduction, dental hygiene (mechanical disruption of plaque), and dental sealants. Treatment: remove decayed material, fill with restoration.

Dental caries is one of the most common chronic diseases globally and remains a major problem in populations with limited fluoride and dental care.

### Periodontal disease

Inflammation of the supporting structures of teeth (gums, periodontal ligament, alveolar bone). Begins as gingivitis (inflammation of gum margins) and can progress to periodontitis (deeper tissue destruction).

The microbiology involves anaerobic bacteria deep in periodontal pockets: *Porphyromonas gingivalis*, *Tannerella forsythia*, *Treponema denticola*, others. Periodontal disease is a leading cause of tooth loss in adults.

Some evidence links periodontal disease to systemic disease — cardiovascular disease, diabetes, preterm birth — possibly through chronic inflammatory mediators or transient bacteremia. The causal relationships are still being worked out.

### Oral candidiasis (thrush)

White patches on the tongue, palate, and other oral mucosal surfaces, caused by *Candida albicans* overgrowth. Risk factors: infancy, immunosuppression, inhaled corticosteroids (in asthma patients), antibiotic use. Treatment: topical nystatin or oral fluconazole.

### Oral herpes

HSV-1, covered in Chapter 21. Cold sores at the lip border, with periodic recurrence.

### Other oral infections

Mumps (paramyxovirus, parotitis with characteristic swelling at the jaw angle), various bacterial infections of dental abscesses, oropharyngeal infections discussed in Chapter 22.

## Bacterial GI infections

The bacterial pathogens divide by mechanism:

**Infection**: live bacteria multiply in the GI tract and cause disease through their own activity (and often toxins).

**Intoxication**: preformed toxin in food causes disease. The bacteria may not even be alive when ingested.

The distinction matters because of timing:

- **Intoxication** symptoms appear quickly — minutes to hours after ingestion — because the toxin is already present.
- **Infection** symptoms appear after an incubation period — typically 12 hours to days — because the bacteria have to multiply and produce damage.

### Staphylococcal food poisoning

*Staphylococcus aureus* growing in inadequately refrigerated food produces preformed enterotoxin (heat-stable — reheating does not destroy it). Ingestion causes vomiting and abdominal cramping within 1–6 hours. Self-limiting in 24 hours. The bacteria themselves may not be alive when ingested.

### Bacillus cereus

Two forms:

- **Emetic form**: vomiting within 1–6 hours of eating contaminated food (classically, fried rice that has been left at room temperature). Preformed toxin.
- **Diarrheal form**: diarrhea within 8–16 hours. Infection-based — the bacteria produce toxin in the gut.

### Clostridium perfringens

Anaerobe. Spores survive cooking; if food is held at warm temperatures, the spores germinate and grow. Produces enterotoxin in the gut after ingestion. Diarrhea and cramping 8–24 hours after eating. Common in institutional settings (steam tables) where food is held warm.

### Botulism

*Clostridium botulinum* produces botulinum toxin, one of the most potent toxins known. Three clinical forms:

- **Foodborne botulism**: from improperly canned food containing preformed toxin. Cranial nerve palsies, descending flaccid paralysis. Respiratory failure if untreated.
- **Infant botulism**: spores ingested by infants (often from honey) germinate in the gut and produce toxin in situ. Constipation, weak cry, poor feeding, hypotonia.
- **Wound botulism**: contamination of a wound with *C. botulinum*. Toxin produced in tissue.

Treatment includes antitoxin (passive immunization) and supportive care. Foodborne botulism has been dramatically reduced by canning regulations and the F₀ standard we met in Chapter 13.

### Salmonella infections

Two clinically distinct groups:

**Non-typhoidal Salmonella** (*S. enterica* serovars Typhimurium, Enteritidis, others): the most common foodborne bacterial illness in the US. Acquired from poultry, eggs, reptiles. Causes acute gastroenteritis (diarrhea, fever, cramping) 6–72 hours after ingestion. Usually self-limiting in 4–7 days. Antibiotics are not routinely indicated and may prolong shedding.

**Typhoid fever** (*S. enterica* serovar Typhi): a distinct clinical disease. Bacteria invade through intestinal lymphoid tissue and disseminate to the bloodstream and reticuloendothelial system. Fever rising over a week, abdominal pain, rose spots, sometimes intestinal hemorrhage or perforation. Treatment with appropriate antibiotics (ceftriaxone, azithromycin, fluoroquinolones depending on resistance pattern). Mary Mallon ("Typhoid Mary") was a chronic gallbladder carrier of *S. Typhi* (Chapter 16). Vaccination is available for travelers to endemic regions.

### Shigellosis

*Shigella* species. ID50 of only about 10–100 organisms. Highly contagious — can spread by hand-to-mouth contact with minimal contamination. Causes bacillary dysentery: bloody mucoid diarrhea, fever, abdominal pain. Treat with appropriate antibiotics (resistance patterns vary).

### Cholera

*Vibrio cholerae*. Acquired from contaminated water or food (particularly shellfish). Produces cholera toxin (an AB toxin that ADP-ribosylates the G protein controlling chloride channels), causing massive secretion of chloride and water into the gut lumen. The clinical picture: "rice water" diarrhea — voluminous, watery stools that can rapidly cause severe dehydration and death.

The treatment is one of the simplest miracles in medicine: **oral rehydration therapy (ORT)**. A solution of salt and sugar in water, drunk at the same rate as fluid is lost. Cholera toxin does not damage the absorptive function of the intestine; the gut can still absorb sodium and water if you provide them with glucose (which co-transports sodium across the brush border). Mortality from severe cholera drops from 50% (untreated) to under 1% with proper rehydration. The Lancet called ORT "potentially the most important medical advance of the [20th] century." [^1]

ORT illustrates a profound point. The disease was understood at the molecular level (cholera toxin's mechanism, the glucose-sodium cotransporter). A simple intervention based on that understanding (salt-sugar-water solution) is cheap, doesn't require trained medical personnel, and saves lives at scale. This is what good biomedical science can do.

### Other Vibrios

*Vibrio parahaemolyticus*, *V. vulnificus*: acquired from contaminated seafood. Can cause gastroenteritis or, in immunocompromised hosts (especially liver disease patients), severe septic infection.

### E. coli enteropathogens

Several pathotypes of *E. coli* cause GI disease:

- **Enterotoxigenic E. coli (ETEC)**: traveler's diarrhea. Heat-labile and heat-stable toxins. Watery diarrhea, usually self-limiting.
- **Enteropathogenic E. coli (EPEC)**: pediatric diarrhea, especially in developing countries.
- **Enteroinvasive E. coli (EIEC)**: dysentery-like illness similar to *Shigella*.
- **Enterohemorrhagic E. coli (EHEC)** including O157:H7: produces Shiga-like toxin. Bloody diarrhea, no fever, can cause hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS, kidney failure with hemolytic anemia). Associated with undercooked ground beef and contaminated produce. The 1993 Jack in the Box outbreak in the western US made O157:H7 a household name.
- **Enteroaggregative E. coli (EAEC)**: persistent diarrhea, particularly in travelers and children.

### Campylobacter

*Campylobacter jejuni*. The most common bacterial cause of gastroenteritis worldwide. Acquired from poultry, contaminated water, unpasteurized milk. Symptoms 2–5 days after ingestion: diarrhea (often bloody), fever, abdominal pain. Important late complication: **Guillain-Barré syndrome** — an autoimmune polyneuropathy that can follow *Campylobacter* infection (the bacterial surface antigens mimic peripheral nerve gangliosides; the immune response cross-reacts).

### Helicobacter pylori

We met Marshall and Warren in Chapter 4. *H. pylori* lives in the stomach mucus layer, protected from acid by the bacterium's urease (which converts urea to ammonia, neutralizing local acid). The bacterium causes:

- **Chronic gastritis**: usually asymptomatic, but the chronic inflammation underlies later complications.
- **Peptic ulcer disease**: 90% of duodenal ulcers and 60–70% of gastric ulcers are *H. pylori*-associated.
- **Gastric adenocarcinoma**: long-term *H. pylori* infection is a major risk factor. *H. pylori* is classified as a Group 1 carcinogen by the WHO.
- **Gastric MALT lymphoma**: B-cell lymphoma of stomach lymphoid tissue, often regressing after *H. pylori* eradication.

Diagnosis: urea breath test (the patient drinks isotope-labeled urea; if *H. pylori* is present, urease produces labeled CO₂ detectable in breath), stool antigen, or biopsy with histology and urease testing. Serology (antibodies) is less useful because it cannot distinguish current from past infection.

Treatment: combination of two antibiotics plus a proton pump inhibitor (PPI) for 10–14 days. Resistance to clarithromycin and metronidazole is rising.

### Clostridioides difficile

*C. difficile* (renamed from *Clostridium difficile* in 2016, though the new name has been slow to adopt). The leading cause of healthcare-associated diarrhea in the US.

Mechanism: broad-spectrum antibiotic use disrupts the normal colonic microbiota; *C. difficile* (which is resistant to most antibiotics, is an endospore-former, and is often picked up during a hospital stay) takes advantage of the open niche, proliferates, and produces toxins A and B. The toxins damage colonic epithelium, causing diarrhea, pseudomembranous colitis, and in severe cases toxic megacolon and perforation.

Treatment: oral vancomycin or fidaxomicin (which paradoxically must be given orally to act locally in the gut; IV vancomycin doesn't help). Severe cases may require surgical intervention.

Recurrence is common — about 20% after a first episode, 40% after a second. For recurrent *C. difficile*, **fecal microbiota transplantation** (FMT) has cure rates over 90%. The principle: restore a healthy colonic microbial community that competitively excludes *C. difficile*. FMT is now an established therapy and represents a striking validation of the colonization-resistance framework.

## Viral GI infections

### Rotavirus

The leading cause of severe diarrheal disease in young children worldwide. Highly contagious. Symptoms: vomiting and watery diarrhea, often lasting 3–8 days. Can cause severe dehydration in infants.

Rotavirus vaccines (Rotarix, RotaTeq) have dramatically reduced rotavirus disease in countries where they are deployed. Pre-vaccine, rotavirus caused several hundred thousand pediatric deaths per year globally; the number has dropped substantially since.

### Norovirus

The most common cause of acute viral gastroenteritis in adults. Highly contagious (low infectious dose), short incubation (12–48 hours), self-limiting (2–3 days). Notorious for outbreaks on cruise ships, in nursing homes, in restaurants. The "winter vomiting disease" of older British literature.

### Hepatitis viruses

Five major hepatitis viruses cause liver inflammation, with distinct epidemiology:

**Hepatitis A (HAV)**: RNA virus, fecal-oral transmission. Acute hepatitis only — no chronic infection. Vaccine-preventable.

**Hepatitis B (HBV)**: DNA virus, transmitted by blood, sexual contact, and mother-to-child. Can cause acute or chronic hepatitis. Chronic HBV is a major cause of liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma. Vaccine (recombinant surface antigen) is highly effective and is standard in childhood immunization.

**Hepatitis C (HCV)**: RNA virus, transmitted primarily by blood (injection drug use, transfusion before screening). About 75% of acute infections become chronic, with risk of cirrhosis and HCC over decades. Directly-acting antivirals (DAAs) developed in the 2010s can cure most HCV infections — a remarkable therapeutic advance.

**Hepatitis D (HDV)**: defective virus that requires HBV co-infection to replicate. Adds severity to HBV.

**Hepatitis E (HEV)**: RNA virus, fecal-oral transmission. Similar profile to HAV but particularly severe in pregnant women.

## Protozoan GI infections

### Giardiasis

*Giardia lamblia*. Flagellated protozoan. Cysts in contaminated water; trophozoites colonize the small intestine. Symptoms: persistent diarrhea, malabsorption, weight loss, often greasy floating stools. Acquired from streams, lakes, contaminated water systems. Treatment with metronidazole, tinidazole, or nitazoxanide.

### Cryptosporidiosis

*Cryptosporidium parvum* or *C. hominis*. Causes watery diarrhea. Cysts are chlorine-resistant; municipal water treatment must use ozonation or filtration to remove them. Major outbreaks have occurred from contaminated municipal water. Self-limiting in immunocompetent hosts; severe and prolonged in HIV/AIDS patients.

### Amebiasis

*Entamoeba histolytica*. Cysts ingested; trophozoites colonize the colon. Most infections asymptomatic. A fraction develop dysentery (bloody diarrhea), and a smaller fraction develop **amebic liver abscess** — the parasite invades the bowel wall, enters the portal circulation, and seeds the liver. Treatment with metronidazole plus a luminal agent (paromomycin) to eliminate cysts.

### Cyclosporiasis

*Cyclospora cayetanensis*. Acquired from contaminated produce. Persistent diarrhea. Treatment with TMP-SMX.

## Helminthic GI infections

### Ascariasis

*Ascaris lumbricoides*. The most common parasitic worm worldwide; over a billion people infected. Adults live in the small intestine. Larvae migrate through lungs (causing transient cough and Loeffler syndrome). Heavy infection can cause intestinal obstruction, particularly in children.

### Hookworm

*Necator americanus*, *Ancylostoma duodenale*. Larvae penetrate skin (usually feet); migrate through lungs; mature in small intestine where they attach and feed on blood. Chronic infection causes iron-deficiency anemia. Endemic in tropical regions with poor sanitation.

### Pinworm

*Enterobius vermicularis*. The most common helminth in developed countries, particularly in young children. Female worms migrate to the anal area at night to lay eggs, causing intense itching. Eggs are picked up on fingers and re-ingested, completing the cycle. Diagnosis by tape test (cellophane tape applied to the perianal area in the morning catches eggs). Treatment with mebendazole or pyrantel pamoate.

### Trichuriasis (whipworm)

*Trichuris trichiura*. Eggs ingested from contaminated soil; adults in the colon. Heavy infection causes chronic diarrhea, anemia, and rectal prolapse in children.

### Tapeworms

*Taenia solium* (pork tapeworm), *T. saginata* (beef tapeworm), *Diphyllobothrium latum* (fish tapeworm). Adults in the small intestine. Larval *T. solium* can encyst in human muscle and brain — neurocysticercosis, a leading cause of acquired epilepsy in endemic regions.

### Flukes

*Fasciola hepatica* (liver fluke), *Clonorchis sinensis* (Chinese liver fluke), *Schistosoma* species (covered in Chapter 25 and Chapter 23).

## What the chapter is really about

Gastrointestinal infection is the single most common category of infectious disease worldwide. Most cases are self-limiting and require only supportive care (hydration, rest). A subset are severe — particularly in young children, in resource-limited settings, in immunocompromised hosts. Rehydration is the single most important treatment for diarrheal disease, and the realization that oral rehydration could replace IV therapy in most cases has saved millions of lives.

The diagnostic approach to acute diarrheal disease typically does not require knowing the specific pathogen, because management is supportive in most cases. Stool culture, parasitology, and specific toxin testing are used when there is bloody diarrhea, persistent illness, severe symptoms, immunocompromise, healthcare association, or public health concern (outbreak investigation, food handler).

For your training, two patterns to carry forward. First: foodborne illness with rapid onset is usually intoxication (preformed toxin); slow onset is infection. Second: post-antibiotic diarrhea, especially in hospital patients, is *C. difficile* until proven otherwise.

## Still puzzling

I do not understand why some people who carry *H. pylori* develop ulcers, others develop gastric cancer, and most develop only chronic gastritis without serious clinical consequences. The bacterial factors (CagA-positive strains are more associated with disease), host factors (genetic polymorphisms in IL-1B), and environmental factors (diet, salt intake) all contribute. But predicting who will progress to disease and who will not, in an individual patient, is currently not possible. This is one of the more important unsolved questions in *H. pylori* clinical practice — should we eradicate the bacterium in everyone, or only in those at high risk?

## What would change my mind

The fecal microbiota transplant story for *C. difficile* has been remarkably successful. If similar microbiota-restoration approaches succeed for inflammatory bowel disease, irritable bowel syndrome, or metabolic conditions, the framework of "infection" versus "dysbiosis" would need to expand substantially.
