# Chapter 04 — Prokaryotic Diversity

*Prokaryotes are not a kind of organism. They are most of the organisms.*

## The question before the answer

If you take a teaspoon of soil from a temperate forest and look at it carefully, you will find somewhere between a billion and ten billion bacterial cells. Several thousand species. Several hundred thousand distinct strains. Some of them you can grow in a petri dish; most of them you cannot. The biomass of bacteria on Earth exceeds the biomass of all the animals on Earth, possibly by an order of magnitude. By cell count, you are vastly outnumbered by the prokaryotes living inside your own gut.

When biology teachers talk about "the bacteria" in introductory classes, they tend to talk about a small set of organisms — *E. coli*, *Staphylococcus*, *Streptococcus*, maybe *Salmonella* and *Mycobacterium*. The medically relevant ones. The cultured ones. The ones with names worth memorizing for a microbiology exam.

That picture is not wrong. It is just radically incomplete. The bacterial and archaeal world is so much larger than what we have learned to grow in laboratories that the rest of microbiology starts to look like a study of an iceberg's tip. The point of this chapter is to give you a working tour of the major prokaryotic groups and to make clear, throughout, which ones are the parts of the iceberg above the waterline and which are the parts that biology has only recently been able to detect at all.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

1. Place a named prokaryote into the right phylum or domain based on a brief description.
2. Distinguish the five classes of Proteobacteria and recognize the key human pathogens in each.
3. Explain the difference between Gram-positive bacteria with high G+C and low G+C, and why that distinction is more than bookkeeping.
4. Recognize the major archaeal groups and describe one feature that makes archaea biochemically alien from a bacterial point of view.
5. Predict what kind of habitat a given prokaryote will thrive in, based on its metabolic strategy.

Prerequisites: Chapters 1–3.

## Where prokaryotes live

There is no place on Earth that has been carefully examined for life and found to be sterile.

Prokaryotes live in deep ocean sediments under several kilometers of rock, fed by hydrogen and sulfur. They live in hot springs at 95°C and in Antarctic brine pools at –20°C. They live inside basalt, slowly metabolizing it. They live in salt-saturated lakes too salty for anything else. They live inside the bodies of other organisms, including you. They live in clouds, in spacecraft clean rooms, in the cooling water of nuclear reactors. *Deinococcus radiodurans* survives doses of ionizing radiation that would kill a human a thousand times over.

If you ask "what is a typical bacterial habitat," there is no honest answer except "Earth."

Prokaryotes also do things ecologically that nothing else on Earth does. Fixing atmospheric nitrogen into a form plants can use — *only* prokaryotes do this. Methanogenesis (producing methane from CO₂ and hydrogen) — only archaea. Oxygenic photosynthesis was invented by cyanobacteria over a billion years before any plant existed and produced essentially all the oxygen in Earth's atmosphere. Without prokaryotes, no soil ecosystem on Earth could function for more than a few weeks before nutrient cycles broke down.

## Symbioses

A prokaryote rarely lives alone. Most live in close association with other organisms — sometimes mutually beneficial, sometimes neutral, sometimes parasitic.

**Mutualism**: both partners benefit. The bacteria in your large intestine (mostly *Bacteroides*, *Firmicutes*, *Bifidobacterium*) digest fibers your enzymes cannot break down, produce vitamins (K, several B vitamins) you need but cannot synthesize, and outcompete pathogens for resources. You provide a warm, stable, nutrient-rich environment.

**Commensalism**: one partner benefits, the other is unaffected. Most of the bacteria on your skin live here. You are barely aware they are there.

**Parasitism**: one partner benefits at the other's expense. Pathogens. We will be discussing them in detail from Chapter 15 onward.

The distinction between these categories is less crisp than the words suggest. A "commensal" organism may, under stress (immunocompromise, broken skin barrier, antibiotic disruption of normal flora), become an "opportunistic pathogen." The same *E. coli* strain that helps you digest food in the gut can kill you if it enters your bloodstream. The category depends on context.

The microbiology of these relationships — the *microbiome*, in current vocabulary — is one of the most active areas in the field right now. The Human Microbiome Project (2007–2016) sequenced the bacteria living at multiple body sites across hundreds of healthy people. The results suggest that the human body harbors something like 10–100 trillion bacterial cells, mostly in the gut. [^1] The composition of that community correlates with disease states, drug responses, and possibly behavior, in ways the field is still trying to characterize.

I want to flag the methodological revolution that made this possible. Before about 2005, microbiology characterized bacteria mostly by culturing them. Maybe 1% of the bacteria in a typical environmental or clinical sample can be cultured. The rest were invisible. *Metagenomic sequencing* — extracting DNA from a complex sample and sequencing all of it without culture — revealed the other 99%. The microbiome as a research field exists because the cultivation bias of the previous century broke when sequencing technology made it irrelevant.

## How prokaryotes are classified

The modern taxonomic system, descended from the Woese-Fox three-domain framework we met in Chapter 1, sorts prokaryotes into phyla based on 16S ribosomal RNA sequence similarity. The current best estimate is somewhere between 50 and several hundred bacterial phyla, depending on how you count and which uncultured groups you accept as full phyla. Of these, only a handful are medically important. We will cover those.

Two organizing distinctions matter most clinically:

**Gram-positive vs. Gram-negative**, which we met in Chapter 2. Most bacterial phyla can be sorted this way, though the picture gets more complex at the edges.

**G+C content**, the ratio of guanine and cytosine bases to total bases in the genome. This was an early molecular distinction, easier to measure than full sequencing, and it remains useful. High G+C Gram-positives are dominated by the phylum Actinobacteria; low G+C Gram-positives are dominated by Firmicutes.

We will go through the major groups in roughly the order a clinical microbiology course covers them.

## The Proteobacteria

The Proteobacteria are the largest and most diverse phylum of bacteria. They are all Gram-negative, all (with rare exception) heterotrophic, and they include most of the bacterial pathogens you will hear named in medicine. The phylum is divided into five classes — alpha, beta, gamma, delta, epsilon — by 16S rRNA sequence.

### Alphaproteobacteria

This class contains the bacteria most closely related to the ancestor of mitochondria, including *Rickettsia*, an obligate intracellular parasite that lives inside host cells (we will discuss it in Chapter 25 in the context of Rocky Mountain spotted fever and typhus).

Also in this class: *Wolbachia*, intracellular bacteria that infect a remarkable fraction of insect species and modify their host's reproduction — sometimes killing male embryos, sometimes feminizing genetic males, sometimes inducing parthenogenesis. *Wolbachia* is one of the more interesting parasites in biology because the boundary between host and parasite gets blurry: it has fundamentally rewired the reproductive biology of its hosts.

Also: *Brucella* (causes brucellosis, a zoonotic disease transmitted from livestock), *Bartonella* (cat scratch fever, trench fever), and the nitrogen-fixing *Rhizobium* species that live in root nodules of legumes and make nitrogen fixation possible for those plants.

### Betaproteobacteria

This class contains *Neisseria*, the genus that includes *N. gonorrhoeae* (gonorrhea) and *N. meningitidis* (a leading cause of bacterial meningitis — and one we will return to in Chapter 26). It also contains *Bordetella pertussis*, the cause of whooping cough, and *Burkholderia*, a genus that includes the cystic fibrosis lung pathogen *B. cepacia*.

### Gammaproteobacteria

The class with the largest number of named pathogens. It contains the family Enterobacteriaceae — gut-dwelling Gram-negative rods including *E. coli*, *Salmonella*, *Shigella*, *Klebsiella*, *Yersinia* (the cause of plague), and *Proteus*. It also contains *Pseudomonas*, including the hospital-acquired *P. aeruginosa*; *Vibrio*, including *V. cholerae*; *Haemophilus influenzae*, formerly a major cause of pediatric meningitis; *Legionella*, the cause of Legionnaires' disease; and *Pasteurella*, named for Pasteur.

When a clinical microbiologist sees Gram-negative rods, the differential diagnosis is heavily weighted toward Gammaproteobacteria.

### Deltaproteobacteria

A smaller class that includes the sulfate-reducing bacteria — anaerobic organisms that respire by reducing sulfate (SO₄²⁻) to hydrogen sulfide (H₂S). These are not human pathogens but are major players in marine sediment chemistry and the global sulfur cycle.

Also includes *Bdellovibrio*, a remarkable predatory bacterium that hunts and consumes other Gram-negative bacteria — there is research interest in whether *Bdellovibrio* could be used therapeutically against antibiotic-resistant pathogens.

### Epsilonproteobacteria

Smallest of the five classes. Includes *Campylobacter jejuni*, a leading cause of bacterial gastroenteritis worldwide, and *Helicobacter pylori*, the bacterium that causes most peptic ulcers.

*H. pylori* deserves a paragraph of its own. Until 1982, peptic ulcers were assumed to be caused by stress and stomach acid. Australian physicians Barry Marshall and Robin Warren proposed that they were actually caused by a bacterium living in the stomach lining — a bacterium that no one had thought could survive the stomach's pH. The medical establishment was skeptical. Marshall, to make the case, drank a culture of *H. pylori* himself, developed gastritis within days, and treated himself with antibiotics, demonstrating the cause-and-effect chain. He and Warren won the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2005. [^2] Peptic ulcers are now treated with antibiotics, not antacids, and the global burden of ulcer disease has dropped substantially.

## Nonproteobacteria Gram-negatives

A handful of medically important Gram-negatives are not Proteobacteria. The most important:

**Spirochetes** are long, helically-coiled Gram-negative cells with a unique flagellar arrangement (axial filaments running inside the periplasm) that lets them screw through viscous media. They are mostly invisible in brightfield microscopy and are typically observed by darkfield. The medically important spirochetes are *Treponema pallidum* (syphilis), *Borrelia burgdorferi* (Lyme disease), and *Leptospira* species (leptospirosis).

**Chlamydiae** are obligate intracellular parasites with a complex two-stage life cycle. *Chlamydia trachomatis* causes the most common bacterial sexually transmitted infection in the world and is also the leading cause of preventable blindness (trachoma).

## Cyanobacteria — the most consequential bacteria in Earth's history

Cyanobacteria perform oxygenic photosynthesis. They use light to split water into oxygen and hydrogen, reducing CO₂ to sugars in the process. They were doing this 2.5 to 3 billion years ago, when the atmosphere had essentially no oxygen.

For roughly a billion years, the oxygen they produced was absorbed by iron in the oceans — there was so much dissolved iron that it scavenged the oxygen and precipitated as iron oxide (banded iron formations, the source of most modern iron ore). Around 2.4 billion years ago, the iron ran out. Cyanobacterial oxygen started accumulating in the atmosphere. This is the Great Oxygenation Event. It killed most life on Earth, which had evolved in an anoxic world and could not handle the oxidative stress. It also set the stage for everything we now consider alive — including the endosymbiotic event that produced mitochondria.

Modern cyanobacteria are still present in oceans, lakes, and soils, still fixing CO₂ and producing oxygen. They are also problematic when they bloom in nutrient-loaded freshwater systems: some species produce neurotoxins (microcystin, anatoxin) that contaminate drinking water.

## The Gram-positives: high G+C and low G+C

Gram-positive bacteria are split into two large phyla by G+C content, with surprisingly different ecologies.

### Actinobacteria (high G+C)

This phylum includes *Mycobacterium* — the genus that contains *M. tuberculosis* (TB) and *M. leprae* (leprosy). Mycobacteria are notable for their unusual cell walls, which contain mycolic acids — long-chain waxy lipids that make the cells acid-fast (Chapter 2) and resistant to many antibiotics and disinfectants. The waxy wall is why TB takes six months of multi-drug therapy to treat: the bacteria are very hard to kill.

Actinobacteria also include the genus *Streptomyces*, which produces about half of all known clinically used antibiotics — streptomycin, tetracyclines, erythromycins, vancomycin, and many others all came from soil-dwelling *Streptomyces* species. The bacteria evolved these compounds, presumably, as antimicrobial weapons against other soil organisms. We use them to do the same job inside us. We will return to this in Chapter 14.

The phylum also includes *Corynebacterium* (which contains *C. diphtheriae*, the cause of diphtheria), *Bifidobacterium* (a beneficial gut resident often added to probiotics), and *Gardnerella vaginalis* (associated with bacterial vaginosis).

### Firmicutes (low G+C)

This phylum is a clinical microbiology workhorse. It contains:

- **Bacillus** — rod-shaped, aerobic, endospore-forming. Includes *B. anthracis* (anthrax) and *B. cereus* (food poisoning).
- **Clostridium** (and the recently split-off **Clostridioides** and **Paeniclostridium**) — rod-shaped, anaerobic, endospore-forming. Includes *C. tetani* (tetanus), *C. botulinum* (botulism), *C. perfringens* (gas gangrene and food poisoning), and *Clostridioides difficile* (a major hospital-acquired infection).
- **Staphylococcus** — Gram-positive cocci in clusters (Chapter 2's Cindy story). Includes *S. aureus* (skin infections, MRSA) and *S. epidermidis* (skin commensal that causes catheter and prosthetic infections).
- **Streptococcus** — Gram-positive cocci in chains. Includes *S. pyogenes* (strep throat, scarlet fever, rheumatic fever, necrotizing fasciitis), *S. pneumoniae* (pneumonia, meningitis), and various viridans streptococci.
- **Enterococcus** — Gram-positive cocci, gut residents that cause urinary tract and bloodstream infections, often antibiotic-resistant.
- **Lactobacillus** — Gram-positive rods that produce lactic acid by fermentation. They are responsible for yogurt, sauerkraut, kimchi, and the acid environment of the healthy vagina that helps prevent infection.
- **Mycoplasma** — Firmicutes that have lost their cell wall entirely. Tiny, free-living, immune to all antibiotics that target cell wall synthesis. *M. pneumoniae* causes walking pneumonia.

## Archaea

The third domain. Archaea look like bacteria under a microscope — small, single-celled, no nucleus. They are, however, biochemically distinct from bacteria in several systematic ways.

**Cell wall**: bacteria use peptidoglycan. Archaea do not. Most archaea instead have a wall of *S-layer proteins* (a regular, two-dimensional crystalline array) or a polymer called pseudopeptidoglycan (chemically distinct from real peptidoglycan despite the name). The Gram stain is unreliable for archaea.

**Membrane lipids**: bacterial and eukaryotic lipid bilayers use fatty-acid chains connected to glycerol by ester linkages. Archaeal lipids use isoprenoid chains connected to glycerol by *ether* linkages. The chemistry is fundamentally different. Some archaea even have membranes made of a single lipid that spans the entire bilayer (a tetraether monolayer), which is unique to archaea and is one reason some thermophilic archaea survive temperatures that destroy bacterial membranes.

**Molecular biology**: archaeal DNA replication, transcription, and translation are run by enzymes that look more like eukaryotic enzymes than bacterial ones. Archaea have histones (DNA-packaging proteins) like eukaryotes do. Their RNA polymerase resembles eukaryotic RNA polymerase II. This is part of why the three-domain tree places Archaea closer to Eukarya than to Bacteria.

The major archaeal phyla:

**Crenarchaeota** are mostly thermophiles — they live in hot springs, deep-sea hydrothermal vents, and acidic geothermal environments. *Sulfolobus* species grow at 80°C and pH 3. *Pyrolobus fumarii* can grow at 113°C, near the boiling point of water.

**Euryarchaeota** is the biggest and most varied phylum. It includes the methanogens (*Methanobacterium*, *Methanococcus* — strict anaerobes that produce methane from H₂ and CO₂; they live in marshes, the deep ocean, cow rumens, and human guts), the halophiles (*Halobacterium*, *Haloferax* — live in salt-saturated environments), and various other extremophiles.

And here is the puzzle that has not gone away since Chapter 1: no confirmed human pathogen among the archaea. Despite living in the human gut, despite being biochemically alien to bacteria (which means many of our immune sensors should not detect them), despite having all the basic machinery of life — none of them have, as far as we know, made the jump to causing human disease. We do not have a good explanation for this. It could be detection bias (we are not looking for them with appropriate methods). It could be that something about their biochemistry — membrane lipids, surface molecules — is incompatible with mammalian infection. It is a real open question.

## What the chapter is really about

Prokaryotic diversity is not a side topic in microbiology. It is the topic that demonstrates why the medically-focused traditional view of bacteria misses most of what bacteria are doing. The pathogens are a small biased sample. The full prokaryotic world — running the nitrogen cycle, the carbon cycle, the sulfur cycle, ferrying electrons through environments no eukaryote could survive — is the actual background condition of biology on Earth.

A useful habit when reading about a new bacterium: place it in its phylum first, ask what its closest relatives are, ask what its environmental niche is, and only then ask whether it is medically relevant. The medical question becomes much less arbitrary when you can see how a pathogen sits inside a broader family of related organisms most of which are doing something innocuous.

## What would change my mind

The classification of prokaryotes into 50–200 phyla based on 16S rRNA is the current consensus, but the boundary lines are being redrawn as new metagenomic data accumulate. If the Candidate Phyla Radiation (CPR) — uncultured bacteria detected only by metagenomic sequencing — turns out to constitute a major branch with cell biology substantially different from the rest of Bacteria, the taxonomic picture in this chapter will need substantial revision.
