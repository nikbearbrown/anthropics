# Chapter 19 — Diseases of the Immune System

*A system that distinguishes self from non-self has two ways to fail.*

## The question before the answer

The adaptive immune system has to do something genuinely hard. It has to recognize and attack everything foreign while ignoring everything that belongs to you. The shapes of "foreign" and "self" overlap, particularly because most of your own proteins are shapes that, at some point in evolutionary history, came from organisms that were not you. The selection process that eliminates self-reactive lymphocytes (Chapter 18) cuts down on the overlap, but it does not eliminate it. The system is calibrated, not perfect.

When the calibration fails in one direction, you get **autoimmune disease** — the immune system attacking healthy tissues as if they were pathogens. When it fails in the other direction, you get **immunodeficiency** — the immune system failing to attack actual pathogens. When the response to a harmless thing is too aggressive, you get **hypersensitivity** — allergy, anaphylaxis. When the system attacks transplanted tissue, you get **transplant rejection**.

Most of clinical immunology is about these failure modes. The point of this chapter is to map them — to understand what is broken in each, what categories the failures fall into, and what clinical patterns they produce.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

1. Distinguish the four types of hypersensitivity and identify the immune mechanism and example clinical conditions for each.
2. Explain why autoimmune disease develops and identify examples of organ-specific and systemic autoimmune diseases.
3. Describe the role of HLAs in transplantation and identify the four major graft types.
4. Distinguish primary from secondary immunodeficiency and provide examples of each.
5. Describe how the immune system responds to tumors and the principles behind tumor immunotherapy.

Prerequisites: Chapters 17 and 18 especially, plus Chapters 1–16.

## Hypersensitivity

The classification of hypersensitivity reactions into four types was proposed by Coombs and Gell in 1963 and remains the standard framework. [^1] The four types are distinguished by mechanism, not severity.

### Type I — Immediate (IgE-mediated)

The classic allergic reaction. On first exposure to an allergen (pollen, peanut, bee venom), some people produce IgE antibodies specific to it. The IgE binds Fc receptors on mast cells and basophils, sensitizing those cells. The person does not yet have symptoms.

On second exposure, the allergen binds to IgE already on mast cells. The cross-linking of IgE triggers the mast cell to release granule contents — histamine, leukotrienes, prostaglandins, tryptase. Histamine causes vasodilation, vascular permeability, and smooth muscle contraction. The result, depending on where and how much: hives, itching, swelling, wheezing, runny nose, abdominal cramps, or anaphylactic shock.

Onset is fast — minutes to an hour after exposure. The severity depends on the dose, the route, and the individual's degree of sensitization.

Common Type I conditions: allergic rhinitis (hay fever), asthma (allergic component), urticaria (hives), atopic dermatitis (eczema, partly Type I), food allergies, drug allergies, anaphylaxis to bee stings or peanuts.

Treatment: avoidance, antihistamines (block H1 receptors), corticosteroids (broad anti-inflammatory), epinephrine for anaphylaxis (reverses vasodilation and bronchospasm), immunotherapy ("allergy shots" — desensitization by giving increasing doses of allergen over time, shifting the response from IgE to IgG).

### Type II — Antibody-mediated cytotoxicity

Antibody (IgG or IgM) binds to cellular antigens, leading to cell destruction through complement activation, opsonization for phagocytosis, or antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity by NK cells.

Examples:

- **ABO incompatibility transfusion reactions**: pre-existing antibodies to blood group antigens that the patient lacks bind to transfused cells, causing massive hemolysis. Donor type A blood transfused to a type O recipient produces this reaction.
- **Hemolytic disease of the newborn**: maternal IgG antibodies against fetal red cell antigens cross the placenta and destroy fetal red cells. Rh incompatibility is the classic example (mother Rh-negative, fetus Rh-positive). Modern prevention uses Rh immunoglobulin to suppress the maternal response.
- **Goodpasture syndrome**: antibodies to a component of basement membrane in kidney glomeruli and lung alveoli. Causes glomerulonephritis and pulmonary hemorrhage.
- **Myasthenia gravis**: antibodies against the acetylcholine receptor at the neuromuscular junction, causing muscle weakness.
- **Graves' disease**: antibodies against the TSH receptor on thyroid cells that activate the receptor, causing hyperthyroidism.

### Type III — Immune complex disease

Antigen-antibody complexes form in the circulation and deposit in tissues, where they activate complement and trigger inflammation. The complexes deposit preferentially in small blood vessels — in skin, kidneys, joints, lungs.

Examples:

- **Serum sickness**: classic Type III reaction, originally seen with horse-serum antitoxins. The patient develops fever, rash, joint pain, and proteinuria 7–10 days after the foreign serum.
- **Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)**: autoimmune disease where antibodies against DNA, RNA, and various nuclear proteins form complexes that deposit in skin, kidneys, joints, brain. The clinical picture is variable and severe.
- **Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis**: immune complexes form after streptococcal infection and deposit in kidney glomeruli.
- **Rheumatoid arthritis**: complex multi-mechanism disease with Type III component (rheumatoid factor — IgM antibody against the Fc region of IgG — forming complexes).
- **Hypersensitivity pneumonitis** ("farmer's lung," "pigeon breeder's lung"): immune complexes deposit in lung tissue after repeated inhalation of antigens.

### Type IV — Delayed-type hypersensitivity (cell-mediated)

T cells, not antibodies, mediate the response. The cells take time to migrate and activate, so the response peaks at 24–72 hours after exposure (hence "delayed").

Examples:

- **Tuberculin skin test (PPD)**: intradermal injection of *M. tuberculosis* protein in a person previously infected produces a raised, indurated area at 48–72 hours, indicating T cell memory of TB.
- **Contact dermatitis**: poison ivy, nickel allergy, latex contact reaction. The allergen acts as a hapten that combines with skin proteins to form an antigenic complex; T cells previously sensitized to this complex generate inflammation on re-exposure.
- **Tuberculosis granuloma**: T cell-mediated walling off of *M. tuberculosis* in lung tissue. Granuloma formation is the body's compromise — the pathogen is contained but not eliminated.
- **Transplant rejection** (chronic, T cell-mediated): T cells attacking the foreign HLA molecules on transplanted tissue.

## Autoimmune disease

Tolerance is the immune system's ability to not attack self. Central tolerance (in thymus and bone marrow) eliminates most self-reactive lymphocytes. Peripheral tolerance (regulatory T cells, anergy, sequestration of antigens behind barriers) handles the ones that escape central selection.

Autoimmune disease arises when tolerance fails. The causes are usually multifactorial — genetic predisposition (certain HLA alleles, defects in regulatory T cells), environmental triggers (infection, drugs), and possibly stochastic events.

Some hypotheses for autoimmune disease initiation:

**Molecular mimicry**: pathogen antigens resemble self antigens; the response to the pathogen cross-reacts with self. Group A streptococcal antigens resemble cardiac myosin; some children who get strep throat develop rheumatic fever as an autoimmune attack on heart valves.

**Bystander activation**: an infection causes nonspecific inflammation that activates self-reactive lymphocytes that were previously dormant.

**Epitope spreading**: an initial response against one antigen expands over time to include related antigens, some of which are self.

**Failure of regulatory T cells**: Tregs that normally suppress self-reactive lymphocytes lose function, allowing autoimmune responses.

**Cryptic antigen exposure**: tissue antigens normally sequestered (in eye, testis, brain) get exposed by injury and trigger immune responses.

Autoimmune diseases are typically divided into organ-specific and systemic.

### Organ-specific autoimmune diseases

The immune response targets one tissue or organ.

- **Type 1 diabetes mellitus**: T cells destroy insulin-producing beta cells in the pancreas. Onset usually in childhood or adolescence. Requires lifelong insulin replacement.
- **Hashimoto thyroiditis**: antibodies against thyroid antigens; chronic destruction of thyroid; hypothyroidism. The most common cause of hypothyroidism in iodine-sufficient regions.
- **Graves' disease**: antibodies activate the TSH receptor; hyperthyroidism.
- **Multiple sclerosis**: T cell attack on myelin in the central nervous system. Relapsing-remitting or progressive demyelination causing variable neurological symptoms.
- **Pernicious anemia**: antibodies against intrinsic factor (needed for B12 absorption); vitamin B12 deficiency; megaloblastic anemia and neurological symptoms.
- **Addison disease**: autoimmune destruction of adrenal cortex; cortisol and aldosterone deficiency.
- **Myasthenia gravis** (covered under Type II): antibodies against acetylcholine receptor.

### Systemic autoimmune diseases

The immune response targets antigens present in many tissues.

- **Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)**: antibodies against nuclear antigens (anti-dsDNA, anti-Sm, antiphospholipid). Affects skin, joints, kidneys, blood, central nervous system, heart, lungs. Predominantly in young women. Highly variable clinical course.
- **Rheumatoid arthritis**: chronic inflammatory arthritis with rheumatoid factor and anti-citrullinated protein antibodies. Joint destruction, but also systemic effects (lung, eye, vascular).
- **Sjögren syndrome**: autoimmune destruction of exocrine glands; dry eyes, dry mouth.
- **Scleroderma**: fibrosis of skin and internal organs.
- **Polymyositis and dermatomyositis**: autoimmune muscle inflammation.

The treatment for autoimmune disease is generally immunosuppression. Corticosteroids broadly suppress immunity. Methotrexate, azathioprine, cyclosporine, and other agents target specific aspects. Newer biologics — TNF inhibitors (infliximab, adalimumab), IL-6 inhibitors (tocilizumab), B cell depletion (rituximab), JAK inhibitors — target specific cytokines or cell types and are revolutionizing the treatment of autoimmune disease.

## Transplantation

Solid organ transplantation (kidney, liver, heart, lung, pancreas) is one of the great achievements of modern medicine. It is also a contest with the immune system.

The host's T cells recognize the donor's MHC molecules (HLA in humans) as foreign. The response is the most powerful T cell response the body produces — orders of magnitude stronger than the response to a typical pathogen, because so much of the T cell repertoire reacts to foreign MHC.

### Types of grafts

**Autograft**: tissue from the patient to the patient. Skin graft from one part of the body to another, autologous bone marrow transplant. No rejection issue because the donor and recipient are the same person.

**Isograft (syngraft)**: from an identical twin. Same HLA. No rejection.

**Allograft**: from a different individual of the same species. Standard transplantation. Rejection is a major issue; HLA matching and immunosuppression are required.

**Xenograft**: from a different species. Hyperacute rejection by preformed antibodies (humans have antibodies against most animal carbohydrate antigens) and aggressive immune responses. Pig heart valves are used (decellularized and chemically treated to remove cells, minimizing rejection). Whole-organ xenotransplantation has been attempted; the first pig kidney transplants into living human recipients took place in 2024.

### Rejection

**Hyperacute rejection**: preformed antibodies (against ABO blood group or HLA) bind donor endothelium immediately on transplantation. Complement activation, thrombosis, and graft destruction within minutes to hours. Prevented by ABO matching and HLA crossmatch testing.

**Acute rejection**: T cell-mediated attack on the graft, peaking at 1–2 weeks. The mainstay of immunosuppressive therapy is to prevent and treat this. With current therapy, acute rejection occurs in 10–30% of solid organ transplants and is usually treatable.

**Chronic rejection**: slow progressive deterioration of graft function over years. Mechanisms include continuing low-grade immune attack, vascular changes, and fibrosis. Less responsive to therapy.

### Graft-versus-host disease (GVHD)

In bone marrow or stem cell transplantation, the situation is reversed: the *donor*'s immune cells recognize the *recipient* as foreign. The donor T cells attack the recipient's tissues, particularly skin, gut, and liver. GVHD is the major complication of allogeneic bone marrow transplant and can be fatal.

Some degree of donor immune activity against the recipient's tumor cells (the **graft-versus-tumor effect**) is actually beneficial in bone marrow transplant for leukemia. The trick is to harness graft-versus-tumor without unleashing severe GVHD.

### Immunosuppression

The pharmacology of preventing rejection is the pharmacology of suppressing T cell function. Common regimens combine:

- **Corticosteroids** (prednisone, methylprednisolone): broad anti-inflammatory and lymphotoxic.
- **Calcineurin inhibitors** (cyclosporine, tacrolimus): block T cell activation.
- **Antimetabolites** (mycophenolate, azathioprine): block lymphocyte proliferation.
- **mTOR inhibitors** (sirolimus, everolimus): block T cell proliferation through a different pathway.
- **Biological agents** (basiliximab, antithymocyte globulin): targeted depletion or blockade of T cells.

The trade-off is severe: enough immunosuppression to prevent rejection means enough immunosuppression to lose normal infection control. Transplant recipients are at greatly elevated risk for opportunistic infections (CMV, EBV-driven lymphoma, *Pneumocystis* pneumonia, fungal infections), and for cancers (especially skin cancer, lymphoma, virus-driven cancers). Lifelong immunosuppression is a chronic balancing act.

## Immunodeficiency

Some patients have immune defects from birth (**primary immunodeficiency**) or acquired during life (**secondary immunodeficiency**).

### Primary immunodeficiencies

More than 400 primary immunodeficiencies have been characterized, mostly genetic defects in immune system components.

Major categories by affected component:

**Antibody deficiencies** are the most common primary immunodeficiencies.

- **X-linked agammaglobulinemia (Bruton)**: defect in BTK gene; B cells fail to develop. Patients have essentially no immunoglobulins and suffer recurrent bacterial infections, particularly with encapsulated organisms (*S. pneumoniae*, *H. influenzae*).
- **Common variable immunodeficiency (CVID)**: heterogeneous group with low immunoglobulins of various classes, recurrent infections, and increased risk of autoimmunity and lymphoma. Often diagnosed in adulthood.
- **Selective IgA deficiency**: low or absent IgA, usually mild or asymptomatic, sometimes recurrent sinopulmonary or GI infections.

**T cell deficiencies** are more severe because they also impair antibody responses (which usually need T cell help).

- **DiGeorge syndrome**: developmental abnormality of the thymus and parathyroid glands; deficient T cell production.
- **Severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID)**: defects in T cell or B cell development; "bubble boy" disease. Untreated, fatal in infancy; treatable by stem cell transplant or gene therapy.

**Complement deficiencies**: lead to specific infection patterns. C3 deficiency: recurrent bacterial infections. C5–C9 deficiency: specifically *Neisseria* susceptibility.

**Phagocyte defects**: chronic granulomatous disease (CGD, defective NADPH oxidase), Chédiak-Higashi syndrome (defective lysosome formation), leukocyte adhesion deficiency.

### Secondary immunodeficiencies

Acquired during life, often more clinically common than primary.

- **HIV/AIDS**: virus directly destroys CD4+ T cells; over years, T cell counts decline and opportunistic infections appear. Diagnosis includes CD4 count and HIV viral load. Antiretroviral therapy can suppress viral replication indefinitely; immune function recovers.
- **Cancer chemotherapy**: cytotoxic drugs that kill rapidly dividing cells also kill rapidly dividing immune cells; severe neutropenia is a common short-term complication.
- **Immunosuppressive therapy**: drugs used for autoimmune disease or transplantation suppress immune function as their mechanism of action. Long-term users are at increased infection risk.
- **Malnutrition**: protein-energy malnutrition impairs immune function. A major cause of immune deficiency in resource-limited settings, particularly for tuberculosis and viral diseases.
- **Aging**: thymic atrophy means fewer new T cells from middle age onward. Immune function declines gradually. This is why influenza and *S. pneumoniae* are particularly dangerous in elderly patients.
- **Diabetes**: impaired neutrophil function and altered tissue healing; characteristic infections.
- **Splenectomy**: loss of the spleen impairs response to encapsulated bacteria, particularly *S. pneumoniae*.
- **Stress and sleep deprivation**: real, measurable effects on immune function, though usually modest.

## Tumor immunology and cancer immunotherapy

Cancer cells are descended from normal cells; they carry the same MHC and many of the same antigens. But they often express **neoantigens** — proteins arising from cancer-specific mutations — that the immune system can recognize.

The immune system does monitor for cancer (**immune surveillance**), and a fraction of cancers are eliminated before clinical detection. Cancers that become clinically detectable have generally evolved mechanisms to evade immune detection: downregulation of MHC class I (so cytotoxic T cells cannot recognize them), expression of immune-suppressive molecules (PD-L1), recruitment of regulatory T cells.

**Immune checkpoint inhibitors** are antibodies that block the inhibitory signals tumors use to silence T cells. Anti-CTLA-4 (ipilimumab) and anti-PD-1/PD-L1 (nivolumab, pembrolizumab, atezolizumab, others) have transformed the treatment of melanoma, lung cancer, kidney cancer, and others. James Allison and Tasuku Honjo shared the 2018 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the underlying discoveries. [^2]

**CAR-T cell therapy** (Chapter 12) engineers patient T cells to express receptors specific for tumor antigens. Highly effective for some leukemias and lymphomas. Currently being developed for solid tumors.

**Tumor vaccines** aim to elicit immune responses against tumor antigens. The HPV vaccine prevents cervical cancer by preventing the HPV infection that causes it. Therapeutic cancer vaccines (against established tumors) have been less successful but remain an active area of research.

The basic principle of cancer immunotherapy: provide the immune system with the tools or signals it needs to do what it was already trying to do, just more effectively.

## What the chapter is really about

The diseases of the immune system are not exceptions to its rules — they are the rules applied imperfectly. Autoimmune disease is the system failing to ignore self. Immunodeficiency is the system failing to detect non-self. Hypersensitivity is the system overreacting. Transplant rejection is the system doing its job, just against tissue that medical intervention has placed where the system finds it.

For clinical practice, the lesson is to look at every immune-related condition through two filters: what is the immune system doing, and is it doing too much or too little. The treatment usually corresponds to the answer. Too much: suppress. Too little: replace or augment. The pharmacology of clinical immunology is the pharmacology of dialing the immune response up or down at specific points.

The cancer immunotherapy story is the most encouraging recent development. Cancers that were uniformly fatal a decade ago — advanced melanoma, advanced non-small-cell lung cancer — now have meaningful response rates with immune checkpoint blockade. The fraction of patients who do not respond is large; the fraction who do is much larger than it was. This is a real victory for immunology, and an unusual one in that it came from basic research on T cell biology, not from drug screening or trial-and-error medicinal chemistry.

## Still puzzling

I do not understand why immune checkpoint inhibitors work spectacularly in some patients with a given cancer and not at all in others. Predicting response remains imprecise. Tumor mutational burden, PD-L1 expression, and other biomarkers help but do not fully predict who will benefit. The biology connecting tumor mutation patterns to T cell recognition and clinical response is still being worked out. I would not be surprised to see substantial revisions to current predictive models in the next several years.

## What would change my mind

The hygiene hypothesis is the standard explanation for the rise in autoimmune and allergic disease in developed countries — that decreased childhood exposure to microbes and parasites has shifted immune development. The hypothesis is plausible and has some experimental support. If carefully designed intervention trials (controlled microbial or helminth exposure in childhood) failed to reduce later autoimmune disease, the hypothesis would need substantial revision. As of this writing, the trials are limited and mixed. The picture is consistent with the hygiene hypothesis but does not prove it. `[verify: 2025 status of hygiene hypothesis intervention trials]`
