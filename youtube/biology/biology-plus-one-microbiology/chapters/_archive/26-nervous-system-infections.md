# Chapter 26 — Nervous System Infections

*The brain is the most carefully defended organ in the body. When the defenses fail, the consequences are worst.*

## The question before the answer

The brain is wrapped in three layers of membrane (the meninges), encased in bone, and supplied by blood vessels that have evolved a particularly tight cellular junction — the blood-brain barrier. The barrier excludes most molecules from passing between blood and brain tissue, including most antibodies, most antibiotics, and almost all microbes. The cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) that bathes the brain and spinal cord is normally sterile, contains few cells, and is regulated to maintain a stable chemical environment.

These defenses are remarkable. They are also a problem when they fail. A bacterial meningitis case — pathogen across the blood-brain barrier and into the meninges — can progress from "patient feels sick" to "patient is dead" in hours. The same barrier that keeps pathogens out also limits the entry of antibiotics, making treatment harder than infections elsewhere. The privileged status of the central nervous system is both protective and punishing.

The point of this chapter is to map the infections that breach this special protection: the bacterial meningitides, the viral encephalitides, the fungal meningitis of immunocompromised patients, the parasitic invasions, and the prion diseases that occupy a category of their own. The clinical urgency is high; the differential is structured; the diagnostic test is often a lumbar puncture; the treatment, when caught early, is often dramatically effective.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

1. Describe the anatomy of the nervous system and explain why it normally lacks microbiota.
2. Read a CSF profile (cell count, glucose, protein, Gram stain) and predict bacterial vs viral vs fungal etiology.

4. Distinguish meningitis from encephalitis by site and clinical pattern.
5. Recognize the clinical features of rabies, polio, and other neurotropic viruses.
6. Describe fungal and protozoan CNS infections and their typical hosts.
7. Describe prion diseases of the nervous system.

Prerequisites: Chapters 1–25, especially Chapter 6 (for acellular pathogens) and 19 (for immunosuppressed hosts).

## CNS anatomy

The **central nervous system** comprises the brain and spinal cord. It is enclosed by:

- **Meninges**: three membrane layers. The outermost is the **dura mater** (tough, fibrous). The middle is the **arachnoid mater** (web-like). The innermost is the **pia mater** (thin, follows the contours of the brain). The space between arachnoid and pia (the **subarachnoid space**) contains cerebrospinal fluid (CSF).
- **CSF**: clear fluid, produced by the choroid plexus, circulates through the ventricles and subarachnoid space, and is absorbed back into venous blood. About 150 mL total volume; 500 mL produced per day; complete turnover every 6 hours.
- **Blood-brain barrier (BBB)**: the endothelial cells of brain capillaries have unusually tight junctions, plus surrounding astrocyte foot processes, that exclude most blood components from brain tissue. The BBB is most effective in protecting the brain parenchyma; it is somewhat less effective at the choroid plexus and at the circumventricular organs.

The **peripheral nervous system** includes the cranial and spinal nerves outside the CNS. The peripheral nerves are less protected and are the route by which some pathogens (rabies, herpes simplex, varicella-zoster) reach the CNS.

**Why no normal microbiota**: the BBB excludes microbes, immune surveillance is constant, and CSF flow physically washes away contaminants. Any bacterium that does enter is rapidly attacked. Sustained microbial presence requires either substantial pathogen virulence (capacity to cross the BBB and resist immune attack) or a breach in the barrier (skull fracture, neurosurgery, indwelling shunt).

## CSF analysis

A lumbar puncture (LP) collects CSF for analysis. The standard panel includes:

**Appearance**: clear (normal), turbid/cloudy (often bacterial), bloody (traumatic tap or subarachnoid hemorrhage), xanthochromic (yellow, suggesting old blood).

**Cell count and differential**: normal is essentially zero, or very low (<5 cells/μL).

**Glucose**: normal is roughly 2/3 of serum glucose (typically 50–80 mg/dL). Low CSF glucose is highly suggestive of bacterial or fungal meningitis (the organisms consume glucose).

**Protein**: normal is 15–45 mg/dL. Elevated with most infections.

**Gram stain and culture**: directly identifies bacteria in many cases of bacterial meningitis.

**Specialized tests**: PCR for viruses or bacterial DNA, antigen tests for specific pathogens, India ink stain for *Cryptococcus*, acid-fast stain for TB meningitis.

The classic patterns:

| Pattern | Cell count | Cell type | Glucose | Protein |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bacterial | >1000/μL | Neutrophils | Low | Very high |
| Viral | 10–500/μL | Lymphocytes | Normal | Modestly elevated |
| Fungal/TB | 10–500/μL | Lymphocytes | Low | High |
| Normal | 0–5/μL | Lymphocytes | 2/3 of serum | <45 mg/dL |

The patterns are useful but not infallible. Early bacterial meningitis can have lymphocytes; partially treated bacterial meningitis can look like aseptic. The CSF picture guides the differential but does not always settle it.

## Bacterial meningitis

A medical emergency. Without treatment, mortality approaches 100%; even with treatment, mortality is 10–25% depending on organism, age, and time to treatment.

Clinical features: classic triad of fever, neck stiffness (nuchal rigidity), and altered mental status. Headache, photophobia, vomiting. In infants, signs are more subtle — irritability, poor feeding, bulging fontanelle. Brudzinski sign (passive neck flexion causes hip flexion) and Kernig sign (resistance to passive knee extension with hip flexed) are classic but have limited sensitivity.

Management urgency: empirical antibiotics should be given immediately after blood cultures, ideally within an hour of presentation. Lumbar puncture follows, but should not delay antibiotics.

- *Neisseria meningitidis* (particularly in college dormitories, military barracks)

- *Streptococcus pneumoniae*

**Empirical antibiotic therapy**: vancomycin plus ceftriaxone covers most cases in adolescents and adults. Add ampicillin for *Listeria* coverage in neonates and adults over 50. Adjust based on culture and sensitivity results.

Adjunctive dexamethasone: corticosteroids reduce morbidity (particularly sensorineural hearing loss) in pneumococcal meningitis. Given just before or with the first antibiotic dose.

### Specific bacterial meningitides

**Pneumococcal meningitis** (*S. pneumoniae*): the most common cause of bacterial meningitis in adults. Often from a contiguous infection (otitis media, sinusitis, pneumonia) or hematogenous spread. Vaccines (PCV13, PCV20) prevent infection by the most common serotypes.

**Meningococcal meningitis** (*N. meningitidis*): can occur in outbreaks. Carried in the throat by 5–25% of healthy people. Transmitted by respiratory droplets. The characteristic petechial or purpuric rash, when present, is highly suggestive — represents disseminated intravascular coagulation from meningococcal sepsis. Vaccines available against serogroups A, C, W, Y (quadrivalent) and B (separate vaccine).

**Hib meningitis**: formerly major, now rare due to Hib conjugate vaccine introduced in the 1990s. The vaccine has been one of the great public health success stories.

**Listeriosis**: *Listeria monocytogenes*. Acquired from contaminated food (soft cheeses, deli meats, raw produce). Particular risk in pregnant women, neonates, elderly, immunocompromised. Causes meningitis, encephalitis, and brainstem infection (rhombencephalitis).

### Tuberculous meningitis

*Mycobacterium tuberculosis* CNS infection. Subacute presentation — fever, headache, mental status changes over weeks. CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis, low glucose, high protein. Cranial nerve palsies (particularly CN VI) common. Diagnosis can be difficult — TB culture takes weeks; PCR has variable sensitivity.

Treatment is prolonged (12+ months) with anti-TB drugs (Chapter 22) plus dexamethasone.

### Neurosyphilis

Discussed in Chapter 23. Late stage of syphilis with various CNS presentations: meningitis (early), tabes dorsalis (loss of dorsal column function), general paresis (psychiatric and cognitive decline), gummatous syphilis (mass lesions). Argyll-Robertson pupil is classic. Diagnosis by CSF serology and PCR. Treatment with IV penicillin.

## Leprosy (Hansen disease)

*Mycobacterium leprae*. Affects peripheral nerves (not CNS proper) and skin. The infection produces granulomatous lesions and progressive peripheral nerve damage, leading to anesthesia, deformity, and disability. The clinical spectrum runs from tuberculoid leprosy (few lesions, strong cellular immunity) to lepromatous leprosy (many lesions, poor cellular immunity).

Treatment is multidrug therapy (dapsone + rifampin + clofazimine) for 1–2 years. WHO programs have substantially reduced global leprosy burden; new cases continue to occur in parts of India, Brazil, Indonesia.

## Toxin-mediated neurological diseases

Two important diseases involve bacterial toxins acting on the nervous system without invasion of CNS tissue.

### Tetanus

*Clostridium tetani*. The bacterium grows in wounds (especially deep puncture wounds) and produces **tetanus toxin (TeNT)**, which travels retrograde up motor neurons to the spinal cord, where it blocks release of inhibitory neurotransmitters (glycine, GABA). The result is unopposed motor neuron firing — spastic paralysis.

Clinical features: lockjaw (trismus from masseter spasm), risus sardonicus (facial muscle spasm producing a grimace), opisthotonos (arched back from extensor muscle spasm), generalized rigidity. Spasms can be triggered by minimal stimuli. Mortality high without supportive care.

Prevention: tetanus toxoid vaccine (in DTaP, Tdap). Booster every 10 years. Tetanus immunoglobulin (passive immunity) given after high-risk wounds in incompletely immunized patients.

### Botulism

We covered the GI form in Chapter 24. The neurological mechanism: **botulinum toxin (BoNT)** blocks acetylcholine release at neuromuscular junctions, causing **flaccid paralysis** (the opposite of tetanus). Cranial nerves are affected first (diplopia, dysphagia, dysarthria), then descending paralysis. Respiratory failure if untreated.

The molecular mechanism is striking. Both TeNT and BoNT cleave SNARE proteins (the proteins that mediate vesicle fusion at synapses). The difference: TeNT preferentially acts on inhibitory neurons in the spinal cord, blocking inhibition; BoNT acts at peripheral cholinergic synapses, blocking excitation. Same toxin family, opposite clinical effects, depending on which synapses they reach.

Treatment includes antitoxin and supportive care, including respiratory support.

## Viral meningitis and encephalitis

**Meningitis**: inflammation of the meninges.

**Encephalitis**: inflammation of the brain parenchyma. Often produces more severe clinical features — altered consciousness, seizures, focal deficits — than meningitis.

**Meningoencephalitis**: both.

Viral meningitis is more common than bacterial but generally less severe. Often called "aseptic meningitis" because cultures are negative on standard media.

### Viral meningitis

Enteroviruses (coxsackievirus, echovirus, enterovirus) cause most cases. Summer and fall seasonality. Most cases self-limited.

Other causes: HSV-2 (particularly recurrent), VZV, EBV, HIV (during seroconversion), West Nile virus, lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus.

Diagnosis: CSF analysis (lymphocytic pleocytosis, normal glucose, modestly elevated protein), PCR for specific viruses.

Treatment: supportive care for most. Acyclovir for HSV.

### Viral encephalitis

**Herpes simplex encephalitis** (HSV-1): the most common cause of sporadic encephalitis in the US. Characteristic temporal lobe involvement on MRI. Without treatment, mortality 70%. With early acyclovir, mortality drops to about 30% but survivors often have significant neurological deficits. Any patient with encephalitis should receive empirical acyclovir while awaiting diagnostic tests.

**Arboviral encephalitis**: arthropod-borne RNA viruses cause seasonal encephalitis:
- **West Nile virus** (WNV): mosquito-borne flavivirus. Common in US summer. Most infections asymptomatic; about 1% develop neuroinvasive disease (meningitis, encephalitis, acute flaccid paralysis). Particularly severe in elderly.
- **Eastern equine encephalitis (EEE)**: rare but extremely severe.
- **Western equine encephalitis (WEE)**, **St. Louis encephalitis**, **La Crosse encephalitis**, **Japanese encephalitis**, **tick-borne encephalitis**: regional varieties.

**Subacute sclerosing panencephalitis (SSPE)**: a rare, fatal, late complication of measles infection (years to decades after the original measles). The defective measles virus persists in CNS tissue and gradually causes neurological deterioration. Now extremely rare because measles incidence is reduced; would become more common if measles re-emerges.

**HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder (HAND)**: HIV in the CNS can cause progressive cognitive decline. Less common with effective ART, but residual cognitive impairment is reported in a fraction of HIV patients on long-term therapy.

### Rabies

*Rabies virus*, a Lyssavirus, family Rhabdoviridae. Acquired from animal bites (dogs in most of the world; bats, raccoons, foxes, skunks in the US). The virus enters peripheral nerves at the bite site, travels retrograde to the spinal cord, then up to the brain. Incubation period typically 1–3 months but can be much longer.

Clinical features: prodrome with fever and paresthesias at the bite site. Then encephalitic phase with characteristic features — hydrophobia (painful spasm of swallowing muscles in response to water), hypersalivation, agitation alternating with lucid intervals, autonomic dysfunction. Less commonly, paralytic ("dumb") form. Once symptoms appear, rabies is essentially uniformly fatal — there are a small number of survivors using the Milwaukee Protocol, but the prognosis remains grim.

Prevention: avoid animal bites. After exposure, **post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP)** is highly effective if given promptly: rabies immunoglobulin (passive immunity, infiltrated around the bite site) plus rabies vaccine (4 doses over 14 days). PEP can prevent rabies even after established exposure — but only before symptoms develop. After symptom onset, treatment is essentially supportive.

Pre-exposure vaccination is recommended for veterinarians, animal handlers, and travelers to endemic areas.

### Polio

*Poliovirus*, an enterovirus. Fecal-oral transmission. Most infections asymptomatic; a small fraction develop **paralytic poliomyelitis** — the virus invades anterior horn motor neurons in the spinal cord, causing flaccid paralysis of limbs.

The Salk vaccine (inactivated, IM) introduced 1955 and the Sabin vaccine (live attenuated, oral) introduced 1961 dramatically reduced polio. The Global Polio Eradication Initiative has reduced cases from 350,000 per year in 1988 to a few dozen per year. As of 2024, wild poliovirus type 2 has been declared eradicated and type 3 nearly so; type 1 persists in Pakistan and Afghanistan. Vaccine-derived poliovirus from the oral live vaccine occasionally causes outbreaks in undervaccinated populations.

## Fungal CNS infections

### Cryptococcal meningitis

*Cryptococcus neoformans* (and *C. gattii* in some regions). Yeasts with prominent polysaccharide capsules that visualize as halos on India ink stain of CSF.

Major cause of meningitis in HIV/AIDS patients with CD4 < 100. Also occurs in transplant recipients and other immunocompromised patients. Less commonly in immunocompetent hosts (*C. gattii*).

Subacute presentation: headache, fever, mental status changes. CSF: lymphocytic pleocytosis, low glucose, high protein, positive India ink, positive cryptococcal antigen.

Treatment: liposomal amphotericin B plus flucytosine for induction, then fluconazole for consolidation and maintenance. In HIV patients, ART is also started, but not immediately (risk of immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome — IRIS — making cryptococcal meningitis worse).

Cryptococcal meningitis is a leading cause of HIV-related mortality globally, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa. Earlier diagnosis (cryptococcal antigen screening) and earlier treatment are public health priorities.

### Other fungal CNS infections

**Coccidioidomycosis**, **histoplasmosis**, **blastomycosis**: endemic mycoses can cause CNS infection (meningitis or focal lesions), particularly in immunocompromised patients.

**Aspergillus** and **Mucor**: can invade the brain in severely immunocompromised hosts, particularly transplant recipients and patients with hematologic malignancies. Often catastrophic.

## Parasitic CNS infections

### Primary amebic meningoencephalitis

*Naegleria fowleri*. Free-living amoeba in warm fresh water. Enters through the nose during swimming or nasal irrigation with contaminated tap water, migrates along olfactory nerves to the brain. Causes a rapidly progressive, almost universally fatal meningoencephalitis. Less than 10 known survivors in history.

Prevention: avoid getting warm freshwater up the nose. Use only sterile or distilled water for nasal irrigation devices.

### Granulomatous amebic encephalitis

*Acanthamoeba* and *Balamuthia mandrillaris*. Subacute presentation in immunocompromised hosts. Very rare; very deadly.

### Cerebral malaria

*Plasmodium falciparum* infection of the brain. Severe complication of malaria — see Chapter 25. Mortality high.

### Neurocysticercosis

*Taenia solium* larval cysts encysting in the brain. Leading cause of acquired epilepsy in many parts of the world. Acquired by ingesting *T. solium* eggs from contaminated food or water (note: not from eating undercooked pork — that gives you the adult tapeworm in the gut; the cysts come from eggs, often from contact with a tapeworm carrier).

Diagnosis: characteristic cystic lesions on brain imaging, serology.

Treatment is nuanced — dead cysts can trigger severe inflammation; treatment of active cysts (with albendazole or praziquantel) may need to be combined with corticosteroids.

### African trypanosomiasis (sleeping sickness)

Discussed in Chapter 25. The second stage of disease involves CNS invasion: sleep disturbance, daytime somnolence, behavioral changes, coma, death.

## Prion diseases

We have discussed prions in Chapters 1 and 6. The prion diseases of the nervous system:

**Sporadic Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (sCJD)**: about 1 case per million per year. Most CJD cases. Rapidly progressive dementia, myoclonus, ataxia. Death within months of onset. No treatment.

**Familial CJD**: 10–15% of CJD cases. Inherited mutations in the PRNP gene.

**Iatrogenic CJD**: from contaminated medical instruments, dura mater grafts, growth hormone from cadaver pituitaries.

**Variant CJD (vCJD)**: acquired from cattle infected with bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE, "mad cow disease"). About 230 cases worldwide, peaking around 2000.

**Kuru**: historic prion disease in Papua New Guinea, transmitted by ritual cannibalism. Discovery by Gajdusek established prion diseases as transmissible.

**Fatal familial insomnia (FFI)**: inherited prion disease with insomnia and autonomic and motor dysfunction. Rare.

**Gerstmann-Sträussler-Scheinker syndrome (GSS)**: inherited prion disease. Rare.

These are uniformly fatal. There is no treatment that alters the course. Diagnosis can be confirmed by 14-3-3 protein in CSF (sensitive but not specific), RT-QuIC assay (more specific), brain MRI, and ultimately autopsy.

## What the chapter is really about

The nervous system is the body's most consequential infection target. Insulted, it does not regenerate. Damaged, the damage is permanent.

The defenses are correspondingly elaborate. The blood-brain barrier, the meningeal layers, the bone enclosure, the CSF flow — all serve to keep pathogens out. When these defenses fail, the time to treatment matters in hours.

For clinical practice, the central pattern is recognition. The triad of fever, headache, and altered mental status, possibly with neck stiffness, in any age group, should trigger immediate concern for meningitis. Empirical antibiotics should follow blood cultures within an hour, even before LP. Empirical acyclovir for suspected encephalitis. Adjustments based on CSF findings, imaging, and the patient's risk factors.

The lessons of this chapter recur across the textbook. Diagnosis is structured. Time is essential. The pathogens are stratified by age, geography, exposure, and immune status. The treatments — for the cases where treatments exist — work when given early. The cases where treatments do not exist (rabies after symptoms, prion disease, advanced cerebral malaria) reinforce the value of prevention and early intervention.

We end the textbook here, on the cases where microbiology matters most acutely. The microbe-human interaction can be commensal, beneficial, pathogenic, or catastrophic. The chapters between Chapter 1 and Chapter 26 have been an attempt to give you the tools to reason about all of those interactions — what the microbes are, what they do, what they need from the host, and how the medical and public health response works when intervention is needed. The next encounter with microbiology will not be a textbook chapter. It will be a patient, a sample, a question. The framework is what carries forward.

## Still puzzling

I do not fully understand why some pathogens (rabies, polio, HSV) are neurotropic and others are not. The receptors that allow CNS access (rabies acetylcholine receptor at neuromuscular junctions, polio CD155, HSV nectin) are present at the periphery. The retrograde transport along axons is exploited by these viruses specifically. Why did neurotropism evolve in some viruses and not others? Presumably the immune privilege of the CNS provides a refuge, but the cost is severe (the host often dies before transmitting). The evolutionary stability of neurotropic viruses is not obvious to me.

## What would change my mind

The prion disease story has held up well as a protein-only mechanism. But the broader category of "prion-like" pathology — protein aggregation diseases including Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, ALS — is being increasingly described in terms similar to classical prion biology. If experimental evidence accumulates that some of these diseases are transmissible between individuals (which has been controversial for years, with some evidence in animal models but limited evidence in humans), the line between prion disease and the major neurodegenerative diseases will need to be redrawn. As of this writing, the consensus is that Alzheimer's and Parkinson's are not transmissible between people under normal conditions.
