# Chapter 01 — An Invisible World

*The history of microbiology is the history of being surprised by what is in a drop of water.*

## TL;DR

For most of human history, the agents of disease were invisible — known by their effects, never by their faces. Microbiology became a science the moment people figured out how to prove that something they could not see caused something they could observe, and the four moves that did the proving (Leeuwenhoek's lens, Pasteur's flask, Koch's culture, Koch's postulates) still shape what counts as evidence today.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

1. **Describe** the spontaneous generation debate and **explain** why Pasteur's swan-neck flask experiment, not argument, settled it.
2. **Apply** Koch's four postulates to a specific infectious disease and **identify** which postulates fail, and why, for a chosen modern pathogen (e.g., HIV, *Mycobacterium leprae*, SARS-CoV-2).
3. **Distinguish** the three domains of life (Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya) by the evidence Woese used and **explain** why viruses and prions do not appear on the tree.
4. **Trace** a single historical claim about disease (Varro's *animalia minuta*, Snow's pump, Koch's anthrax) back to the primary source and **evaluate** what evidence the claim actually rested on.
5. **Read** a binomial species name like *Escherichia coli* correctly and **explain** what each part of the name does in the Linnaean system.
6. **Critique** the claim that "all microbes are harmful" using what the chapter establishes about the human microbiome and beneficial fermentation.

Prerequisites: Curiosity. A willingness to be surprised by a drop of water.

## The question before the answer

Here is something worth thinking about before we go any further.

For thousands of years — all of recorded history, most of human prehistory — people got sick from invisible things. They ate bread that rose because of invisible things. They made wine, beer, cheese, yogurt, soy sauce, kimchi, and kefir because of invisible things. They died in plagues caused by invisible things. A few of them suspected the invisible things were there. None of them could see one.

Then, in 1675, in a textile shop in Delft, a man who inspected fabric for the city looked through a glass bead at a drop of rainwater and saw them moving.

That is an extraordinary thing to sit with. Not that the creatures were there — they had always been there — but that the difference between an invisible world and a visible one was, in the end, the quality of a single piece of ground glass.

What I want to do in this chapter is take you through the story of how we found out what microbes are. Not just the events, but the reasoning — the kinds of evidence people had, what that evidence could and could not prove, and why each new tool forced a revision of the picture. Because microbiology is not a finished subject. It is a discipline that has been wrong in interesting ways, and understanding how it got things wrong is the fastest path to understanding how it gets things right.

## Living with the invisible

The story starts, as many stories in biology do, with food.

In 7000 BC, in a village called Jiahu on the floodplain of the Yellow River, somebody figured out how to ferment rice, honey, and fruit into something alcoholic. We know this because their pottery jars survived, and three thousand years later archaeologists found residue inside them: tartaric acid from grapes or hawthorn fruit, calcium oxalate from honey, beeswax, rice starch breakdown products — the chemical fingerprints of fermentation, preserved in clay. [^1]

The villagers of Jiahu did not know what fermentation was. They did not know that *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, a single-celled fungus, was doing the work. They knew that if you combined these things in a covered jar and waited, the liquid became something different. Something useful. Something worth repeating.

This is the pattern for thousands of years: humans depending on microorganisms without knowing microorganisms exist. Cheese, wine, beer, bread, yogurt, pickled vegetables, kimchi, sauerkraut, fish sauce, kefir, kombucha — every one of these is microbial work, harnessed by people who had no concept of a microbe. The fermentation gets its name from the Latin *fervēre*, to boil, because the carbon dioxide bubbling out of solution looks like boiling. There is no fire. The "boiling" is metabolism.

The implicit trade-off: use the microbes that help you eat, and die from the ones that kill you. You cannot separate the populations. You can only learn which preparations work — which temperatures, which timings, which vessels — and pass the recipe down.

Some people, over this long stretch of time, suspected they were not alone.

In Greece, the explanation was *miasma*: bad air, the exhalations of marshes and rotting matter. The word *malaria* preserves this theory — *mal'aria*, bad air. The theory was wrong about the mechanism (the danger in a swamp is mosquitoes, not the air) but right about a correlation: swampy, stagnant places genuinely were dangerous. A wrong theory that generates correct predictions about where not to camp is still a useful theory.

Hippocrates (460–370 BC) refused supernatural explanations and argued that illness had natural causes in the patient or the environment. His near-contemporary Thucydides (460–395 BC) survived the Athenian plague — which killed perhaps a third of the city between 430 and 410 BC — and recorded a detail so important it often gets buried: those who had recovered from the disease did not catch it again, even when nursing the actively sick. [^2]

That sentence is the first written record of acquired immunity. Thucydides had no idea what was happening inside those bodies. He did not know about antibodies or T cells or immunological memory. He had a correlation, observed in his own neighbors, and the discipline to write it down. The mechanism came two and a half millennia later. The observation came first.

Then there is Varro.

Marcus Terentius Varro (116–27 BC) was a Roman polymath whose agricultural manual, *De Re Rustica*, contains a passage that should be famous. Advising on where to build a farmhouse, he writes:

> "Precautions must also be taken in neighborhood swamps... because certain minute creatures [*animalia minuta*] grow there which cannot be seen by the eye, which float in the air and enter the body through the mouth and nose and there cause serious diseases." [^3]

That is 36 BC predicting the germ theory of disease. Wrong in some details — the route of transmission for malaria is mosquito bite, not airborne particles, and his *minuta* were an inference from correlation, not something he had observed. But right in the essential shape of the claim: *there exist creatures too small to see that cause disease*. That claim is 2,000 years old and still true.

What Varro could not do was produce evidence anyone else was obligated to believe. He had an insight. He did not have a proof. And that, more than any single discovery, is what the next phase of microbiology would supply.

## The discovery arc — how do you prove an invisible cause?

The chapter from here on is one long question, asked four different ways: how do you prove that something you cannot see causes an effect you can observe?

That question has four answers, and each answer is forced by the limits of the previous one. Argument fails — Varro had the right idea and could not convince anyone. So a lens is built, and demonstration replaces argument. But a lens shows you the creatures; it does not show you what they do. So an experiment is designed that rules out the alternative explanation — spontaneous generation. But ruling out spontaneous generation does not show that *this* microbe causes *this* disease. So a culture method is invented, and a postulate set is written down to tell you when the inference is sound. Each move solves the problem the previous move could not.

We will walk through them in order.

### Move 1 — Leeuwenhoek's lens (demonstration replaces argument)

Return to Delft. 1675.

Antonie van Leeuwenhoek was a linen draper who inspected fabric for the city — counting threads, judging weave. Grinding lenses was a hobby he refused to share with anyone. His microscopes were not the compound instruments that natural philosophers used. They were simpler: a single glass bead, mounted in a brass plate, with a pin to hold the specimen and screws to focus. They look primitive. They magnified up to roughly 270 times — more than any compound microscope of the era, because a small sphere of glass is more curved, and more curvature means more magnification. [^4]

He looked at everything. Plaque from his own teeth. Stool from people with diarrhea. Pond water. Pepper infusions. Blood. Rainwater that had stood in a covered tub on his roof for several days.

Through the bead, in the rainwater: things swimming. He counted them, estimated "more than a million" in a single drop, and wrote letters to the Royal Society in London describing what he called *animalcules* — little animals.

The Royal Society was skeptical. They sent a delegation to verify. The delegation confirmed it. In 1680, Leeuwenhoek was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society without ever leaving Delft.

From his drawings — still in the archives — we know he was looking at bacteria, protists, sperm cells, and yeast. He did not use any of those names. Those names came later, from a science that needed centuries to develop the categories.

What the lens changed was not the existence of microbes. They had always been there. What it changed was the *kind of claim* you could make. Before Leeuwenhoek, "there exist tiny creatures in this water" was an argument. After Leeuwenhoek, it was a thing you could show someone through a glass bead. The argument moved from rhetoric to demonstration.

But the lens raised a new question. If you boil a broth — kill everything in it — and leave it open, microbes appear. Where do they come from? Two answers competed.

### Move 2 — Pasteur's flask (experiment beats opinion)

The first answer was *spontaneous generation*: living things arise from non-living matter under the right conditions. Maggots arise from rotting meat. Mice arise from grain and rags. Microbes arise from broth. This was a serious scientific position, defended by serious people. Aristotle held a version of it. So did most natural philosophers up through the 18th century.

The second answer was *biogenesis*: living things come only from other living things. Microbes in broth came from microbes that were already in the air or on the glass.

The argument over which was right ran for two hundred years and was conducted, mostly, with experiments that did not quite settle it.

In 1668, the Italian physician Francesco Redi placed meat in three jars — one open, one sealed, one covered with gauze. Maggots appeared only on the open meat. Maggots came from flies, not from the meat itself. That killed spontaneous generation for animals you could see, but proponents fell back to the microbial scale. Maybe small things arose spontaneously even if large things did not.

In 1745, the English priest John Needham boiled mutton broth, sealed it in flasks, and observed it cloud with microbial growth. He claimed this proved spontaneous generation. In 1768, the Italian Lazzaro Spallanzani repeated the experiment with longer boiling and tighter sealing, and got clear broth. Needham countered that Spallanzani had killed off the "vital force" that spontaneous generation needed. The exchange was a stalemate because each side could explain the other's results without giving up its position. This is what unsettled science looks like.

The settlement came from a chemist with a glassblower.

Louis Pasteur (1822–1895) designed a flask whose neck was bent into a long S-curve — a *swan-neck* flask. He boiled broth in the flask. The neck stayed open. Air could pass freely in and out. But airborne particles — including microbes — would settle in the dip of the S and never reach the broth.

The broth stayed clear. For months. For years.

Then Pasteur tilted the flask so the broth ran into the bend and touched the trapped particles. Within days, the broth clouded with microbial growth.

That was the experiment. Air alone did not generate microbes. Microbes carried by air did. The flask did not just argue against spontaneous generation; it produced a clean dissociation between "exposure to air" and "exposure to airborne microbes" — a distinction the previous experiments could not enforce. Pasteur announced the result in 1859 and 1864. The position held, and spontaneous generation as a scientific hypothesis was over. [^5]

The lesson is methodological. Two hypotheses that explain all the same evidence can be debated forever. The way out is an experimental design that they predict differently. Pasteur did not win by being more eloquent. He won by building a flask whose result could only be explained one way.

Pasteur went on to show that fermentation was the work of living microorganisms, that microbes could be killed by brief heating (giving us *pasteurization*), and that vaccines against chicken cholera, anthrax, and rabies could be made by attenuating the causative organisms. In 1885, he gave the rabies vaccine to a nine-year-old boy named Joseph Meister, who had been bitten by a rabid dog. Meister survived. Pasteur was a chemist. Many doctors of his era resented him for it.

But Pasteur's flask still did not prove that microbes cause specific diseases. It proved microbes come from microbes. The next move had to connect a particular microbe to a particular illness.

### Move 3 — Germ theory and the clinicians who arrived first

Germ theory — the claim that specific microorganisms cause specific diseases — is often credited to Pasteur and Koch, and that credit is roughly right for the experimental proof. But the clinical evidence that diseases were transmissible from person to person, and that hygiene could break the chain, was being assembled by physicians who had no microscope at all.

In 1846, Ignaz Semmelweis was an obstetrician at Vienna General Hospital, where one maternity ward (run by physicians) had a maternal mortality rate around 16% from puerperal fever, while another ward (run by midwives) had a rate around 2%. Semmelweis noticed that the physicians' ward was staffed by doctors who came directly from performing autopsies on women who had died of the same fever. He instituted handwashing in chlorinated lime. Mortality on the physicians' ward dropped to roughly that of the midwives' ward. [^6]

Semmelweis had no germ theory. He talked about "cadaverous particles." His colleagues mostly rejected him. He died in an asylum in 1865 — possibly from sepsis acquired during a beating by guards, which would be the most painful irony in the history of medicine.

In 1854, the London physician John Snow mapped cholera deaths in the Soho neighborhood and found them clustered around a single water pump on Broad Street. He persuaded the local authority to remove the pump handle. The outbreak ended. Snow had no microbe. He had a map and a hypothesis that the disease was transmitted by water, not air. He was right thirty years before Koch isolated *Vibrio cholerae*. [^7]

In 1865, Joseph Lister, a Scottish surgeon, read Pasteur's work on fermentation and inferred that wound infections might also be microbial. He introduced carbolic acid as a surgical antiseptic. Post-surgical mortality at his hospital dropped dramatically. [^8]

The point is that germ theory was assembled from two directions at once: experimentalists working with broths and animals, and clinicians watching patients. Pasteur did not invent germ theory alone. He produced the experimental backbone for a clinical pattern that practicing physicians had already started to recognize.

### Move 4 — Koch's postulates (the rules for inferring cause)

Robert Koch (1843–1910) was a German country doctor who began doing microbiology in a home laboratory paid for by his wife. He was meticulous in a way Pasteur was not. He developed techniques that the field still uses: growing bacteria in **pure culture** on solid medium (agar, suggested to him by Walther Hesse — whose wife Fanny remembered using it in her grandmother's jam recipes, in what is possibly the most consequential cooking tip in the history of biology); staining specimens with dyes so they could be seen; photographing bacteria through the microscope. In 1876 he identified *Bacillus anthracis* as the cause of anthrax. In 1882, *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* as the cause of tuberculosis. In 1883, *Vibrio cholerae* as the cause of cholera. [^9]

A **pure culture** is exactly what it sounds like — a colony grown from a single founding cell, isolated from all other species. Before agar plates, microbiologists worked in liquid broths where contamination was constant. The agar plate let you streak bacteria across a flat surface, separate them physically, let them grow into visible colonies, and pick one. Everything downstream — identifying which species caused which disease, testing which drugs killed which species, sequencing which genomes belonged to which lineage — depended on being able to grow one species at a time.

In 1884, Koch articulated what are now called **Koch's postulates**: the four conditions that must hold before you can claim a microbe causes a disease.

1. **The organism must be present in every case of the disease, and absent from healthy hosts.**
2. **The organism must be isolated from the diseased host and grown in pure culture.**
3. **The cultured organism, introduced to a healthy host, must cause the same disease.**
4. **The organism must be recoverable from that newly diseased host and re-cultured.**

These are not just a checklist. They are a philosophical statement about what it means to claim causation in biology. Postulate 1 is *correlation* — the organism and the disease occur together. Postulate 2 is *isolation* — the organism can be separated from everything else it usually travels with. Postulate 3 is *intervention* — introducing the organism produces the disease, ruling out the possibility that the organism is along for the ride. Postulate 4 is *replication* — the cycle closes; the organism that comes out is the same as the one that went in.

This is, in modern language, a causal inference framework. Postulate 1 is association. Postulate 3 is the intervention that distinguishes association from cause. Postulates 2 and 4 enforce that the entity you are reasoning about is the same entity throughout.

Koch's postulates are also where infectious-disease biology met its first hard wall. The postulates assume the pathogen can be cultured (Postulate 2), produces disease in every infected host (Postulate 1), and produces it in an experimental animal that resembles the human case (Postulate 3). None of these always hold.

- **Viruses fail Postulate 2** by the original wording. Viruses cannot be grown on agar; they need living cells. The postulates had to be loosened to "grown in pure culture *or* propagated in a host" before viral pathogens like polio or influenza could meet them.
- ***Mycobacterium leprae*, the cause of leprosy, fails Postulate 2** absolutely. It has never been grown in pure culture on any artificial medium. The pathogen is identified by morphology, by molecular tests, and by inoculation into the nine-banded armadillo.
- **HIV fails Postulate 3 in its strict form.** The ethical bar to inoculate a healthy human is unmeetable, and there is no animal model that produces AIDS identical to the human disease.
- **Asymptomatic carriers fail Postulate 1.** Many pathogens — *Salmonella* Typhi (Typhoid Mary), *Helicobacter pylori*, SARS-CoV-2 — colonize healthy hosts who never develop disease. The "must be present in every case" half holds; the "absent from healthy hosts" half fails.
- **Polymicrobial infections fail the whole framework.** Periodontal disease, bacterial vaginosis, and inflammatory bowel disease involve consortia of organisms; no single isolate causes the syndrome.

The literature's response to these failures is **Stanley Falkow's molecular Koch's postulates** (1988): a reformulation in terms of virulence genes, not organisms. A gene is implicated in pathogenesis if (1) it is found in pathogenic strains and not in non-pathogenic ones, (2) disrupting it reduces virulence, and (3) restoring it restores virulence. [^10] The shift is from organism-as-cause to molecule-as-cause, which lets the framework absorb pathogens that resist culture, asymptomatic carriage, and polymicrobial syndromes — though it adds new failure modes of its own, which we will revisit in Chapter 15.

What I want you to take from this is the shape of the move, not the postulates as a rule. Each new class of pathogen forced microbiology to extend its framework for inferring cause. The postulates from 1884 are still in use because the *logic* — correlate, isolate, intervene, replicate — is sound, even when the specific procedures have to change.

## A worked example — anthrax in 1876

Walk through Koch on his most famous case.

In 1876, anthrax was killing cattle across rural Germany. Farmers knew the disease moved through herds. Veterinarians had observed rod-shaped objects in the blood of dying animals — Pollender and Davaine had seen them in the 1850s — but it was not clear whether the rods were causing the disease, the result of the disease, or unrelated debris in dead tissue. The question was causation. The evidence was correlation.

Koch worked in a darkened room in his home in Wollstein, in what is now Poland. He had a microscope, a stash of cattle blood from dying animals, and a supply of mice. Here is how the postulates apply, in order.

**Postulate 1 — Present in every case.** Koch examined blood from many sick cattle and consistently found the rod-shaped bacterium *Bacillus anthracis*. He examined blood from healthy cattle and found none. The correlation held tightly. *Postulate 1 satisfied.*

**Postulate 2 — Isolated and grown in pure culture.** Koch took blood from a dying animal and transferred a drop into the aqueous humour of an ox's eye — a clear, nutrient-rich fluid he could use as a growth medium. The bacteria multiplied. He transferred a drop of that growth to a fresh aqueous humour preparation. They grew again. He did this many times, in series, each time using only material from the previous step. By the end, no original animal tissue could have been carried through. The bacteria were growing as a pure culture — descended from his original isolate, with no other organisms present. (Agar plates came later. In 1876 Koch was still using liquid media.) *Postulate 2 satisfied.*

**Postulate 3 — Causes the same disease in a healthy host.** Koch took a sample from his pure culture — many generations removed from the original cow — and inoculated healthy mice. The mice died of anthrax, with the same clinical signs as the cattle. *Postulate 3 satisfied.*

**Postulate 4 — Recoverable from the newly diseased host.** Koch examined the blood of the dead mice. The same rod-shaped bacteria were present. He could re-isolate them and grow them in fresh culture, completing the cycle. *Postulate 4 satisfied.*

Notice what is happening, philosophically. Before Koch, "anthrax is caused by these rods" was a hypothesis. After Koch, it was an inference backed by an unbroken chain: this disease, this organism, isolated, transferred, produces the same disease in a fresh host, where the organism can be re-isolated. The chain has no place for "it was already in the mouse" or "something else came along with the rods." Each postulate closes a possible alternative explanation.

Koch's anthrax paper of 1876 is the founding document of medical microbiology. Within a decade, he and his students had identified the causative organisms of tuberculosis, cholera, diphtheria, typhoid fever, gonorrhea, pneumonia, tetanus, and plague. The pattern Koch worked out on a small mammal in his home lab became the procedure for an entire field.

Compare this with cholera. Snow had Postulate 1 in 1854 — the disease clustered around the Broad Street pump — but he could not isolate *Vibrio cholerae*. He had a strong correlation and no organism in hand. Koch isolated *V. cholerae* in 1883 during an outbreak in Egypt and India. Snow was right thirty years before Koch made it provable. The story is not that Snow was wrong; it is that the framework for what counts as proof shifted, and a piece of clinical reasoning that had been an inference became a demonstration.

## Sorting it — the modern picture of life

Once you can see thousands of different organisms, the next problem is what to call them and how to organize them.

**Linnaeus, 1735.** Carl Linnaeus, a Swedish botanist with a gift for organizing the natural world, published an 11-page booklet called *Systema Naturae*. He sorted all nature into three kingdoms — animal, plant, mineral — and within the living kingdoms, a hierarchy of nested groups: kingdom, class, order, family, genus, species. He also introduced a convention that has outlasted everything else in his system: every species gets a two-word name. The genus, capitalized and italicized. The species, lowercase and italicized. *Homo sapiens*. *Felis catus*. *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*. When you see *E. coli*, that is shorthand for *Escherichia coli* — the first letter of the genus, then the full species name. The convention is followed today by every biology paper in every language, which is the mark of a design that got something exactly right.

Microbes had no place on Linnaeus's tree. They were too small to have been studied systematically when he wrote.

**Whittaker, 1969.** As microscopy and biochemistry matured, kingdoms were added: Protista for single-celled eukaryotes, Monera for prokaryotes, Fungi pulled out from Plantae. Robert Whittaker's five-kingdom tree — Animalia, Plantae, Protista, Fungi, Monera — was the standard in textbooks for the next twenty years.

Notice what is missing from all of these trees: viruses. They are not cells, so there is no branch to put them on.

**Woese and Fox, 1977.** In the late 1960s, the American microbiologist Carl Woese started worrying about a quiet problem: how do you compare two organisms that look nothing alike? Whittaker's tree used visible features. But what feature do you compare between two bacteria that both look like rods?

Woese's answer: stop looking at the outside. Look at a molecule that every cell on Earth must have — the gene encoding the small subunit of ribosomal RNA (**16S rRNA** in prokaryotes; 18S in eukaryotes). Ribosomes make proteins. Every cell needs ribosomes. The 16S sequence is essential, conserved, and present in everything living. If two cells share more 16S sequence, they share more recent ancestry. If they share less, they diverged earlier. The molecule is a clock, ticking at roughly known rates, going back billions of years.

In 1977, Woese and George Fox published their results. [^11] One group of prokaryotes — the methanogens, the halophiles, the thermophiles — had a 16S sequence so different from other bacteria that they could not be on the same branch. They were a separate **domain** of life. Woese called them Archaea.

The implication: all life on Earth falls into three deep groups. **Bacteria. Archaea. Eukarya.** And Archaea, biochemically, look more like Eukarya than like Bacteria — suggesting that the split between those two domains is more recent than the split that produced Bacteria.

Most microbiologists hated this when it came out. They had been calling certain cells "bacteria" for a century. By the 1990s the three-domain tree was the consensus. We now believe all life on Earth shares a single common ancestor, called **LUCA** — the Last Universal Common Ancestor — living perhaps 3.5 to 4 billion years ago. Three lineages descend from LUCA. Every cell you have ever seen, eaten, or been, is in one of those three domains.

A working scale ladder:

| Object | Size |
|--------|------|
| Smallest naked-eye object | ~100 μm |
| Typical human cell | ~10 μm |
| Typical bacterial cell | ~1 μm |
| Typical virus | ~0.1 μm (100 nm) |
| Prion | ~0.01 μm (10 nm) |

Each rung is a factor of ten. From naked-eye visibility to a prion is four orders of magnitude. Microbes are unified not by what they are but by how small they are.

## Common misconceptions

**"All microbes are harmful."** This is almost the opposite of the truth. Of the bacterial species we have characterized, fewer than 100 are routine human pathogens. The human gut alone carries somewhere around 10^13 to 10^14 bacterial cells — a count comparable to the number of human cells in your body — and the overwhelming majority are commensal or beneficial. They produce vitamin K, ferment dietary fiber into short-chain fatty acids your colon uses for fuel, train your immune system, and exclude pathogenic colonizers by occupying the niche. The Human Microbiome Project (2007–2016) catalogued thousands of bacterial species living on healthy people across skin, gut, mouth, and vagina, and the catalog is still expanding. [^12] If your only model of microbes is "things to be killed," you will not be able to make sense of why broad-spectrum antibiotics cause *Clostridioides difficile* overgrowth, why fecal microbiota transplant reverses it, or why germ-free mice raised in sterile isolators have stunted immune development. The pathogens get the press. The commensals do most of the work.

**"Koch's postulates always apply."** They apply when the pathogen can be cultured, produces disease in every infected host, can be tested in an experimental animal, and acts alone. They fail when any of those conditions breaks. HIV cannot ethically be tested in healthy humans and has no perfect animal model. *Mycobacterium leprae* has never been cultured on artificial medium. *H. pylori* colonizes most of the global population without producing disease. Periodontal disease, bacterial vaginosis, and most cancers with a microbial component involve consortia, not single agents. The postulates are a logical framework, not a procedure that runs automatically. Modern microbiology uses molecular Koch's postulates and other extensions — and even those leave cases unresolved. Treating Koch's 1884 list as a settled checklist misreads what causal inference in biology actually requires.

**"Pasteur invented germ theory alone."** Pasteur did the experimental work that proved microbes caused fermentation, spoilage, and several specific diseases. He did not invent the idea. Varro had it in 36 BC. Fracastoro proposed *seminaria contagionis* — "seeds of contagion" — in 1546. Semmelweis acted on it in 1846 without naming it. Snow mapped cholera transmission in 1854 without isolating an organism. Lister translated Pasteur's work into surgical practice in 1865. Koch produced the framework that made the inference rigorous. Germ theory is a collective achievement assembled from experimentalists, clinicians, and epidemiologists, and the standard textbook story that attributes it to one person is a useful simplification that becomes misleading if you stop there.

## What would change my mind

The germ theory of disease — the claim that specific microbial agents cause specific infectious diseases — would need significant revision if a class of disease currently treated as infectious turned out, on repeated independent investigation, to be caused by a non-biological agent that propagates by an unknown mechanism (something that is neither a cell, a virus, nor a prion-like protein-templating system). No such case currently exists; prions extended the category but did not break it. A genuine fourth class — something that transmits, propagates, and causes disease but has no known molecular vector — would force a re-examination of what we mean by an infectious agent. The three-domain phylogeny would similarly need revision if a fourth domain were confirmed by independent rRNA and whole-genome analysis. The CPR (Candidate Phyla Radiation) bacteria, discovered by metagenomics around 2015, have been argued by some researchers to merit a major branch reclassification, but consensus currently places them within Bacteria. `[verify: current status of CPR taxonomy as of 2026]`

## Still puzzling

I do not fully understand why no archaeon has been confirmed as a human pathogen — they live in the gut, they have the genetic flexibility to acquire virulence factors, and yet to date none appear to cause disease, and the various explanations on offer (immune sensor mismatch, slow growth, niche incompatibility) feel partial. I am similarly unsettled by the microbiome's challenge to "one organism, one disease" — many syndromes (IBD, periodontitis, bacterial vaginosis, perhaps obesity) involve community-level shifts that resist being pinned on a single causal agent, and the field is still working out what causal language even means at the community scale. And I want to know what fraction of microbial diversity is still uncultured — the often-cited figure of 99% comes from environmental rRNA surveys and may be revised significantly as new cultivation techniques come online.

This is the LLM-driven build for Chapter 1. You will use an LLM (Claude, ChatGPT, or Gemini) to generate an interactive HTML page that visualizes the chapter's discovery arc. The point is not to draw a pretty timeline; the point is to externalize the reasoning chain so you can see, at a glance, which discoveries depended on which earlier ones, where clinical evidence ran ahead of experimental evidence, and where Koch's postulates can and cannot be applied.

### Why this exists

You just read 5,000 words about how a science came into being. The information is in your head as prose, which means it is structured the way prose structures things: sequentially, with emphasis on what was written most recently. A timeline rearranges the same information by date, and an interactive timeline lets you re-rearrange it by other features — clinical vs. experimental, before vs. after the microscope, applicable to Koch's postulates vs. not. The rearrangement is the learning.

You are also practicing the skill the rest of this book will lean on heavily: getting an LLM to produce a working artifact by specifying it precisely.

### The simulation prompt

Paste the following into your LLM of choice. The framework is **Show / Say / Constrain / Verify** — show the LLM the data, say what you want, constrain the shape, verify the output before trusting it.

````
I am a student of microbiology. Build me a single self-contained
HTML file named 01-discovery-timeline.html. It should run in a
modern browser with no external dependencies (no CDNs, no
frameworks — vanilla HTML, CSS, JavaScript inline).

**SHOW (the data)**

The timeline must include these events. Each event has: date,
person/people, location, the specific contribution, type
(experimental / clinical / theoretical), and whether the event
relates to Koch's postulates (and if so, which postulate it
exemplifies or violates).

- 7000 BC, Jiahu, China. Earliest known fermentation residue.
  (Theoretical/Practical. Pre-microbe awareness. No postulate.)
- 36 BC, Marcus Terentius Varro, Rome. *Animalia minuta*
  hypothesis in *De Re Rustica*. (Theoretical. No postulate.)
- 1546, Girolamo Fracastoro, Italy. *Seminaria contagionis* —
  seeds of contagion. (Theoretical. No postulate.)
- 1668, Francesco Redi, Italy. Meat-in-jars experiment disproving
  spontaneous generation of maggots. (Experimental. No postulate.)
- 1675, Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, Delft. First microscopic
  observation of *animalcules*. (Experimental. No postulate.)
- 1745, John Needham, England. Boiled-broth experiment claiming
  to support spontaneous generation. (Experimental. No postulate.)
- 1768, Lazzaro Spallanzani, Italy. Boiled-and-sealed experiment
  refuting Needham. (Experimental. No postulate.)
- 1846, Ignaz Semmelweis, Vienna. Handwashing intervention
  reduces puerperal fever mortality. (Clinical. No postulate
  named, but evidence consistent with Postulate 1 reasoning.)
- 1854, John Snow, London. Broad Street pump cholera map.
  (Clinical/epidemiological. Postulate 1.)
- 1859/1864, Louis Pasteur, France. Swan-neck flask experiment
  disproving spontaneous generation. (Experimental. No postulate.)
- 1865, Joseph Lister, Glasgow. Carbolic acid antisepsis in
  surgery. (Clinical. Postulate 1 reasoning.)
- 1876, Robert Koch, Wollstein. *Bacillus anthracis* identified
  as cause of anthrax. (Experimental. All four postulates.)
- 1882, Robert Koch. *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*. (Experimental.
  All four postulates.)
- 1883, Robert Koch. *Vibrio cholerae*. (Experimental. All four
  postulates. Note: Snow's 1854 map is now confirmed.)
- 1884, Robert Koch. Postulates formally articulated.
  (Theoretical. The framework itself.)
- 1885, Louis Pasteur. Rabies vaccine, Joseph Meister.
  (Experimental/Clinical. Postulate 3 applied to prevention.)
- 1892, Dmitri Ivanovsky, Russia. Filterable agent of tobacco
  mosaic — first hint of viruses. (Experimental. Postulate 2
  fails — cannot be cultured.)
- 1928, Alexander Fleming, London. Penicillin discovered.
  (Experimental. Downstream of postulates.)
- 1977, Carl Woese and George Fox. 16S rRNA reveals Archaea as
  a third domain. (Experimental. Reshapes the tree.)
- 1982, Stanley Prusiner. Prion hypothesis published.
  (Theoretical. Breaks Postulate 2 — no nucleic acid to
  culture.)
- 1988, Stanley Falkow. Molecular Koch's postulates published.
  (Theoretical. Reformulation for the post-culture era.)
- 2007–2016, Human Microbiome Project. Catalogs commensal flora.
  (Experimental. Challenges "one organism, one disease.")

**SAY (what to build)**

Build a horizontal scrollable timeline. Events appear as nodes
positioned by date along the horizontal axis. Pre-1600 events
are on a compressed log-like scale; 1600 onward is on a linear
scale (so the Golden Age decades are readable). Each node shows
date and a one-line label. Clicking a node opens a side panel
with: full description (2–3 sentences from the data above),
type tag, and the postulate(s) it relates to.

Above the timeline, three toggle controls:
1. "Era zoom" — buttons for All / Ancient / Microscope era /
   Golden Age / Modern. Selecting an era smooth-scrolls and
   zooms to that range.
2. "Filter by type" — checkboxes for Experimental / Clinical /
   Theoretical. Unchecking a type fades those nodes to ~20%
   opacity; clicking still works.
3. "Koch's postulates" — a separate panel on the right side.
   Four checkboxes, one per postulate, plus a free-text input
   for "Hypothetical disease name." When the student checks
   which postulates apply to their hypothetical disease, the
   panel shows: "This pattern is consistent with [closest
   matching pathogen class in the data]." For example, all
   four checked → "classical bacterial pathogen (e.g.,
   anthrax)." Postulate 2 unchecked → "unculturable pathogen
   (e.g., *Mycobacterium leprae*, viruses pre-1949)."

Below the timeline, draw connector lines between events that
have a causal/intellectual dependency. Examples: Pasteur 1859
→ Lister 1865 (Lister cited Pasteur). Snow 1854 → Koch 1883
(cholera causation finally proved). Koch 1884 → Falkow 1988
(reformulation). Hovering a node highlights its connectors.

**CONSTRAIN (the shape)**

- Single HTML file, ~600 lines max. Vanilla JS only. No
  external libraries.
- Color scheme: muted background, three distinguishable colors
  for the three event types. Accessible contrast (WCAG AA).
- Mobile-readable: timeline scrolls horizontally on narrow
  screens; the postulates panel collapses to a button.
- All data hardcoded in a JS array at the top of the script —
  easy for the student to edit and re-run.
- No fabricated dates or attributions. If you are uncertain
  about any date in the data I gave you, do not guess —
  flag it in a comment at the top of the file.

**VERIFY (before trusting the output)**

Before I run this, tell me:
1. Which dates in the data did you cross-check against your
   knowledge, and which (if any) are you uncertain about?
2. Which connector dependencies did you draw, and what is your
   justification for each?
3. Are there any events you would add or remove from the
   timeline if your goal were to teach the discovery arc
   from invisible-to-visible-to-causal-inference?

After your answer, output the complete HTML file in a single
code block.
````

Save the response as `01-discovery-timeline.html` and open it in a browser.

### Exploration tasks

Now use the timeline as a thinking tool, not a thing you built and put away.

1. **Filter to clinical only.** Look at Semmelweis, Snow, and Lister. What did clinical evidence establish that experimental evidence had not yet? How many years separated each clinical observation from its experimental confirmation? What does the gap suggest about how a working physician should treat evidence that is consistent with a pattern but lacks a known mechanism?

2. **Filter to experimental only.** Notice how spontaneous generation comes up three times — Redi (1668), Needham (1745), Spallanzani (1768), Pasteur (1859/1864). The debate kept resurfacing because each experiment left an escape hatch. Identify the escape hatch in each one, and explain why Pasteur's swan-neck flask closed all of them.

3. **Apply Koch's postulates to a real pathogen.** Use the right-side panel. Try HIV first. Which postulates do you check? Which do you leave unchecked? What does the panel return? Repeat for *Mycobacterium leprae*, SARS-CoV-2, and *Helicobacter pylori*. For each, write one sentence on which postulate fails and what modern microbiology uses in its place.

4. **Find the missing link.** The timeline gives you events. Pick any two consecutive nodes and write the sentence that connects them — what did the later discovery use, refute, or extend from the earlier one? Some pairs have clear links (Pasteur → Lister). Some are surprising (Spallanzani → Pasteur, almost a century apart, on essentially the same experiment).

5. **Edit the data.** Open the HTML file in a text editor. The events are in a JS array at the top. Add one event that the chapter mentions but the prompt omitted (Thucydides' observation of acquired immunity in 430 BC, for example, or the 1980 discovery that *Helicobacter pylori* causes ulcers). Re-save, refresh the browser. Watch the timeline rearrange itself around the new data. This is the smallest version of the skill the rest of the book will demand: change one input, see how the picture changes.
