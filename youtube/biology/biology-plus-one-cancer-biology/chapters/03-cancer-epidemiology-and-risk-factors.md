# Chapter 3 — Cancer Epidemiology and Risk Factors

*The rate at which cells accumulate mistakes depends on what you expose them to.*

---

Here is the question that organizes everything in this chapter: given a cell, what makes it more likely to acquire the mutations that lead to cancer?

That question has an answer. Several answers, actually, and they are not mysterious. They are quantified, replicated, and in many cases, actionable. Tobacco use causes roughly 22 percent of cancer deaths worldwide. UV radiation causes roughly 90 percent of skin cancers. HPV causes essentially all cervical cancer. Hepatitis B and C drive most liver cancer in the parts of the world where those infections are common. A pathogenic *BRCA1* variant raises lifetime breast cancer risk to somewhere between 65 and 80 percent. These are not correlations you have to squint to see. These are causes, established by the best evidence medicine knows how to produce.

The reason this chapter needs saying is that cancer is often discussed as though it is mysterious — as though cells simply go wrong for reasons we cannot know, and what happens to you is fate. Some of it is. Some cancers really do arise from replication errors that accumulate despite every protection. But most do not. Most cancers carry the fingerprints of what they were exposed to. Modern cancer genomics can read a tumor's mutation pattern and tell you whether the cell was bathed in tobacco carcinogens, or UV light, or aflatoxin, or APOBEC enzyme activity gone wrong. The signature is in the sequence. The cause is often knowable.

What epidemiology gives you is the prior probability. *Given that this person is a 65-year-old male with a 40 pack-year smoking history and works around asbestos*, what is the probability their lung mass is cancer? That prior shapes everything — the workup, the screening policy, the prevention message. And it shapes the questions a biologist asks. The Bradford Hill criteria — strength, consistency, specificity, temporality, biological gradient, plausibility, coherence, experiment, analogy — are the rules epidemiologists use to decide whether an association is causal. A finding that smoking correlates with lung cancer in twenty independent studies, with a 20-fold relative risk, with a dose-response gradient, with a biologically plausible mechanism, with the right temporal sequence, and with animal experiments that reproduce the effect is not just a correlation. It is a cause.

The honest version of the opening sentence is: some cancers are bad luck, some are environment, some are genes, and most are some combination — but the ratios matter for what we can actually do.

## The global picture

In 2022, the International Agency for Research on Cancer estimated 20 million new cancer diagnoses and 9.7 million deaths worldwide. These numbers come from the GLOBOCAN database, which compiles registry data from over 185 countries. They are the best numbers we have. They are also imperfect — cancer registries are comprehensive in high-income countries and sparse in many low- and middle-income ones. The numbers from rich countries are close to truth. The numbers from poor countries are best-effort extrapolations.

The top five cancers by incidence were lung, breast, colorectal, prostate, and stomach. Lung was also the top killer — about 1.8 million deaths. The leading cancer in women was breast. The pattern shifts dramatically by geography. In sub-Saharan Africa, cervical cancer is still a leading cause of cancer death in women, because HPV vaccination and Pap smear screening are not yet widely available. In East Asia, liver cancer rates are elevated because of hepatitis B and aflatoxin exposure. In the United States and Western Europe, lung cancer rates have been declining for decades as smoking rates dropped, but obesity-related cancers — colorectal, postmenopausal breast, endometrial, pancreatic — are rising.

What is striking is that the *kinds* of cancer track lifestyle and infection patterns more closely than they track genetics. Japanese women in Japan have low rates of breast cancer. Japanese-American women in the United States have nearly the same breast cancer rates as European-American women. The genes did not change. The environment did. And by "environment" the epidemiologist means everything outside the genome — diet, behavior, exposures, healthcare, culture, structure. The genome sets certain limits. The environment determines where within those limits you land.

Cancer is also overwhelmingly a disease of age. The probability that any given cell will accumulate enough mutations to become cancerous in a year is small. The probability that *some* cell, somewhere in your body, will do so over 70 years is much higher. Roughly half of cancer diagnoses occur after age 65. Risk doubles approximately every 15 years through middle age. This is one reason cancer incidence is rising globally — populations are aging — and one reason "winning the war on cancer" is a misleading frame. As long as people live long enough, some cells will accumulate the wrong mutations. You can shift the curve. You cannot eliminate the disease entirely.

## Genes and environment

The dichotomy *genes versus environment* is the cleanest place in cancer epidemiology to find a misleading question.

Around 5 to 10 percent of cancers are caused primarily by inherited high-penetrance mutations. *High-penetrance* means: if you have the mutation, your lifetime risk of cancer is dramatically elevated — typically 50 to 90 percent for the specific cancers associated with that gene. *BRCA1* and *BRCA2* are the most famous. A woman with a pathogenic *BRCA1* variant has a lifetime breast cancer risk of about 65 to 80 percent and a lifetime ovarian cancer risk of about 40 to 60 percent. *TP53* causes Li-Fraumeni syndrome. *APC* causes familial adenomatous polyposis. *MLH1*, *MSH2*, *MSH6*, and *PMS2* cause Lynch syndrome. *RB1* causes retinoblastoma. These mutations run in families. They can be tested for. They are the clearest proof that the sequence of a cell's genome shapes its cancer risk — which is the principle from the previous chapter extended to the population level.

The next layer is moderate-penetrance variants. *CHEK2*, *PALB2*, *ATM* — these confer meaningfully elevated cancer risk but not the 70-percent-lifetime risk of *BRCA1*. A pathogenic *CHEK2* variant roughly doubles a woman's breast cancer risk, from around 12 percent in the general population to around 25 percent. That changes screening recommendations without being the crisis a *BRCA1* variant is.

The deepest layer is low-penetrance common variants — hundreds of single-nucleotide polymorphisms, each shifting risk by 5 to 20 percent, that can be combined into a polygenic risk score. These are beginning to enter clinical practice for some cancers. They are not yet definitive enough for most clinical decisions.

The other 90 to 95 percent of cancers are not driven primarily by inherited mutations. The mutations that cause them are *somatic* — acquired in cells over a lifetime, usually because of environmental exposure. Modern cancer genomics can read the mutational signatures out of a tumor and often reconstruct what the cell was exposed to. A C-to-A transversion at GpCpN motifs is a tobacco signature. A C-to-T transition at dipyrimidines is a UV signature. A pattern of C-to-T transitions at TpCpN motifs implicates APOBEC enzyme activity. The signature is in the DNA.

The interaction is where it gets interesting. A person carrying a *BRCA1* mutation who never gets pregnant, uses estrogen replacement therapy, and drinks heavily has a different cancer trajectory than a *BRCA1* carrier who has children at 22, breastfeeds, and abstains from alcohol. The germline mutation sets the stage. The environment fires the gun. The dichotomy was always wrong.

## Tobacco

Tobacco use causes about 22 percent of cancer deaths worldwide and roughly 30 percent in the United States. Lung cancer is the canonical case — smokers are about 20 times more likely to develop lung cancer than nonsmokers — but tobacco causes cancers of the oral cavity, pharynx, larynx, esophagus, stomach, pancreas, kidney, bladder, cervix, and causes acute myeloid leukemia. It is the largest preventable cause of cancer death in human history.

The chemistry of why is not complicated. Tobacco smoke contains over 70 known human carcinogens — benzo[a]pyrene, N-nitrosamines, polonium-210, formaldehyde, aromatic amines, acetaldehyde, and others. These enter the bloodstream through the lungs, distribute throughout the body, and bind covalently to DNA. The adducts distort the helix in ways that cause replication errors. The errors accumulate over decades. Eventually, in some cell, the errors converge on the genes that matter — typically *TP53*, *KRAS*, *EGFR*, *CDKN2A* in lung cancer — and the lineage transforms.

The dose-response is steep and clean. A heavy smoker (more than 20 cigarettes a day for more than 30 years) has roughly a 20-fold elevated lung cancer risk. A light smoker has a 3-to-5-fold elevated risk. Even passive smoke exposure raises lung cancer risk by about 25 percent. These numbers come from massive cohort studies — the British Doctors Study, which ran from 1951 to 2001 and followed 34,000 male doctors; the American Cancer Society's Cancer Prevention Studies; the Whitehall Studies. They are among the most replicated findings in all of epidemiology.

Quitting at any age reduces risk. Quitting before 40 returns lung cancer risk to nearly that of a never-smoker. Quitting at 60 still cuts the risk substantially over the following decade. The damage is real, and the repair is also real.

## Alcohol

Alcohol causes about 4 percent of cancers globally. The implicated sites are mouth, throat, esophagus, liver, breast, and colon. The mechanism is partly direct — ethanol is metabolized to acetaldehyde, a carcinogen that binds DNA — and partly indirect, through obesity, altered hormone levels, and liver damage.

For breast cancer, even moderate drinking raises risk. Each daily drink raises breast cancer risk by about 7 to 10 percent. A woman who drinks two drinks a day has roughly a 30 to 50 percent higher relative risk than a non-drinker. The absolute increase in any single year is small, but it accumulates over a lifetime of drinking.

For liver cancer, the relationship is more dose-dependent and tied to chronic damage. Heavy alcohol use causes cirrhosis, and cirrhotic livers — scarred, inflamed, constantly regenerating — are fertile ground for hepatocellular carcinoma. Hepatitis B and C, separately and in combination with heavy drinking, accelerate the process. In sub-Saharan Africa and parts of East Asia, aflatoxin contamination of stored grains adds another layer of liver carcinogen.

## Obesity and metabolic factors

Obesity is causally linked to at least 13 different cancers — colorectal, postmenopausal breast, endometrial, esophageal adenocarcinoma, pancreatic, kidney, liver, gallbladder, ovarian, thyroid, gastric cardia, multiple myeloma, and meningioma. The mechanisms are several and overlapping. Adipose tissue is an endocrine organ, not inert storage. Excess fat raises circulating estrogen (which drives some breast and endometrial cancers), elevates insulin and insulin-like growth factor 1 (which signal through proliferative pathways), and creates a chronic low-grade inflammatory state that promotes mutation accumulation and creates a tumor microenvironment that favors cancer growth.

In high-income countries, obesity is now the second most important modifiable cancer risk factor after tobacco. As smoking rates have fallen, obesity rates have risen, and the relative contribution of obesity to cancer mortality has been increasing. The estimated attributable fraction in the United States is around 8 percent and growing.

What makes obesity epidemiologically difficult is that it sits downstream of so many structural factors — diet quality, physical activity, sleep, food access, food economics, the marketing budgets of ultra-processed food companies, and the design of cities. Treating obesity as a personal choice misses most of the causal structure. The cancer epidemiology is clear regardless: more body fat over more years correlates with higher cancer risk in specific tissues, and reducing it reduces risk.

## Diet and physical activity

Diet beyond obesity is harder to study cleanly. Some findings are robust. Processed meat — bacon, sausage, ham, hot dogs — is classified by IARC as Group 1 carcinogenic to humans, primarily for colorectal cancer. The estimated relative risk increase is modest (about 17 percent per 50 grams per day), but the mechanism is established: N-nitroso compounds, heterocyclic amines, and heme iron are the culprits, and the association is consistent across study designs. Red meat is classified as Group 2A — probably carcinogenic. High intake of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and fiber is consistently associated with lower colorectal cancer risk.

Beyond those findings, the diet literature gets messier. Individual micronutrients have been repeatedly studied with inconsistent results. Beta-carotene was a leading anti-cancer candidate in the 1980s based on observational data; when it was tested in large randomized trials in smokers, it actually *increased* lung cancer risk, possibly because of pro-oxidant effects at supraphysiologic doses. Vitamin D has been studied extensively with mixed results. The honest summary is that whole-food dietary patterns matter; individual nutrient supplements rarely do what the observational data suggested they would.

Physical activity reduces cancer risk through multiple pathways — weight regulation, reduced inflammation, improved insulin sensitivity, modulation of circulating hormones, and effects on immune surveillance. Higher activity levels consistently correlate with lower rates of colorectal, postmenopausal breast, endometrial, kidney, esophageal, and bladder cancer, with typical relative risk reductions of 10 to 30 percent for the most active compared to the least active.

## Infections

About 13 percent of cancers worldwide are caused by infectious agents. The list is short and the implications are large.

Human papillomavirus causes essentially all cervical cancer, most anal cancers, and a substantial fraction of oropharyngeal cancers. Two high-risk types — HPV-16 and HPV-18 — account for the majority. The HPV vaccine prevents the initial infection in vaccinated populations. Australia, which implemented universal adolescent HPV vaccination in 2007, is on track to effectively eliminate cervical cancer in the coming decades. The biology here is one of the cleanest cancer-prevention stories ever told.

Hepatitis B and C cause hepatocellular carcinoma. Hepatitis B is preventable by vaccination — the first cancer-preventing vaccine, in widespread use since the 1980s. Hepatitis C is curable with direct-acting antiviral therapy in most cases. Liver cancer rates have declined dramatically in countries with universal hepatitis B vaccination and aggressive hepatitis C treatment.

*Helicobacter pylori* causes the majority of stomach cancers and most gastric MALT lymphomas. The bacterium colonizes the stomach lining, induces chronic inflammation, and over decades drives progression from gastritis through intestinal metaplasia to adenocarcinoma. *H. pylori* is curable with a one-to-two-week antibiotic course. Population-level eradication programs in high-incidence countries are reducing stomach cancer rates.

Epstein-Barr virus is implicated in Burkitt lymphoma (especially in equatorial Africa), nasopharyngeal carcinoma (especially in East Asia), Hodgkin lymphoma, and some gastric cancers. HTLV-1 causes adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma, endemic in parts of Japan and the Caribbean. HIV does not cause cancer directly but suppresses immune surveillance and enables Kaposi sarcoma, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, and cervical cancer.

The infection-to-cancer relationships matter for two reasons. Biologically, they show how viral oncoproteins — HPV E6 and E7 targeting p53 and Rb, hepatitis B X protein, EBV LMP1 — illuminate normal tumor-suppressor function. They are windows into the machinery of cancer, opened by the pathogens that learned to exploit it. Practically, they are prevention targets. The HPV vaccine is the most powerful cancer-prevention intervention developed in the past half century.

## Radiation and the sun

Ionizing radiation — X-rays, gamma rays, alpha and beta particles — damages DNA directly, ionizing molecules and creating reactive species that break strands. Exposure raises cancer risk roughly linearly with dose. The classic studies are of atomic bomb survivors in Hiroshima and Nagasaki, nuclear workers, radon-exposed underground miners, and patients given therapeutic radiation for other diseases. The relative risks per unit dose are well-characterized.

Diagnostic imaging contributes some exposure. A chest X-ray delivers about 0.1 millisievert. A CT scan delivers 5 to 15. The cumulative dose matters. Children are more sensitive than adults. The clinical principle — use imaging when it changes management, use the lowest dose that produces an interpretable image — is *as low as reasonably achievable*.

Radon, a naturally occurring radioactive gas that seeps from soil and rock, is the second leading cause of lung cancer after smoking in the United States. It accumulates in basements. Testing kits are inexpensive. Mitigation, where elevated levels are found, is a one-time installation.

Ultraviolet radiation is non-ionizing but still mutagenic. UV-B causes pyrimidine dimers — the same lesion described in the previous chapter — and is the principal cause of most skin cancers: basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and melanoma. Tanning beds deliver intense UV-A and UV-B and are classified as Group 1 carcinogens. Sunscreen at SPF 30 or higher, protective clothing, and avoiding midday sun are the main preventive measures.

## Occupational and environmental exposures

A long list of chemicals and exposures are known human carcinogens. The IARC's Group 1 list includes asbestos, benzene, vinyl chloride, formaldehyde, chromium VI, nickel compounds, arsenic, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, and dozens more. Most were identified through occupational epidemiology — workers in specific industries developed specific cancers at elevated rates, and identifying the responsible exposure led to regulation.

Asbestos and mesothelioma is the canonical case. Asbestos fibers, inhaled and lodged in the pleura, cause an otherwise rare cancer of the pleural lining. Latency from exposure to diagnosis is typically 30 to 40 years. Mesothelioma was vanishingly rare before industrial asbestos use, and became increasingly common in shipbuilders, construction workers, and insulation installers. Recognition of the link in the 1960s and 70s led to bans in high-income countries, but the long latency means cases are still appearing. In countries where asbestos is still used, the epidemic is ahead of them, not behind.

Fine particulate matter — PM2.5 — is an IARC Group 1 carcinogen. Lung cancer rates rise with chronic exposure. The effect is smaller than tobacco's per individual but enormous in population terms, because air pollution affects everyone in a region rather than only smokers.

## Disparities

Within and between countries, cancer outcomes are unequal in ways that are not explained by biology.

In the United States, Black Americans have higher cancer mortality rates than white Americans for most cancer types. Native Americans have higher mortality for specific cancers. Rural patients have worse outcomes than urban ones, on average. Uninsured patients die more often than insured ones. These patterns are reproduced in registry data across decades.

Some of the disparity reflects biology. Triple-negative breast cancer — a particularly aggressive subtype — is more common in women of African ancestry. Prostate cancer in Black men is more often fast-growing and lethal rather than the slow-growing variant that screening can largely defer. Chronic stress has been mechanistically linked to gene expression changes in tumor cells through cortisol signaling. These differences are real.

Most of the disparity is structural. Black Americans are less likely to be screened at recommended intervals, less likely to receive guideline-concordant treatment, diagnosed at later stages, less likely to be referred to specialized cancer centers, and less likely to participate in clinical trials. Controlling for stage at diagnosis, Black patients often have worse outcomes — which means the healthcare system is delivering different care to different people at identical points in their disease.

Income matters across all racial groups. Uninsured patients are diagnosed later and treated less aggressively. Lack of paid sick leave during treatment is associated with worse outcomes. Distance to a comprehensive cancer center predicts survival in rural areas. These are not subtle effects. They are large, consistent, and produced by the structure of the system delivering care.

Globally, the disparities are starker still. Low- and middle-income countries account for about 70 percent of global cancer deaths and receive a fraction of the cancer-related research funding. Childhood cancer survival rates in low-income countries are around 20 percent. In high-income countries, they are over 80 percent. The biology is identical. The access is not. The difference between those two numbers is not science. It is logistics, resources, and political will.

## What this chapter gives you

The risk factors are knowable. The mechanisms are tractable. The interventions exist. Most cancers are preventable in principle — through reducing tobacco use, vaccinating against HPV and hepatitis B, treating *H. pylori*, screening for early-stage disease, regulating carcinogens, addressing obesity, and closing the structural inequities that determine who gets screened and treated well.

Most cancers do not happen because of one bad gene. They happen because cells accumulate mutations over years, and the rate of accumulation depends on what the cells are exposed to and on the body's capacity to repair damage. Some of those exposures are personal. Many are not. The honest medical conversation about cancer risk is probabilistic: *given these exposures, given this age, given this family history, given this access to care*, what is the prior probability that this person will develop this cancer in the next decade?

The biology of the rest of this book lives downstream of that prior.
