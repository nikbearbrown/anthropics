## Card 01 — Uplift Modeling from Scratch with Claude

- **Source**: Domain — uplift modeling / causal ML core
- **Lane**: BUILD
- **Hook**: "Your model predicts who will buy — but it can't tell you who will buy *because* of your ad. That distinction costs companies millions."
- **The artifact**: ~50-line Python script that generates a synthetic A/B dataset (treatment/control groups with confounding), trains a two-model uplift estimator (T-learner), ranks customers by predicted uplift, and prints a gain table showing cumulative incremental conversions at each decile.
- **Prompt seed**: `claude "Write a Python uplift model: generate 2000 rows with features age/income/prior_purchase, a binary treatment T, and outcome Y where the true CATE varies by segment. Fit a T-learner (two separate LogisticRegression models). Compute individual uplift scores. Print a Qini gain table (deciles, cumulative uplift, random baseline). No external causal libraries — only sklearn and numpy."`
- **Read / check**: Verify Qini curve shows positive area vs random. Check that high-uplift decile outperforms low-uplift decile by at least 2×. Confirm treated and control model coefficients differ meaningfully.
- **Human supplies**: Nothing — fully synthetic data generated by the script.
- **Output medium**: Manim animated bar chart revealing uplift by decile, Qini curve tracing over random baseline.
- **The change**: Before: "target everyone who looks likely to buy." After: "target only those whose purchase probability *increases* due to the intervention."
- **Teardown angle**: The T-learner separates outcome models for treated and control — CATE = E[Y|T=1, X] − E[Y|T=0, X]. The gap between the two models is the causal effect, not the correlation.
- **Exclusions**: No propensity score weighting in this episode; no DragonNet. Keep it T-learner to isolate the concept.
- **Score**: 10

## Card 02 — S-Learner vs T-Learner vs X-Learner: Meta-Learner Showdown

- **Source**: Domain — meta-learner taxonomy in causal ML
- **Lane**: BUILD
- **Hook**: "Three algorithms. Same data. Wildly different CATE estimates. Which meta-learner do you trust?"
- **The artifact**: ~60-line script that generates a synthetic dataset with heterogeneous treatment effects (effect is large for young users, near-zero for older users), then fits S-learner, T-learner, and X-learner, computes each model's CATE estimates, and prints a comparison table of mean absolute error vs ground truth CATE by age quartile.
- **Prompt seed**: `claude "Write a Python meta-learner comparison: synthetic dataset n=3000 with feature age (18-65), binary treatment T, outcome Y = 2 + 0.3*age*(T==1) + noise. Fit: (1) S-learner: one RandomForest with T as feature; (2) T-learner: two separate RandomForests; (3) X-learner: propensity-weighted residual approach. Compute predicted CATE for each. Print MAE vs true CATE, stratified by age quartile. No causalml library — implement X-learner manually."`
- **Read / check**: S-learner should underperform when treatment effect is heterogeneous. X-learner should outperform T-learner when treatment groups are unbalanced. Verify ground truth CATE formula matches the data generating process.
- **Human supplies**: Nothing — fully synthetic data with known ground truth.
- **Output medium**: Manim animated grouped bar chart showing MAE by age quartile for each meta-learner.
- **The change**: Before: "pick one algorithm." After: "the right meta-learner depends on sample size, treatment assignment balance, and whether heterogeneity is in the outcome or the assignment."
- **Teardown angle**: X-learner's key innovation — impute counterfactual outcomes using the other group's model, then regress the imputed treatment effect. This corrects for imbalance the T-learner ignores.
- **Exclusions**: No DML, no neural net learners. This is the taxonomy episode.
- **Score**: 10

## Card 03 — Double/Debiased ML: Partialling Out Confounders

- **Source**: Domain — double machine learning (Chernozhukov et al. 2018)
- **Lane**: BUILD
- **Hook**: "You want the effect of price on demand. But dozens of variables confound both. OLS gives you a biased estimate. Double ML gives you an honest one."
- **The artifact**: ~50-line script implementing DML from scratch: generate synthetic data where X (confounders) affects both treatment D (price) and outcome Y (demand), fit two ML models (residualize Y on X, residualize D on X), then regress Y-residuals on D-residuals. Print the DML estimate vs OLS estimate vs ground truth, and the 95% confidence interval.
- **Prompt seed**: `claude "Implement Double ML (Chernozhukov 2018) from scratch in Python. Synthetic data: 10 confounders X, treatment D = 0.5*X1 + X2 + noise, outcome Y = 1.5*D + 0.8*X1 - 0.6*X3 + noise (true treatment effect = 1.5). Step 1: fit RandomForest to predict Y from X, get residuals. Step 2: fit RandomForest to predict D from X, get residuals. Step 3: regress Y-residuals on D-residuals with OLS. Print: DML estimate, OLS estimate, truth (1.5), 95% CI. No econml library."`
- **Read / check**: DML estimate should be within 0.1 of 1.5. OLS estimate should be biased (further from 1.5). Confidence interval should contain 1.5. Run with different random seeds to confirm stability.
- **Human supplies**: Nothing — fully synthetic.
- **Output medium**: Manim animated number line showing OLS estimate vs DML estimate vs ground truth, with CI bracket.
- **The change**: Before: "use OLS and hope for the best." After: "partial out confounders with ML first, then estimate the causal parameter on clean residuals."
- **Teardown angle**: The "double" means two ML stages — one for the outcome, one for the treatment. The key insight: ML models absorb nonlinear confounding that OLS misses, but you regress residuals linearly to get interpretable causal estimates.
- **Exclusions**: No cross-fitting (sample splitting) in this episode — introduce that in the follow-up on variance.
- **Score**: 9

## Card 04 — Heterogeneous Treatment Effects: Finding Who Benefits

- **Source**: Domain — HTE estimation, subgroup analysis in causal ML
- **Lane**: BUILD
- **Hook**: "The average treatment effect is 2 percentage points. But for one segment it's 15. For another it's negative. The average is hiding the story."
- **The artifact**: ~55-line script: synthetic clinical trial data (n=2000) with features age/biomarker/comorbidity, binary treatment, outcome. Fit a Causal Forest (manual approximation using Random Forest + T-learner), generate CATE scores, then identify the top-benefit and bottom-benefit subgroups and compute their realized outcomes. Print a subgroup comparison table.
- **Prompt seed**: `claude "Write a Python HTE analysis: synthetic RCT data n=2000, features: age (30-80), biomarker (0-1 continuous), comorbidity (binary). True CATE = 0.2*biomarker - 0.003*age + 0.1. Treatment T~Bernoulli(0.5), outcome Y = T*CATE + 0.5*biomarker + noise. Fit T-learner (GradientBoosting). Compute individual CATEs. Split into top/bottom quartile by predicted CATE. For each quartile: print mean CATE, mean Y|T=1, mean Y|T=0, sample size. Identify the feature that best separates high-benefit from low-benefit patients."`
- **Read / check**: Top-quartile CATE should be substantially higher than bottom quartile. The biomarker should emerge as the key moderator. Verify the T-learner's CATE estimates correlate with ground-truth CATE (Pearson r > 0.5).
- **Human supplies**: Nothing — fully synthetic. Real trial: human supplies the de-identified RCT dataset.
- **Output medium**: Manim animated scatter plot of biomarker vs predicted CATE, color-coded by benefit quartile.
- **The change**: Before: "treat everyone the same if ATE is positive." After: "precision targeting — the biomarker tells you who benefits."
- **Teardown angle**: HTE analysis is the bridge from population-level evidence to individual-level decisions. The causal forest / T-learner estimates heterogeneity; the subgroup analysis makes it actionable.
- **Exclusions**: No AIPW, no Targeted Learning. Keep it T-learner + subgroup analysis.
- **Score**: 9

## Card 05 — Policy Learning: Optimal Treatment Assignment Rules

- **Source**: Domain — policy learning, empirical welfare maximization
- **Lane**: BUILD
- **Hook**: "You have CATE estimates for 10,000 users and a budget to treat only 30%. How do you decide who gets the treatment?"
- **The artifact**: ~45-line script: given pre-computed CATE scores (simulated), implement three assignment policies — (1) random, (2) treat top-k by predicted CATE, (3) constrained optimization (treat if CATE > threshold adjusted for budget). Simulate outcomes under each policy. Print a welfare comparison table: expected incremental outcome per 1000 users treated under each policy.
- **Prompt seed**: `claude "Write a Python policy learning comparison. Simulate: 10000 users, true CATE ~ N(0.05, 0.03), features correlated with CATE. A model predicts CATE with noise (correlation=0.7 with truth). Budget: treat top 30%. Compare policies: (1) random 30% selection, (2) treat top 30% by predicted CATE, (3) threshold policy maximizing expected welfare under budget. Compute: actual incremental outcome (true CATE for treated), print policy comparison table. Show how much the optimal policy outperforms random."`
- **Read / check**: Top-k policy should outperform random by approximately 0.7× (correlation-based improvement). Threshold policy should match or slightly beat top-k. Confirm budget constraint is respected (exactly 30% treated).
- **Human supplies**: Nothing — fully synthetic. Real deployment: human supplies the production model's CATE scores and cost structure.
- **Output medium**: Manim animated bar chart comparing welfare gain per 1000 users across the three policies.
- **The change**: Before: "treat everyone who looks like a good candidate." After: "formalize the assignment rule — budget-constrained welfare maximization."
- **Teardown angle**: Policy learning converts CATE estimates into decision rules. The key insight: a noisy CATE estimator can still produce near-optimal policies if the correlation with truth is high enough.
- **Exclusions**: No distributional robustness, no doubly-robust policy evaluation. Focus on the core assignment problem.
- **Score**: 9

## Card 06 — Propensity Score Weighting: Balancing Observational Data

- **Source**: Domain — inverse probability weighting (IPW) in causal ML
- **Lane**: BUILD
- **Hook**: "Your treated users are younger and wealthier. Your control users are older and poorer. A naive comparison is useless. Propensity score weighting fixes it."
- **The artifact**: ~50-line script: generate observational dataset with severe treatment/control imbalance (treatment probability depends on age and income), estimate propensity scores with logistic regression, compute IPW-weighted ATE, compare to naive (unadjusted) ATE and to true ATE. Print a balance table (SMD before/after weighting) for three covariates.
- **Prompt seed**: `claude "Write a Python IPW demonstration: observational data n=3000, covariates age~N(40,10) and income~N(50k,15k). Treatment P(T=1|X) = sigmoid(-2 + 0.05*age + 0.00003*income). True ATE = 3.0. Outcome Y = 3*T + 0.1*age + 0.00002*income + noise. Fit logistic regression for propensity scores. Compute: (1) naive ATE (treated mean - control mean), (2) IPW ATE using 1/p(T|X) weights. Also compute standardized mean differences (SMD) for age and income before and after weighting. Print all three ATEs and the balance table."`
- **Read / check**: Naive ATE should be biased (differs from 3.0 by > 0.5). IPW ATE should be within 0.2 of 3.0. SMD after weighting should be < 0.1 for all covariates.
- **Human supplies**: Nothing — fully synthetic.
- **Output medium**: Manim animated balance plot showing SMD before (red bars) vs after weighting (green bars), then number line comparing the three ATE estimates.
- **The change**: Before: "compare treated vs control naively." After: "reweight controls to look like the treated population, then compare."
- **Teardown angle**: IPW works by down-weighting controls who look nothing like the treated and up-weighting those who do. The propensity score is the balancing score — conditioning on it is equivalent to conditioning on all confounders.
- **Exclusions**: No doubly-robust estimator (AIPW) in this episode. No overlap trimming.
- **Score**: 8

## Card 07 — Causal Discovery: Learning the DAG from Data

- **Source**: Domain — constraint-based causal discovery (PC algorithm)
- **Lane**: BUILD
- **Hook**: "You have 8 variables and no domain knowledge about which causes which. Can you learn the causal graph from correlation patterns alone?"
- **The artifact**: ~50-line script: generate data from a known DAG (5 nodes, 6 edges), run a simplified PC algorithm (independence tests → skeleton → orient v-structures), output the discovered adjacency matrix. Compare discovered edges to ground truth. Print precision, recall, and structural Hamming distance.
- **Prompt seed**: `claude "Implement a simplified PC algorithm for causal discovery in Python. Ground truth DAG: X1->X2, X1->X3, X2->X4, X3->X4, X4->X5, X2->X5. Generate n=2000 samples from linear Gaussian SCM. Implement: (1) correlation-based skeleton (threshold p<0.05 partial correlation tests), (2) v-structure orientation (find X-Z-Y where X,Y independent given nothing but dependent given Z). Compare recovered skeleton and v-structures to ground truth. Print precision, recall, structural Hamming distance."`
- **Read / check**: Skeleton precision should be > 0.7 at n=2000. V-structures should be correctly identified. Print the discovered adjacency matrix and compare visually to the true DAG.
- **Human supplies**: Nothing — fully synthetic with known ground truth DAG.
- **Output medium**: Manim animated DAG construction — nodes appear, then edges animate in as the algorithm discovers them, with correct edges green and errors red.
- **The change**: Before: "assume the causal graph from domain knowledge." After: "test independence relationships in data to constrain the graph."
- **Teardown angle**: The PC algorithm exploits the Markov condition — conditional independencies in data correspond to missing edges in the DAG. It cannot orient all edges (Markov equivalent graphs exist), but it orients v-structures uniquely.
- **Exclusions**: No score-based methods (GES, NOTEARS). No latent variable models. PC algorithm only.
- **Score**: 8

## Card 08 — CATE Validation: Calibration and the Qini Coefficient

- **Source**: Domain — CATE model evaluation and validation
- **Lane**: BUILD
- **Hook**: "You've built a CATE model. How do you know it's actually measuring causal effects and not just correlations?"
- **The artifact**: ~55-line script: train two models (a good T-learner and a deliberately bad model that just predicts outcome, not uplift), then evaluate both using (1) Qini coefficient (area between uplift curve and random), (2) AUUC (area under uplift curve), (3) calibration check — does mean CATE in top decile match the observed treatment effect in that decile? Print a validation scorecard.
- **Prompt seed**: `claude "Write a Python CATE validation script. Synthetic RCT data n=4000, features X (5 variables), true CATE varies by X1 (large for X1>0, near-zero for X1<0). Treatment T~Bernoulli(0.5). Build two models: (1) good T-learner (separate RandomForest for T=1 and T=0 groups), (2) bad model (single RF predicting Y, treating CATE as Y|T=1 prediction). Evaluate both: Qini coefficient, AUUC, decile calibration (compare mean predicted CATE to observed Y|T=1 - Y|T=0 in each decile). Print validation scorecard for both models."`
- **Read / check**: Good T-learner Qini should be > 0.1 (positive). Bad model Qini should be near 0 or negative. Calibration should show T-learner is calibrated in top deciles, bad model is not.
- **Human supplies**: Nothing — synthetic RCT with known ground truth. Real-world: human supplies held-out RCT data for validation.
- **Output medium**: Manim animated Qini curves for both models overlaid, then calibration bar chart by decile.
- **The change**: Before: "trust the CATE model because it was trained on causal data." After: "validate with Qini and calibration — a model that predicts high outcome is not the same as a model that predicts high uplift."
- **Teardown angle**: The Qini coefficient is the causal analog of AUC — it measures how much incremental outcome you capture by targeting in order of predicted CATE vs random. Calibration checks whether the CATE estimate is on the right scale.
- **Exclusions**: No R-learner evaluation, no CATE-Net. Focus on Qini + calibration as the two key validation tools.
- **Score**: 8
