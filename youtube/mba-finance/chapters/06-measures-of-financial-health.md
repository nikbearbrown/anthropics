# Chapter 6 — Measures of Financial Health
*Why the same number can mean triumph for one firm and catastrophe for another.*

---

Two firms each report $30 million of net income for the year. Same number, same line on the income statement.

The first is a small specialty retailer with $100 million of revenue and $80 million of total assets. Thirty million dollars on that base means it earned 30 cents of profit on every dollar of revenue and 37.5 cents on every dollar of assets. By any measure, it is an extraordinary business.

The second is a large industrial manufacturer with $50 billion of revenue and $80 billion of total assets. Thirty million dollars on *that* base means it earned 0.06 cents on every dollar of revenue and 0.04 cents on every dollar of assets. By any measure, it is failing.

Same net income. Opposite stories. What separates them is scale, and the tool for making scale-adjusted comparison possible is the ratio.

The four financial statements you read in Chapter 5 produce dozens of numbers per company. Reading those numbers is necessary but not sufficient. Turning them into a diagnosis — can this company pay its bills, manage its inventory, cover its debt, generate returns? — is what ratio analysis does. This chapter is about how that works, and why it works the way it does.

---

## What a ratio is, and why it beats raw numbers

A financial ratio is the quotient of two numbers, both from the firm's financial statements. Net income divided by revenue. Current assets divided by current liabilities. The technical definition is that simple, and the simplicity is the source of the power.

A ratio does three things that raw numbers cannot.

It controls for scale. The opening puzzle showed exactly this: $30 million of net income is meaningless without a denominator. Once you divide by revenue or by total assets, the number becomes comparable across firms of any size. This is why ratios are the lingua franca of cross-firm analysis.

It surfaces relationships that neither statement alone reveals. Return on assets, for instance, divides net income from the income statement by total assets from the balance sheet. Neither statement separately tells you how productively the firm's assets generate earnings. The ratio does.

It enables benchmarking against industry averages, peer firms, and the firm's own history. A debt-to-equity ratio of 1.5 is neutral in isolation; compared to an industry average of 0.8, it tells you the firm is significantly more leveraged than peers; compared to its own history of 0.5, it tells you something has changed.

Ratios are organized into five families based on the financial dimension they measure: efficiency, liquidity, solvency, market value, and profitability. The five overlap — they are views into one connected reality, not five separate boxes. But each family asks a distinct question, and understanding why the question is distinct is more useful than memorizing the formulas.

One limitation worth naming at the start: ratios describe what has been. They do not predict what will be. A firm with three years of expanding margins can see those margins collapse in year four. And ratios are only as comparable as the underlying accounting — two firms using different inventory methods or different depreciation policies can show very different ratios for genuinely similar businesses. The footnotes matter.

## Efficiency: how well does the firm use what it owns?

The efficiency ratios ask one question in several forms: given the assets on the balance sheet, how hard is the firm working them?

**Total asset turnover** is the cleanest version:

$$\text{Asset Turnover} = \frac{\text{Net Sales}}{\text{Average Total Assets}}$$

A ratio of 1.2 means the firm generates $1.20 of revenue for every dollar of assets it holds. Capital-intensive industries — heavy manufacturing, utilities — have low asset turnover by structural necessity; a steel mill cannot generate many dollars of revenue per dollar of blast furnace. Software and services firms have high turnover because their assets are mostly intangible. Comparing asset turnover across industries is like comparing miles per gallon between a semi-truck and a bicycle. Compare within an industry.

**Accounts receivable turnover** asks how quickly the firm collects what it's owed:

$$\text{AR Turnover} = \frac{\text{Net Credit Sales}}{\text{Average Accounts Receivable}}$$

A firm with $100 million of credit sales and average receivables of $25 million turns its receivables four times per year — meaning it collects every 90 days on average. Fast collection is good for liquidity. Slow collection means cash is sitting in customers' hands instead of the firm's bank account, and some of it may never arrive.

**Inventory turnover** and its companion **days' sales in inventory** measure how quickly the firm sells through its stock:

$$\text{Inventory Turnover} = \frac{\text{Cost of Goods Sold}}{\text{Average Inventory}}$$

$$\text{Days' Sales in Inventory} = \frac{\text{Ending Inventory}}{\text{COGS}} \times 365$$

A grocery chain might hold 20–30 days of inventory. An aerospace manufacturer might hold 200–300 days. Neither is alarming given the industry; the business model dictates the appropriate level. What *is* alarming is a trend: a sporting-goods retailer whose days' sales in inventory rises from 90 to 150 to 243 over three years is accumulating inventory faster than it's selling — a warning that either demand is softening or purchasing decisions are misaligned with reality.

## Liquidity: can the firm pay its bills next month?

Liquidity ratios measure the firm's ability to meet short-term obligations. The time horizon here is less than a year, which is why the ratios live almost entirely on the current portion of the balance sheet.

**Current ratio:**

$$\text{Current Ratio} = \frac{\text{Current Assets}}{\text{Current Liabilities}}$$

A ratio of 2.0 means current assets are twice current liabilities — a comfortable buffer. Below 1.0 means the firm's short-term obligations exceed its short-term resources, which is worrying unless the firm has reliable access to credit lines or generates cash so quickly that it doesn't need a buffer (fast-food chains fit this description).

**Quick ratio** (sometimes called the acid-test ratio) strips out the slowest-converting current assets:

$$\text{Quick Ratio} = \frac{\text{Cash} + \text{Short-Term Investments} + \text{Accounts Receivable}}{\text{Current Liabilities}}$$

Inventory may take months to convert to cash. Prepaid expenses may never convert at all. The quick ratio asks the harder question: can the firm meet its current liabilities from *genuinely* liquid assets, without relying on selling its product? For a firm whose inventory is seasonal, whose receivables are clean, and whose suppliers are patient, the current ratio is fine. For a firm in financial stress, the quick ratio is the number that matters.

**Cash ratio** is the most conservative:

$$\text{Cash Ratio} = \frac{\text{Cash and Cash Equivalents}}{\text{Current Liabilities}}$$

Most healthy firms run cash ratios well below 1.0. Sitting on enough cash to cover all current liabilities entirely is inefficient — cash should be working. Apple is an exception, deliberately maintaining enormous cash and securities balances that exceed its current liabilities by a wide margin; that's a strategic choice about capital allocation, not a default state of financial health.

The three ratios form a progression from generous to conservative in what they count as liquid. Using all three together tells you something using any one alone cannot: where the firm's short-term resources are concentrated.

## Solvency: can the firm survive the long run?

Solvency ratios shift the time horizon from months to years and ask whether the firm can service its long-term debt.

**Debt-to-assets** measures what fraction of the asset base is financed by creditors:

$$\text{Debt-to-Assets} = \frac{\text{Total Liabilities}}{\text{Total Assets}}$$

A ratio of 0.6 means 60 cents of every asset dollar was borrowed. The remaining 40 cents came from equity. Higher ratios mean more leverage — which amplifies both gains and losses.

**Debt-to-equity** expresses the same information as a ratio of debt to equity rather than debt to assets:

$$\text{Debt-to-Equity} = \frac{\text{Total Liabilities}}{\text{Total Equity}}$$

The two ratios contain mathematically equivalent information; you can derive one from the other. Practitioners use whichever is more conventional in their industry.

**Times interest earned (TIE)** is the most immediately actionable solvency ratio:

$$\text{TIE} = \frac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{Interest Expense}}$$

A TIE of 5 means the firm's operating earnings are five times its interest obligations. Lenders and bond-rating agencies watch this number closely. Below 1.5 is concerning. Below 1.0 means the firm cannot cover interest expense from operating earnings — a near-term default risk.

What makes TIE particularly important for the equity research project is that many corporate debt agreements include minimum TIE requirements as covenants. If the firm's TIE drops below the covenant level, the lender can call the debt or impose penalties. The 10-K's debt footnotes will spell out these covenants; reading them is part of the solvency analysis.

## Market value: what does the stock price reflect?

Market-value ratios connect the stock price to the firm's financial statements. They are the bridge between accounting and investment analysis.

**Earnings per share (EPS):**

$$\text{EPS} = \frac{\text{Net Income} - \text{Preferred Dividends}}{\text{Weighted Average Common Shares Outstanding}}$$

Public companies report two versions: basic EPS (above) and diluted EPS, which adjusts the share count for all securities that *could* be converted into shares — options, convertible bonds, warrants. Diluted EPS is slightly lower and is the more conservative measure. It's the number analysts use.

A subtlety that trips up many first-time readers: a firm can increase EPS without increasing earnings, simply by buying back its own stock. Reduce the denominator and the quotient rises mechanically. This is why many large public companies — Apple is the most aggressive — run buyback programs at the scale of tens of billions of dollars per year. Whether a specific buyback creates or destroys shareholder value depends on whether the firm bought at a price above or below intrinsic value. A buyback above intrinsic value is the economic equivalent of management gifting shareholders' money to whoever sold.

**Price-to-earnings ratio (P/E):**

$$\text{P/E} = \frac{\text{Market Price per Share}}{\text{EPS}}$$

A P/E of 20 means investors are paying $20 for each dollar of annual earnings. Trailing P/E uses the past twelve months' EPS; forward P/E uses analysts' estimate of next year's. Forward P/E is what equity research uses — you're buying future earnings, not past ones. A forward P/E meaningfully below the industry average suggests the market views the firm's earnings as less reliable or less growth-oriented than peers. Meaningfully above suggests the market expects faster growth.

**Book value per share** compares accounting equity to the market price:

$$\text{Book Value per Share} = \frac{\text{Total Equity} - \text{Preferred Equity}}{\text{Common Shares Outstanding}}$$

The ratio of market price to book value per share is the **price-to-book (P/B) ratio**. A P/B above 1 means the market values the firm at more than its accounting equity — implicitly pricing intangibles like brand, intellectual property, and growth opportunities that the balance sheet doesn't capture. A P/B below 1 means the market values the firm at *less* than accounting equity, which can signal undervaluation or a signal that the reported book value is overstated.

## Profitability: what does the firm earn on what it owns and runs?

Profitability ratios measure how much profit the firm generates from its operations and capital base.

**Profit margin:**

$$\text{Profit Margin} = \frac{\text{Net Income}}{\text{Net Sales}}$$

Apple's profit margin runs around 25%. Walmart's runs around 2–3%. Both are extraordinarily successful businesses. The difference is not a measure of quality; it reflects radically different business models. A grocery chain with a 25% margin would be under investigation for price-fixing. Compare within industries.

**Return on assets (ROA):**

$$\text{ROA} = \frac{\text{Net Income}}{\text{Average Total Assets}}$$

ROA captures both pricing efficiency and asset efficiency in one number. It answers: for every dollar of assets on the balance sheet, how many cents of profit did the firm earn this year?

**Return on equity (ROE):**

$$\text{ROE} = \frac{\text{Net Income} - \text{Preferred Dividends}}{\text{Average Common Equity}}$$

ROE is the single most-watched profitability metric in equity analysis. It tells you what the firm earns on the capital that common shareholders have invested. A 2020 cross-industry snapshot showed the US market average around 8%, with ranges from roughly negative 47% (air transportation during COVID) to over 70% in some health-care subsegments. A stable industrial firm with ROE in the high teens or twenties is performing strongly. A software firm with a light asset base can achieve ROE above 30% without unusual leverage.

The problem with ROE in isolation is that it can be inflated by debt. Finance the firm more heavily with debt, shrink the equity denominator, and ROE rises — even if operating performance is flat. This is where DuPont decomposition becomes the essential tool.

---

## DuPont: what is actually driving ROE?

Start with the standard formula and multiply by 1 twice in a way that rearranges the algebra:

$$\text{ROE} = \frac{\text{Net Income}}{\text{Equity}} \times \frac{\text{Sales}}{\text{Sales}} \times \frac{\text{Total Assets}}{\text{Total Assets}}$$

Rearranging:

$$\text{ROE} = \underbrace{\frac{\text{Net Income}}{\text{Sales}}}_{\text{Profit Margin}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{Sales}}{\text{Total Assets}}}_{\text{Asset Turnover}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{Total Assets}}{\text{Equity}}}_{\text{Equity Multiplier}}$$

Each factor is a ratio already introduced. The equity multiplier — total assets over total equity — is leverage expressed differently. A firm with no debt has an equity multiplier of 1.0. A firm with assets equal to twice its equity has a multiplier of 2.0.

The decomposition reveals the three independent drivers of ROE: operating efficiency (how much of each revenue dollar becomes profit), asset efficiency (how much revenue each asset dollar generates), and financial leverage (how much of the asset base is financed by debt rather than equity).

Here is why this matters analytically. Two firms can show identical ROE through very different means:

**Firm A** — profit margin 10%, asset turnover 1.0, equity multiplier 2.5. ROE = 25%. The returns are substantially driven by leverage.

**Firm B** — profit margin 25%, asset turnover 1.0, equity multiplier 1.0. ROE = 25%. The returns are entirely driven by operational excellence.

Both show 25% ROE. But if interest rates rise, or if business deteriorates, Firm A's returns can collapse quickly — the leverage that amplified the gains now amplifies the losses. Firm B's returns are more durable. The single ROE number hides this structure; DuPont surfaces it.

<!-- → [TABLE: DuPont comparison of two firms with identical ROE — columns: firm, profit margin, asset turnover, equity multiplier, ROE, what happens in a downturn — should make the fragility contrast visual] -->

A full DuPont analysis on a real industrial firm: net sales $5,000M, net income $400M, average total assets $4,000M, average equity $1,500M.

- Profit margin: $400M / $5,000M = 8%
- Asset turnover: $5,000M / $4,000M = 1.25
- Equity multiplier: $4,000M / $1,500M = 2.67

ROE = 8% × 1.25 × 2.67 = **26.7%**

A peer firm with identical margin and turnover but no debt — equity multiplier of 1.0 — would earn 8% × 1.25 × 1.0 = **10%**. The leverage adds 17 percentage points of ROE. That 17-point contribution will be the first thing that disappears if the firm's earnings weaken.

<!-- → [CHART: bar chart showing DuPont decomposition for five industries — stacked bars showing profit margin contribution, asset turnover contribution, leverage contribution to ROE — student should see how different industries achieve their ROE through different means] -->

Industry context matters for ROE just as it does for every other ratio. A 13% ROE for a recreational-products company is above its industry norm and impressive. A 13% ROE for a software firm is below its industry norm and worth investigating. The ROE table from a 2020 US industry snapshot shows this range clearly: health-care facilities near 70%, software systems near 28%, the total market average near 8%, air transportation negative 47% during the pandemic. None of these numbers means anything without the industry context.

## The system of ratios

No single ratio is the diagnosis. The diagnosis comes from reading all five families together.

A firm can look healthy on liquidity and dangerous on solvency — plentiful current assets, heavy long-term leverage. It can show strong profit margins with poor asset turnover, or strong asset turnover with margins too thin to survive a revenue decline. The DuPont decomposition can show that an impressive ROE is entirely a function of leverage that would unwind in a downturn. The market-value ratios can show that despite strong fundamentals, the stock is priced for perfection — or priced as if the market has already written the firm off.

Working through all five families for a given firm is not tedious box-checking. Each ratio asks a question the others don't, and the questions together form an interrogation. At the end of the interrogation, you have a picture of what kind of firm this is: how it generates returns, where it is fragile, what it is priced to do, and where the risks are concentrated.

That picture is what a ratio analysis actually delivers. For the equity research project, it becomes Section 4 of your report. The valuation and capital-structure analysis in subsequent chapters — the DCF model, the WACC calculation, the forecasting — assume you have this picture in hand before you start.

## What would change my mind

The chapter argues the five-family framework is the right organizing structure for diagnosing a firm's financial health. I would revise if (a) industry-specific frameworks consistently outperformed it within their sectors — there is evidence for this in financial-firm analysis, where bank-specific ratios (Tier 1 capital, net interest margin, efficiency ratio) largely replace the general framework — but for industrials and consumer firms the five families remain the working consensus, or (b) machine-learning firm-classification approaches produced diagnostics more reliably useful than human-readable ratios. Some quantitative funds use this approach. Fundamental analysis still runs on ratios.

## Still puzzling

The cleanest unresolved tension in ratio analysis is the difference between using ratios as a diagnosis — understanding one firm deeply — and using them as a screen — filtering many firms quickly to find candidates for deeper work. The rules of interpretation genuinely differ. A firm with high leverage might fail a screen and be explained by a diagnosis; a firm with low ROE might fail a screen and pass a diagnosis if the underlying economics are strong. I have not found a clean way to teach both uses in a single chapter without conflating them. The project's structure tilts toward diagnosis. Screen-driven analysis is a topic for a later course.
