# Introduction

A domain expert sits at a desk, late, holding a document that looks finished.

It is a creative brief, or a case memo, or a discharge summary, or a chapter — the artifact varies by trade, but the moment does not. Forty seconds ago there was a blank screen and a prompt. Now there are headings, sections, a confident professional register, the right vocabulary in the right places. The thing on the screen reads the way work in this field is supposed to read. The expert skims it, and something is wrong, and they cannot yet name what.

That faint unease is the subject of this book.

It is not the unease of bad output. Bad output is easy. Ugly logos, broken code, a citation to a paper that does not exist — these announce themselves, and a professional rejects them in a glance. The dangerous output is the output that looks done. It arrives polished enough to stop your judgment before your judgment has begun, and it does this most reliably to the people best equipped to catch it, because they are the ones who recognize the shape of competent work and trust the recognition.

There is a precise mechanism behind that feeling, and the first chapter takes it apart. For now, the name will do: the fluency trap. The book is, at bottom, a sustained argument that the trap is real, that it is closing on a great many fields at once, and that there is a specific structural defense — not refusing the tools, which protects nothing, but knowing exactly where in your work the decisions live and refusing to let the machine make those.

That defense has a name too, and it is the title. AI+1. Read the equation literally: a domain expert — the **1** — plus AI fluency. You are the 1. The "+1" is not the tool bolted onto a competent professional; the 1 is the professional, kept load-bearing, with the AI layered on top for the things AI does well. Speed. Variation. The friction of a first draft. The frame is not about balance or supervision or keeping a human vaguely in the loop. It is about which layer carries your name when the work goes out the door.

This book teaches one thing, in order, by building one object. The object is a short, AI-native textbook — yours, in your field — and the pipeline that produces it runs from a two-hour planning session through automated drafting, a human rewrite, a finishing pass, and a final build into something you can sell. The running example throughout is a graphic designer with a decade of client work and a single anchor account, building a book called *AI for Designers: A Practitioner's Guide*. You will watch that book get made at every stage, including the stages where it nearly goes wrong.

The promise is narrow and I want to be honest about its edges. By the end you will have a rough draft of your own AI+1 textbook, you will know precisely where your judgment is the product and where the pipeline runs without you, and you will be able to explain to a skeptical colleague why the two-hour planning session is the highest-leverage work in the entire process. That is the trade this book makes: it teaches less than a survey would, and teaches it in sequence, because the thing worth learning here is judgment, and judgment is learned in order or not at all.

The chapters are not a list of topics. They are a route. The artifact at the end — a forty-line scaffold, a directory of files, a draft that arrived in an afternoon — turns out to be cheap. It was always going to be cheap; that is what the tools are for. The thing that is not cheap, the thing that survives the whole pipeline and is the actual product, is the layer of you that the machine cannot reach.

That layer has a place where it gets sewn into the work. By the time the book names it, you will have felt it for eight chapters.

Let's begin where the trap does.
