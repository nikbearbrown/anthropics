## Candidate 01 — The Water Bottle Paradox: Why Price Lives in the Relationship, Not the Product
- Source: `mba-case-studies/chapters/07-pricing-framework.md`
- Topic: STRATEGY & CASE ANALYSIS
- Hook: A bottle of water is worth $1 at a grocery store and $20 at the end of a desert hike — same molecules, same bottle, completely different price. The value is not in the thing.
- Key case: Bottled water at a grocery store vs. at the end of a long hike — identical product, radically different willingness to pay — used to open the chapter's foundational insight.
- The Question: If price is not a property of a product, how do you ever know what to charge?
- Core idea: Price is a property of the relationship between a product and a buyer at a specific moment — defined by willingness to pay (ceiling), cost-plus (floor), and competitor pricing (anchor). Strong pricing analysis triangulates all three and places the actual price deliberately within the resulting range.
- Visual object: Three-lens diagram — ceiling (value-based), floor (cost-plus), anchor (competitor) converging on a pricing range, with "pricing power zone" and "commodity zone" labeled.
- Manim move: accumulate (three separate lines build and then collapse into a single range band); transform (commodity scenario where ceiling barely clears floor, contrasted against pricing-power scenario)
- Example seed: An enterprise software company builds a tool that reduces finance team month-end close time by 40%. At a fully-loaded labor rate of $80/hr and 2,000 hours of annual close work, that is $64,000 in value delivered. A 50% value-capture split suggests a $32,000 price. A competitor charges $25,000. Cost-plus floor is $18,000. The three lenses converge: $28,000–$32,000 is the zone. The video shows how the triangle tightens.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: c2v (enterprise office, finance team at computers)
- Prerequisites: Basic understanding of profit margins; no framework knowledge needed
- Exclusions: Omit portfolio pricing complexity, loss-leader mechanics, penetration vs. skimming strategy detail
- Score: 10/10

## Candidate 02 — The BCG Matrix Cash Machine: Why Your Best Business Funds Your Riskiest Bet
- Source: `mba-case-studies/chapters/13-bcg-matrix-framework.md`
- Topic: STRATEGY & CASE ANALYSIS
- Hook: A profitable, boring business with no growth is the engine that funds the exciting, cash-burning business that everyone actually talks about. Treat them the same way and you destroy both.
- Key case: Bruce Henderson's 1970 two-page memo that sorted all business units into Stars, Cash Cows, Question Marks, and Dogs — changing capital allocation logic for fifty years.
- The Question: How do you know which businesses to pour money into and which to harvest — when they all look like they need more resources?
- Core idea: Market growth rate is a proxy for cash consumption; relative market share is a proxy for cost advantage via the experience curve. Together they determine whether a business unit generates or consumes cash — and whether that generation/consumption is temporary or structural. A healthy portfolio has enough Cash Cows to self-fund its Stars without external capital.
- Visual object: 2×2 BCG matrix with cash-flow arrows between quadrants — Cows feeding Stars, Stars maturing into Cows, Question Marks either ascending or decaying to Dogs.
- Manim move: spread (four quadrants build from center); accumulate (cash arrows appear sequentially showing the funding flow); decay (Question Mark that receives half-hearted investment slides toward Dog)
- Example seed: A consumer electronics company has a flagship phone (Cash Cow: 28% relative share, 8% market growth, $400M free cash flow) and a smart home division (Star: 12% relative share, 35% growth, burning $150M). The phone funds the smart home bet. The video traces the dollar from the boring business to the exciting one — and shows what happens when a CFO cuts the Star's funding to improve near-term earnings.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: c2v (boardroom with portfolio chart), geo (market growth chart)
- Prerequisites: Basic understanding of revenue and profit; no framework knowledge needed
- Exclusions: Omit TAM/SAM/SOM detail, experience curve math derivation, platform-business exceptions to the framework
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 03 — The Synergy Trap: Why Most Acquisitions Destroy the Value They Buy
- Source: `mba-case-studies/chapters/09-mergers-and-acquisitions-framework.md`
- Topic: STRATEGY & CASE ANALYSIS
- Hook: AOL paid $165 billion for Time Warner and wrote down $99 billion in losses within two years. The strategic logic was real. The analysis was wrong about something more fundamental than the strategy.
- Key case: AOL-Time Warner (2000): content plus distribution, dial-up plus cable, the largest corporate merger in history becoming one of the greatest value destructions in corporate history.
- The Question: If the synergies were real, why did the deal destroy almost everything it bought?
- Core idea: Acquiring companies systematically overbid because synergy projections are optimistic and competitive pressure pushes premiums above the level synergies justify. The net benefit test — present value of synergies minus the premium paid minus integration costs — is the calculation most acquirers lose before the deal closes. AOL's specific failure was a technological trajectory problem: their business model did not survive broadband, and they were acquiring a position in a world that was already passing.
- Visual object: Net benefit equation with four bars: standalone value + synergies − premium − integration costs = net benefit (bar can go negative); a timeline showing synergy realization windows (cost synergies arrive early, revenue synergies arrive late and are the most likely to disappoint).
- Manim move: accumulate (bars build left to right); split (synergy timeline splits into cost-synergy track vs. revenue-synergy track, showing how far out revenue actually arrives); collapse (the net benefit bar drops below zero when premium is inflated and revenue synergies disappoint)
- Example seed: A deal generates $500M in projected synergies. The acquiring company pays a $700M premium. Integration costs another $150M. Net benefit: −$350M before any risk adjustment. The video shows this calculation on screen and then asks: why do boards approve deals where the math fails this obviously? The answer is momentum, narrative, and who is incentivized to walk away.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: c2v (boardroom handshake, deal announcement), geo (timeline chart)
- Prerequisites: Basic understanding of revenue, profit, and company value; no prior framework knowledge needed
- Exclusions: Omit DCF mechanics detail, comparable company method specifics, regulatory review process
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 04 — The Five Forces Nobody Taught You: Why Industry Structure Beats Execution
- Source: `mba-case-studies/chapters/12-strategic-frameworks-porters-five-forces-value-chain.md`
- Topic: STRATEGY & CASE ANALYSIS
- Hook: A well-run company in a structurally bad industry earns worse returns than an equally well-run company in a structurally good one. Porter's claim made strategists uncomfortable in 1979. It still does.
- Key case: Pharmaceutical company at patent expiration — the moment when all five forces transform simultaneously: entry barriers collapse, substitutes (generics) arrive, buyer power increases, the competitive structure changes overnight.
- The Question: If execution matters, why do some entire industries persistently earn high margins while others persistently earn almost nothing — regardless of how good any individual company is?
- Core idea: Five structural forces determine how much of the value an industry creates gets captured by the companies competing in it vs. being competed away to suppliers, customers, or new entrants. The forces interact — analyzing them independently misses how a change in one (patent expiration collapsing entry barriers) propagates through others (substitutes arrive, buyer power surges). Industry structure is the game; company execution is how well you play within it.
- Visual object: Five-force diagram centered on competitive rivalry, with four surrounding forces and arrows showing direction of competitive pressure — then a pharmaceutical lifecycle overlay showing how patent expiration changes every arrow simultaneously.
- Manim move: scan (the five forces appear and light up around the central rivalry node); transform (pharmaceutical pre-patent state morphs to post-patent state, arrows reverse or intensify); trace (dollar value flows from the industry to each force, showing who captures what)
- Example seed: The airline industry earns near-zero average returns despite high operational complexity and genuine managerial skill. The video maps the five forces on airlines — intense rivalry (commoditized seats, price-sensitive travelers), high buyer power (aggregator platforms), high supplier power (jet fuel duopoly, labor unions, Boeing/Airbus), substitute threat (videoconferencing for business travel) — and shows why structure produces chronic underperformance regardless of which carrier you examine.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: c2v (boardroom strategy session, pharmaceutical lab), geo (industry profitability comparison chart)
- Prerequisites: Basic business vocabulary (revenue, profit, competition); no prior framework knowledge
- Exclusions: Omit value chain analysis detail (covered separately), platform/digital adaptation caveats, generic strategies discussion
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 05 — The New Coke Problem: Why Getting the Research Right Is Not Enough
- Source: `mba-case-studies/chapters/06-new-product-introduction-framework.md`
- Topic: STRATEGY & CASE ANALYSIS
- Hook: Coca-Cola ran rigorous blind taste tests. Consumers preferred the new formula. The research was correct. The product failed within 79 days.
- Key case: New Coke (1985) — a case study in answering the wrong question precisely. The research measured flavor preference; the product was a cultural artifact. Changing the formula was perceived not as an improvement but as a betrayal of something that belonged to the people who drank it.
- The Question: How can you do the research correctly and still be completely wrong about whether a product will succeed?
- Core idea: A product launch framework — financing, value chain configuration, break-even analysis — organizes all the components correctly. But it assumes you have identified what product you are actually launching and what customers value about it. If that prior question is wrong, rigorous framework analysis produces precise answers to the wrong question. The framework cannot protect against misspecification of the product itself.
- Visual object: Break-even diagram (revenue line crossing total cost line) with a highlighted "but" annotation — then a two-panel contrast: the New Coke framework analysis (all green checkmarks: financing fine, value chain existing, break-even trivial) vs. the actual failure dimension (customer identity, cultural meaning, brand as owned object) which sits entirely outside the framework's scope.
- Manim move: morph (framework analysis checklist transforms from all-green to the actual failure dimension that framework cannot see); split (the analytical space splits into "what framework covers" vs. "what determined the outcome")
- Example seed: A framework analysis of New Coke in 1985: financing trivial (ample cash), value chain already in place (existing manufacturing and distribution), market enormous (highest brand recognition on earth), break-even not a concern. All boxes checked. Framework said proceed. The video asks: what question was the research actually answering? "Do consumers prefer this flavor?" — correct answer: yes. What question needed answering? "What do consumers value about Coca-Cola?" — never asked.
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: c2v (grocery store aisle, consumer research lab), raster (New Coke can archival image)
- Prerequisites: No prior knowledge needed
- Exclusions: Omit financing and debt/equity analysis detail, the geographic expansion variant of the framework, detailed production/procurement mechanics
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 06 — The Value Chain Weak Link: Why Excellence in One Stage Cannot Save a Broken Chain
- Source: `mba-case-studies/chapters/04-value-chain-analysis.md`
- Topic: STRATEGY & CASE ANALYSIS
- Hook: A company with world-class manufacturing and catastrophic distribution will fail. The world-class manufacturing cannot compensate. A chain snaps at its weakest link regardless of how strong the other links are.
- Key case: The $135 gap in a running shoe — raw materials cost $15, consumer pays $150. Where does the other $135 come from, and where does it disappear when a company's margins collapse?
- The Question: When a company's profits erode, how do you find which stage of the value chain is responsible — without auditing every stage?
- Core idea: A business is a sequence of activities, each consuming resources and adding (or destroying) value. The value chain maps these activities and gives you a vocabulary to name where deterioration is happening. The diagnostic discipline is not auditing the entire chain — it is using cost benchmarks and performance divergence from competitors to identify the weak link, then concentrating analytical attention there.
- Visual object: Seven-stage value chain (R&D → Sourcing → Manufacturing → Logistics → Distribution → Retail/Marketing → After-Sales) with cost annotations at each stage — one stage highlighted in red showing cost overrun or performance divergence.
- Manim move: scan (camera moves left-to-right across the chain, pausing at each stage); collapse (costs concentrate on fewer units as volume drops in the manufacturing stage, showing fixed-cost leverage); trace (a defect caught in manufacturing vs. one that reaches the customer — cost multiplier tracked through the chain)
- Example seed: A beverage company's gross margins fall from 38% to 29% over three years. Revenue is stable. The video uses the value chain to ask the directed question: where in the seven stages does 9 points of margin disappear? By looking at a line chart of their logistics costs relative to the industry (rising from 15% to 26% of COGS in two years), the chain reveals its weak link — not manufacturing, not marketing, but a new distribution center that never reached operational efficiency.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: c2v (factory floor, distribution warehouse), geo (cost-divergence line chart)
- Prerequisites: Basic understanding of revenue, costs, and profit margins
- Exclusions: Omit support activities detail, platform adaptation of the framework, Porter's 1985 taxonomy lecture
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 07 — The 1% Fallacy: Why "We Only Need 1% of a $50 Billion Market" Is Almost Always Wrong
- Source: `mba-case-studies/chapters/13-bcg-matrix-framework.md`
- Topic: STRATEGY & CASE ANALYSIS
- Hook: "We only need 1% of a $50 billion market." It sounds humble. It implies $500 million in revenue. It is almost always a fantasy — because capturing 1% of a vast abstract market requires not a share calculation but winning specific customers from specific competitors in specific channels.
- Key case: TAM/SAM/SOM framework applied to a consumer electronics company entering the smart home market — showing how a $80 billion TAM becomes a $5.4 billion SAM becomes a realistically achievable SOM in the tens of millions for a new entrant.
- The Question: How do you get from "the market is huge" to "here is what we can actually capture" — and why does the math so often fail at the last step?
- Core idea: TAM (theoretical ceiling) → SAM (constrained by your actual business model, geography, and customer scope) → SOM (realistically capturable given competitive dynamics and execution capacity). The move from TAM to SAM applies the constraints of current capability; the move from SAM to SOM is a market share projection that must be benchmarked against historical penetration rates for comparable situations. A bottom-up cross-check (start from the customer unit, build up) that diverges from the top-down estimate tells you which assumption is wrong.
- Visual object: Three nested funnels (TAM → SAM → SOM) with constraint labels at each transition — then a split screen showing top-down calculation vs. bottom-up calculation, and the gap between them.
- Manim move: collapse (TAM funnel narrows to SAM as geographic, channel, and product-scope constraints appear); split (top-down and bottom-up calculations run in parallel; when they converge the answer is credible; when they diverge the model is flagged); accumulate (bottom-up: customer count × average contract value builds to a total)
- Example seed: Smart home market: global TAM $80B. Company sells only in North America, mid-market consumers, security use cases only (30% of smart home). SAM: roughly $7.2B. Three established competitors hold 65% of that SAM. Company invests $12M in year-one marketing. A bottom-up estimate using realistic unit counts and average selling prices produces $15M–$20M in year-one revenue — 0.2% of TAM, not 1%. The gap between the TAM pitch and the bottom-up reality is the lesson.
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: c2v (pitch meeting, whiteboard), geo (funnel diagram overlay)
- Prerequisites: Basic understanding of market share and revenue concepts
- Exclusions: Omit BCG matrix mechanics, experience curve derivation, portfolio management detail
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 08 — The Commoditization Trap: When the Triangle Closes and You Have No Room Left
- Source: `mba-case-studies/chapters/07-pricing-framework.md`
- Topic: STRATEGY & CASE ANALYSIS
- Hook: A company's product is priced at $200. Its cost to produce is $190. The market won't pay more than $210. The competitor charges $185. This is not a pricing problem. It is a structural problem that pricing alone cannot solve.
- Key case: The commodity diagnosis — when value-based ceiling barely exceeds cost-plus floor, pricing strategy reaches its limits. The triangle closes. What needs to change is not the price.
- The Question: How do you know when you have a pricing problem vs. a structural competitive problem — and what does the answer force you to do?
- Core idea: Pricing triangulation (ceiling, floor, anchor) is not just a tool for setting price — it is a diagnostic for competitive position. When the ceiling barely exceeds the floor, the company is in a commodity trap: it cannot raise price without losing customers, cannot lower costs without investment, and cannot escape by adjusting the number. The resolution is not a pricing decision — it is repositioning, differentiation, or cost restructuring. The framework tells you which problem you have.
- Visual object: Three-lens pricing diagram where ceiling and floor converge close together with the competitor anchor below — "commodity zone" label appears; contrasted against the same diagram for a well-differentiated product where the gap is large and the competitor anchor is well below the ceiling.
- Manim move: transform (well-differentiated triangle morphs into commodity triangle as competition increases and differentiation erodes); trace (dollar value of pricing power shrinks as the ceiling drops toward the floor)
- Example seed: Two companies, same product category. Company A: ceiling $280, floor $120, competitor $195. Pricing power zone is large; pricing is a genuine lever. Company B: ceiling $210, floor $190, competitor $185. The triangle barely closes. Raising price loses customers. Cutting price loses the business. The video diagnoses Company B's situation and names the actual problem: the differentiation strategy failed, not the pricing execution.
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: c2v (manufacturing floor, retail shelf comparison)
- Prerequisites: Basic understanding of costs and competitive pricing; viewing Candidate 01 first is ideal but not required
- Exclusions: Omit strategic pricing objective detail (penetration, skimming, loss-leader), willingness-to-pay estimation process
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 09 — What Starbucks Understood: Growth Is the Output of Mechanisms, Not Targets
- Source: `mba-case-studies/chapters/08-growth-framework.md`
- Topic: STRATEGY & CASE ANALYSIS
- Hook: In the 1990s, Starbucks opened one new store every business day and accelerated rather than cannibalizing itself. The counterintuitive hypothesis: put two stores within a few blocks of each other and total traffic exceeds what one would have generated alone.
- Key case: Starbucks' urban clustering strategy — a specific, testable theory about how density creates habituation, habituation drives frequency, and frequency drives revenue per user — executed at scale.
- The Question: What is the difference between a growth target and a growth mechanism — and why does only one of them produce a strategy?
- Core idea: Growth is not a target. It is the output of a specific mechanism. The Ansoff Matrix provides the strategic space (existing/new products × existing/new markets, with risk increasing diagonally); the mechanism thinking asks which specific process generates the required growth for this company at this moment. The analytical work is connecting growth mechanisms to the capabilities required to execute them — and identifying the feasibility constraint (operational, capability, or financial) that is binding.
- Visual object: Ansoff 2×2 (market penetration / market development / product development / diversification) with risk gradient from low to high across the diagonal — then a Starbucks overlay showing the clustering mechanism operating inside the "market penetration" quadrant with a specific mechanism diagram.
- Manim move: spread (Ansoff quadrants build from center with risk gradient); trace (Starbucks clustering: store density → habituation → frequency → RPU, with feedback loop); accumulate (strategic gap calculation: market tailwind + strategic gap = required growth, showing how much work the strategy actually has to do)
- Example seed: A company with $500M revenue in a 9%-growing market sets a 15% growth target. Market tailwinds deliver 9% for free. The 6% gap is what strategy has to generate. The video decomposes that 6% into revenue-per-user growth vs. new user acquisition, shows which mechanisms are available for each, and asks: which mechanism does this company have the capability to actually execute?
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: c2v (urban street with coffee shop), geo (Ansoff matrix overlay, growth gap chart)
- Prerequisites: Basic understanding of revenue and market share
- Exclusions: Omit organic vs. inorganic distinction detail, M&A growth mechanics, detailed feasibility analysis
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 10 — The SWOT Trap: Why Completing the Matrix Is Not the Same as Doing the Analysis
- Source: `mba-case-studies/chapters/11-common-frameworks-swot-pestel.md`
- Topic: STRATEGY & CASE ANALYSIS
- Hook: The most widely used framework in business education is also the most frequently misused — not because it is wrong but because completing it is mistaken for doing the analysis. The matrix is the inventory. Analysis is what you do with the inventory.
- Key case: A company lists "committed to quality," "passionate employees," "established brand," and "strong distribution network" as Strengths. The competitive comparison test reveals that three of four fail to qualify — they describe parity, not genuine advantage.
- The Question: How do you distinguish a genuine strength from flattering self-description — and why does that distinction determine whether your strategy will work?
- Core idea: SWOT and PESTEL are inventory frameworks, not analytical ones. Their job is to ensure comprehensiveness before analysis begins. The analytical work — prioritizing which cells are most decisive for this specific strategic question, applying the competitive comparison test to Strengths, identifying calibrated Threats rather than listing everything — is produced by the person, not the framework. The cross-matrix (SO, WO, ST, WT strategies) is where inventory becomes strategy — but only when the inventory is accurate.
- Visual object: 2×2 SWOT matrix with four quadrant labels — then a red/green coding pass showing which Strengths pass the competitive comparison test vs. which are parity disguised as advantage — then the cross-matrix arrows showing which strategy types are possible given the accurate inventory.
- Manim move: scan (matrix fills quadrant by quadrant); transform (Strengths cell re-codes from green to red/green as the competitive comparison test is applied — some items turn red); split (the cross-matrix splits strategies into SO/WO/ST/WT quadrants, showing which are credible given accurate vs. inflated Strengths)
- Example seed: Company lists four Strengths. Test each: "committed to quality" — does the company have demonstrably superior quality in ways customers value and competitors cannot easily match? If quality is a baseline expectation for the industry, it fails. "Strong distribution network" — is the distribution measurably better than relevant competitors in ways that create customer value? If yes, it passes. The video demonstrates that rigorous SWOT analysis has fewer items in each cell than a casual one — and that the difference determines whether SO strategies are credible.
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: c2v (strategy meeting, whiteboard), geo (competitive benchmarking chart)
- Prerequisites: No prior framework knowledge needed; familiar to anyone who has taken a business course
- Exclusions: Omit PESTEL dimension detail, the TOWS matrix history, threat calibration mechanics
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 11 — Reading the Room: Why McKinsey, BCG, and Bain Are Testing Different Things
- Source: `mba-case-studies/chapters/18-reading-the-room-mckinsey-bcg-bain-interview-styles.md`
- Topic: STRATEGY & CASE ANALYSIS
- Hook: A candidate who prepares perfectly for BCG walks into McKinsey and uses the same approach. Different test. The format is the message — and the message tells you exactly what skill is being weighed.
- Key case: McKinsey's interviewer-led format vs. BCG's candidate-led format — structural choices that reflect each firm's theory of what a consultant's job actually is, not surface stylistic preference.
- The Question: If all three firms want the same underlying skill — structured thinking under uncertainty — why do their interview formats require such different preparation and reward different behaviors?
- Core idea: McKinsey's format mirrors engagement reality: analysts answer questions the partner puts in front of them with precision and speed. BCG's format tests whether you can navigate an ambiguous investigation under your own power. Bain's conversational texture tests whether excellent analysis surfaces through genuine collaboration. The highest-leverage skill differs by format; optimal preparation requires calibrating explicitly, not just practicing more.
- Visual object: Three-column comparison (McKinsey / BCG / Bain) with rows for format type, what it primarily tests, highest-leverage skill, and how errors are handled — then a visual showing the same analytical moment playing out differently in each format.
- Manim move: split (three firm formats split into separate parallel tracks); compare (the same candidate behavior — changing a position when challenged — is evaluated differently in each format; McKinsey: note the correction; BCG: observe hypothesis revision in the candidate-led flow; Bain: assess collaborative engagement quality)
- Example seed: A candidate gets a math wrong mid-case. At McKinsey: narrate the correction explicitly, connect what the corrected answer means for the strategy. At BCG: catch it yourself while driving the investigation, update the hypothesis, specify the next question — the self-correction is itself a signal of intellectual honesty. At Bain: the conversational texture makes recovery natural, but the analysis must be sound regardless. Same mistake, three different scores based on three different signals the format is designed to catch.
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: c2v (consulting office interview setting)
- Prerequisites: Basic familiarity with what a case interview is; most valuable for MBA students recruiting into consulting
- Exclusions: Omit digital assessment (McKinsey Solve / BCG Casey) detail, written case format mechanics, group case behavior
- Score: 8/10
