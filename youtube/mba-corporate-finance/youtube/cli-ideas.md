## Candidate 01 — Build a WACC Sensitivity Dashboard with Claude Code
- Source: mba-corporate-finance/chapters/05-the-cost-of-capital-and-the-wacc.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: A WACC of 8% vs. 8.6% changes an NPV by 15%. That 60-basis-point difference comes from one analyst's choice of beta window and one choice of equity risk premium. This card builds the sensitivity table that makes the subjectivity visible.
- The artifact: A Python WACC sensitivity calculator: inputs are the six WACC components (market equity E, market debt D, pretax cost of debt Rd, tax rate T, beta β, risk-free rate Rf, equity risk premium ERP); sweeps beta (e.g., 0.8 to 1.6) and ERP (4% to 8%) in a 5×5 grid; outputs a color-coded heatmap of WACC values with the "base case" WACC highlighted; each cell is color-coded green (lower WACC → more projects pass) to red (higher WACC → fewer projects pass).
- Prompt seed: `claude "Write a Python WACC sensitivity calculator. Inputs: E=1840, D=660, Rd=0.052, T=0.24, Rf=0.042. Sweep beta from 0.8 to 1.6 in 5 steps and ERP from 0.04 to 0.08 in 5 steps. For each (beta, ERP) combination: Re = Rf + beta × ERP; WACC = (E/(E+D)) × Re + (D/(E+D)) × Rd × (1-T). Produce a 5×5 pandas DataFrame of WACC values and plot as a seaborn heatmap with the base case (beta=1.1, ERP=0.06) highlighted with a red border. Label each cell with the WACC value (2 decimal places)."`
- Read / check: Verify base-case WACC: Re = 0.042 + 1.1 × 0.06 = 0.108; WACC = (1840/2500) × 0.108 + (660/2500) × 0.052 × 0.76 = 0.0795 + 0.0104 = 0.0899 ≈ 9.0% (note: the chapter uses 8% — difference may reflect target vs. book weights; verify the weights used match the chapter's target-capital-structure convention); confirm the heatmap range spans roughly 7.5% to 11% across the sweep; verify the base case cell has a distinct visual marker.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Nothing — fully synthetic based on the Halverson Industries running case from the chapter. Real company data would require pulling market cap and bond yields from Bloomberg or the company's IR site.
- Output medium: Manim (heatmap appearing cell by cell from top-left to bottom-right; color gradient from green to red; base-case cell highlighted with a pulsing border; the "impact on NPV" annotation appearing below)
- The change: Add a third dimension — the debt-to-capital weight (from current 26% to target 30%) — and show how the WACC shifts when the firm moves toward its target capital structure. Ask Claude to compute the WACC at both weight scenarios and show the impact on the $87.4M NPV from the chapter's Plant 4 case.
- Teardown angle: The WACC looks like a single precise number. It is actually a surface over multiple contested inputs. The sensitivity table forces every analyst to see how much of the "yes/no" verdict on a capital project depends on choices that reasonable people disagree about — before the number goes to the board.
- Exclusions: Beta estimation (separate card in computational finance book); adjusted present value (APV) method; project-specific vs. firm-average WACC distinction; CAPM vs. Fama-French for cost of equity.
- Score: 10/10

- Teardown angle: A number that can't be questioned isn't a number — it's a prayer. The terminal value is where most of the NPV lives and where the inputs are most uncertain. ## Candidate 03 — Build a DCF Valuation Model with Claude Code

- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: Cardinal Flow Systems has $400M in revenue and $80M in EBITDA. What is it worth as a standalone company — before synergies, before a deal premium? The DCF gives a range. - The artifact: A Python three-method M&A DCF: (1) standalone DCF (project FCFF from EBITDA margin, apply WACC, add terminal value), (2) comparable-transactions EV/EBITDA multiple (range 8–12× applied to $80M EBITDA), (3) synergy-adjusted DCF (add haircutted cost and revenue synergies from chapter). Outputs a "valuation bridge" showing minimum, midpoint, and maximum for each method; plotted as a Manim football-field chart (standard M&A valuation range visualization).
- Prompt seed: `claude "Write a Python M&A valuation model for Cardinal Flow Systems. Method 1 (Standalone DCF): EBITDA=$80M, margin=20%, revenue=$400M. Project FCFF for 5 years assuming 5% annual growth, 8% EBITDA margin compression to 18% by year 5, capex=4% revenue, ΔWC=1% revenue growth. Terminal value = FCFF_5 × (1+2%) / (10%-2%). Method 2 (Comparables): EV/EBITDA range [8×, 10×, 12×] applied to $80M. Method 3 (Synergy-adjusted): add cost synergies=$25M/yr × 0.75 haircut at 10% WACC, revenue synergies=$7.5M/yr × 0.40 haircut. Output: three valuation ranges and a football-field chart."`
- Read / check: Verify standalone DCF produces a result in the $450–650M range (the chapter estimates $550M as the midpoint); verify EV/EBITDA comparables produce $640M–$960M; verify haircutted cost synergy NPV ≈ $25M × 0.75 / 0.10 = $187.5M and haircutted revenue synergy NPV ≈ $7.5M × 0.40 / 0.10 = $30M; confirm the football-field chart shows three horizontal bars with the $700M offer price marked as a vertical line.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Real precedent transaction data (the EV/EBITDA range of 8–12× is stated in the chapter as "typical for mid-cap industrial flow-control" — a real analysis would require a sourced database of comparable transactions from PitchBook or CapitalIQ). Synthetic ranges are explicitly acceptable for the video.
- Output medium: Manim (football-field chart — three horizontal bars representing DCF range, comparables range, synergy-adjusted range; the $700M offer price appears as a vertical dashed line cutting across all three; the "acquirer value creation zone" labeled between the offer price and the synergy-adjusted maximum)
- The change: Run the same model with the revenue synergy haircut changed from 40% to 20% (more pessimistic — reflects the empirical finding that revenue synergies rarely materialize). Show how the deal verdict changes — does the $700M offer still look attractive when you're more conservative on revenue synergies?
- Teardown angle: Three methods triangulate. No single method is sufficient. Disagreements between methods are informative rather than annoying — a DCF that is well below the comparables range is either a conservative analyst or evidence that the market is pricing in synergies that you haven't identified yet. The football-field is the most honest visual in M&A because it shows the range of what "value" means, not a single spuriously precise number.
- Exclusions: Leveraged buyout (LBO) returns-based valuation; sum-of-parts analysis; discounted equity residual model; merger arbitrage spread.
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 04 — Build a Modigliani-Miller Tax Shield Calculator with Claude Code
- Source: mba-corporate-finance/chapters/07-capital-structure-theory-the-modigliani-miller-world.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: Modigliani and Miller proved that capital structure doesn't matter — in a world with no taxes. The moment you add one friction (interest deductibility), debt creates real firm value. The tax shield is not an accounting trick. It is free money from the government, and its present value is exactly T × D.
- The artifact: A Python MM tax shield calculator: takes levered and unlevered firm parameters (EBIT, cost of equity unlevered, debt level, tax rate, pretax cost of debt); computes: (1) unlevered firm value (V_U = EBIT × (1-T) / r_U), (2) tax shield PV (T × D), (3) levered firm value (V_L = V_U + T×D per MM Proposition I with taxes), (4) cost of levered equity (MM Proposition II: r_E = r_U + (r_U - r_D) × (D/E) × (1-T)); plotted as a Manim bar chart showing V_U, tax shield, and V_L side by side.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Write a Python Modigliani-Miller tax shield calculator. Inputs: EBIT=20, r_u=0.10 (unlevered cost of equity), D=60 (debt), r_d=0.05 (pretax cost of debt), T=0.24 (tax rate). Compute: V_U = EBIT×(1-T)/r_u; tax_shield_PV = T × D; V_L = V_U + tax_shield_PV; E = V_L - D; r_E = r_u + (r_u - r_d) × (D/E) × (1-T) [MM Proposition II with taxes]. Output: V_U, tax shield PV, V_L, E, r_E. Plot a three-bar chart: V_U, tax shield, V_L. Then: sweep debt from 0 to 100 and plot V_L as a function of D — showing linear value increase from tax shield."`
- Read / check: Verify V_U = 20×(1-0.24)/0.10 = 20×0.76/0.10 = $152M; verify tax shield = 0.24×60 = $14.4M; verify V_L = 152 + 14.4 = $166.4M; verify E = 166.4 - 60 = $106.4M; verify r_E = 0.10 + (0.10 - 0.05) × (60/106.4) × (1-0.24) = 0.10 + 0.05 × 0.564 × 0.76 ≈ 0.10 + 0.0214 ≈ 0.1214; confirm the V_L vs. D sweep produces a linear upward-sloping line (MM with taxes predicts 100% debt is optimal — this is the "MM world" extreme that the next chapter corrects with bankruptcy costs).
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Nothing — fully synthetic. MM is a theoretical framework; all inputs are illustrative from the chapter's Halverson case.
- Output medium: Manim (three-bar chart animating: V_U in blue, tax shield layer stacked on top in gold, V_L total labeled; then a second panel showing the linear V_L vs. D sweep)
- The change: Relax the "no bankruptcy costs" assumption — add a quadratic distress-cost function (distress_costs = 0.01 × D^2) and show how the optimal capital structure is no longer "100% debt" but a finite leverage ratio that balances the tax shield against rising distress costs. This is the static trade-off theory, and the visual makes it click.
- Teardown angle: The most useful theorems describe a world that doesn't exist. MM's perfect-world result is not a failure — it is a precise map of which frictions are responsible for capital structure mattering. Taxes produce the tax shield. Bankruptcy costs produce the optimal leverage ceiling. Information asymmetry produces the pecking order. The theorem without taxes is the baseline that makes the real-world adjustments legible.
- Exclusions: APV (adjusted present value) vs. WACC debate; Hamada equation for relevering beta; Miller's personal tax equilibrium; signaling theory of debt.
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 05 — Build a Dividend vs. Buyback IRR Comparison with Claude Code

- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: A company has $100M in free cash flow and two choices: pay a dividend or buy back stock. In a world of equal taxes and rational markets, these are identical. In the real world, they're not — and this card runs the numbers to show exactly when buybacks win.

- Prompt seed: `claude "Write a Python dividend vs. buyback comparison. Company: FCF=$100M, shares=50M, price=$40, EPS=$1.60. Scenario A (dividend): declare $2/share dividend. After-tax dividend income = $2 × (1 - dividend_tax_rate). Scenario B (buyback): company repurchases shares at $40. Shares repurchased = FCF/price = 2.5M. New share count = 47.5M. New EPS = total earnings / 47.5M. Shareholder who sells 5% of 10,000-share position: capital gains = 500 shares × ($40 - cost_basis). Output: after-tax income per shareholder in each scenario, EPS before and after buyback, and the breakeven capital gains tax rate at which dividends become preferable. - Read / check: Verify dividend scenario: $2 × 10,000 shares = $20,000 pre-tax, $20,000 × (1-0.15) = $17,000 after-tax; verify buyback scenario: shareholder sells 500 shares at $40 = $20,000 proceeds, gain = 500 × ($40-$30) = $5,000, tax = $5,000 × 0.15 = $750, net = $19,250 (buyback is slightly better when taxes are equal because cost basis partially shelters gain); verify EPS accretion: if total earnings = $80M (EPS × shares), new EPS = $80M / 47.5M ≈ $1.684 vs. prior $1.60 (+5.25% accretion); verify the breakeven tax rate where both scenarios produce equal after-tax cash.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Nothing — fully synthetic. All numbers are illustrative from the chapter's capital structure framework. Real company payout ratios and buyback volumes are available from 10-K filings.
- Output medium: Manim (side-by-side table: left column = dividend scenario, right column = buyback scenario; each row appearing one by one: pre-tax cash, after-tax cash, EPS before, EPS after; the winner box highlighted in green at the bottom)
- The change: Run the comparison at three capital gains tax rates (15%, 23.8% NIIT rate for high-income investors, 40% hypothetical future rate) and show how the buyback advantage changes as the tax rate on capital gains rises toward the dividend rate.
- Teardown angle: In a world of equal tax rates and frictionless markets, dividends and buybacks are mathematically identical — Modigliani and Miller proved this in 1961. In the real world, taxes, signaling effects, and the psychology of regular income vs. capital appreciation make the choice consequential. The CFO who understands the math chooses the instrument that delivers the most after-tax value — not the one that feels most "shareholder-friendly."
- Exclusions: Share dilution from option grants offsetting buybacks; dividend sustainability and dividend irrelevance proposition; special dividends; spin-offs and asset divestitures as alternative forms of capital return.
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 07 — Build a Real Options "Expand or Abandon" Valuation with Claude Code
- Source: mba-corporate-finance/chapters/06-risk-adjusted-rates-and-real-options.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: Standard NPV treats a project as a now-or-never bet. But management has options: wait, expand if things go well, abandon if they don't. Ignoring these options understates a project's value by an amount that can swing the go/no-go decision. This card values the option to expand.
- The artifact: A Python real option valuation using a simple binomial tree: inputs are base NPV (without option), expansion factor (if things go well, project is worth X times the base), probability of up/down moves, expansion cost, and risk-free rate; outputs the option value (the NPV uplift from having the expansion option), the option-adjusted NPV, and a 2-period binomial tree showing node values and probabilities; plotted as a Manim animated binomial tree.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Write a Python real option valuation (expansion option) using a two-period binomial tree. Inputs: base_value=55, u=1.4 (up factor), d=0.7 (down factor), expansion_cost=20, expansion_factor=2.0 (expansion doubles the project if exercised), rf=0.05 (risk-free rate per period). Risk-neutral probability: p = (1+rf - d)/(u - d). Tree: V_uu = base × u^2, V_ud = base × u × d, V_dd = base × d^2. Option value at each terminal node: max(expansion_factor × V - expansion_cost - V, 0) = max((expansion_factor-1) × V - expansion_cost, 0). Roll back at risk-neutral probabilities. Output: option value, option-adjusted NPV = base_value + option_value, and the full binomial tree node values."`
- Read / check: Verify risk-neutral probability: p = (1.05 - 0.7)/(1.4 - 0.7) = 0.35/0.70 = 0.50; verify V_uu = 55 × 1.96 = $107.8, V_ud = 55 × 0.98 = $53.9, V_dd = 55 × 0.49 = $26.95; verify option value at V_uu = max((2-1) × 107.8 - 20, 0) = max(87.8, 0) = $87.8; option value at V_ud = max(33.9, 0) = $33.9; option value at V_dd = max(6.95, 0) = $6.95; roll back with p=0.5 to get period-1 and period-0 option values; confirm option-adjusted NPV > base_value.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Nothing — fully synthetic. Real options are theoretical; the binomial tree is a teaching construct. Real-world application requires project-specific volatility estimates from comparable investments.
- Output medium: Manim (binomial tree animating from root (period 0) outward to terminal nodes; each node appearing with its value labeled; option value computed at terminal nodes in gold; rollback values appearing at period-1 and period-0 nodes; the final option-adjusted NPV appearing below the tree)
- The change: Add the option to abandon — if the project value falls below a threshold, management can shut it down and recover some salvage value. Show how the combined option (expand up, abandon down) produces an even higher option-adjusted NPV than the expansion option alone, and why this asymmetry is the intuition behind why real options are valuable.
- Teardown angle: Real options analysis does not change the physics of the project. It changes what you're measuring. Standard NPV measures the expected value assuming management is passive — locked in to the decision made at t=0. Option-adjusted NPV measures the expected value assuming management is active — responding to information as it arrives. The difference is the value of flexibility, which DCF systematically ignores.
- Exclusions: Black-Scholes for continuous-time real options; Monte Carlo simulation for path-dependent options; staged investment (compound option) valuation; patent as a real option.
- Score: 7/10

## Candidate 09 — Build an Operational Risk Scenario Modeler with Claude Code
- Source: mba-corporate-finance/chapters/12-operational-risk-management.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: A firm's earnings don't just vary because of market conditions. They vary because suppliers fail, regulatory decisions change, and key systems go down. Operational risk is the risk that never appears in the CAPM beta — but shows up as a catastrophic quarter when it materializes.
- The artifact: A Python Monte Carlo operational risk modeler: inputs are a base EBITDA, a list of operational risk events (each with probability and severity as % EBITDA impact), and number of simulation runs; outputs: (1) mean and standard deviation of simulated annual EBITDA, (2) 95th and 99th percentile loss scenarios (Value at Risk equivalents), (3) probability distribution of annual EBITDA with a histogram; plotted as a Manim animated histogram building from simulation runs.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Write a Python Monte Carlo operational risk modeler. Inputs: base_ebitda=100, n_simulations=10000, risk_events=[{'name': 'supplier disruption', 'prob': 0.10, 'severity': -0.15}, {'name': 'regulatory fine', 'prob': 0.05, 'severity': -0.25}, {'name': 'cyber incident', 'prob': 0.08, 'severity': -0.20}, {'name': 'key customer loss', 'prob': 0.06, 'severity': -0.30}].
