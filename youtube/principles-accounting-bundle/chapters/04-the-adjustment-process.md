# Chapter 4 — The Adjustment Process

## 4.1 Opening: When the trial balance lies

Mark Summers's first month at Supreme Cleaners ends. He pulls the unadjusted trial balance you produced at the end of Chapter 3. It balances — total debits $38,900, total credits $38,900. But Mark's accountant takes one look at the supplies account and frowns. The trial balance shows Supplies on hand at $800, the amount Mark bought at the beginning of October. The accountant walks back to the storage closet. There is maybe $300 worth of cleaning chemicals and packaging left. Five hundred dollars of supplies have been used in October. The trial balance does not know that. The journal does not know that. No transaction was recorded for the consumption — there was no invoice, no check, no event that triggered an entry. The supplies just got *used*, gradually, as Mark cleaned clothes.

The same problem hides in several other places on the trial balance. Mark's commercial press, recorded at $12,000, has been running for a month — its useful life has shortened by some amount, but no journal entry was made for the wear. Mark's last employee paycheck covered work through October 25; the employee has worked another five days but won't be paid until November 5. Wages have been earned but not yet recorded. A customer paid $300 in advance for a season of dry-cleaning service in September; some of that prepayment was earned during October, but no entry has been made to convert the prepayment from a liability into revenue.

The unadjusted trial balance is mechanically correct. It is also incomplete. It does not yet reflect the *passage of time* — the gradual consumption of supplies and equipment, the accrual of obligations and revenues that the rules of accrual accounting require to be recorded even when no cash has changed hands and no document has triggered an entry. The job of this chapter is to fix that. Adjusting entries are the period-end journal entries that bring the books from "what got recorded as it happened" into compliance with accrual accounting before the financial statements are produced.

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:

- Explain why the unadjusted trial balance contains accounts whose balances are incorrect at period-end, even when every prior transaction was journalized correctly.
- Distinguish *deferrals* (cash already exchanged, revenue or expense not yet earned/incurred) from *accruals* (revenue or expense already earned/incurred, cash not yet exchanged).
- Identify the four common types of adjusting entries: prepaid expenses, unearned revenues, accrued revenues, accrued expenses.
- Record adjusting entries for supplies used, depreciation of equipment, prepaid insurance, unearned service revenue, accrued wages, and accrued interest revenue.
- Distinguish the *fiscal year* from the *calendar year* and explain why some companies choose a fiscal year that doesn't end December 31.

You walk in with the unadjusted trial balance from Chapter 3. You walk out with the adjusted trial balance — the one the financial statements will actually be built from in Chapter 5.

Why does it matter? Because accrual accounting is what GAAP requires for almost every reporting context that matters — public-company filings, audited statements, lender disclosures, regulatory filings. Cash-basis accounting is permitted for some small businesses and certain industries, but as soon as a business grows beyond a certain scale, accrual is the standard. And accrual accounting is what makes the income statement and the balance sheet meaningful across periods — it matches revenues to the period that earned them and expenses to the period that consumed them, regardless of when the cash actually moved. Adjusting entries are the mechanism that makes accrual accounting actually work in practice.

## 4.2 Why the trial balance has incorrect balances at period-end

Three structural reasons accounts on the unadjusted trial balance carry incorrect balances at period-end, even when every recorded transaction was journalized correctly.

The first is *consumption that happens gradually, without a triggering event*. Cleaning supplies get used a tiny bit at a time across the month. Office equipment ages and loses value with use, day by day. Insurance coverage expires hour by hour over the policy term. None of these consumption events generates an invoice or a payment or a paper trail of any kind. They just *happen*, distributed across time. The accounting system, which records when triggered by an event, has nothing to trigger on — until the period-end adjustment makes the consumption visible.

The second is *obligations that have accrued without a triggering event*. An employee earns wages over each working day. Interest accrues on a loan day by day. Property taxes accrue over the property tax year. None of these get paid daily; the cash payment happens periodically — payroll runs every two weeks, interest is paid quarterly, property taxes might be paid annually. The accrual happens continuously; the cash event happens occasionally. The unadjusted trial balance, which records on cash events, lags reality.

The third is *revenue earned without a triggering event* — the mirror image. A customer prepays for a year of service; the service is delivered gradually across the year. Interest is earned on a savings account day by day; the bank credits the account quarterly. Rent is collected at the start of a quarter for occupancy that the tenant uses across all three months. The earning happens continuously; the cash event happened earlier (or, less commonly, will happen later) and was recorded as a liability or receivable at the time. The unadjusted trial balance carries that liability or receivable at its full original amount until something forces a re-look.

The pattern: any time the *economic event* (consumption, accrual, earning) is decoupled from the *cash event* that the journal originally recorded, the books fall behind reality between the cash event and the period-end adjustment. Adjusting entries are how the books catch up.

## 4.3 Accrual accounting, the accounting period, and the fiscal year

Three concepts ground the rest of the chapter.

*Accrual accounting* records revenue when earned and expenses when incurred, regardless of when cash changes hands. *Cash-basis accounting* records both only when cash moves. We met both of these in Chapter 2; this chapter assumes accrual basis going forward unless otherwise noted, because accrual is what GAAP requires.

The *accounting period* is the span of time the financial statements cover. Common periods: monthly, quarterly, annually. The *time period assumption* — which we met in Chapter 3 — is what allows the business's continuous economic life to be sliced into reportable chunks. Without a defined accounting period, "net income for the period" has no meaning.

The annual accounting period can be either a *calendar year* (January 1 to December 31) or a *fiscal year* (any twelve-month span the business chooses, ending on the last day of any month other than December). Many U.S. retailers use a fiscal year ending in late January or early February — after the holiday-shopping rush has wrapped up and inventory has been counted. A school district might use a fiscal year ending June 30, aligning with the academic year. The federal government's fiscal year runs October 1 to September 30. The choice of fiscal year reflects when the natural rhythm of the business reaches a point where counting inventory and closing the books is cheapest.

*Interim periods* are the shorter reporting windows within the annual period — typically quarters for public companies. The SEC requires quarterly filings (Form 10-Q) for publicly traded companies; the annual filing is the 10-K. Each interim period requires its own adjusting entries.

The trade-off in choosing a fiscal year: a calendar year aligns with the U.S. tax year (which is January–December for most individual taxpayers) and with consumer mental models — the *2025 financials* mean what the calendar suggests. A fiscal year aligned with the business's natural cycle gives more accurate inventory counts and a less rushed close, at the cost of being out of sync with the tax year and requiring a translation when comparing to calendar-year peers.

## 4.4 The four kinds of adjusting entries

Every adjusting entry falls into one of four categories. Two are *deferrals* (cash exchanged earlier; the earning or consumption happens later) and two are *accruals* (earning or consumption now; cash exchanges later).

### Deferral 1: Prepaid expenses (a deferred expense)

The pattern: cash was paid for an expense that benefits multiple future periods. At the time of payment, the journal entry was Debit *Prepaid Asset* / Credit *Cash* — recording an asset, not an expense, because the benefit had not yet been consumed. As periods pass, portions of the asset get consumed and need to be moved into expense.

Example. On October 1, Supreme Cleaners pays $1,200 for a 12-month insurance policy. The original entry: Debit Prepaid Insurance $1,200, Credit Cash $1,200. By October 31, one month of insurance has been consumed. The adjusting entry, dated October 31:

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Insurance Expense | $100 | |
| Prepaid Insurance | | $100 |

The Insurance Expense account picks up the $100 of consumption during October ($1,200 ÷ 12 months). The Prepaid Insurance account drops from $1,200 to $1,100 — the eleven months of coverage still ahead. Each subsequent month repeats this entry until the policy is fully consumed.

The same pattern applies to *supplies*. Mark bought $800 of supplies on October 5 (Debit Supplies, Credit Accounts Payable). At the end of October, $300 of supplies remain. The adjusting entry: Debit Supplies Expense $500, Credit Supplies $500. Supplies expense rises by $500; the supplies asset falls from $800 to $300.

A special case is *depreciation* — the gradual allocation of a long-term asset's cost across the periods that benefit from its use. Mark's $12,000 commercial press has an estimated useful life of, say, ten years. Straight-line depreciation allocates $12,000 ÷ 120 months = $100 per month to expense. The adjusting entry, October 31:

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Depreciation Expense | $100 | |
| Accumulated Depreciation — Equipment | | $100 |

A wrinkle: the credit goes to *Accumulated Depreciation*, a contra-asset account that sits on the balance sheet as a deduction from Equipment, rather than directly reducing the Equipment account. This preserves the historical cost of the equipment ($12,000) on the books while showing the accumulated wear separately. We'll develop depreciation in detail in Chapter 11.

### Deferral 2: Unearned revenues (a deferred revenue)

The mirror image. Cash was received for revenue that hasn't been earned yet. At the time of receipt, the journal entry was Debit *Cash* / Credit *Unearned Revenue* — recording a liability, not a revenue, because the service hadn't been delivered. As the service is delivered, portions of the liability get moved into revenue.

Example from the source: a printing-services company collects $4,000 in advance from a customer for printing work to be done over the next four months. Original entry: Debit Cash $4,000, Credit Unearned Revenue $4,000. After one month, suppose $1,000 of services has been delivered. The adjusting entry:

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Unearned Revenue | $1,000 | |
| Service Revenue | | $1,000 |

Unearned Revenue (a liability) decreases by $1,000. Service Revenue (an income statement account) increases by $1,000. The customer's prepayment is being earned across time, and the books track the conversion month by month.

### Accrual 1: Accrued expenses (expense incurred, cash not yet paid)

The pattern: an expense has been incurred during the period but no cash payment has yet been made. The original journal entry never happened, because there was no cash event to trigger it. The adjusting entry creates the record.

Example. Supreme Cleaners' employee earned wages from October 26 through October 31 — five days of work — but won't be paid until November 5 when payroll runs. Suppose the wages owed for those five days total $250. The adjusting entry, October 31:

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Wages Expense | $250 | |
| Wages Payable | | $250 |

Wages Expense increases by $250 (the work was performed in October; the matching principle requires the expense to land in October). Wages Payable, a current liability, increases by $250 (the obligation to pay exists at October 31). When the November 5 payroll is run, the $250 portion gets debited against Wages Payable rather than going through Wages Expense again — the November cash payment doesn't double-count the expense.

The same pattern handles *accrued interest expense* on a loan, *accrued utilities expense* for a billing cycle that crosses period-end, and *accrued income tax expense* for the period.

### Accrual 2: Accrued revenues (revenue earned, cash not yet received)

The mirror. Revenue has been earned during the period but no cash has been received and no invoice has yet been billed. The adjusting entry records both the revenue and the receivable.

Example. Suppose Supreme Cleaners' business savings account earned $30 of interest in October that will be credited by the bank on November 1. The adjusting entry, October 31:

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Interest Receivable | $30 | |
| Interest Revenue | | $30 |

Interest Receivable (an asset) increases by $30. Interest Revenue increases by $30. When the bank actually credits the account on November 1, the journal entry is Debit Cash $30, Credit Interest Receivable $30 — the receivable is collected, the revenue is not double-counted.

The same pattern handles *accrued service revenue* (work done at period-end, invoice not yet sent) and *accrued rent revenue* (a tenant's rent for the period earned but not yet collected).

The structural pattern across all four:

| Type | Cash event | Earning/consumption event | Adjusting entry pattern |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prepaid expense | Earlier | Now (gradually) | Debit expense, Credit asset |
| Unearned revenue | Earlier | Now (gradually) | Debit liability, Credit revenue |
| Accrued expense | Later | Now | Debit expense, Credit liability |
| Accrued revenue | Later | Now | Debit asset, Credit revenue |

A regularity worth memorizing: every adjusting entry hits *one income-statement account* (a revenue or an expense) and *one balance-sheet account* (an asset or a liability). Cash is *never* part of an adjusting entry. The cash event happened earlier (deferrals) or will happen later (accruals); the adjusting entry is fixing the timing mismatch between cash and earning, not recording cash itself.

## 4.5 The adjusted trial balance

After all adjusting entries are journalized and posted to the ledger, a new trial balance is prepared — the *adjusted trial balance*. It serves the same checkpoint function as the unadjusted trial balance: total debits should equal total credits. If they don't, an error has been introduced during the adjusting process.

For Supreme Cleaners, suppose the adjusting entries at October 31 are:

1. Insurance Expense $100 / Prepaid Insurance $100 (assumes a $1,200 prepaid insurance policy bought in addition to the Chapter 3 transactions)
2. Supplies Expense $500 / Supplies $500 ($300 of supplies remain on hand)
3. Depreciation Expense $100 / Accumulated Depreciation — Equipment $100
4. Wages Expense $250 / Wages Payable $250
5. Interest Receivable $30 / Interest Revenue $30

The supplies adjustment alone changes Supplies from $800 to $300. The depreciation adjustment introduces a new contra-asset account (Accumulated Depreciation) at $100 that sits on the balance sheet as a deduction from Equipment. The wages adjustment introduces a new current liability (Wages Payable) at $250. The interest adjustment introduces a new asset (Interest Receivable) at $30 and a new revenue account (Interest Revenue) at $30. The insurance adjustment, if Mark had purchased the insurance, would split the $1,200 between current expense and remaining prepaid asset.

The adjusted trial balance integrates all of these into the period-end view: every account at the right balance for the financial statements. From the adjusted trial balance, Chapter 5 will produce the actual income statement, statement of owner's equity, balance sheet, and statement of cash flows for October.

The trade-off implicit in the adjustment process: adjusting entries buy *accrual accuracy* (revenues matched to the period that earned them, expenses matched to the period that consumed the related resources) at the cost of *judgment and estimation* (depreciation depends on an estimate of useful life; supplies remaining depends on a physical count; accrued wages depend on a calculation of hours worked but not yet paid). Adjusting entries are where bookkeeping ends and accounting judgment begins.

## 4.7 Chapter summary

You walked into this chapter with an unadjusted trial balance that mechanically balanced. You walk out with an adjusted trial balance that reflects accrual accounting at period-end.

Adjusting entries exist because the unadjusted trial balance has accounts whose balances are incorrect at period-end — not from any error in the recording process, but because consumption and accrual happen continuously while the journal records discretely. Three structural causes: gradual consumption without a triggering event (supplies, depreciation, prepaid insurance); obligations accruing without a triggering event (wages, interest, taxes); revenues earning without a triggering event (interest income, services-at-period-end, rent earned).

Four types of adjusting entries cover the full taxonomy. *Prepaid expenses* and *unearned revenues* are deferrals, where cash was exchanged earlier and the earning or consumption is happening now. *Accrued expenses* and *accrued revenues* are accruals, where the earning or consumption is now and cash exchanges later. The pattern across all four: every adjusting entry hits one income-statement account and one balance-sheet account, never cash.

The single most important idea to carry forward: adjusting entries are not corrections of bookkeeping errors. They are the mechanism by which accrual accounting is actually implemented in practice. The bookkeeper records what triggers an entry; the accountant adjusts at period-end for what didn't trigger. Both are necessary; neither is sufficient.

The common mistake to watch for: confusing the original journal entry (when cash moves) with the adjusting entry (when earning or consumption is recognized). For a deferral, the original entry created the asset or the liability; the adjusting entry moves portions of it to revenue or expense. For an accrual, no original entry happened; the adjusting entry creates the record.

## 4.8 Connections forward

You'll take the adjusted trial balance, prepare the four financial statements (income statement, statement of owner's equity, balance sheet, statement of cash flows), and produce the *closing entries* — the period-end entries that zero out revenue, expense, and dividend accounts so the next period starts fresh, while preserving the cumulative balance in retained earnings. By the end of Chapter 5, you'll have walked through one complete accounting cycle, from the first transaction to the closing entries that prepare for the next period.
