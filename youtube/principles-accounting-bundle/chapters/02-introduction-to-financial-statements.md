# Chapter 2 — Introduction to Financial Statements

## 2.1 Opening: An IPO at the closing bell

On September 28, 2017, a company you had probably never heard of opened on the public stock market. Roku, Inc. — the streaming-television-device maker — had filed a Form S-1 with the Securities and Exchange Commission three weeks earlier, on September 1, disclosing the financial information any retail investor or institutional fund manager would need to decide whether to buy. The company estimated, the night before the IPO, that its shares would price somewhere between $12 and $14, raising more than $117 million. By the closing bell on the 28th, Roku stock was trading at $23.50 — nearly double the top of its expected range.

Read what just happened slowly. A new entity offered a slice of itself to the public. Tens of thousands of strangers — some institutional investors with teams of analysts, some individuals trading from their phones — decided how much that slice was worth. They priced it at almost two times what the company itself had thought, the night before. The mechanism that made all of this possible — the only mechanism, really — was the company's financial statements. The Form S-1 contained an income statement, a statement of stockholders' equity, a balance sheet, a statement of cash flows, and the notes that go with them. Without those documents, no one outside Roku's offices could have priced the shares at all. With them, ten thousand people could read the same numbers and form ten thousand independent valuations, and the market between those valuations decided the price.

That is what financial statements *do*. They translate a company's life into a form that strangers can read and act on. Four standardized documents, prepared in a fixed order, every period, by every public company, by hundreds of thousands of private companies, by governmental entities and not-for-profits with adapted versions of the same forms. Get those four documents into your hands and you can ask the questions that matter — is this organization profitable, is it solvent, is its cash position safe, is its growth real or borrowed? You may not always answer those questions correctly. But the questions become askable.

This chapter introduces the four statements. Chapter 1 told you the architecture exists; this chapter shows you what it produces. By the end of it, you should be able to:

- Name the four core financial statements, in the order they are prepared, and explain what each one is *for*.
- Define the elements that appear on each statement — revenue, expense, gain, loss, asset, liability, equity, cash flow — and identify which statement each belongs on.
- Calculate net income from a list of revenues, expenses, gains, and losses; calculate ending equity from a starting balance, contributions, distributions, and net income; calculate equity from assets and liabilities on a balance sheet date.
- Distinguish the three major legal business structures (sole proprietorship, partnership, corporation) and explain how the statements' terminology shifts across them.
- Distinguish cash-basis from accrual-basis accounting, and explain why the statement of cash flows exists separately from the income statement under accrual accounting.

You walk in with Chapter 1's vocabulary and one new prerequisite: the *business entity concept* — the rule that the personal transactions of an organization's owners and employees are kept separate from the organization's books. The owner's personal credit card bill does not appear in the business's expenses. The business's revenue does not appear on the owner's tax return as wages. The line is structural and it underlies every example in this chapter.

Why does it matter? Because financial statements are not just reporting tools. They are the only common language by which an organization's economic life can be communicated to outsiders. The person reading them might be a banker deciding on a loan, a regulator reviewing an annual filing, a board member evaluating a CEO, a job candidate trying to gauge stability before signing. The reader is at a distance from the organization. The four statements are how the distance gets closed.

## 2.2 The four statements, in the order accountants prepare them

Four statements, four jobs, four time orientations, prepared in a fixed sequence because each one feeds the next.

The *Income Statement* measures the organization's financial performance *for a period of time* — a month, a quarter, a year. It answers a single question: did the organization make or lose money during that period, and from what? Its elements are *revenues* (inflows from the organization's primary operations), *expenses* (the costs of producing those inflows), *gains* (inflows from peripheral activities like selling a piece of equipment for more than its book value), and *losses* (outflows from peripheral activities). The bottom line is *net income* (when revenues plus gains exceed expenses plus losses) or *net loss* (when the opposite is true). This statement is prepared first because the net income figure flows into the next statement.

The *Statement of Owner's Equity* tracks how the organization's net worth changed *across that same period*. It starts with beginning equity, adds investments by owners (cash or other assets contributed in exchange for an ownership claim), adds net income (or subtracts net loss) from the income statement, subtracts distributions to owners (called *dividends* in a corporation, *withdrawals* or *drawings* in a sole proprietorship), and lands on ending equity. This statement is prepared second because the ending equity figure flows into the third statement.

The *Balance Sheet* reports the organization's financial position *on a single date* — usually the last day of the period covered by the income statement and statement of owner's equity. The shift in time orientation is important: the first two statements describe a flow over time; the balance sheet is a snapshot. Its elements are *assets* (resources the organization owns or controls — cash, accounts receivable, inventory, equipment, buildings, intangibles like trademarks), *liabilities* (amounts the organization owes — accounts payable, wages payable, notes payable, mortgages), and *equity* (what is left for the owners after liabilities are subtracted from assets). The defining identity of the balance sheet, which we will return to in Chapter 3, is *Assets = Liabilities + Equity*. The ending equity from the second statement appears here on the equity side.

The *Statement of Cash Flows* reports the cash inflows and cash outflows for the same period as the income statement, broken into three categories: cash from operating activities, cash from investing activities, and cash from financing activities. This statement exists because the income statement is prepared on the *accrual basis* — recording revenues when earned and expenses when incurred, regardless of when cash actually changes hands — and a reader needs a separate document to know whether the organization's cash position is healthy or precarious. We'll come back to this in §2.4.

The trade-off this design embeds: any one statement, read alone, is misleading. An organization can have a great income statement and a terrible cash position (it earned the revenue but customers haven't paid yet). It can have a strong balance sheet and a weak income statement (assets accumulated in years past, current operations bleeding). It can have steady cash flow but a hollow equity position (taking on debt to keep paying dividends). The four statements are designed to be read *together*. The Roku Form S-1 included all four, and the investors who bought at $23.50 were doing so by reading all four — not just the income statement.

A common misconception worth surfacing now: students often treat the income statement as "the most important" of the four, because it shows profit. That is a habit borrowed from headline-grabbing stories about company earnings. Practitioners reading a complete set of statements will often spend the most time on the cash-flow statement and the notes to the balance sheet. Earnings can be smoothed, optimized, even fabricated; cash is harder to fake. Madoff's scheme, which we met in Chapter 1, would have shown beautifully on a pure income statement; it would have shown impossibilities on a cash-flow statement that anyone bothered to reconcile.

## 2.3 The income statement and statement of owner's equity, with a real worked example

Meet Chris. On August 1, 2020, she starts a sole proprietorship called Chris's Landscaping — a small landscaping service operating out of her family's tractor in exchange for a fee. By the end of August, she checks her business checking account and finds $250 in it. She thought she had been paid more than that. Where did the money go?

Her records show: she earned $1,400 from her customers. She paid $100 to fix the tractor's brakes. She paid $50 for fuel. She paid $1,000 to an insurance company for a year of business insurance. The math closes: $1,400 in, $1,150 out, $250 left. The August income statement for Chris's Landscaping looks like this.

**Chris's Landscaping — Income Statement — For the month ended August 31, 2020**

| Element | Amount |
|---|---|
| Revenue | $1,400 |
| Less: Expenses | |
|     Brake repair | $100 |
|     Fuel | $50 |
|     Insurance | $1,000 |
| Total expenses | $1,150 |
| **Net income** | **$250** |

The formula underneath: *Revenue − Expenses = Net Income (or Net Loss)*. Adding gains and losses, the full version is *Revenue + Gains − Expenses − Losses = Net Income (or Net Loss)*. Chris had no gains or losses in August.

A subtlety the income statement is hiding from you: the $1,000 insurance payment was for a *year* of coverage, not a month. Under *cash-basis* accounting — which Chris is informally using because her business is brand-new and small — the $1,000 hits August expenses because it was paid in August. Under *accrual-basis* accounting, only $1,000 ÷ 12 = $83.33 would hit August's expenses, and the remaining $916.67 would sit on the balance sheet as a *prepaid asset*, then move into expense $83.33 at a time over the next eleven months. The two accounting bases produce different income statements from the same transactions. The accrual basis is what GAAP requires for most organizations; we'll return to it in §2.4 and develop it formally in Chapter 4.

For now, hold the simpler cash-basis number: net income $250 for August.

Move to the Statement of Owner's Equity. Chris started August with $0 of equity in the business — it didn't exist before August 1. She made no separate cash investment in the business beyond what she earned, and she took no distributions out (she didn't pay herself a salary or withdraw the cash). So the statement is short:

**Chris's Landscaping — Statement of Owner's Equity — For the month ended August 31, 2020**

| Element | Amount |
|---|---|
| Capital, August 1, 2020 | $0 |
| Plus: Investments by owner | $0 |
| Plus: Net income (from income statement) | $250 |
| Less: Withdrawals | $0 |
| **Capital, August 31, 2020** | **$250** |

The $250 ending equity is the same number that will appear on the August 31 balance sheet, in the equity section. That is what "interrelated statements" means in practice: the same number, computed on the income statement, flows through the equity statement, and lands on the balance sheet. If a beginning student gets only one piece of structural intuition from this chapter, get this one — the four statements are not four independent reports, they are one connected accounting of the organization's economic life, told from four different vantage points.

The four elements of the income statement deserve a closer look because each has a sharp definition.

*Revenue* is the value of goods and services sold to customers in the organization's main line of business, during the period. For Chris's Landscaping, revenue is what she earned from landscaping work for clients. For a coffee shop, revenue is what was rung up at the register for coffee, food, gift cards, and merchandise. For a software company, revenue is what subscribers paid for access to the software. Revenue, in FASB's formal language, is "inflows or other enhancements of assets … from delivering or producing goods, rendering services, or other activities that constitute the entity's *ongoing major or central operations*." The phrase that matters there is *ongoing major or central*. Revenue is from the main thing the business does.

*Expense* is the cost of generating that revenue. For Chris, the brake repair, the fuel, and the insurance were expenses. For a coffee shop, the coffee beans, the milk, the barista's wages, the rent, and the utilities are expenses. Expenses are the outflows the business takes on in order to be able to take in revenue.

*Gain* is an inflow from a transaction that is *peripheral* or *incidental* to the main business — not part of revenue. If Chris had bought a piece of land for $1,500 (intending to use it for a storage facility), then sold it for $2,000 because she found a better option, the $500 difference would be a gain, not revenue. Chris's main business is landscaping, not real estate. The $500 increases the business's value the same way revenue would, but reporting it as a gain rather than revenue tells a reader something important: this is not part of the ongoing earning power of the business, it's a one-off. A reader trying to forecast Chris's next month wouldn't expect the $500 to recur.

*Loss* is the mirror image of a gain — an outflow from a peripheral or incidental transaction. If Chris had sold the same land for $1,200 instead of $2,000, the $300 shortfall against her purchase price would be a loss.

The distinction between revenue and gain (and between expense and loss) matters for one reason above all others: *ongoing earning power*. A business with $1,000,000 of revenue and $50,000 of net income looks different to a banker than a business with $200,000 of revenue, $750,000 in gains from selling off old equipment, and $50,000 of net income. The bottom line is the same. The future is not. The first business has a self-sustaining engine; the second has just sold off its furniture. Investors learn to read the income statement structurally, not just the bottom line. *Quality of earnings* — the share of net income coming from sustainable revenue versus from one-off gains — is one of the most consequential analytical moves a reader of financial statements can make. We'll return to it in later chapters.

The Statement of Owner's Equity has two elements not captured on the income statement: *investments by owners* and *distributions to owners*. An investment by owners is what happens when an owner contributes more cash (or other assets) to the business in exchange for additional ownership claim — the owner is putting capital in. A distribution to owners is the reverse — the business is paying out cash (or other assets) to the owners as a return on their investment. In a sole proprietorship like Chris's Landscaping, distributions are *withdrawals* or *drawings*. In a corporation, they are *dividends*. The vocabulary changes; the underlying activity is the same — equity is moving from the business's books to the owners' personal books.

When the Roku S-1 we opened the chapter with showed the company's investments by owners, those were the proceeds from the IPO — the company sold shares to the public and the public's $117 million (and ultimately more) became the company's invested capital. Distributions had not started; Roku had no dividend at the time of the IPO. Equity, on the company's books, was substantially higher after September 28 than before, by virtue of an investment-by-owners transaction at scale.

## 2.4 The balance sheet, equity as a concept, and the statement of cash flows

The balance sheet shifts the time orientation. The income statement and the statement of owner's equity describe the period — the month, the quarter, the year. The balance sheet describes a *single date*, usually the last day of the period: what the organization owns, owes, and is worth at that exact moment.

Three categories sit on the balance sheet, and they balance.

*Assets* are resources the organization owns or controls that are expected to provide economic benefit. They split two ways. By *time-to-cash*: a *current asset* (or short-term asset) is expected to be converted to cash, sold, or consumed within one year — cash itself, accounts receivable, inventory, supplies. A *noncurrent asset* (or long-term asset) is expected to provide value for more than a year — equipment, land, buildings, vehicles, long-term investments. By *physical form*: a *tangible asset* has physical substance (a building, a truck, an inventory of goods); an *intangible asset* does not (a trademark, a patent, a copyright, goodwill). A trademark you cannot touch — but you can prevent others from using, you can sell, you can borrow against. It has value, even though it has no physical existence. The intangible-asset category has grown substantially as the economy has shifted toward software, brands, data, and intellectual property; modern balance sheets often have more value in intangibles than in tangibles.

*Liabilities* are amounts the organization owes to others. Like assets, they split by time-to-payment. *Current liabilities* (or short-term liabilities) are due within a year — accounts payable to suppliers, wages payable to employees, taxes payable, the current portion of long-term debt due within a year. *Noncurrent liabilities* (or long-term liabilities) are due more than a year out — long-term notes payable, mortgages payable, bonds payable.

*Equity* is what is left for the owners after liabilities are subtracted from assets. The accounting equation that defines the balance sheet is *Assets = Liabilities + Equity*, or rearranged, *Equity = Assets − Liabilities*.

The clearest way to internalize what equity is: think about a house. Suppose a family buys a $200,000 home with a $25,000 down payment and a $175,000 mortgage. On the day of closing, what is their equity in the house? The asset (the home) is worth $200,000. The liability (the mortgage) is $175,000. Their equity is $200,000 − $175,000 = $25,000. If the home appreciates to $250,000 next year and the mortgage is paid down to $170,000, equity rises to $80,000 — partly because the asset got more valuable, partly because the liability shrank. If the home depreciates to $180,000 and the mortgage is still $175,000, equity falls to $5,000. The same arithmetic governs every organization. Equity is not money in a separate pile; it is the residual claim of the owners after every other claim has been settled.

For Chris's Landscaping on August 31, 2020 (cash-basis), the balance sheet looks like this — assuming her only asset is the $250 in the business checking account, she has no liabilities, and her equity is the $250 she earned during August.

**Chris's Landscaping — Balance Sheet — As of August 31, 2020**

| Assets | | Liabilities and Equity | |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash | $250 | Liabilities | $0 |
| | | Owner's equity (Capital) | $250 |
| **Total assets** | **$250** | **Total liabilities and equity** | **$250** |

The two sides balance — they always do. That is not coincidence; the accounting equation guarantees it.

Now, the *Statement of Cash Flows*. The reason this statement exists is the gap between accrual-basis income and actual cash. Under cash-basis accounting, the income statement and the cash flow statement would tell the same story — every recorded transaction would involve cash. Under accrual-basis accounting, they don't. A landscaping business that performed $500 of work on April 1 with payment due in 45 days would record $500 of revenue on April 1 (accrual) but receive zero cash until May 16. A printer-supply purchase made on July 17 with payment due in 15 days would record $160 of expense on July 17 (accrual) but disburse zero cash until August 1. By the time a quarter closes, an income statement under accrual accounting can show strong net income while the cash-flow statement shows the company is bleeding cash, or weak net income while cash is piling up. *Both are true.* The two statements are answering different questions.

The cash flow statement organizes cash movement into three categories. *Operating activities* — cash flows from the day-to-day business of the organization (cash received from customers, cash paid to suppliers and employees). *Investing activities* — cash flows from buying or selling long-term assets (purchasing equipment, selling a building, buying or selling investments in other companies). *Financing activities* — cash flows from raising or returning capital (borrowing money, repaying loans, issuing stock, paying dividends). Each category gets its own subtotal; the three subtotals plus the beginning cash balance equal the ending cash balance — which had better match the cash figure on the balance sheet, or something is wrong upstream.

The trade-off here, structural: accrual accounting buys *better matching of revenues to the period that earned them* at the cost of *obscuring the cash position*. The cash flow statement is the corrective. Together, the income statement (under accrual) and the cash flow statement (in its own format) give a reader both pictures — which costs the organization nothing it wasn't already producing.

The most important comparison a reader of complete financial statements can make is between net income and cash flow from operations. When net income is positive but operating cash flow is consistently negative, something is wrong — the income exists on paper but is not being collected. When operating cash flow exceeds net income, the company is generating real cash that the accrual rules are conservatively delaying onto the income statement. Either pattern, sustained, tells a reader something the income statement alone cannot.

## 2.5 Synthesis: where the legal structure shifts the vocabulary

Three legal structures are common in U.S. business. The accounting work is essentially the same across them, but the vocabulary on the financial statements shifts.

A *sole proprietorship* is a single individual operating a business — Chris's Landscaping is the example we've been using. It is the easiest structure to form (a business license, sometimes a fictitious-name registration, and the books begin), receives favorable tax treatment (income passes through to the owner's individual return, taxed once), and gives the owner full control. The trade-offs: *unlimited personal liability* (if the business is sued or goes into debt, the owner's personal assets are at risk), a *limited life* (the business doesn't outlive the owner unless explicitly transferred), *limited capital-raising* (no shareholders to sell to), and *limited expertise* (the proprietor's skill set is the business's skill set). On the financial statements, the equity section is called *Capital* or *Owner's Equity*, and distributions are called *withdrawals* or *drawings*.

A *partnership* is two or more people contributing money, property, or services to operate as co-owners. It shares most of the sole proprietorship's advantages and disadvantages — pass-through taxation, fairly easy formation, unlimited personal liability (modified in some structures like the Limited Liability Partnership) — and adds *broader access to capital and expertise* (more partners means more contributed capital and more skill sets) at the cost of *increased complexity to form* (the partnership agreement specifying decision-making authority, profit-sharing, dispute resolution must be written carefully). On the financial statements, the equity section becomes *Partners' Capital*, with separate capital accounts for each partner.

A *corporation* is a legally distinct entity — separate from its owners — with shareholders, a board of directors, and managers acting as agents. The major advantages are *limited liability* (shareholders' personal assets are not at risk for corporate debts) and *easy access to large amounts of capital* (the corporation can sell stock to investors) and *unlimited life* (the corporation survives changes in ownership). The major disadvantage is *double taxation*: the corporation pays income tax on its profits, and shareholders pay income tax again on dividends received. On the financial statements, the equity section becomes *Stockholders' Equity*, with line items including *Common Stock* (par value of issued shares), additional paid-in capital, and *Retained Earnings* (the accumulated net income not yet distributed as dividends). Distributions to owners are *dividends*.

Roku, in our opening scene, is a corporation. The September 2017 S-1 was the formal disclosure required when a corporation issues stock to the public for the first time — an *initial public offering* or IPO. Once the offering closed, Roku was a *publicly traded company*, meaning its stock could be bought and sold on a stock exchange (NASDAQ, in Roku's case) by anyone. A *privately held company* is a corporation whose stock is not available to the general public — it might be tightly held by founders, employees, or a small group of investors, but the public cannot buy in. The accounting work on the financial statements is the same regardless of public/private status; what changes is who reads them and what regulatory disclosures are required.

The four statements close the loop:

- **Income statement** — flow over period — *Revenue + Gains − Expenses − Losses = Net Income*
- **Statement of owner's equity (or stockholders' equity)** — flow over period — *Beginning Equity + Investments + Net Income − Withdrawals/Dividends = Ending Equity*
- **Balance sheet** — snapshot on date — *Assets = Liabilities + Equity*
- **Statement of cash flows** — flow over period — *Operating + Investing + Financing = Change in Cash; Beginning Cash + Change = Ending Cash*

The same set of transactions feeds all four. The four are read together. And the design philosophy — same data, four vantage points, prepared in a fixed order, recoverable into the underlying transactions — is what allows ten thousand strangers to value a company none of them work for at $23.50 a share, or $14, or $5, all on the same day, all from the same disclosed numbers.

## 2.7 Chapter summary

You walked into this chapter knowing the architecture of financial reporting exists. You should walk out able to read the artifacts that architecture produces.

Four statements, four jobs. The Income Statement measures performance over a period: revenues plus gains minus expenses minus losses equals net income. The Statement of Owner's Equity tracks the change in net worth over the same period: beginning equity plus investments plus net income minus withdrawals or dividends equals ending equity. The Balance Sheet reports the financial position on a single date: assets equal liabilities plus equity. The Statement of Cash Flows reports the cash inflows and outflows for the period, organized into operating, investing, and financing activities, and exists because accrual-basis income and actual cash are not the same thing.

The four statements are interrelated — net income from the first flows to the second; ending equity from the second flows to the third; ending cash from the fourth must reconcile to cash on the third. They are designed to be read together. Reading any one in isolation produces a misleading view.

The single most important idea to carry forward: every transaction the organization undertakes affects multiple statements simultaneously, in disciplined ways. A sale of services on credit increases revenue (income statement), increases accounts receivable (balance sheet), and does *not* affect cash (cash flow statement) until the customer pays. A purchase of equipment for cash decreases cash (balance sheet, cash flow statement) and increases an asset (balance sheet) but does *not* affect the income statement directly — the cost will hit expense gradually, through depreciation, over the equipment's useful life. The mechanics of how these multi-statement effects work are what Chapter 3 builds.

The common mistake to watch for: reading the income statement as if net income equaled cash. The accrual basis breaks that intuition; the cash flow statement exists to repair it.

You should now be able to explain to someone with no accounting background what each of the four statements is for, why they're prepared in a particular order, why they have to be read together, and why the legal structure of the business changes the vocabulary on the equity section without changing the underlying mechanics.
