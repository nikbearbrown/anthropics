# Chapter 16 — Statement of Cash Flows

## 16.1 Opening: The statement that catches what the others miss

A company reports $50 million of net income for the year. The press release leads with that number; the analyst calls focus on it; the headline reads *Strong Earnings*. The next morning a different release goes out: an earnings restatement, a covenant violation, a sudden need for emergency financing. How can a profitable company need emergency cash?

The answer is in the cash flow statement, the fourth and most diagnostic of the four core financial statements. Net income is an *accrual* concept — revenue recognized when earned, expenses when incurred, regardless of cash timing. A company can be earning real revenue, signing real contracts, and shipping real products while its cash position deteriorates because customers aren't paying, inventory is piling up, equipment investments are draining the bank account, or debt service is consuming everything coming in. The income statement does not show any of this. The balance sheet shows the resulting cash level but not how it got there. The statement of cash flows shows *exactly* how it got there, organized into three categories that map cleanly onto how the business is actually using and generating cash.

This chapter develops that statement in detail. By the end of it, you should be able to:

- Distinguish *operating activities*, *investing activities*, and *financing activities*, and identify which transactions belong in which.
- Prepare the operating section of the cash flow statement using the *indirect method* — starting from net income and adjusting for non-cash items and working-capital changes.
- Prepare the operating section using the *direct method* — listing actual cash receipts and disbursements by category.
- Prepare the investing and financing sections.
- Reconcile the change in cash from the cash flow statement to the change in cash on the balance sheet.
- Use the relationships between net income and operating cash flow as analytical signals about quality of earnings and working-capital trends.

You walk in with all fifteen prior chapters of accounting machinery. You walk out with the synthesizing statement that ties them together.

Why does it matter? Because most major corporate failures, when they finally happen, were visible in the cash flow statement long before they showed up in the income statement. Quality of earnings, sustainability of operations, the relationship between reported profit and actual cash generation — these are what the cash flow statement reports. An analyst who reads only the income statement and balance sheet is reading two-thirds of the document. The cash flow statement is the third part, and often the most informative.

## 16.2 The three categories

Every cash inflow and outflow falls into one of three categories.

*Operating activities* — cash flows from the core operations of the business. Cash received from customers; cash paid to suppliers, employees, landlords, utilities, taxes, and interest; cash from operating leases (under current GAAP); cash from any other operating revenues and expenses. This section answers the most consequential cash question: *is the company's actual business generating cash, or is it consuming cash?*

*Investing activities* — cash flows from buying and selling long-term assets and investments. Cash spent acquiring property, plant, and equipment; cash received from selling those assets; cash spent buying or received from selling investments in other companies' securities; cash spent or received from making or collecting loans (other than trade receivables, which are operating). This section answers: *what is the company doing with its long-term capital base — growing it, shrinking it, or holding steady?*

*Financing activities* — cash flows from raising or returning capital. Cash from issuing stock or bonds; cash from borrowing or repaying debt; cash paid as dividends. This section answers: *how is the company funding itself — through debt, through equity, through internal cash generation, or through some combination?*

The structural logic: each category isolates one dimension of the company's cash story. The three subtotals (Operating, Investing, Financing), plus the beginning cash balance, equal the ending cash balance. Reconciliation forces the statement to be internally consistent.

Three classification subtleties worth noting up front. *Interest paid* is operating under U.S. GAAP (counterintuitive — it's the cost of financing, but the FASB classified it as operating decades ago and never moved it). *Interest received* is also operating. *Dividends received* are operating. *Dividends paid* are financing. Under IFRS, companies have more flexibility in classifying interest and dividends, which is one reason cross-border comparability requires care.

## 16.3 The indirect method (operating section)

Two methods exist for preparing the operating section. Both produce the same cash-from-operations figure. Most U.S. companies use the *indirect method* because it's easier to compute from accrual-basis financials.

The indirect method starts with *net income* (an accrual figure) and reconciles to *cash from operations* by adjusting for:

1. *Non-cash income statement items* — depreciation, amortization, depletion, stock-based compensation, gains/losses on asset sales, deferred income tax. These are added back (or subtracted) because they affected net income but didn't affect cash.

2. *Changes in working-capital accounts* — receivables, inventory, prepaid expenses, accounts payable, accrued liabilities, unearned revenue. Increases in current assets reduce cash; decreases increase cash. Increases in current liabilities provide cash; decreases use cash.

The pattern, in summary form:

```
Net Income                                $X
Add: Depreciation and amortization        +X
Add: Stock-based compensation             +X
Less: Gain on sale of equipment           -X (or Add: Loss)
Add/Less: Deferred tax                    ±X
Less: Increase in accounts receivable     -X
Less: Increase in inventory               -X
Add: Increase in accounts payable         +X
Add: Increase in accrued liabilities      +X
Add/Less: Other working capital changes   ±X
                                         ----
Cash from Operating Activities            $X
```

Why each adjustment? Net income includes depreciation as an expense even though no cash went out — depreciation is added back to undo that. An *increase* in accounts receivable means the company recognized revenue but the customer hasn't paid yet — net income is higher than cash receipts by the increase, so the increase is subtracted. An *increase* in inventory means the company spent cash buying inventory that hasn't been sold — cash decreased by an amount that didn't yet hit COGS. An *increase* in accounts payable means the company received goods or services but hasn't paid yet — expense was recorded, but cash stayed.

A worked example. A company reports the following for the year:

- Net income: $80,000
- Depreciation expense: $25,000
- Increase in accounts receivable: $12,000
- Decrease in inventory: $5,000
- Increase in accounts payable: $8,000
- Decrease in accrued liabilities: $3,000
- Gain on sale of equipment: $2,000

Operating section, indirect method:

```
Net Income                                $80,000
Add: Depreciation                         +25,000
Less: Gain on sale of equipment           -2,000
Less: Increase in receivables             -12,000
Add: Decrease in inventory                +5,000
Add: Increase in accounts payable         +8,000
Less: Decrease in accrued liabilities     -3,000
                                         --------
Cash from Operating Activities            $101,000
```

Cash from operations ($101,000) exceeds net income ($80,000) — the company is generating real cash from operations beyond what the income statement reports. This is generally a healthy signal.

If the relationship had been reversed — net income $80,000 but cash from operations only $40,000 — the gap would warrant investigation. The most common causes: receivables growing faster than sales (collections lagging), inventory building up (sales weakening relative to purchasing), aggressive revenue recognition (revenue booked that hasn't been collected and may not be collectible).

## 16.4 The direct method (operating section)

The direct method lists actual cash receipts and cash payments by category, rather than reconciling from net income.

```
Cash received from customers              $620,000
Cash paid to suppliers                    -(280,000)
Cash paid to employees                    -(150,000)
Cash paid for operating expenses          -(60,000)
Cash paid for interest                    -(15,000)
Cash paid for income taxes                -(14,000)
                                         --------
Cash from Operating Activities            $101,000
```

The direct method is more intuitive — it shows actual cash movements rather than reconciling adjustments. It's also more useful for analysts trying to understand the structure of the company's cash generation. But it's harder to prepare from typical accrual-basis records, because the accounting system doesn't usually track "cash received from customers" as a single number — it tracks revenue and changes in receivables, from which the cash receipt is *derived*. The FASB has long encouraged the direct method but most companies use indirect because it's easier.

When a company uses the direct method, it must also disclose a reconciliation from net income to cash from operations (essentially, the indirect-method calculation) in a supplemental schedule. The two methods produce the same answer; they just present it differently.

## 16.5 The investing section

Cash flows from acquiring or disposing of long-term assets and investments.

Typical items:

- Purchase of property, plant, and equipment: cash outflow
- Sale of property, plant, and equipment: cash inflow (at the cash received, not at gain/loss)
- Purchase of long-term investments (other companies' stocks/bonds held long-term): outflow
- Sale of long-term investments: inflow
- Loans made to other parties: outflow
- Collections on those loans: inflow
- Acquisitions of other companies (cash portion): outflow
- Divestitures of business units (cash portion): inflow

The investing section is typically *negative* for growing companies — they're investing in their long-term asset base, which means cash going out. A company with consistently positive investing cash flow is either selling off assets (winding down) or running an investment-heavy business model where investment maturities exceed new investment purchases.

A worked example:

```
Purchase of equipment                     $(60,000)
Sale of old equipment                       12,000
Purchase of investment securities         (40,000)
Sale of investment securities              25,000
                                         --------
Cash from Investing Activities            $(63,000)
```

The company spent $63,000 net on long-term assets and investments during the period.

## 16.6 The financing section

Cash flows from raising or returning capital — debt and equity transactions with creditors and owners.

Typical items:

- Issuance of common or preferred stock: cash inflow
- Repurchase of treasury stock: outflow
- Borrowing on long-term debt: inflow
- Repayment of long-term debt principal: outflow
- Borrowing on short-term notes payable (excluding trade payables): inflow
- Repayment of those notes: outflow
- Cash dividends paid: outflow

The financing section signals the company's capital-structure strategy. Persistent *positive* financing cash flow (issuing debt and equity faster than repaying) is the pattern of a growing company funding expansion externally. Persistent *negative* financing (paying down debt, repurchasing stock, paying dividends faster than issuing new capital) is the pattern of a mature company returning value to capital providers.

A worked example:

```
Issuance of long-term debt                $50,000
Repayment of long-term debt              (20,000)
Issuance of common stock                  30,000
Cash dividends paid                      (15,000)
                                         --------
Cash from Financing Activities           $45,000
```

## 16.7 The full cash flow statement

Pulling all three sections together:

```
Cash from Operating Activities            $101,000
Cash from Investing Activities            (63,000)
Cash from Financing Activities             45,000
                                         --------
Net Increase in Cash                       $83,000
Cash at Beginning of Year                  60,000
                                         --------
Cash at End of Year                        $143,000
```

The ending cash figure must match the Cash and Cash Equivalents line on the year-end balance sheet. The beginning cash matches the prior year's ending cash. Reconciliation is structural; if the numbers don't tie out, an error has been made somewhere in the statement.

The relationship of operating, investing, and financing cash flows over multiple years tells a story. A healthy growth-stage company often shows positive operating CF, negative investing CF (heavily investing in capacity), and positive financing CF (raising capital to fund the investment). A healthy mature company often shows positive operating CF, negative investing CF (replacement-level capex), and negative financing CF (returning capital to investors). A struggling company often shows negative operating CF (the business is consuming cash), supplemented by positive financing (issuing debt or equity to cover the gap) — this pattern is unsustainable and is the financial signature of a company in distress.

## 16.8 What the cash flow statement diagnoses

Three diagnostic relationships are worth memorizing.

*Operating CF vs. net income*. As §16.3 noted, a sustained gap (in either direction) between net income and operating cash flow signals working-capital changes or earnings-quality issues. Healthy companies usually have operating CF reasonably close to net income plus depreciation; persistent divergence warrants investigation.

*Operating CF vs. capex (investing CF for PP&E)*. A company that consistently generates more operating cash flow than it spends on capex has *free cash flow* — cash available for debt repayment, dividends, or repurchases. A company whose capex consistently exceeds operating cash flow is consuming capital and must finance the gap externally; this is fine for a growth-stage company but troubling for a mature one.

*Net change in cash vs. change in net working capital*. The two should bear a sensible relationship. If cash falls but receivables and inventory are growing rapidly, working capital is tying up cash. If cash falls and there's no obvious working-capital growth, look harder — debt repayments, share repurchases, or off-balance-sheet activity may be the explanation.

The cash flow statement is the single most useful tool for evaluating the *economic health* of a business as opposed to its accrual-accounting profitability. The two are related; they are not the same.

## 16.10 Chapter summary

You walked into this chapter with three financial statements. You walk out with the fourth — the most diagnostic of the set.

The cash flow statement organizes a company's cash inflows and outflows into three categories: operating (the core business), investing (long-term asset and investment activity), and financing (debt and equity capital activity). The three subtotals plus beginning cash equal ending cash, which must match the balance sheet. Reconciliation enforces internal consistency.

The operating section can be prepared by the *indirect method* (start with net income, adjust for non-cash items and working-capital changes) or the *direct method* (list cash receipts and disbursements directly). Both produce the same answer; indirect is dominant in U.S. practice.

The investing and financing sections list specific cash transactions in long-term assets and capital-structure changes respectively.

The single most important idea: net income and cash from operations are different numbers, and the relationship between them is one of the most diagnostic signals in financial analysis. Companies with sustained operating cash flow well above net income tend to be high-quality earners. Companies with sustained operating cash flow well below net income are working-capital-intensive, growing, or have earnings-quality issues that bear investigation.

The common mistake to watch for: treating the cash flow statement as redundant because the balance sheet already shows beginning and ending cash. The change in cash is on the balance sheet; *why* it changed — operating, investing, or financing — is only on the cash flow statement, and that *why* is the analytical content.

The accounting cycle is not different in structure from one organization to the next. The complexity scales with the size of the business — a Fortune 500 close has thousands of accounts and hundreds of adjusting entries, where a sole proprietorship has dozens of each — but the fundamental moves are the same. Record transactions in journal entries that keep the accounting equation in balance. Adjust at period-end for accruals and deferrals. Prepare financial statements from the adjusted trial balance. Close temporary accounts. Move forward.

The four financial statements together are the language by which a company's economic life is communicated to anyone who needs to evaluate it — investors, lenders, regulators, employees, customers, the company's own management. They are not perfect — the historical-cost convention, the accrual basis, the GAAP/IFRS divergence, the limits of disclosure all impose costs on the readability of the result. ## 16.12 A final analytical example

To consolidate the chapter, walk through the diagnostic value of a complete cash flow statement on a real-style company. Suppose a hypothetical retailer reports for the year:

- Net income: $40 million
- Depreciation and amortization: $25 million
- Increase in receivables: $12 million
- Increase in inventory: $35 million
- Decrease in accounts payable: $8 million
- Stock-based compensation: $10 million
- Capital expenditures (PP&E purchases): $80 million
- Long-term debt issued: $50 million
- Dividends paid: $15 million
- Treasury stock repurchased: $20 million

Indirect method operating section:

```
Net Income                                $40
+ Depreciation/amortization                25
+ Stock-based compensation                 10
- Increase in receivables                 (12)
- Increase in inventory                   (35)
- Decrease in accounts payable             (8)
                                         -----
Cash from Operating Activities            $20
```

Investing section:

```
Capital expenditures                      $(80)
                                         -----
Cash from Investing Activities            $(80)
```

Financing section:

```
Issuance of long-term debt                 $50
Dividends paid                            (15)
Treasury stock repurchased                (20)
                                         -----
Cash from Financing Activities             $15
```

Net change in cash: $20 + ($80) + $15 = ($45 million). The company's cash balance fell by $45 million during the year despite reporting $40 million of net income.

What does this say diagnostically?

Operating cash flow ($20M) is half of net income ($40M). The bulk of the gap is in working capital — a $35M inventory build and a $12M receivables increase that operations didn't absorb. The receivables increase could be a sales-growth signal; the inventory build is more concerning, particularly if sales aren't growing in proportion. Stock-based compensation adds back $10M but represents real dilution to existing shareholders, even though it's not a current cash drain.

Capital expenditure ($80M) is double operating cash flow. The company is investing heavily — fine for a growth-stage company, expensive for one without external funding capacity. The financing section reveals that the company funded the gap with $50M of new long-term debt, while simultaneously returning $35M to shareholders through dividends and buybacks. The combined message: the company is relying on debt to fund both expansion and shareholder returns, while operating cash flow is well below what would be needed for either alone.

A reader who looked only at the income statement would see $40M of net income and conclude the year went well. A reader who looked at the cash flow statement would see a company that is consuming capital, accumulating inventory faster than it can sell it, and increasing leverage to maintain shareholder returns. Both pictures are part of the same set of financial statements; both are technically accurate; the cash flow statement is far more useful for evaluating whether the company is on a sustainable trajectory.

This is what the cash flow statement is for. It is the diagnostic instrument that catches what the others miss.
