# Chapter 15 — Partnership Accounting

## 15.1 Opening: Two friends, one business

Maya and Theo open a graphic-design studio together. Maya brings $40,000 in cash; Theo brings $25,000 in cash plus a $15,000 desktop computer setup. They agree to share profits and losses equally. They want a structure simpler than a corporation — no shareholders, no board of directors, no Form S-1 — but more formal than each running a separate sole proprietorship that happens to share an address. The legal structure is a *partnership* — two or more people contributing money, property, or services to operate a business as co-owners.

Partnerships occupy a particular niche in U.S. business. They were the dominant form for professional service firms (law, accounting, medicine, architecture) for most of the twentieth century. They remain common for small joint ventures, real estate investments, and certain professional practices. They have been substantially displaced for everything else by the *limited liability company (LLC)*, which provides corporate-style liability protection with partnership-style pass-through taxation. But the accounting concepts of partnership remain important: LLC accounting at the entity level looks much like partnership accounting, and many real LLCs are structured as multi-member partnerships for tax purposes.

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:

- Distinguish a *general partnership* from a *limited partnership* and from an *LLP* (limited liability partnership).
- Journalize partnership formation, including non-cash contributions valued at fair market value.
- Apply alternative *profit-and-loss-sharing* arrangements: equal sharing, fixed ratios, salary allowances and interest on capital before distribution.
- Journalize the *admission* of a new partner (by purchase of an existing interest, or by investment in the partnership).
- Journalize the *withdrawal* of a partner.
- Journalize *partnership liquidation*: sell assets, pay liabilities, distribute remaining cash to partners according to the agreed scheme.

## 15.2 Forms of partnership

A *general partnership (GP)* is the simplest form: two or more general partners, each with full management authority, full profit-and-loss participation, and full personal liability for partnership obligations. The simplicity is the cost — every partner is exposed to the actions of every other partner, and personal assets are at risk for partnership debts.

A *limited partnership (LP)* has at least one general partner (with management authority and unlimited liability) and at least one limited partner (whose liability is limited to their investment, who has no day-to-day management role). LPs are common in private investment funds — the fund manager is the general partner, the investors are limited partners.

A *limited liability partnership (LLP)* is a variant where every partner has limited liability for the firm's debts and for the malpractice of other partners (though typically each partner remains personally liable for their own malpractice). LLPs became the dominant structure for U.S. law and accounting firms after a wave of partnership liability claims in the 1990s made the unlimited-liability features of GPs intolerable.

The *limited liability company (LLC)*, as mentioned, is a hybrid that has displaced LPs and GPs for most new small-business formation. LLCs offer corporate-style liability protection with substantial flexibility in management structure and pass-through taxation. The accounting at the entity level is partnership-style; the legal protections are corporate-style.

## 15.3 Partnership formation

When the partnership is formed, each partner's contribution is recorded at *fair market value*, with a credit to that partner's *capital account*.

For Maya and Theo:

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Cash | $40,000 | |
| Maya, Capital | | $40,000 |

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Cash | $25,000 | |
| Equipment | $15,000 | |
| Theo, Capital | | $40,000 |

Each partner's capital balance starts at $40,000 — each contributed $40,000 of value, even though the composition differed. The non-cash contribution is recorded at fair market value, *not* at the contributing partner's historical cost. If Theo had originally paid $20,000 for the equipment but it's worth $15,000 now, the journal entry uses $15,000 — the partnership starts with assets at current value, and Theo individually bears the historical loss on his contribution.

A *partnership agreement* (sometimes called *articles of partnership*) is the written document that codifies the deal: contributions, profit-and-loss-sharing arrangement, management roles, withdrawal procedures, dissolution mechanics. The agreement is the partnership's analog of corporate bylaws. Drafting it carefully at formation prevents most of the disputes that destroy partnerships in practice.

## 15.4 Profit and loss sharing

In the absence of a partnership agreement specifying otherwise, partnership law typically defaults to *equal* sharing of profits and losses regardless of capital contribution. Most agreements override this default with one of several sharing methods.

*Fixed ratio*: profits and losses split in a stated ratio — 60/40, 1/3 each among three partners, etc. The ratio is fixed regardless of individual contribution amounts or work effort.

*Capital ratio*: profits split in proportion to capital balances. Aligns reward with invested capital.

*Salary allowance plus residual*: each partner is "paid" a salary allowance (e.g., $50,000 to Maya, $40,000 to Theo) before any profit is shared. The salary is *not actually paid* — it's a profit-allocation mechanism. After salary allowances, any remaining profit (or loss) is shared in a stated ratio.

*Interest on capital plus residual*: each partner receives interest on their capital balance (e.g., 6% of beginning-of-year capital) before profit is shared. Aligns reward with both capital and effort.

Combinations of the above are common. A typical arrangement: salary allowance to each working partner, interest on capital to all partners, residual split equally.

Worked example. Suppose Maya and Theo's first year produces $80,000 of net income. Their agreement provides: Maya salary $30,000, Theo salary $25,000, 6% interest on each partner's beginning capital, residual shared equally.

Salary allowances: $30,000 + $25,000 = $55,000.
Interest on capital: $40,000 × 6% = $2,400 each = $4,800 total.
Allocated so far: $55,000 + $4,800 = $59,800.
Residual to share equally: $80,000 − $59,800 = $20,200; $10,100 each.

Total to Maya: $30,000 + $2,400 + $10,100 = $42,500.
Total to Theo: $25,000 + $2,400 + $10,100 = $37,500.

Closing entry at year-end (analog to closing Income Summary into Retained Earnings, but here into capital accounts):

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Income Summary | $80,000 | |
| Maya, Capital | | $42,500 |
| Theo, Capital | | $37,500 |

Each partner's capital account grows by their share of the year's net income. If the year had produced a loss, the entry would reverse — Income Summary credited, capital accounts debited (reducing their balances).

## 15.5 Admission of a new partner

Two structural mechanisms exist for adding a new partner.

### Purchase of an existing interest

The new partner buys a portion of an existing partner's interest, typically at negotiated price. The transaction is *between the partners* — the partnership itself doesn't gain new capital.

If a third partner Yusuf buys 25% of Maya's interest for $15,000 in a side transaction, the partnership's books reflect only the transfer:

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Maya, Capital | (25% of Maya's capital balance) | |
| Yusuf, Capital | | (same amount) |

The $15,000 cash flows directly from Yusuf to Maya, outside the partnership's books. Maya's capital account drops; Yusuf's appears with the corresponding balance. Total partnership capital is unchanged.

### Investment in the partnership

The new partner contributes assets (typically cash) to the partnership in exchange for a capital interest. The partnership grows; existing partners' interests dilute proportionally.

Suppose Yusuf instead invests $30,000 cash directly into the partnership for a one-third interest. Maya and Theo's combined capital before Yusuf was $80,000 (assuming no profits yet); after Yusuf, total partnership capital is $80,000 + $30,000 = $110,000. Yusuf's one-third interest = $36,667. But Yusuf only contributed $30,000.

The discrepancy must be reconciled. Two approaches:

*Bonus method*: the difference is treated as a bonus to or from the existing partners.

If Yusuf is paying *less* than the proportional value (here $30,000 for $36,667 of interest), Maya and Theo are giving up $6,667 of their existing capital to attract Yusuf — perhaps because Yusuf brings unique skills or an existing client base. The bonus comes out of Maya's and Theo's capital accounts in their profit-sharing ratio.

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Cash | $30,000 | |
| Maya, Capital | $3,333 | |
| Theo, Capital | $3,333 | |
| Yusuf, Capital | | $36,667 |

If Yusuf is paying *more* than proportional (e.g., $50,000 for a one-third interest), the bonus flows in the other direction — Yusuf's investment is recorded at the proportional value, with the excess credited to Maya and Theo proportionally.

*Goodwill method*: the difference is treated as an unrecorded asset or implicit valuation. Less common in modern practice; the bonus method is dominant under U.S. GAAP.

## 15.6 Withdrawal of a partner

The reverse case. A partner leaves the partnership, taking their share. The mechanics depend on whether the withdrawing partner is paid by the partnership directly or by a continuing partner.

*Withdrawal at recorded book value*: the simplest case. The partnership pays the withdrawing partner an amount equal to their capital balance.

If Theo (capital $37,500 + retained earnings) withdraws and is paid $40,000 cash from the partnership:

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Theo, Capital | $40,000 | |
| Cash | | $40,000 |

(Assuming Theo's capital balance is $40,000 at withdrawal.)

*Withdrawal at more or less than book value*: a bonus passes between the withdrawing and continuing partners.

If the partnership pays Theo $50,000 to withdraw (because the partnership has unrecorded goodwill, or because the continuing partners want to ensure clean separation), the $10,000 excess is a bonus to Theo from the continuing partners' capital:

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Theo, Capital | $40,000 | |
| Maya, Capital | $5,000 | |
| Yusuf, Capital | $5,000 | |
| Cash | | $50,000 |

Maya and Yusuf each give up $5,000 of their capital to settle the withdrawal cleanly.

*Death of a partner* technically dissolves the partnership under common-law rules, though most agreements provide for continuation by the surviving partners with the deceased partner's interest paid to their estate.

## 15.7 Partnership liquidation

The end of the partnership. Three steps:

1. *Sell the partnership's noncash assets* for cash. Any gain or loss on sale is allocated to the partners in their profit-and-loss-sharing ratio.

2. *Pay all partnership liabilities* in full.

3. *Distribute remaining cash* to the partners according to their *capital balances* (after allocation of liquidation gains and losses).

Worked example. The partnership's balance sheet at the start of liquidation:

```
Cash:           $20,000
Other assets:   $80,000
Total assets:   $100,000

Liabilities:    $30,000
Maya, Capital:  $40,000
Theo, Capital:  $30,000
Total L&E:      $100,000
```

Maya and Theo share profits 60/40.

Step 1: sell the other assets for $50,000 (a $30,000 loss). Allocate the loss: Maya 60% × $30,000 = $18,000; Theo 40% × $30,000 = $12,000.

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Cash | $50,000 | |
| Loss on Liquidation (or directly to capital) | | |
| Maya, Capital | $18,000 | |
| Theo, Capital | $12,000 | |
| Other Assets | | $80,000 |

After Step 1: Cash $70,000; Maya capital $22,000; Theo capital $18,000; liabilities $30,000.

Step 2: pay liabilities.

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Liabilities | $30,000 | |
| Cash | | $30,000 |

After Step 2: Cash $40,000; Maya capital $22,000; Theo capital $18,000.

Step 3: distribute remaining cash according to capital balances.

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Maya, Capital | $22,000 | |
| Theo, Capital | $18,000 | |
| Cash | | $40,000 |

After Step 3: all balances are zero. The partnership is dissolved.

A wrinkle: if a liquidation loss leaves a partner with a *deficit* capital balance (negative), the partner is legally required to contribute additional cash to cover the deficit. If the partner is unable (insolvent), the deficit is allocated to the remaining partners in their P&L ratio. This is one structural reason general partners take partnership debts seriously — they can be called on for personal contributions if the partnership liquidates with losses exceeding their capital.

## 15.9 Chapter summary

You walked into this chapter knowing about sole proprietorships and corporations. You walk out with the partnership form that sits structurally between them.

A partnership has two or more co-owners, each with a capital account on the books. Profit-and-loss sharing is governed by the partnership agreement; common arrangements include fixed ratios, capital ratios, salary allowances, and interest on capital. Net income closes into the partner capital accounts in their respective shares.

Admission of a new partner can occur by purchase of an existing interest (between partners, off the partnership books) or by investment in the partnership itself (which grows the partnership's capital). The bonus method handles the difference between the new partner's contribution and proportional value.

Withdrawal reverses the admission process. Liquidation sells noncash assets, pays liabilities, and distributes remaining cash to partners by capital balance — with any liquidation losses allocated to capital accounts in the partners' P&L ratio.

The LLP form (and the LLC, structurally) has displaced general partnerships for most professional services, providing limited liability while preserving partnership-style pass-through taxation and capital-account accounting.

The single most important idea: a partnership's accounting is dominated by *capital accounts* — one per partner, tracking each partner's separate equity claim. The income statement is unitary; the balance sheet's equity section partitions across partners. Every transaction that changes equity must be allocated correctly to the right capital account.

The common mistake to watch for: confusing salary allowances and interest on capital with actual cash payments. They aren't paid in cash — they're profit-allocation mechanisms. A salary allowance to a partner is a *share of profit*, not a wage subject to payroll taxes.

## 15.10 The drawings account, in passing

A small but worth-naming mechanic. Partners often take cash out of the partnership periodically — for living expenses, to fund their own taxes, simply to receive the value they've earned. These withdrawals are recorded in a *Drawings* account (one per partner, mirroring the temporary-account pattern from sole proprietorships):

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Maya, Drawings | $5,000 | |
| Cash | | $5,000 |

At year-end, each partner's Drawings account is closed against their Capital account:

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Maya, Capital | $5,000 | |
| Maya, Drawings | | $5,000 |

The drawings are *not* expenses. They are not deducted from net income. They are distributions to the owner, parallel to a corporate dividend.

A partner who takes drawings exceeding their share of profits will see their capital account decline over time. Some agreements limit drawings to a stated maximum or allow them only after a partnership-wide threshold is reached.

## 15.11 Comparing equity sections across business forms

A consolidating note. The balance-sheet equity section now has three forms in your repertoire.

*Sole proprietorship*: a single line, *Owner's Capital*, that captures the cumulative effect of investments, profits, losses, and withdrawals.

*Partnership*: one *Capital* line per partner — *Maya, Capital*, *Theo, Capital*, etc. — each tracking that partner's portion of the cumulative equity. The total of all capital accounts equals total partnership equity.

*Corporation*: multiple lines — *Common Stock*, *Preferred Stock*, *Additional Paid-in Capital*, *Retained Earnings*, *Treasury Stock* (contra) — that together capture corporate equity. The total of all components equals total stockholders' equity.

The structural difference: sole-proprietorship and partnership equity is *partitioned by owner identity* (one line per owner). Corporate equity is *partitioned by source* (paid-in vs. earned vs. repurchased), with the partition by ownership occurring outside the books in shareholder records. The accounting reflects how each form of business actually thinks of its owners — as identifiable individuals (sole prop and partnership) versus as fungible shareholders (corporation).

For the LLC, the form most modern small businesses adopt, the equity section follows partnership conventions when the LLC has multiple members, sole-proprietorship conventions when it has a single member. The legal protections are corporate-style; the accounting is not.

## 15.12 Tax pass-through and the form of taxation

A note that ties the chapter to the tax structures partners actually face. A partnership, unlike a C corporation, *does not pay federal income tax at the entity level*. Instead, the partnership files an information return (IRS Form 1065) reporting its income and how it was allocated among the partners. Each partner receives a *Schedule K-1* showing their share of the partnership's income, deductions, credits, and other tax items. The partner reports those items on their personal income tax return and pays tax at their individual rates.

This is *pass-through taxation*. The same income is taxed once — at the partner level — rather than twice (once at the entity level and again at the shareholder level, as happens with C corporations).

Pass-through taxation has consequences. A partner can owe tax on partnership income that was *not distributed* — if the partnership earned $100,000 of net income but distributed only $20,000 in cash, the partner still pays tax on their full share of the $100,000. The partner's basis in the partnership (analogous to the capital account) increases by their share of income and decreases by distributions. The mechanics are complex enough that K-1s are notoriously frustrating for individual taxpayers and are typically prepared by tax professionals.

The same pass-through structure applies to S corporations, LLCs taxed as partnerships, and certain trusts. C corporations pay entity-level tax; pass-through entities don't. The choice between corporate and pass-through structure for a small business is, more than anything else, a tax-planning decision driven by current and projected income levels and the owners' personal tax situations.

## 15.13 A worked liquidation with deficit

To consolidate the deficit-allocation rule mentioned in §15.7, walk through one case. Three partners — A, B, C — share profits and losses equally. At the start of liquidation:

- Cash $10,000
- Other assets $50,000 (book value)
- Liabilities $30,000
- A's Capital $10,000
- B's Capital $12,000
- C's Capital $8,000

The other assets are sold for $20,000 — a $30,000 loss, allocated $10,000 to each partner. After the sale, capital balances become:

- A: $10,000 − $10,000 = $0
- B: $12,000 − $10,000 = $2,000
- C: $8,000 − $10,000 = ($2,000) — a deficit

After paying the $30,000 liabilities, cash on hand: $10,000 + $20,000 − $30,000 = $0. Total capital: $0 + $2,000 − $2,000 = $0. Books balance.

If C is solvent and contributes $2,000 cash to clear the deficit:

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Cash | $2,000 | |
| C, Capital | | $2,000 |

That cash is then distributed to B (the only partner with positive capital):

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| B, Capital | $2,000 | |
| Cash | | $2,000 |

If C is *insolvent* and cannot contribute the $2,000, the deficit is allocated to A and B in their P&L sharing ratio (here, 50/50 since A's capital was wiped to zero and B is the only one remaining with capital). Both A and B absorb $1,000 of C's deficit.

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| A, Capital | $1,000 | |
| B, Capital | $1,000 | |
| C, Capital | | $2,000 |

Now A's capital is ($1,000) — a deficit — and B's is $1,000. A is presumably solvent, so contributes $1,000:

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Cash | $1,000 | |
| A, Capital | | $1,000 |

Cash distributed to B:

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| B, Capital | $1,000 | |
| Cash | | $1,000 |

If A is also insolvent, B absorbs the deficit. The structural rule is that any partner with personal liability who can pay must pay; deficits not coverable by their original holders fall on remaining partners. This is one of the unattractive features of general partnerships in liquidation that motivated the migration to LLP and LLC structures.
