# Chapter 3 — Analyzing and Recording Transactions

## 3.1 Opening: Mark Summers opens his dry cleaner

Mark Summers is finishing college and wants to open a dry-cleaning business. He has the name picked out — *Supreme Cleaners*. He has a rough idea of his startup costs: presses, hangers, a steam unit, racks of plastic. He has a banker who has agreed in principle to extend a loan if Mark can show monthly financial information. He has a small group of family members who might be willing to invest in the venture in exchange for a share of ownership. What Mark does not have, yet, is any way to *record* what happens once Supreme Cleaners begins operating — the cash that comes in, the equipment that goes out the door, the wages that get paid, the bills that arrive, the customers who pay on the spot versus the ones who run a tab.

Without a recording system, none of the rest of this matters. The banker cannot lend without monthly financials. The investors cannot evaluate their stake without an account of how the money was spent. The IRS cannot be paid (or argued with) without a record of what was earned. And Mark himself cannot tell whether the business is making money. The four financial statements you met in Chapter 2 are the *output*. The recording system is the *engine* that produces them.

This chapter is about that engine. By the end of it, you should be able to:

- State the accounting equation, and identify how each kind of business activity affects it.
- Explain why double-entry bookkeeping forces every transaction to keep the equation in balance.
- Apply the rules of debits and credits to the major account categories — assets, liabilities, equity, revenues, expenses, dividends — and identify each account's normal balance.
- Take a list of business transactions and prepare journal entries, post them to ledger accounts (using T-accounts), and produce an unadjusted trial balance.
- Identify the foundational accounting principles and assumptions (going concern, separate entity, monetary unit, time period, revenue recognition, expense recognition, full disclosure, conservatism) that govern how transactions get recorded.

You walk in with Chapter 2's vocabulary plus an awareness that financial statements have to come from *somewhere*. This chapter shows you the somewhere. The mechanics are not glamorous — they are arithmetic, conventions, and the discipline of writing every transaction down twice in compatible ways. But the discipline is everything. Every working accounting system on earth — from a sole proprietor's desktop QuickBooks file to SAP installations running multinationals — is built on the moves this chapter teaches.

Why does it matter? Because the four statements you met in Chapter 2 are not produced by inspiration. They are produced by a strict, rule-bound process that takes individual transactions, records each one in two compatible places, sorts them into accounts, sums the accounts, and assembles the totals into the four reports. If the recording is sloppy, the statements are wrong. If the recording is disciplined, the statements can be trusted to within the limits of the rules themselves. Banks and investors and regulators rely on that discipline. Mark Summers cannot get his loan without it.

## 3.2 Principles, assumptions, and the conceptual framework

Before any transaction gets recorded, the recording system rests on a set of conventions that determine *how* to record. These are the principles, assumptions, and concepts that constitute U.S. GAAP's *conceptual framework*, set primarily by the Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB) you met in Chapter 1.

Four assumptions about the entity being measured:

The *separate entity assumption* says the business is its own accounting entity, distinct from its owners and from any other business. Mark Summers's personal credit card does not appear in Supreme Cleaners' books. Supreme Cleaners' bank account does not commingle with Mark's grocery money. We met this idea in Chapter 2 as the business entity concept.

The *going concern assumption* says the business is presumed to continue operating into the foreseeable future. This presumption justifies a number of accounting conventions — recording equipment at historical cost and depreciating it over its useful life, for example, makes sense only if you expect the business to be around long enough to use the equipment. If a business is *not* a going concern (it is in liquidation, say), the conventions change.

The *monetary unit assumption* says transactions are recorded in a stable monetary unit — the dollar, in the U.S. — and that the dollar is treated as if it were stable in value across the period being measured. Inflation is real and known to accountants, but the basic financial statements ignore it, recording at historical dollars rather than inflation-adjusted ones.

The *time period assumption* (or *periodicity assumption*) says the life of the business can be divided into reporting periods — a month, a quarter, a year — for which financial statements can be prepared. Without this assumption, the "income statement for the period" is meaningless; with it, every income statement is anchored to a defined time interval.

Four principles about how to measure:

The *historical cost principle* says assets are recorded at the price actually paid to acquire them, not at their estimated current market value. A building bought for $400,000 in 2010 still appears on the balance sheet at $400,000 (less accumulated depreciation), even if its market value is now $700,000. The trade-off is direct: historical cost is *verifiable* (a closing document exists) but can be *out of date*; market value is current but harder to determine objectively.

The *revenue recognition principle* says revenue is recorded when it is *earned* — when the goods or services have been delivered to the customer — regardless of when cash changes hands. Mark's dry cleaner records revenue when a customer's clothes are returned cleaned, not when the customer pays.

The *expense recognition principle* (also called the *matching principle*) says expenses are recorded in the period that matches the revenue they helped generate. The wages of the dry-cleaning employees who cleaned Tuesday's batch of clothes get matched to the revenue from those same clothes, even if payroll runs on Friday.

The *full disclosure principle* says any information that could affect a reader's understanding of the financial statements should be disclosed — typically in the notes to the financial statements. Pending lawsuits, accounting policy changes, related-party transactions, and other context get disclosed even when they don't fit cleanly into one of the four numerical statements.

Two further conventions affect judgment calls:

*Conservatism* says when there is genuine uncertainty, choose the treatment less likely to overstate income or assets or understate expenses or liabilities. If a customer's likelihood of paying is doubtful, account for the doubt. If equipment may have lost value, recognize the loss rather than waiting.

*Materiality* says the rules don't have to be applied to immaterial amounts. A $5 stapler that will last for years could technically be capitalized as an asset and depreciated, but the cost of doing so exceeds any benefit, so businesses expense it immediately. The rule has discretion built in; the discretion is bounded by the requirement that materiality decisions can't be used to obscure something a reader would care about.

The trade-off across the framework: the rules buy *comparability and reliability* at the cost of *some degree of fit and timeliness*. Historical-cost reporting is reliable but understates the value of long-held appreciating assets. Conservatism prevents inflated earnings but can also delay recognition of genuine gains. The conventions are a chosen compromise between several reader needs, and the compromise is not perfect from any single reader's perspective.

## 3.3 The accounting equation and double-entry bookkeeping

The core identity of accounting:

**Assets = Liabilities + Equity**

Read it as a sources-and-claims statement. *Assets* are what the organization owns or controls. *Liabilities* are claims by creditors against those assets. *Equity* is the residual claim by owners. Everything the organization has came from one of two places — borrowed (liability) or invested by owners and retained from earnings (equity). The two sides of the equation must be equal because they are *the same things* counted two different ways.

When Mark Summers contributes $20,000 of his own money to launch Supreme Cleaners, the equation moves on both sides simultaneously: Cash (an asset) increases by $20,000, and Owner's Capital (equity) increases by $20,000. *Assets up $20,000 = Liabilities (no change) + Equity up $20,000*. Balanced.

When Mark borrows $15,000 from the bank to buy equipment, both sides move again: Cash (asset) increases by $15,000, and Notes Payable (liability) increases by $15,000. *Assets up $15,000 = Liabilities up $15,000 + Equity (no change)*. Still balanced.

When Mark uses $12,000 of that cash to buy a commercial press, two assets shift: Cash (asset) decreases by $12,000, Equipment (asset) increases by $12,000. The total of assets is unchanged; liabilities and equity are unchanged. *Net change zero on both sides*. Still balanced.

This is the rhythm. Every transaction has at least *two* effects on the equation, and those effects keep the equation in balance. The system that enforces this discipline is *double-entry bookkeeping* — every transaction recorded in at least two accounts, in compatible ways, so the books can never silently fall out of balance.

The expanded accounting equation breaks equity into its component pieces — useful because equity moves for several different reasons, and accountants want to track each reason separately:

**Assets = Liabilities + Common Stock + Retained Earnings + Revenues − Expenses − Dividends**

(For a sole proprietorship like Supreme Cleaners, the equity components reduce to Owner's Capital plus revenues minus expenses minus withdrawals. For a corporation they take the form above.)

The five components on the equity side each have a directional relationship to equity. Common Stock (or Owner's Capital) is direct equity from owner contribution — it adds to equity. Retained Earnings is accumulated past profit retained in the business — it adds to equity. Revenues add to equity in the current period. Expenses subtract from equity. Dividends (or owner withdrawals) subtract from equity. The expanded equation makes visible *why* equity changed, not just that it did.

## 3.4 Debits, credits, and the normal balance

Double-entry bookkeeping uses two columns to record changes to every account: the *debit* column on the left and the *credit* column on the right. The visualization is the *T-account* — literally the letter T, with the account name at the top, debits on the left, credits on the right.

```
        Cash
   +-------------+
   |             |
   |   Debits    |   Credits
   |   (Left)    |   (Right)
   |             |
```

A debit is *not* an increase or a decrease in the abstract; it is a *left-side entry*. A credit is *not* a decrease or an increase; it is a *right-side entry*. Whether a debit increases or decreases the balance of a particular account depends on what kind of account it is. Memorize this:

| Account type | Increases on the | Decreases on the | Normal balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Asset | Debit (left) | Credit (right) | Debit |
| Expense | Debit (left) | Credit (right) | Debit |
| Dividends / Withdrawals | Debit (left) | Credit (right) | Debit |
| Liability | Credit (right) | Debit (left) | Credit |
| Common Stock / Owner's Capital | Credit (right) | Debit (left) | Credit |
| Retained Earnings | Credit (right) | Debit (left) | Credit |
| Revenue | Credit (right) | Debit (left) | Credit |

The *normal balance* of an account is the side it usually carries — the side it increases on. Asset accounts (Cash, Accounts Receivable, Inventory, Equipment) normally carry debit balances. Liability accounts (Accounts Payable, Notes Payable) normally carry credit balances. Revenue accounts normally carry credit balances; expense accounts normally carry debit balances. The expanded accounting equation, read across the chart above, gives a memorable shorthand: *DEAD CRLS* — Debits Expenses Assets Dividends; Credits Revenues Liabilities Stock(holders' equity).

Two facts make double-entry bookkeeping work:

First, *every transaction has equal debits and credits*. Total debits = total credits, every time. This is what keeps the accounting equation in balance — if the debits and credits balance for every transaction, the equation balances after every transaction.

Second, *the balance of each account is the running difference between its total debits and total credits*. After all the entries are posted, an account's balance is on whichever side has the larger total.

This sounds abstract. Let's record Mark's three transactions from §3.3 as journal entries:

**Transaction 1:** Mark contributes $20,000 cash to launch Supreme Cleaners.

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Cash | $20,000 | |
| Owner's Capital | | $20,000 |

Cash (asset) increases on the debit side. Owner's Capital (equity) increases on the credit side. Debits ($20,000) equal credits ($20,000). Balanced.

**Transaction 2:** Mark borrows $15,000 from the bank.

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Cash | $15,000 | |
| Notes Payable | | $15,000 |

Cash (asset) increases on the debit side. Notes Payable (liability) increases on the credit side. Balanced.

**Transaction 3:** Mark buys a commercial press for $12,000 cash.

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Equipment | $12,000 | |
| Cash | | $12,000 |

Equipment (asset) increases on the debit side. Cash (asset) decreases on the credit side. Both are assets — one going up, one going down — but the journal entry pattern is the same: total debits equal total credits.

This is the entire mechanic. Every transaction Supreme Cleaners ever does will be recorded in this format, and every entry will balance.

## 3.5 The chart of accounts and the first four steps of the accounting cycle

Before any transaction can be journalized, the business needs a *chart of accounts* — the organized list of every account it uses, with each account assigned a number and a name. A simple chart of accounts for Supreme Cleaners might look like this:

| Account # | Account name | Type |
|---|---|---|
| 101 | Cash | Asset |
| 105 | Accounts Receivable | Asset |
| 110 | Supplies | Asset |
| 150 | Equipment | Asset |
| 201 | Accounts Payable | Liability |
| 220 | Notes Payable | Liability |
| 301 | Owner's Capital | Equity |
| 305 | Owner's Withdrawals | Equity (contra) |
| 401 | Service Revenue | Revenue |
| 501 | Wages Expense | Expense |
| 510 | Rent Expense | Expense |
| 520 | Utilities Expense | Expense |
| 530 | Supplies Expense | Expense |

The numbering convention varies — some firms use four-digit codes, some use thousands-based blocks for each account type — but the structure is universal: assets in one block, liabilities in another, equity in a third, revenues and expenses in their own blocks. Once a chart of accounts is established, every transaction gets recorded in accounts that are already on the list. New activity that doesn't fit the existing chart triggers a chart update before recording.

The *accounting cycle* is the sequence of steps that takes the business from individual transactions to financial statements. The first four steps — the ones this chapter covers — are:

1. **Identify and analyze transactions** — recognize that an event has occurred that affects the accounting equation, identify which accounts are affected, decide whether each account is debited or credited, and confirm that debits equal credits.

2. **Record transactions in the journal** — write the journal entry, in chronological order, in the *general journal*. Each journal entry records the date, the account names being debited and credited, the amounts, and a brief explanation. The general journal is sometimes called the *book of original entry* — every transaction's first appearance in the records.

3. **Post journal entries to the general ledger** — transfer each debit and credit from the journal into the relevant account in the *general ledger*. The general ledger is the master collection of every account, with running balances. Visualizing each account as a T-account is the conceptual shorthand; physically, in modern systems, the ledger is a database table.

4. **Prepare an unadjusted trial balance** — at the end of the period, list every account in the ledger with its current balance, in two columns (debit and credit). If the books have been kept correctly, total debits equal total credits. The trial balance is *unadjusted* because there are several end-of-period adjustments still to make — those are Chapter 4's territory. The trial balance is the bridge between the recording phase and the reporting phase of the accounting cycle.

The trade-off of this four-step structure: it buys *traceable accuracy* (every reported number can be traced back through the ledger to a journal entry to an original transaction document) at the cost of *deferred summarization* (the balance of any account at any moment requires looking at the ledger; the financial statements come at the *end* of the period). Modern computerized systems collapse the time delay — the ledger updates in real time as journal entries are posted — but the conceptual sequence is the same.

## 3.6 A worked example: Supreme Cleaners' first month

Mark opens Supreme Cleaners on October 1. During October, the following transactions occur. We'll journalize each, post to T-accounts, and end with a trial balance.

| Date | Transaction |
|---|---|
| Oct 1 | Mark contributes $20,000 cash to start the business. |
| Oct 1 | Mark borrows $15,000 from the bank, signing a note. |
| Oct 2 | Buys a commercial press for $12,000 cash. |
| Oct 5 | Buys $800 of cleaning supplies on account (to be paid later). |
| Oct 10 | Pays $1,200 cash for October rent. |
| Oct 15 | Performs dry-cleaning services for cash, $2,400. |
| Oct 20 | Performs services on account, $700 (customer pays later). |
| Oct 25 | Pays $1,500 cash in employee wages. |
| Oct 28 | Receives $400 cash from October 20 customer. |
| Oct 30 | Mark withdraws $500 cash for personal use. |

The journal entries:

- **Oct 1a:** Debit Cash $20,000, Credit Owner's Capital $20,000
- **Oct 1b:** Debit Cash $15,000, Credit Notes Payable $15,000
- **Oct 2:** Debit Equipment $12,000, Credit Cash $12,000
- **Oct 5:** Debit Supplies $800, Credit Accounts Payable $800
- **Oct 10:** Debit Rent Expense $1,200, Credit Cash $1,200
- **Oct 15:** Debit Cash $2,400, Credit Service Revenue $2,400
- **Oct 20:** Debit Accounts Receivable $700, Credit Service Revenue $700
- **Oct 25:** Debit Wages Expense $1,500, Credit Cash $1,500
- **Oct 28:** Debit Cash $400, Credit Accounts Receivable $400
- **Oct 30:** Debit Owner's Withdrawals $500, Credit Cash $500

Posting to T-accounts (showing only the running totals):

- **Cash:** Debits $20,000 + $15,000 + $2,400 + $400 = $37,800. Credits $12,000 + $1,200 + $1,500 + $500 = $15,200. Balance (debit): $22,600.
- **Accounts Receivable:** Debit $700, Credit $400. Balance (debit): $300.
- **Supplies:** Debit $800. Balance (debit): $800.
- **Equipment:** Debit $12,000. Balance (debit): $12,000.
- **Accounts Payable:** Credit $800. Balance (credit): $800.
- **Notes Payable:** Credit $15,000. Balance (credit): $15,000.
- **Owner's Capital:** Credit $20,000. Balance (credit): $20,000.
- **Owner's Withdrawals:** Debit $500. Balance (debit): $500.
- **Service Revenue:** Credits $2,400 + $700. Balance (credit): $3,100.
- **Rent Expense:** Debit $1,200. Balance (debit): $1,200.
- **Wages Expense:** Debit $1,500. Balance (debit): $1,500.

Unadjusted trial balance, October 31:

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Cash | $22,600 | |
| Accounts Receivable | $300 | |
| Supplies | $800 | |
| Equipment | $12,000 | |
| Accounts Payable | | $800 |
| Notes Payable | | $15,000 |
| Owner's Capital | | $20,000 |
| Owner's Withdrawals | $500 | |
| Service Revenue | | $3,100 |
| Rent Expense | $1,200 | |
| Wages Expense | $1,500 | |
| **Totals** | **$38,900** | **$38,900** |

Total debits equal total credits. The books balance. The trial balance is the raw material from which Chapter 4's adjusting entries and Chapter 5's full set of financial statements will be built.

If the trial balance had *not* balanced, an error has occurred somewhere upstream. Common errors: a transposition (wrote $1,500 as $1,050), a one-sided entry (debited an account but forgot the matching credit), a posting error (wrote the right amount in the wrong account or on the wrong side). The differences themselves often hint at the type of error — a discrepancy divisible by 9 frequently indicates a transposition; a discrepancy equal to twice some account balance frequently indicates a debit posted as a credit. Tracking down errors before producing the financial statements is the last task of the recording phase.

## 3.8 Chapter summary

You walked into this chapter knowing the four financial statements are the output of the accounting process. You walk out knowing how the input gets recorded.

The accounting equation — **Assets = Liabilities + Equity** — is the identity that every transaction has to respect. Double-entry bookkeeping is the discipline that enforces it: every transaction gets recorded with equal debits and credits in compatible accounts. Debits are left-side entries; credits are right-side entries. Whether either one increases or decreases an account's balance depends on what kind of account it is. Asset, expense, and dividend accounts increase on the debit side and have debit normal balances. Liability, equity, and revenue accounts increase on the credit side and have credit normal balances.

The first four steps of the accounting cycle — analyze transactions, journalize, post to the ledger, prepare an unadjusted trial balance — produce the raw material for everything that follows. The trial balance is the checkpoint: if total debits don't equal total credits, an error has occurred and must be located before the books move forward.

The principles and assumptions that govern the recording — separate entity, going concern, monetary unit, time period, historical cost, revenue recognition, expense recognition, full disclosure, conservatism, materiality — are the conventions that turn the recording from arbitrary into something a reader can trust.

The single most important idea to carry forward: the trial balance balances when the *mechanics* are correct, not when the *content* is correct. A perfectly balanced trial balance can still reflect a journal entry posted to the wrong account, a transaction that was missed entirely, or a sale recorded at the wrong amount as long as both sides matched. The trial balance catches some errors and silently passes others. Chapter 4's adjusting entries and Chapter 8's internal controls exist partly to catch the errors the trial balance doesn't.

The common mistake to watch for: students often try to memorize "what increases on the debit side" by intuition rather than by the structural rule. Intuition fails — there is no obvious reason a revenue account should *increase* on a credit side until you see how it sits in the expanded accounting equation as a positive contribution to equity, which itself increases on the credit side. Learn the structure; the intuition follows.

## 3.9 Connections forward

Chapter 4 takes the unadjusted trial balance and shows the *adjusting entries* — the period-end journal entries that bring the books into accrual-basis compliance before the financial statements are prepared. Accrued revenues, accrued expenses, prepaid assets that have been used up, deferred revenues that have been earned, depreciation, supplies consumed: each is an end-of-period adjustment that moves numbers between accounts to reflect what actually happened during the period, not just what was recorded in real time. By the end of Chapter 4, you'll have an adjusted trial balance — the input the financial statements are actually built from.
