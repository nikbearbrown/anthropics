# Chapter 1 — The Nature of Science and Physics

*What it means to pin a number down.*

---

There is a small cylinder of platinum-iridium alloy sitting in a vault outside Paris. For 130 years it had a very specific job. It *was* the kilogram. Not *represented* the kilogram. Not *approximated* it. It *was* it. If you wanted to know, with full philosophical rigor, what one kilogram of mass actually felt like, the answer ended at that cylinder. There was no deeper fact. The cylinder was the fact.

And here is the thing that should bother you: the cylinder was changing.

Not by much — maybe fifty micrograms over a century, about the mass of an eyelash. Replicas of it, held in guarded vaults from Maryland to Tokyo, were calibrating against the original every few decades and drifting from it in different directions. Nobody could say which replica was right, because the original was, by definition, exactly one kilogram, *even if it was changing*. That was what made it the standard. Which meant, technically, that the mass of everything else in the universe was drifting too — because the yardstick was drifting.

On May 20, 2019, the General Conference on Weights and Measures voted to end this arrangement. The kilogram would no longer be the cylinder. It would be a number — a particular fixed value of Planck's constant, $h = 6.62607015 \times 10^{-34}$ joule-seconds, from which mass could be derived through quantum mechanics and a precision instrument called a Kibble balance. The cylinder keeps its name. It lost its job.

I want to start here because this story is not really about the kilogram. It is about what physics actually is. Not the equations on the chalkboard, not the lab reports, not the list of formulas at the back of the book. Physics is the discipline of deciding, with the smallest expressible uncertainty, what your words and your numbers *mean*. The kilogram redefinition happened because "the cylinder in Paris" stopped being a precise enough meaning. The universe offers better ones.

This chapter is about three commitments that physicists make, every time, before any particular equation is even on the table.

## The first commitment: that nature has discoverable order

Here is the first thing you have to accept to do physics: that when you do the same experiment twice, in the same conditions, you get the same result. Not approximately the same. The same. And that the reason you get the same result is not luck, but the expression of something real about how the universe is structured.

This sounds obvious until you think about how strange it actually is. You drop a ball from a height of one meter. It takes a certain amount of time to fall. You drop the same ball from the same height the next day, in a different laboratory, in a different country, with different scientists watching. It takes the same amount of time. Not because the scientists agreed it would. Because something about the universe — a regularity, a lawfulness — made it happen that way.

Physics proceeds by taking that regularity seriously. We call the compressed mathematical statements of those regularities *laws*. Newton's second law, $F = ma$, is a law. It does not say why force equals mass times acceleration; it says that if you set up the calculation this way, the numbers will come out right, in every case, every time, as far as we have ever checked. A law is the tightest expressible compression of a pattern in nature.

But a law is not the whole story. Laws need *context* — surrounding explanation, a structure for understanding what they are laws *about*, why they hold in the regime they hold and not in some other. That surrounding structure is what we call a *theory*. Newton's theory of mechanics is not just $F = ma$. It is a way of thinking about how objects interact through forces, what forces actually are, how they combine, how they change motion over time. The theory wraps around the laws and makes them *make sense*.

And below the theory, sitting at the most local and provisional level, are *models* — mental pictures you build because the actual thing is too small, too large, too fast, or too abstract to see directly. The planetary atom, with electrons orbiting a nucleus like planets around a sun, is a model. It is wrong in several technical senses — electrons do not actually orbit, they occupy probability clouds — but it is right enough to predict the spectrum of hydrogen with surprising accuracy. A model is justified by what it lets you compute. When it fails, you replace it.

The common confusion is to think theories are weak and laws are strong — that a theory "becomes a law" once it's proven. This gets it exactly backwards. They are different *kinds* of objects, not different rungs of confidence. Newton's law of gravitation lived *inside* Newton's theory of gravity, which lived inside a broader Newtonian worldview. When Einstein arrived, the theory was revised (gravity is the curvature of spacetime, not a force at a distance) but several of the laws — approximate forms that work in weak fields and low velocities — survived essentially intact. Theories grow and change. Laws, in the regimes where they were found, tend to stick.

One more thing worth saying about this first commitment: it comes with a domain. Every theory has a regime of applicability — a range of conditions within which it works essentially exactly, outside of which you reach for something deeper. Classical mechanics works when objects move at less than about 1% the speed of light, when they are larger than a microscope can resolve, when gravitational fields are weak. Inside those bounds, it is not approximately right. It is right. The atomic bomb did not invalidate Newton's $F = ma$ for baseballs. It revealed that you need different physics when you start asking about the interior of an atomic nucleus.

Knowing the domain is part of knowing the theory.

## The second commitment: that every quantity must be tied to a unit you can defend

In 1967, the second was redefined.

For most of human history, a second was $\frac{1}{86{,}400}$ of a mean solar day — a bookkeeping convenience, tied to the rotation of the Earth. This worked until clocks got accurate enough to notice that the solar day was not a fixed length. The Earth's rotation is slowing, dragged down by tidal friction, by mass redistributed after every earthquake. A second built on the day was a second built on a wobbling platform.

So the international community moved the platform. From 1967 onward, the second has been the time it takes for an undisturbed cesium-133 atom to complete exactly 9,192,631,770 oscillations between two specific energy states. That number is not round. It was chosen to match the old second as closely as possible while making the new definition exact. The cesium atom oscillates at the same frequency anywhere in the universe, in any laboratory, at any temperature close to absolute zero. The second is now as portable and reproducible as the universe allows.

This is the move physics keeps making, over and over, with every fundamental unit: pull the standard off something that drifts and onto something that doesn't. The meter used to be a platinum-iridium bar. Now it is the distance light travels in vacuum in $\frac{1}{299{,}792{,}458}$ of a second — a number fixed by international agreement, from which length is derived through the measured speed of light. The kilogram, as we saw, followed in 2019. Every base unit of the International System of Units is now defined by a constant of nature.

<!-- → [TABLE: all seven SI base units — quantity, unit name, symbol, and the constant of nature or exact value it is now defined by, post-2019 redefinitions] -->

The SI system uses seven fundamental units; in this book we will use four of them constantly:

| Quantity | Unit | Symbol |
|---|---|---|
| Length | meter | m |
| Mass | kilogram | kg |
| Time | second | s |
| Electric current | ampere | A |

Everything else is **derived** from these four. Speed is length divided by time, so its unit is $\text{m/s}$. Force is mass times acceleration, and acceleration is length divided by time squared, so a newton is $1 \text{ N} = 1 \text{ kg} \cdot \text{m/s}^2$. Energy is force times distance, so a joule is $1 \text{ J} = 1 \text{ kg} \cdot \text{m}^2\text{/s}^2$.

This derivation structure is not just bookkeeping. It tells you what a unit physically *means*. A joule is not a magic word. It is the energy required to push with one newton through one meter, which is exactly what the dimensional decomposition says it is. And dimensional analysis — keeping track of units as algebraic quantities that multiply and cancel — becomes a parallel proof that your calculation is correct. If your final answer for an energy comes out with units of $\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}$ — that is momentum, not energy — you know immediately, without checking a single number, that you have made a structural error somewhere.

The metric prefixes extend this leverage across thirty-six orders of magnitude. A nanometer is $10^{-9}$ meters; a gigahertz is $10^9$ hertz; a femtosecond is $10^{-15}$ seconds. The prefixes run from atto ($10^{-18}$) to exa ($10^{18}$), and every unit in the system uses the same set of them. There is no "1,728 cubic inches per cubic foot" memorization tax. Every conversion is a count of zeros.

### How unit conversion actually works

Here is the practical skill: treat units as algebraic quantities that obey the rules of multiplication and division. To convert $30.0 \text{ km/h}$ to meters per second, build a chain of fractions where each one has the unit you want to cancel on the bottom and the unit you want to keep on top:

$$30.0 \frac{\text{km}}{\text{h}} \times \frac{1{,}000 \text{ m}}{1 \text{ km}} \times \frac{1 \text{ h}}{3{,}600 \text{ s}} = 8.33 \frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}.$$

The "km" cancels. The "h" cancels. What remains uncancelled is your answer's units. If you set up the fractions correctly — always keeping what you want and cancelling what you don't — the answer comes out right automatically.

Now sanity-check it. A car going $30 \text{ km/h}$ — roughly 19 mph — is moving at a moderate city speed, covering maybe one car-length per second, where a car is about three meters. Eight or nine meters per second is completely consistent with that estimate. If the calculation had returned $0.83$ or $83$, you would know immediately that you had inverted a fraction somewhere. The sanity check is the guardrail.

## The third commitment: that every number must carry an honest uncertainty

Here is the thing about the cesium atom oscillating $9{,}192{,}631{,}770$ times per second: I told you that number as if it were exact. It is exact — by definition, since 1967. But most numbers in physics are not exact by definition. Most of them are the result of measurement, and every measurement has an irreducible fuzziness.

You measure a rod with a millimeter ruler. You read $11.0$ cm. But the last digit — the millimeter digit — is one you estimated between the nearest lines. It might be $10.95$; it might be $11.05$. The measurement is not $11.0$ cm. It is $11.0 \pm 0.05$ cm, where the $\pm$ carries the claim: I believe the true value falls in this range.

Without the $\pm$, the number $11.0$ is half a story. Stating only the central value and suppressing the uncertainty is a form of dishonesty by omission — not a lie about the number you measured, but a lie about how well you measured it. *Every measured number in physics comes with an uncertainty. The discipline is to say what it is.*

Three distinct words are often confused here. **Accuracy** is closeness to the true value. **Precision** is closeness of repeated measurements to each other. These can come apart. A measurement can be highly precise — the same wrong answer four times in a row — without being accurate. It can be accurate on average without being precise. **Uncertainty** is the formal quantitative statement of your confidence: $A \pm \delta A$. You can be precise without being accurate; you can be accurate without being precise; but you cannot honestly report a measurement without an uncertainty.

### Propagation

The deeper consequence comes when you use measurements in calculations. If you measure a floor's length as $4.00 \pm 0.08 \text{ m}$ (2% uncertainty) and its width as $3.00 \pm 0.03 \text{ m}$ (1% uncertainty), and you multiply them to get the area, the answer is not "exactly" $12.0 \text{ m}^2$. The area inherits the uncertainties of the measurements it is built from.

The rule for multiplication and division is clean: the percent uncertainty in the result is the sum of the percent uncertainties of the inputs.

$$\text{\%unc}_A = 2\% + 1\% = 3\%.$$

Three percent of $12.0 \text{ m}^2$ is $0.36 \text{ m}^2$, which rounds to $0.4 \text{ m}^2$. The honest answer is $12.0 \pm 0.4 \text{ m}^2$.

Notice what this means for complex calculations: uncertainty grows as you compound measurements. A calculation that chains five measured inputs will have an uncertainty larger than any single input. You cannot conjure precision the inputs don't have.

### Significant figures

Significant figures are the shorthand for encoding uncertainty in the way you write a number. The last digit you write is, by convention, the first digit that carries any uncertainty. A measurement of $36.7 \text{ cm}$ has three significant figures because the 7 — the millimeter digit — is the one you estimated.

Two governing rules:

**For multiplication and division:** the result has the same number of significant figures as the input with the fewest.

So $\pi \times r^2$ with $r = 1.2 \text{ m}$ — two sig figs — gives $4.5 \text{ m}^2$, not $4.5238934 \text{ m}^2$. The extra digits are computational noise. Writing them implies precision your input did not have.

**For addition and subtraction:** the result has no more decimal places than the input with the fewest decimal places.

So $7.56 \text{ kg} - 6.052 \text{ kg} + 13.7 \text{ kg} = 15.2 \text{ kg}$, not $15.208 \text{ kg}$. The $13.7 \text{ kg}$ term is precise only to the tenths place; the result can be no more precise than that.

Zeros deserve special attention. $0.053$ has two significant figures (the leading zeros are placeholders, not measurements). $10.053$ has five (the zero between the 1 and the 5 is genuinely measured). Trailing zeros in a whole number — like $1{,}300$ — are ambiguous: write $1.3 \times 10^3$ or $1.300 \times 10^3$ to make the precision explicit.

### The Fermi estimate — when rough is right

There is a skill physicists develop that sits alongside careful measurement, and it is, in some ways, more important in daily practice: the order-of-magnitude estimate. Enrico Fermi was famous for it. The point is to get an answer right within a factor of ten, using nothing but reasoning and round numbers.

How tall is a 39-story building? You don't have a measuring tape. But you know the average adult is roughly 2 meters tall, and one story of a building is roughly two adults stacked end to end (generous for floor structure and ceiling). So:

$$\text{height} \approx \frac{2 \text{ m}}{\text{person}} \times \frac{2 \text{ persons}}{\text{story}} \times 39 \text{ stories} = 156 \text{ m}.$$

That is the estimate. Whether the real answer is 130 m or 175 m, you are in the right ballpark. You would know immediately if a careful calculation returned $1{,}560$ m. The estimate is not the final answer; it is the sanity check that tells you when the final answer has gone off the rails.

The deepest thing about Fermi estimation is what it trains you to do: identify the assumptions you are making, state them explicitly, and reason from them rather than from memorized formulas. A physics student who can Fermi-estimate is a student who knows what their calculation is actually *doing*, not just how to push the symbols around.

## What the three commitments make together

Step back and look at what these three commitments are.

The first says: nature has order. Experiments repeated in the same conditions give the same results. Patterns exist, and they can be compressed into laws and organized into theories.

The second says: words must be precise. Every quantity has a unit, and every unit must be tied to something definite — ideally a constant of nature that means the same thing in Tokyo, in Sèvres, on a satellite, on the surface of Mars.

The third says: numbers must be honest. Every measured value carries an uncertainty. Calculations propagate that uncertainty forward. Significant figures are not a stylistic convention; they are the claim you are making about how well you know your answer.

These three do not sit independently. They braid. The kilogram had to be redefined in 2019 precisely because the cylinder in Paris could no longer support the precision that modern measurements demanded. The second commitment — tie every unit to something definite — ran into the third: a metal artifact changing by tens of micrograms per century is not definite enough when you need to measure mass to one part in $10^9$. The redefinition lifted the kilogram off the cylinder and onto Planck's constant because the universe respects Planck's constant in a way it does not respect a piece of metal.

This is the scale shift I want you to feel. Down at the level of the cesium atom, oscillating more than nine billion times per second. Up at the level of an international vote deciding what the kilogram shall be. The seemingly mundane task of agreeing on units is, when you look at it closely, one of the deepest acts of cooperation our species engages in. Every measurement you make in a laboratory anywhere on Earth is comparable to every measurement in every other laboratory on Earth because of agreements exactly like that one. The science is collaborative all the way down to its definitions.

![Stylized representation of the IBM stop-motion film "A Boy and His Atom" showing individual xenon atoms positioned one at a time by a scanning tunneling microscope tip on a copper substrate. 242 frames; one xenon atom per pixel.](../images/01-introduction-the-nature-of-science-and-physics-fig-02.png)

In 1013, a research group at IBM Almaden made a stop-motion movie — *A Boy and His Atom* — by using a scanning tunneling microscope to push individual xenon atoms into position, one per pixel, 242 frames. The boy is built from atoms. The "ball" he plays with is one atom. You can watch it. A century earlier, no one had ever *seen* an atom. Atoms were a model — a useful picture that explained why gases behaved as they did. Now there are movies of them.

That arc, from useful fiction to photographable fact, is what the three commitments are in service of.

## What would change my mind

The chapter argues that the SI system's coupling of every fundamental unit to a constant of nature — the kilogram to Planck's constant, the meter to the speed of light, the second to the cesium hyperfine transition — represents a strict improvement over artifact-based definitions. The argument would need revision if a future redefinition demonstrated that one of these "constants" was itself drifting at a precision relevant to measurement, requiring yet another reformulation.

## Still puzzling

The deepest question this chapter raises and does not answer: *why* is the universe cooperative? Why do the same mathematical regularities work in a physics lab in Boston and in the atmosphere of a star ten billion light-years away? We can demonstrate the cooperation. We can sometimes derive one regularity from a deeper one. But the meta-question — *why does nature have such order at all?* — sits at the edge of what physics can address. Eugene Wigner called it, in 1960, "the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics in the natural sciences." The puzzle has not gone away.
