# Chapter 4: Forces and Newton's Laws of Motion

**Suggested titles:**
1. Why Things Move the Way They Do: The Three Laws That Govern Motion Everywhere
2. Force as the Currency of Change: How Newton Translated Pushes into Mathematics
3. The Hidden Grammar of Motion: Three Laws That Explain Every Collision, Fall, and Launch

**TL;DR:** Force is not what keeps things moving; it is what changes motion. Newton's three laws state that unforced objects persist in their state, that net force produces acceleration proportional to mass, and that forces always come in equal and opposite pairs acting on different objects. These laws are universal—they work on Earth, in orbit, and everywhere else.

---

## 1. Chapter Opening: What Makes a Dolphin Jump?

A dolphin speeds through warm water at constant velocity. Its body moves in one direction, at one speed, unchanging. Then something shifts. The tail thrashes against the water. In that instant—that exchange—the dolphin accelerates upward. Its body climbs out of the ocean. For a moment it hangs in air, slowing as gravity pulls. Then it falls back, water rupturing as it re-enters.

Watch that sequence again. What changed? Not the dolphin's shape. Not its size. The forces. The dolphin applied force to water. Water applied force back. The outcome: acceleration upward.

Newton spent years asking this question differently. He did not ask "why does the dolphin move?" He asked "why does the dolphin *change* how it moves?" And he wrote three sentences—austere, mathematical, nowhere near as obvious as they seem now—that answer the question so completely that they work for dolphins and for planets, for falling apples and for rockets escaping Earth's gravity.

This chapter is about those three sentences. And about what they actually mean—not the textbook paraphrase, but the machinery underneath.

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- Draw a free-body diagram identifying all external forces acting on an object
- Explain why an object at rest does not move (and why motion persists without force)
- Predict the acceleration of an object when you know the net force and its mass
- Recognize action-reaction force pairs and understand why they do not cancel

### Prerequisites

You should be comfortable with:

- Vectors and vector addition
- Acceleration as the rate of change of velocity
- The distinction between speed and velocity

### Why This Matters

Forces are the mechanism. Everything that happens—every collision, every orbit, every jump—is force translating into acceleration. If you understand how force works, you understand the grammar of change itself. Nothing in physics happens without it.

## 2. Core Concept 1: Force and Its Measurement

### The Cold Open: Pushing a Desk

Two students stand in front of a desk. One pushes. Nothing moves. The other joins. Both push in the same direction. Still nothing. Now one of them pushes from the opposite side. The first student, slightly stronger, keeps pushing. The desk slides. It moves in the direction the stronger student is pushing.

Change one variable. The second student, now on the same side, pushes harder. The desk accelerates. Faster. The two forces add. They combine into what we call a net force.

This is the first thing to know: force is what happens when you push or pull. It is not motion itself. It is the *cause* of motion. Or more precisely, it is the cause of *change* in motion.

### The Mechanism: What Force Actually Is

The word *force* comes from the Latin *fortis*, meaning strong. But strength here is not metaphor. It is a measurable quantity—a push or pull that has a specific magnitude and direction. Force is a vector. It has size and arrow.

But what does that mean? When you push on a wall, you exert force. The wall exerts force back on your hand. Not equally distributed over your hand's surface—concentrated where you push hardest. In physics, we represent that force as a vector: a line segment with length proportional to how hard you push, and an arrow showing which way the push goes.

The SI unit of force is the newton (N). One newton is defined as the force needed to accelerate a 1-kilogram object at 1 meter per second squared. This definition ties force directly to mass and acceleration. It says: force is what causes change in how fast something is moving, and that change scales with how much matter is involved.

When multiple forces act on the same object, they do not remain separate. They combine. Add them together—respecting their directions, because force is a vector—and you get the *net force* or *total force*. If I push a cart with 50 newtons to the right, and friction opposes with 30 newtons to the left, the net force is 20 newtons to the right.

This is not obvious in daily experience. You feel friction. You feel air resistance. These are forces hidden in the air and the floor, and they consume the effort you put in. But they are still forces. The net force is what remains after all of them are combined.

### A Named Trade-off: Contact Forces vs. Field Forces

Some forces require physical contact. Friction does. When you rub your hands together, friction acts where skin touches skin. Push an object across a table: friction lives in the contact between object and table. These are *contact forces*.

Other forces reach across empty space. Earth's gravity pulls a falling apple without the apple touching Earth. A magnet pulls a paper clip across a gap. No contact needed. These are *field forces*, and they act everywhere, even where there is nothing to push or pull against.

The trade-off: contact forces are easier to visualize and to control (you can see where they happen), but field forces are more fundamental. They work even in vacuum. Gravity holds the moon in orbit. No medium, no contact. Just the field.

### Worked Example: Free-Body Diagram of a Hanging Mass

Consider a 5-kilogram mass hanging from a rope attached to the ceiling. Three forces act on this mass:

1. Gravity pulls downward with magnitude $W = mg = (5 \text{ kg})(9.80 \text{ m/s}^2) = 49 \text{ N}$.
2. Tension in the rope pulls upward with magnitude $T$.
3. No other forces act horizontally.

To draw a free-body diagram, represent the mass as a single point. Draw two arrows from that point: one pointing up (tension), one pointing down (weight). If the mass is hanging stationary, the two forces must have equal magnitude. So $T = 49 \text{ N}$.

If the arrows are different lengths, the mass is accelerating. Longer upward arrow? The mass accelerates upward. Longer downward arrow? The mass accelerates downward. The *difference* between the two forces—the net force—determines the acceleration.

[FIGURE: A point with two arrows: one up labeled T (magnitude 49 N), one down labeled W (magnitude 49 N). Caption: Free-body diagram of a hanging 5-kg mass at rest. The arrows have equal length because the mass is not accelerating. The net force is zero.]

### Misconceptions

**"More forces mean the object moves faster."** No. The object moves faster only if the *net force* is greater. If you push a cart to the right with 100 newtons, and someone else pushes to the left with 100 newtons, the cart does not move. The net force is zero.

**"Gravity and the normal force are action-reaction pairs."** No. If you stand on the floor, gravity pulls you down (Earth on you). The normal force pushes you up (floor on you). They are equal and opposite, but they act on the same object. An action-reaction pair involves two objects. Action: you push the floor. Reaction: the floor pushes you. We will return to this.

## 3. Core Concept 2: Newton's First Law and Why Motion Persists

### The Cold Open: The Air Hockey Table

Imagine an air hockey table. When the air is on, the puck glides across the surface at constant velocity with barely any change in speed. It will keep gliding forever—or until it hits the wall. When you turn the air off, friction now acts. The puck slows. It stops. The difference is not the puck. The difference is the force.

Newton's first law says: a body at rest stays at rest. A body in motion stays in motion at constant velocity. Unless a net force acts on it.

This contradicts intuition. You slide a book across a table. It slows down. Stops. Your intuition says motion is temporary, that objects naturally decay to rest. But your intuition is wrong about the cause. The book does not stop on its own. Friction stops it. Friction is a force. Turn off friction—the way the air hockey table does—and the book glides forever.

### The Mechanism: Inertia as the Resistance to Change

The property of an object that makes it resistant to change in motion is called *inertia*. A baseball has less inertia than a truck. The baseball is easier to accelerate. The truck is harder. Why?

Because inertia depends on mass. Mass is the quantity of matter—the number and types of atoms—in an object. More atoms, more mass, more inertia. More inertia means more force is needed to produce the same change in motion.

Here is the key distinction: mass does not change with gravity. Weigh a kilogram on Earth and on the moon, and its weight changes (because gravity is weaker on the moon). But the mass—the amount of stuff—stays one kilogram. Mass is intrinsic. Weight is the gravitational force, and it depends on where you are.

Friction, formally, is an external force that opposes relative motion or prevents slipping. It comes in kinds: kinetic friction (opposing sliding), static friction (preventing sliding from starting), rolling resistance, and drag (through fluids). In each case, friction resists motion. It is why the book slows. Why the car needs engine power to cruise at constant speed. Why the world does not let moving things persist.

But the law is still true. It just says: if you remove friction, motion persists. The air hockey table proves it.

### A Named Trade-off: Static vs. Kinetic Friction

Static friction prevents motion from starting. You push a heavy crate. It does not budge until you exceed the maximum static friction. Then it starts to slide.

Once it is sliding, kinetic friction takes over. Kinetic friction is usually smaller than the maximum static friction. This is why an object, once moving, is easier to keep moving than to get moving in the first place. The trade-off: static friction is stronger but only prevents change, while kinetic friction is weaker but acts while motion is happening.

### Worked Example: Pushing a Cart at Constant Velocity

A man pushes a shopping cart at constant velocity with a force of 50 newtons to the right. The cart is not accelerating (constant velocity means no acceleration). By Newton's first law, the net force must be zero.

What is the friction?

If the push is 50 N to the right and the net force is zero, then friction must be 50 N to the left. The two forces cancel. They add to zero. The cart glides forward at constant speed because these forces balance.

Mathematically: $F_{\text{push}} + F_{\text{friction}} = F_{\text{net}} = 0$. So $F_{\text{friction}} = -50 \text{ N}$ (the negative sign indicates opposite direction).

[FIGURE: A simple cart. Arrow pointing right labeled "Push = 50 N". Arrow pointing left labeled "Friction = 50 N". Net force label: zero. Caption: A cart moving at constant velocity. The applied force and friction are equal and opposite, so the net force is zero, and the cart neither speeds up nor slows down.]

### Misconceptions

**"Newton's first law only applies to objects at rest."** No. It applies equally to objects in motion. An object in motion at constant velocity experiences zero net force. A stationary object also experiences zero net force. The law unifies both.

**"Inertia is the same as friction."** No. Inertia is a property of the object (its resistance to acceleration because of its mass). Friction is a force (a surface opposing motion). A ball rolling on a frictionless surface still has inertia—it will maintain its velocity—but no friction slows it.

## 4. Core Concept 3: Newton's Second Law and the Cause of Acceleration

### The Cold Open: Two Basketball Players and an Accelerating Sled

Push a basketball. It accelerates fast. Push a truck. It accelerates slowly. Same force applied. Different results because different mass.

Now consider Newton's sled experiment from the 1960s. A rocket sled accelerates along rails. The rockets push with a force, producing an acceleration. The operators could measure both. They found that if they doubled the force, the acceleration doubled. If they doubled the mass on the sled, the acceleration halved. Force and acceleration vary together. Mass and acceleration vary inversely.

This is the experimental fact. Newton's second law is the mathematical statement of that fact.

### The Mechanism: F = ma and What It Really Means

Newton's second law states:

$$\mathbf{F}_{\text{net}} = m \mathbf{a}$$

Read this carefully. The net external force equals mass times acceleration. Not weight. Not speed. Acceleration—the *rate of change* of velocity.

What does this mean?

It means force is the instrument of change. If you apply net force, the object accelerates. The acceleration is in the same direction as the net force. And the acceleration is proportional to force and inversely proportional to mass.

Rearrange the equation:

$$\mathbf{a} = \frac{\mathbf{F}_{\text{net}}}{m}$$

The larger the net force, the larger the acceleration. The larger the mass, the smaller the acceleration. This makes intuitive sense. A more massive object is harder to accelerate.

The equation defines the newton: one newton is the force that accelerates one kilogram at one meter per second squared. So the units work out: newtons are kilograms times meters per second squared. The definition of force *is* built into Newton's second law.

### A Named Trade-off: Precision at the Cost of System Definition

To apply Newton's second law correctly, you must define your *system*—the object or objects whose motion you are analyzing. Once you define the system, you count only external forces (forces acting on the system from outside). Internal forces (forces between objects within the system) cancel out and can be ignored.

This is powerful. It simplifies problems enormously. But it requires discipline. Choose the wrong system, and you will count the wrong forces and get the wrong answer.

Example: Two children push a wagon. If your system is just the wagon, the forces from the children are external and must be counted. If your system is the two children plus the wagon, then the forces the children apply to the wagon are internal and cancel, and you must count only external forces like friction from the ground and the push of Earth's gravity.

### Worked Example: Lawn Mower Acceleration

A person pushes a lawn mower with a net external force (the applied push minus friction) of 51 newtons parallel to the ground. The mass of the mower is 240 kilograms. What is its acceleration?

Using Newton's second law:

$$\mathbf{a} = \frac{\mathbf{F}_{\text{net}}}{m} = \frac{51 \text{ N}}{240 \text{ kg}} = 0.21 \text{ m/s}^2$$

The mower accelerates at 0.21 meters per second squared. This means its speed increases by 0.21 meters per second every second. A person can sustain this for several seconds before reaching their top speed, at which point they only need to push hard enough to overcome friction and maintain constant velocity.

[FIGURE: A stick figure pushing a lawn mower. Arrow pointing right labeled "Net force = 51 N". Below: "a = F/m = 51 N / 240 kg = 0.21 m/s²". Caption: Applying Newton's second law to find acceleration. The net force and mass determine the result.]

### Misconceptions

**"Weight and mass are the same thing."** No. Mass is the quantity of matter (kilograms). Weight is the force of gravity on that mass (newtons). On the moon, a kilogram has less weight but the same mass. The equation is $W = mg$, where $g$ is the local acceleration due to gravity. On Earth, $g = 9.80 \text{ m/s}^2$. On the moon, $g = 1.67 \text{ m/s}^2$.

**"Newton's second law tells us why things fall."** Not quite. It tells us *how much they accelerate* when gravity acts on them. Gravity is the force. The second law is the relationship between that force and the resulting acceleration. The "why" is deeper and belongs to a later conversation.

## 5. Core Concept 4: Newton's Third Law and Why Forces Come in Pairs

### The Cold Open: A Swimmer Pushes Off

A swimmer stands in the shallow end of a pool, feet against the wall. She pushes backward against the wall with her feet. Her feet accelerate backward (into the wall). The wall accelerates her body forward (out into the pool). How? She pushed the wall. The wall pushed her. Same magnitude. Opposite direction. She is now the system moving away from the wall, accelerating because the wall exerts a force on her.

This is the counterintuitive part. When she pushes the wall, the wall does not just passively yield. The wall pushes back. The wall always pushes back. Every push produces a reaction push. Every force produces an equal and opposite force back on the source.

### The Mechanism: Action-Reaction Pairs and Different Objects

Newton's third law states: whenever a first object exerts a force on a second object, the first object experiences a force equal in magnitude but opposite in direction.

Forces come in pairs. But here is the critical point that confuses almost everyone: the two forces in a pair act on *different objects*. They do not cancel each other because they are not adding up on the same thing.

The swimmer pushes the wall (action). The wall pushes the swimmer (reaction). Both forces have magnitude $F$. They point in opposite directions. But one acts on the wall. One acts on the swimmer. They cannot cancel because they are not acting on the same object. The swimmer accelerates because the wall's force acts on the swimmer. The wall does not accelerate (because the wall is attached to the pool, which is attached to Earth, which is enormous) because we do not count the swimmer's force in isolation—we count net forces.

Consider a book resting on a table. Gravity pulls the book downward (weight). The table pushes the book upward (normal force). These forces are equal and opposite. But are they an action-reaction pair?

No. They both act on the book. A true action-reaction pair is: gravity (Earth pulls book down) and the book pulls Earth up. Or: normal force (table pushes book up) and book pushes table down. In each pair, the two forces act on different objects.

This distinction is foundational. Confuse it, and Newton's third law seems to say "nothing can accelerate because forces always cancel." But that is wrong. Forces cancel only when they act on the same object. Action-reaction pairs act on different objects.

### A Named Trade-off: Simplicity vs. Completeness

Newton's third law is completely general. Every force is paired with an equal and opposite reaction. But in practice, we often ignore the reaction force because it acts on something we do not care about.

When you jump, you push the ground downward. By Newton's third law, the ground pushes you upward with equal force. But we do not care about the force on the ground (the ground is too massive to accelerate noticeably). We care about the force on you. So we simplify: we say "the ground exerts an upward force on the jumper" and ignore the downward force on the ground.

This is practical simplification, not a violation of the law. The law still holds. We just ignore part of it because it is not relevant.

### Three Specific Forces: Normal Force, Tension, and Thrust

**Normal force** is the outward push that a surface exerts on an object resting on it, perpendicular to the surface. It prevents the object from falling through. For an object at rest on a horizontal surface, the normal force equals the weight (the object's mass times $g$). Mathematically: $N = mg$. This is not the same as the weight itself—the weight is downward, the normal force is upward—but their magnitudes are equal when there is no vertical acceleration.

**Tension** is the pulling force in a rope, cable, or chain. When an object hangs from a rope, the rope exerts an upward force (tension). By Newton's third law, the object exerts a downward force on the rope. For a stationary hanging mass, the tension equals the weight: $T = mg$. If the rope is massless (a good approximation in most problems), tension is the same at both ends of the rope.

**Thrust** is the forward force on an object (like a rocket) in response to the backward expulsion of mass (like exhaust). The rocket pushes gas backward. The gas, by Newton's third law, pushes the rocket forward. This is not "pushing against air" or "pushing against the ground." A rocket works better in vacuum, where it can expel gas more easily. The reaction force is the thrust.

### Worked Example: Accelerating a Cart with Newton's Third Law

A physics teacher (mass 65 kg) pushes a cart (mass 12 kg) with equipment (mass 7 kg) across the floor. The teacher's foot applies a backward force of 150 newtons on the floor. By Newton's third law, the floor exerts a forward force of 150 newtons on the teacher (and therefore on the entire system: teacher + cart + equipment). Friction opposes with a force of 24 newtons.

What is the acceleration of the system?

First, find the net external force:
$$\mathbf{F}_{\text{net}} = 150 \text{ N} - 24 \text{ N} = 126 \text{ N}$$

Next, find the total mass:
$$m = 65 + 12 + 7 = 84 \text{ kg}$$

Now apply Newton's second law:
$$\mathbf{a} = \frac{\mathbf{F}_{\text{net}}}{m} = \frac{126 \text{ N}}{84 \text{ kg}} = 1.5 \text{ m/s}^2$$

The system accelerates at 1.5 meters per second squared. Note that we did not count the force the teacher exerts on the cart (the teacher pushing the cart's handle) because that is an internal force within our system. We only count external forces: the floor on the system, and friction opposing the system.

[FIGURE: A teacher with hands on a cart filled with equipment. Arrows labeled: "Floor pushes system: 150 N (right)" and "Friction opposes: 24 N (left)". Below: "Net force = 126 N. System mass = 84 kg. Acceleration = 1.5 m/s²". Caption: Defining the system carefully allows us to apply Newton's second and third laws correctly. Internal forces (teacher to cart) cancel; external forces determine the acceleration.]

### Misconceptions

**"Action and reaction cancel, so nothing accelerates."** No. They cancel only if they act on the same object. Because they act on different objects, they do not cancel. The swimmer accelerates away from the wall because the wall's force acts on the swimmer. The wall does not accelerate noticeably because the pool and Earth are massive.

**"The normal force is always equal to the weight."** Not always. The normal force equals the weight only when there is no acceleration perpendicular to the surface. If you stand on a scale in an accelerating elevator, the normal force (which the scale measures) is larger if the elevator accelerates upward, smaller if it accelerates downward.

## 6. Integration and Synthesis

Newton's three laws form a coherent story.

The first law says: motion persists without force. An object at rest stays at rest. An object in motion stays in motion at constant velocity. Change requires force.

The second law quantifies the change. The net force and the mass determine how much the motion changes. Double the force, double the acceleration. Double the mass, halve the acceleration. The law is proportional, mathematical, testable.

The third law ensures the whole system is consistent. When one object exerts force, something else exerts an equal and opposite force. This is not a balance within a single object. It is a statement about the world: forces are relational. A and B cannot interact without each feeling the same force, in opposite directions, acting on different bodies.

Together, these laws explain everything that moves. A person walking (pushing ground backward, ground pushing person forward). A car accelerating (wheels pushing road backward, road pushing car forward). A rocket launching (expelling exhaust backward, exhaust pushing rocket forward). A ball falling (Earth pulling ball, ball pulling Earth—negligibly, because Earth is massive). A book at rest on a table (gravity pulling down, table pushing up, both balanced).

Scale oscillation: these laws work at every scale. A dust particle (net force zero, it drifts at constant velocity). An apple (one newton of gravitational force, tiny acceleration). A human (hundreds of newtons, measurable acceleration). A truck (thousands of newtons, still measurable but slower acceleration because of mass). A planet (gravitational forces are vast, but so is planetary mass). A star (even more so).

The laws work because they are about *relationships*, not about specific materials or scales. They work in air, in water, in vacuum. On Earth, on the moon, in the deepest space. They are not approximate. They are exact (until you get to relativistic speeds or quantum scales, but that is another conversation).

## 8. Chapter Summary

Force is the instrument of change. It is what moves things, stops things, and redirects them. Force has magnitude and direction (it is a vector). When multiple forces act on an object, they combine into a net force.

**Newton's first law:** A body at rest remains at rest, and a body in motion remains in motion at constant velocity, unless acted on by a net external force. This means motion persists naturally. Only force changes motion. Friction is a force; it opposes motion but does not prove the law wrong.

**Inertia and mass:** The tendency of an object to resist acceleration is inertia. Inertia is proportional to mass—the quantity of matter in an object. Mass does not depend on gravity; weight does. A kilogram is always a kilogram. But its weight on Earth is 9.8 newtons; on the moon, 1.67 newtons.

**Newton's second law:** $\mathbf{F}_{\text{net}} = m \mathbf{a}$. The net external force determines acceleration. Acceleration is directly proportional to force and inversely proportional to mass. Double the force, double the acceleration. Double the mass, halve the acceleration.

**Newton's third law:** Forces come in pairs: action and reaction. But the two forces act on different objects, so they do not cancel. When the swimmer pushes the wall, the wall pushes the swimmer with equal force, and the swimmer accelerates away because the wall's force acts on the swimmer.

**Key forces:** Normal force (perpendicular push from a surface), tension (pull along a rope or cable), thrust (reaction force from expelled mass). Each can be analyzed using free-body diagrams and Newton's second law.

**Free-body diagrams:** Draw the object as a point. Represent all external forces as arrows, with length proportional to magnitude and direction showing where the force acts. This is the first step in solving any force problem.

**System definition:** Choose carefully which objects are part of your system. External forces (from outside) must be counted. Internal forces (between objects in the system) can be ignored because they cancel. The right choice of system makes problems simple.

## 9. Connections Forward

Newton's laws are universal, but their consequences vary with circumstances. In the coming chapters, you will see:

- **Motion in two dimensions:** Here we assumed forces and motion along a line. But the real world is 2D and 3D. The same laws apply; you just decompose forces into components and apply Newton's second law separately to each direction.

- **Circular motion:** When an object moves in a circle, it accelerates (its direction changes). The force required to produce that change is called centripetal force, and it points toward the center. Newton's second law still applies.

- **Gravity and orbits:** The gravitational force between two objects depends on their masses and the distance between them. Plug that force into Newton's second law, and you can predict orbits, predict tides, even predict the eventual fate of the universe.

- **Momentum and impulse:** Force is not the only way to think about change in motion. The product of mass and velocity (momentum) is conserved in collisions. Understanding momentum often makes collision problems simpler than analyzing forces.

- **Energy and work:** Force and displacement together determine energy transfer. A force that moves an object does work. Accumulate enough work, and you raise the object's kinetic energy (speed) or potential energy (height). Newton's laws and energy are two languages for the same physics.

The three laws Newton wrote are among humanity's most powerful insights. They survived 300 years of experimental scrutiny. They work at every scale from atoms to galaxies. And they are still our first, best description of how the physical world responds to force.

during the yank) is small enough not to drag the coin along
significantly.

Vary it: try with the paper held against the coin gently (slow

**Demo B — F = ma Pulley Setup**

Setup: Cart mass M; hanging mass m; string ideally inextensible.

Predicted acceleration (from F = ma applied to both objects):
a = mg / (M + m), where g = 9.8 m/s² and the system mass is
M + m.

Compare measured a to predicted. Differences are likely from
friction in the cart's wheels or the pulley.
