*The broker who decides once, writes it down, and trains the team moves faster and safer than the broker who decides every time.*

![A broker's desk with two stacks of paper](images/11-building-your-ai-workflow-fig-01.png)
*Figure 11.1 — A broker's desk with two stacks of paper*

There is an expensive way to use AI and a cheap way to use AI. The expensive way is to decide from scratch every time. Can I use it on this email? This lease abstract? This valuation screen? This client memo? Each decision burns attention. Each inconsistent answer creates inconsistent risk. The broker who operates this way is not protecting himself — he is deferring the protection decision until the moment it is hardest to make correctly, which is when he is under deadline pressure on a live transaction.

The cheap way is to make the decisions once, write them down, and stop making them again. Not a ninety-page policy document. A one-page operating rule that tells everyone on the team — including the broker himself — which tasks AI may start, which tasks require review before output goes anywhere, and which tasks AI may not own at all. The decision gets made once, in a calm moment, with time to think. Then it gets applied every time, under any pressure.

This is not a compliance exercise. It is a cognitive efficiency exercise. The workflow exists so the broker's attention goes to the work that requires judgment, not to the meta-question of whether AI is appropriate for this particular task on this particular Tuesday.

## Why repeated decisions are expensive

The problem with case-by-case AI decisions is not that any individual decision is wrong. It is that the cumulative cost of making the same class of decision repeatedly is high, and the inconsistency that results creates risk that compounds.

Consider a team of four agents, each using AI tools on their own judgment. Agent A uses AI to draft client emails and checks them carefully. Agent B uses AI to draft client emails and sends them without review because she is running late. Agent C does not use AI at all because he is not sure if it is allowed. Agent D uses AI for everything including the final recommendation memo, because no one told him not to.

![Four-agent scenario ](images/11-building-your-ai-workflow-fig-02.png)
*Figure 11.2 — Four-agent scenario *

The inconsistency is the risk. If something goes wrong in Track B or Track D, the broker supervising the team cannot point to a documented standard that was violated. There is no standard. There is just four people doing what seemed right to them individually. California DRE guidance on licensee supervision makes clear that the responsible broker carries accountability for agents' professional conduct — including, now, AI-assisted conduct. The broker who cannot describe the team's AI workflow is the broker who cannot demonstrate the supervision that the license requires. [unverified — needs source confirmation before publication]

## The two lists and what goes between them

The structure of any practical AI workflow in CRE is built on three categories. The names do not matter. The logic does.

The first category is tasks AI may start without special review — what I will call the Delegate list. These are pattern-shaped tasks where the tool's output is a draft or a first pass that will be checked before it does anything. Drafting outreach emails from a template. Summarizing an offering memorandum for internal use. Extracting key fields from a lease for a comparative matrix. Organizing tour notes into a structured format. Pulling comparable sales from a database into a formatted comp sheet. For these tasks, the machine does the pattern work and a human checks the output before it leaves the building. The review is light because the risk is light — the output is internal, the stakes are low, and the checking step is fast.

The second category is tasks where AI may not own the final output — the Guard list. These are judgment-shaped tasks where the output carries professional accountability and the tool cannot supply the judgment that accountability requires. The final recommendation to a client. The pricing opinion. The negotiation position. The disclosure decision. The lease clause interpretation that changes the economics. The final valuation memo. For these tasks, AI may assist with information gathering or drafting, but a human professional makes the final call and signs their name to it. Not because the tool always gets it wrong, but because the accountability cannot transfer. [unverified — needs source confirmation before publication]

Between these two lists is the largest category in practice: Delegate with Review. Tasks where AI does the first pass and a human does a substantive review before the output crosses from internal work to client-facing work. This is where most of CRE practice lives. A lease abstract that goes to a client. A market summary that informs a recommendation. A due diligence flag list that shapes the buyer's decision. The review step is not a formality. It is the step that converts an AI output into a professional work product.

## What review actually means

One of the most common mistakes in AI workflow design is treating review as rereading. Review is not rereading. Review is checking the specific failure modes associated with the specific task.

For a lease abstract, the failure modes are: missed clauses with economic consequences, paraphrasing that loses a qualifier, co-tenancy or kick-out provisions that change the risk profile of the deal. Review means checking those specific things — not reading the abstract from top to bottom to confirm it is fluent, but checking the clauses that matter most against the source language.

For a comp sheet, the failure modes are: off-market trades included, distressed dispositions treated as market evidence, comps outside the relevant submarket selected because they are geographically close. Review means auditing the comp set against those criteria.

For a market summary, the failure modes are: statistics without a source, trends presented as current that reflect data that is months old, submarket-specific dynamics that a national model would not capture. Review means checking sources and flagging where local knowledge differs from the automated summary. [unverified — needs source confirmation before publication]

## The leasing workflow, built out

A leasing workflow covers the tasks that AI assists with from first contact through executed lease. The pattern work is substantial. The judgment work is decisive.

AI may start: drafting initial outreach from a client brief, summarizing competing space options, extracting key terms from a proposed lease for comparison, organizing space tour notes, pulling lease comparables for negotiation preparation.

Human review covers: the economics of the deal for this client, the clause materiality assessment, the relationship signals the broker has that are not in any document, and the advice to the client about whether to proceed. The outreach email that goes to a counterparty should be checked for tone and accuracy. The lease abstract that goes to a client should be checked against the source language for the seven clauses most likely to change the economics. The negotiation preparation memo should be reviewed before it shapes a conversation.

![Leasing workflow from first contact to executed lease](images/11-building-your-ai-workflow-fig-05.png)
*Figure 11.5 — Leasing workflow from first contact to executed lease*

The workflow matters most at the lease review stage. This is where AI tools that summarize and abstract are most useful and most dangerous. They are most useful because a 60-page commercial lease with six amendments is genuinely difficult to hold in working memory. They are most dangerous because the summary that looks complete may be missing the clause that changes the deal. The workflow answer is the one from Chapter 8: the seven clauses most likely to carry economic consequences — co-tenancy, kick-out, rent escalation triggers, assignment restrictions, CAM cap provisions, termination rights, renewal option conditions — are checked against source language, not against the abstract. The abstract gets the broker to the right page. The source language is what the broker relies on.

## The investment sales workflow, built out

An investment sales workflow covers the tasks that AI assists with from opportunity identification through close. The pattern work is substantial on the front end. The judgment work is decisive in the middle and at the end.

AI may start: extracting data from offering memoranda, formatting comp sheets, summarizing market reports for submarket context, screening opportunities against investment criteria, organizing due diligence documents.

Human review covers: the underwriting assumptions, the valuation story, the buyer pool analysis, the cap rate judgment, and the recommendation to the client. A comp sheet that the broker did not audit is a comp sheet that may contain off-market trades, distressed dispositions, and transactions with conditions the model cannot see. An OM summary that has not been checked against the source documents is an OM summary that may be missing the clause that changes the economics. ## The team supervision problem

The workflow that protects the broker is the workflow that the whole team uses. A broker who has a personal AI discipline but no documented team standard is still exposed through the agents he supervises. [unverified — needs source confirmation before publication]
<!-- FACT-CHECK FLAG: UNVERIFIED — see factchecks/11-building-your-ai-workflow-assertions.md -->

California DRE guidance is explicit that the responsible broker carries accountability for licensee conduct. When an agent on the team sends a client memo that contains AI output presented as professional judgment, the broker is accountable — not because the broker wrote it, but because the broker supervises the agent and the broker's name is on the practice.

The team workflow needs to answer four questions. Who may use which tools? For a small Westside team, the answer is probably: all agents may use approved tools for Delegate and Delegate with Review tasks; final client-facing outputs require broker review before delivery. What must be checked? The review step should be specific to the task, not generic. What goes in the file? At minimum: the prompt or query used, the AI output received, the source documents checked, the edits made, and the final deliverable. What triggers escalation to the broker, to counsel, or to the client? A conflict between the AI output and the source documents is an escalation trigger. A material omission in a lease abstract is an escalation trigger. A valuation figure that cannot be defended against a specific comp set is an escalation trigger.

The ISO/IEC 42001 AI management standard and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework both address these questions at enterprise scale. A small team does not need enterprise governance. It needs the same logic in lighter form: define use, specify review, document decisions, escalate when the review turns up a problem.

## The workflow as a one-page operating rule

The goal of the workflow is a document short enough to be posted, specific enough to be followed, and durable enough to survive deadline pressure.

A one-page operating rule for a Westside CRE team has six components. First, approved tools — the specific AI tools the team is authorized to use for specific task categories. Second, the Delegate list — tasks AI may start with light review. Third, the Delegate with Review list — tasks requiring substantive review before client delivery, with the specific failure modes to check for each task type. Fourth, the Guard list — tasks AI may not own. Fifth, file documentation requirements — what evidence of the AI-assisted process must be retained. Sixth, disclosure triggers — when clients or counterparties must be informed that AI was used in the work product. [unverified — needs source confirmation before publication]

The rule should be reviewed quarterly, or whenever a major new tool enters the practice. Tools change. Failure modes change. The underlying logic does not change: AI accelerates pattern work, professional judgment owns accountability, and the workflow is the mechanism that keeps the two correctly assigned.

## The misconceptions, examined directly

The first misconception is that a workflow slows the team down. The opposite is true. A documented workflow eliminates the repeated attention cost of case-by-case decisions. The team does not ask whether AI is appropriate for this lease abstract — the workflow already answered that question. The time saved on meta-decisions is time applied to the actual work.

The second misconception is that only large firms need AI governance. Small teams face the same client exposure and the same license obligations that large firms face. The difference is that small teams need lighter tools. A one-page operating rule is a governance document. It does not require a compliance department.

The third misconception is that review means rereading everything. Review means checking the failure modes specific to the task. A lease abstract review checks seven clauses against source language. A comp sheet review audits the selected transactions. A market summary review checks the sources and tests the local-dynamics claims. The review is targeted, not exhaustive. A targeted review can be done under deadline pressure. An exhaustive one cannot.

## What you should be able to do now

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to describe your practice's AI workflow in one page: the tools approved for use, the tasks that fall into Delegate versus Delegate with Review versus Guard, the specific failure modes to check for each review task, the file documentation requirements, and the escalation triggers. If you cannot describe it in one page, the workflow is not finished yet.

The broker in the opening case did not need better AI tools. He needed a decision made once, written down, and applied consistently — so his attention could go to the transactions that required it, not to the meta-question of whether AI was appropriate for this particular task.

That decision, written down, is the workflow.

**Apply:** Build a first draft of a one-page AI operating rule for your own practice. Populate the Delegate list, the Delegate with Review list, and the Guard list with specific tasks from your current workflow. For each Delegate with Review task, name the failure mode the review step is checking for.

**Analyze:** Return to the four-agent scenario from this chapter. Identify which of the four tracks represents the greatest professional risk and explain why. Then identify the one addition to the team workflow that would have the highest risk-reduction value.

**Create:** Write the file-documentation standard for one specific AI-assisted task in your practice. The standard should be specific enough that a junior agent could follow it without asking for clarification.
