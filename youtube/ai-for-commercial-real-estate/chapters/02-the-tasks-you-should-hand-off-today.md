That's the work I want to talk about. Not the hard work — the weight-bearing decisions, the read on where a market is heading, the negotiation judgment that comes from watching a cycle. That work is yours and it stays yours. I'm talking about the other kind: the work that is necessary but not irreplaceable, shaped by pattern rather than judgment, where the output is a first draft that needs checking rather than a conclusion that needs defending.

The question brokers get wrong is "Can AI do this?" The right question is: "Can AI produce the first pass safely — if I know exactly what to check?"

That's a different question. It has a different answer. And it gives you something useful: a working line.

## What a Working Line Actually Is

When Feynman was trying to explain something, he'd stop and ask: what is the simplest question whose answer I don't yet know? That's not a rhetorical move. It's a diagnostic. The question locates exactly where understanding ends and confusion begins.

The working line in this chapter does the same thing. On one side: tasks where AI can produce the first pass and you can verify the result without professional judgment. On the other side: tasks where verification *is* the professional judgment, and you can't separate them.

That line isn't fixed. It depends on the task, the context, and what the output will be used for. But it's not fuzzy either. Once you've thought through it carefully for a given category of work, you know where you stand.

There are three positions on the line, and they're worth naming precisely.

**Delegate.** The task is pattern-shaped, the source material is verified, and a wrong output would cost you revision time, not a client relationship or a legal exposure. Hand it off, review the result, make corrections, ship it.

**Delegate with Review.** The task is more consequential — maybe it touches economic terms, lease obligations, or market claims a client will rely on. AI can still do the first pass, but the review step is structural, not casual. You're not spot-checking. You're verifying sources, checking dates, and asking whether the output matches what your local knowledge tells you.

**Guard.** The task requires judgment that cannot be separated from its context: valuation opinions, negotiation decisions, legal interpretation, client recommendations. AI can help you think, but the output belongs to you and cannot be delegated without also delegating accountability.

Most brokers who get into trouble with AI are doing one of two things: treating Delegate with Review tasks as Delegate tasks (skipping the review), or trying to Guard tasks (handing off the conclusion). The working line prevents both.

## The First List: Documents and Drafts

The clearest case for delegation is the blank page. Writing a property description, drafting an offering memorandum, generating email templates, producing market-update copy — these tasks have a structure, a voice, and a set of facts. The structure is fixed by convention. The voice is yours to establish. The facts have to come from verified source material.

What AI does well here is exactly what it sounds like: it fills the structure with language. Given a building's square footage, its amenity list, its lease terms, its submarket story, and a sense of tone, a good language model can produce a first draft in seconds that would have taken you an hour. That draft will be grammatically correct, structurally coherent, and built to the conventions of the genre.

What you do with that draft is not optional. You read it. You check every factual claim against the source. You ask whether the language is accurate, compliant, on-brand, and appropriate for this specific client. You add the relationship-informed observation — the thing you know about this building or this market that isn't in the source material — because that's why the client hired you.

The test for whether you've reviewed adequately: could you explain, to the client, every factual claim in the output? If you can, the delegation worked. If there's a sentence you're not sure about, that's the sentence that will come back to you.

The presentation outline case is worth a brief stop. AI is good at generating structure — here are the five sections of a client pitch, here is the logical order of the argument. What it cannot do is know which sections matter for *this* client, *this* deal, *this* relationship. The structure is the easy part. The selection is the judgment part. Delegate the structure. Guard the selection.

## The Second List: Documents and Data Processing

If the blank-page case is about language, the data-processing case is about repetition. Lease clause extraction, rent-roll cleanup, comparable transaction formatting, pro forma population from source documents — these are tasks where the work is iterative, source-bound, and structurally identical every time you do it.

The value here is different from the drafting case. You're not buying a first draft. You're buying time — the forty minutes of moving numbers between columns, the hour of pulling operative clauses from a lease and flagging the renewal options, the afternoon of standardizing comp notes into a table format. That time is real, and getting it back is real.

The model that works in production is structured this way: AI extracts or organizes the data; you verify the result against the source; you make corrections; you use the output. That middle step — verify against the source — is not optional. It is the model. Dealpath's AI Extract feature, which moves OM and flyer data into structured fields, builds in an explicit review step precisely because that step is what makes the output usable rather than dangerous. The tool knows it cannot be the last step. That is the right design philosophy.

Here is where the working line does its clearest work. Rent roll cleanup is a Delegate with Review task. The output might look correct — the numbers are in the right columns, the formatting is clean — but if a unit was miscategorized or a square footage was pulled from the wrong field, the error now looks authoritative because it's in a table. A table that looks right is worse than a spreadsheet that looks messy, because you stop looking at the table. You have to look at the table.

For lease abstraction, the stakes are higher. Lease clause extraction is useful — it saves time on the mechanical work of identifying operative language. But the interpretation of what that language means, whether a particular clause creates a risk for the client, whether the renewal option is favorable given current market rates — that is Guard territory. AI can find the clause. You determine what the clause means. [unverified — needs source confirmation before publication]

## The Third List: Research and Screening

The research case is where things get more interesting, because the tasks are genuinely useful and the failure modes are less visible.

Initial market screening, public tenant-credit summaries, zoning-code plain-language summaries, demographic compilation, foot-traffic pattern summaries — these all begin as pattern-shaped tasks. There are conventions for what a market summary looks like, there are public sources for demographic data, there are zoning codes that say what they say. AI can compile, summarize, and structure that material faster than any human researcher.

The failure mode is not hallucination, exactly — though that happens. The more common failure is confidence without currency. An AI summarizing a submarket's vacancy story will produce a fluent, well-structured paragraph. But if the source data it's drawing on is six months old, the paragraph will be fluently wrong about a market that moved. It will not flag its own staleness. It will not know that the tenant you're tracking just signed elsewhere, or that a new lease renegotiation changed the effective rent picture.

This is why each research task on the list gets a verification rule, not just a review.

The verification rule is specific: source the claim, check the date, ask whether local knowledge contradicts the output. That last question is the one brokers skip. You have market knowledge that the model does not have. When the output matches your intuition, you tend to let it go without checking. That's exactly when you should check — not because the output is wrong, but because the test of your market knowledge is whether it can catch errors, and you can only test that by comparing.

The zoning case deserves a specific note. Zoning-code plain-language summaries are useful for initial screening — is this site potentially viable for this use? — and dangerous for anything beyond that. Zoning determinations are jurisdictional, interpretive, and subject to staff discretion in ways that a plain-language summary cannot capture. The AI summary can tell you whether the code text permits the use. It cannot tell you whether the planning department has been interpreting that code text in a way that would affect your client's project. For initial screening, useful. For reliance, Guard.

## The One Question

Here is the simplest version of everything in this chapter. Before you let an AI output move from your internal workflow to anything that influences a client decision, ask one question:

**If this output were wrong, what would it cost?**

The answer sorts the task for you. If the cost is revision time — you sent a draft that needed editing — that's a Delegate task and the review is calibrated to that cost. If the cost is a client relationship — you forwarded a market claim that turned out to be six months stale and the client made a decision based on it — that's Delegate with Review and the verification rule is structural. If the cost is a legal exposure, a missed economic term, or a valuation error — that's Guard, and no first pass from AI belongs in the client-facing output without your explicit professional judgment stamped on it.

This is not a complicated framework. It's a single question. The reason it works is that it forces you to think about consequence rather than capability. AI is capable of many things that are still too consequential to delegate without tight review. The capability test tells you whether the tool can do the task. The consequence test tells you how careful you have to be with the result.

## The Culver City Case

Take a concrete example, from a real submarket, to make the application specific.

You're preparing a market-update email for a client with Culver City creative office exposure. Your source material is verified: current vacancy rate, current asking rents, three recent leasing transactions with verified terms. That's your input. You ask the AI for three draft versions — different tones, different emphasis — from that source material. It produces three versions. You read all three. You remove any claim that isn't sourced directly to your verified input — any sentence that sounds like a market conclusion you didn't give it. You add one observation that comes from your own knowledge of the submarket: maybe you know that the tenant pool has been more cautious than the headline leasing activity suggests, or that a specific block has absorbed differently than the broader market.

Then you check one more thing: does the submarket story in the draft match the metro-level narrative, or does it differ? If it differs, you explain why. If you can't explain why, you're not ready to send the email.

*Figure 2.2 — Visual of the three-step delegation loop *

The lesson is in the framing: you delegated the blank page. You did not delegate the market opinion. The draft gave you language. You gave it the judgment that makes the language true.

The limit is symmetrical: if your source data is stale or wrong, the AI will produce a draft that makes stale data sound confident. That is worse than a draft that sounds uncertain. Confident and wrong is harder to catch than uncertain and wrong, because certainty suppresses the instinct to verify. Your source material is your responsibility before you touch the tool.

## Two Misconceptions Worth Correcting

**More AI use means better practice.** It does not. Faster production of low-value noise is faster production of low-value noise. Delegation is valuable when it returns broker time to judgment work — not when it returns broker time to producing more AI output. The test of good delegation is whether you spend the recovered time on something that requires professional judgment. If you use it to generate more first drafts, you've created a production machine without improving the product.

**If a task is safe once, it is safe always.** Context changes risk. A property description is a low-risk Delegate task until it contains a square footage you didn't verify, a zoning claim from the wrong source, or a tenant restriction the client will rely on. The task classification holds when the input conditions hold. When the inputs change — when the source material is less certain, when the client relationship is higher stakes, when the output will be used in a different way — you reclassify.

## Where the Line Moves Next

This chapter is a snapshot. The working line I've described is calibrated to what the tools can reliably do in 2026 — language generation, document processing, structured research — and what they reliably cannot do: carry source provenance, apply professional judgment, account for local context.

If the tools change, the line moves. Specifically: if AI tools begin to automatically carry verified source provenance — if the output includes not just the claim but the dated source and a verification trail — then the review step for some Delegate with Review tasks becomes lighter, because part of the review is done by the tool itself. The governance frameworks are pushing in that direction. NIST's AI Risk Management Framework and ISO 42001 both emphasize documentation and transparency in AI outputs, and tool designers who take those frameworks seriously will build verification provenance into the product.

Until that happens, the review step is yours. The line stays where it is.
