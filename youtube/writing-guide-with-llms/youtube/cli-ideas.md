## Candidate 01 — Build the Writing Pack Inventory with Claude (LLM Exercise, Ch 1)

- Source: writing-guide-with-llms/chapters/01-the-things-we-carry-experience-culture-and-language.md + LLM Exercise
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: Every writer enters the semester carrying a pack — the languages, literacy experiences, and cultural contexts that will shape how they write. Most students free-write their way through this. The interview method is different: Claude asks one question at a time, follows up on what's underneath, and then gives the inventory back as a clean document the writer carries through the whole course.
- The artifact: A CLI Python interactive session (~20 lines) that runs Claude as a structured interviewer: one question at a time, 6 required topics (household languages, first writing for a reader, a teacher who made you feel like a writer, the online place where you found your voice, a piece of writing you've returned to, your first LLM writing experience), with follow-up prompts for any answer that has "more underneath it." After the interview, Claude produces a 200–300 word written inventory of the writer's specific literacy history. The inventory is Claude's synthesis of the writer's own answers — not invented.
- Prompt seed: `claude "I'm starting a college writing course. Interview me about what I'm 'carrying' into the classroom — my language, literacy, and cultural experiences. Ask me one question at a time. Cover: (1) languages spoken at home and differences between them, (2) first writing I produced for a reader other than a teacher, (3) a moment a teacher made me feel like a writer and a moment one made me feel like I wasn't, (4) the online place where I learned to write in a voice, (5) a piece of writing I've returned to more than once, (6) the first time I used an LLM to help me write. After all 6 (with follow-up where needed), summarize my 'pack' in 200-300 words. Do not write prose for me. Give it back as a clean inventory."`
- Read / check: Verify Claude asks questions one at a time and waits for answers (interactive structure); confirm the inventory synthesis is built from the writer's actual answers (not invented specifics); check that the inventory is 200-300 words and reads as a usable document the writer could reference across a semester.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): The writer's actual literacy history — the inventory is only as specific as the answers given. For the video, use pre-written realistic answers to the 6 questions (a real person's literacy history, anonymized) to demonstrate the interview-to-inventory sequence at speed.
- Output medium: screen-recording mp4 (screen-rec of interactive terminal session: each question appearing, a pause for the answer (shown as pre-typed text for video pacing), then the follow-up, then the 200-300 word inventory printing at the end — the inventory is the payoff)
- The change: After the inventory is produced, run a second prompt: "Given this inventory, what is the one writing strength and the one writing challenge this writer should be most aware of going into an argumentative research paper?" Shows the inventory being used as a diagnostic tool, not just a reflection exercise.
- Teardown angle: The interview forces specificity that free-writing avoids. Claude cannot produce the inventory without your answers — but it can ask the questions that find the specific material that generic prompts miss. The inventory belongs to the writer; the interview belongs to Claude.
- Exclusions: Full first-chapter discussion of multilingualism, code-switching, and identity (covered in ch03); writing autobiography as memoir; language acquisition theory; the digital literacy framework (ch02).
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 02 — Build the Position Argument Scaffold with Claude (LLM Exercise, Ch 12)

- Source: writing-guide-with-llms/chapters/12-position-argument-practicing-the-art-of-rhetoric.md + LLM Exercise
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: The position argument requires a debatable claim, evidence-based defense, and engagement with the strongest objections. Most students draft first and discover the weak spots in revision. The scaffold builds the analytical structure before the prose — identifying which objections the writer is least prepared to address while there is still time to get more evidence.
- The artifact: A CLI Python interactive session (~25 lines) that runs Claude as a 4-step analytical coach: (1) stress-test the claim (3 diagnostic questions, one at a time), (2) construct the 3 strongest objections a hostile reader would raise and identify which one the writer is least prepared for, (3) for each supporting point, identify the strongest evidence in the provided sources (and flag points with weak/no support), (4) propose a sharpened one-sentence claim incorporating the revisions from steps 1-3. Claude does not draft the argument — the drafting is the writer's.
- Prompt seed: `claude "I'm writing a 1,500–2,500 word position argument for a college writing class. The genre requires a debatable claim, evidence-based defense, and engagement with the strongest objections. My issue: [TOPIC]. My tentative claim: [CLAIM]. My sources so far: [LIST]. Run me through 4 steps, one at a time, waiting for my response: (1) stress-test my claim — is it specific, debatable, defensible with my evidence? Ask 3 diagnostic questions. (2) Construct the 3 strongest objections a hostile reader would raise — do not soften them. Tell me which I am least prepared to address. (3) For each supporting point, identify the strongest evidence in my sources. Flag any point with weak or no support. (4) Propose a one-sentence sharpened claim. Do not draft the argument."`
- Read / check: Verify Claude runs the 4 steps in sequence and waits for responses (does not compress all 4 into one output); confirm the hostile objections are genuinely hostile (not softened versions of real objections); check that the evidence-flag is specific (not "you need more evidence" but "point 2 has no evidence in the sources you provided — this is a gap").
- Human supplies (Claude can't): A real argument topic, claim, and source list — the scaffold is only as good as the material the writer brings. For the video, use the EAT-Lancet food-sustainability topic from ch14 with 3 real sources to demonstrate the full 4-step sequence.
- Output medium: screen-recording mp4 (screen-rec of interactive session: issue/claim/sources pasted in, then Claude's 4 steps appearing sequentially with the writer's responses typed between each — the sharpened claim appearing at step 4 as the payoff)
- The change: After the 4-step scaffold, ask Claude to produce a one-paragraph counterargument to the sharpened claim — specifically the objection the writer was identified as least prepared for. Then ask: "What one additional source would most directly address this counterargument?" This produces the research-gap → source-search transition.
- Teardown angle: The scaffold builds the analytical structure before the prose. The drafting is the writer's. Claude finds the gaps; the writer decides what to do with them. Most students find their weakest objection in revision — the scaffold finds it before the draft.
- Exclusions: Full position argument structure (introduction, body, conclusion); rhetorical analysis of a position argument (ch11); the specific Toulmin model beyond claim-warrant-evidence; MLA/APA formatting.
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 03 — Build the Annotated Bibliography Sharpener with Claude (LLM Exercise, Ch 16)

- Source: writing-guide-with-llms/chapters/16-annotated-bibliography-gathering-evaluating-and-documenting-sources.md + LLM Exercise
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: The annotation does 3 things, not 1: summary (what the source argues), evaluation (why it's credible), and relevance (how it connects to your specific thesis). Most draft annotations fail the evaluation and the relevance. Claude interrogates them one source at a time — but the revision is the writer's.
- The artifact: A CLI Python script (~25 lines) that takes N draft annotations (pasted as text, one per source) and runs a 4-question interrogation for each: (1) does the summary capture what the source argues or slide into a related claim? (2) does the evaluation engage seriously with credibility (author credentials, venue, methodology)? (3) does the reflection place the source in the developing argument with enough specificity? (4) is it 100-200 words? Output: per-annotation revision targets, not rewrites. The revision is the writer's.
- Prompt seed: `claude "I'm building an annotated bibliography for an argumentative research paper. Below are my draft annotations. I have read each source carefully. For each annotation, interrogate it on 4 criteria: (1) SUMMARY ACCURACY: does the summary capture what the source actually argues, or am I sliding into a related claim? Be specific about any drift. (2) CREDIBILITY ENGAGEMENT: does the evaluation engage with author credentials, publication venue, and methodology — or is it generic ('this is a credible source')? Name what's missing. (3) RELEVANCE SPECIFICITY: does the reflection name which other sources it connects to and how? Does it name a specific place in the argument where this source is used? (4) LENGTH: is it 100-200 words? If not, where to tighten or expand. Do not rewrite my annotations. Identify what needs fixing. Annotations: [PASTE]"`
- Read / check: Verify Claude interrogates each annotation separately (not a general comment on all); confirm the "summary accuracy" feedback identifies specific claim drift (not just "this is vague"); check that the credibility feedback names what is missing (not just "evaluate credibility better"); confirm the word count check is accurate.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Draft annotations from real sources — the writer must have actually read the sources and written draft annotations. For the video, use 3 synthetic annotations of varying quality (one good, one with summary drift, one with generic credibility) to demonstrate all 4 failure modes.
- Output medium: screen-recording mp4 (screen-rec of terminal: 3 draft annotations pasted in, then Claude's interrogation appearing — each annotation gets its 4-criterion audit, revision targets highlighted in specific quoted language from the draft — NOT rewrites, targets)
- The change: After the interrogation, run a second prompt on the weakest annotation: "What specific information would I need to add to pass criteria 2 (credibility evaluation)? Name the specific type of information — author's institutional affiliation, peer-review status of the journal, sample size of the study — that I can find by re-reading the source." This produces a targeted re-reading checklist.
- Teardown angle: The act of writing the annotations changes the argument. When you have to explain not just what the source says in your favor but what it actually says — including what complicates your position — you sometimes find that your thesis needs to move. The interrogation accelerates that discovery.
- Exclusions: Citation format (APA/MLA/Chicago); full bibliography management (Zotero); source finding/database navigation; the difference between annotated bibliography and literature review.
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 04 — Build the Proposal Problem Statement Drill with Claude (LLM Exercise, Ch 8)

- Source: writing-guide-with-llms/chapters/08-proposal-writing-about-problems-and-solutions.md + LLM Exercise
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: "Right now, [specific group] is experiencing [specific consequence] because of [specific cause]. Existing interventions are [working/not/partial] because [reason]. I propose [specific intervention] to [specific outcome]." If you can fill in those brackets concretely, you have a proposal. If you can't, you have a topic. The drill fills the brackets.
- The artifact: A CLI Python interactive session (~20 lines) where Claude runs the diagnostic sentence protocol from ch08: the writer pastes their draft problem statement, Claude identifies which brackets are empty (vague gestures vs. concrete specifics), asks one question at a time to fill each empty bracket, and then produces the completed diagnostic sentence as the deliverable. Output: the filled diagnostic sentence, plus a one-sentence assessment of which failure mode (too big, fake, already solved) the original statement was closest to.
- Prompt seed: `claude "A student has written a draft proposal problem statement. Run them through the ch08 diagnostic sentence protocol. The sentence form is: 'Right now, [specific group] is experiencing [specific consequence] because of [specific cause]. Existing interventions [describe] are [working/not/partial] because [reason]. I propose [specific intervention] to [specific outcome].' Step 1: identify which brackets in the draft statement are empty (vague group, vague consequence, vague cause, no intervention named). Step 2: ask one question per empty bracket — wait for the answer — until all brackets can be filled concretely. Step 3: produce the completed diagnostic sentence. Step 4: identify which classic failure mode the original statement was closest to (too big, fake problem, already solved). Draft problem statement: [INPUT]"`
- Read / check: Verify Claude asks one question at a time and waits for answers (interactive structure); confirm the completed diagnostic sentence is genuinely specific (group, consequence, cause, intervention, outcome — all filled with real content from the writer's answers, not placeholders); check that the failure-mode identification is accurate and references the ch08 categories.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): The writer's actual problem for their proposal — Claude fills the brackets from the writer's answers. For the video, use a too-broad topic ("mental health among college students") and demonstrate how the bracket-filling protocol narrows it to a proposal-scale problem.
- Output medium: screen-recording mp4 (screen-rec of interactive session: draft statement input, then Claude's bracket-identification appearing, then questions appearing one at a time with answers typed, then the completed diagnostic sentence printing — the transformation from vague to specific visible on screen)
- The change: After the diagnostic sentence is complete, run the feasibility check: "Given this completed problem statement, identify the specific actor who could implement this intervention and the timeline under which it would be feasible (one semester? One year? One legislative cycle?). Flag if the intervention is not within the implementing actor's authority."
- Teardown angle: The diagnostic sentence is not a formula — it is a test. If you can complete it, you have a proposal. If you cannot, you have a topic and a drafting process that will expose the gap at 800 words instead of catching it at 2 sentences.
- Exclusions: Full proposal structure beyond problem definition; solution design; implementation plan; the Pronovost central-line case beyond using it as the genre example.
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 05 — Build the Literacy Narrative Hinge Finder with Claude (LLM Exercise, Ch 4)

- Source: writing-guide-with-llms/chapters/04-literacy-narrative-building-bridges-bridging-gaps.md + LLM Exercise
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: The literacy narrative requires one moment when something changed — when you gained a competence in communication you didn't have before. Most writers circle the experience without landing on the hinge. Claude asks 5 questions, one at a time, that push fragmentary memories toward specificity. After 5 answers, it identifies which moment is the hinge and why.
- The artifact: A CLI Python interactive session (~20 lines) that runs Claude as a hinge-finder: writer provides a literacy (any communication competence — reading, writing, music, code, conversation, sport) and 5 fragmentary memories. Claude asks 5 questions one at a time pushing for specificity (not "what happened?" but "what did you see?" and "what did you do that you wouldn't have done the week before?"). After 5 answers, Claude identifies the hinge moment and explains in 2-3 sentences why that moment, not the others. Claude writes no prose for the essay.
- Prompt seed: `claude "I'm writing a literacy narrative for a college composition class. A literacy narrative is a story about gaining a competence in communication. I am NOT asking you to write the narrative — help me find the hinge moment (the single moment when something changed). My literacy: [LITERACY]. Five fragmentary memories: [LIST]. Ask me 5 questions, one at a time, pushing me to make these memories more specific — more concrete detail, more sensory, more precise about what changed. After my 5 answers, identify which specific moment has the strongest claim to being the hinge and explain in 2-3 sentences why that moment, not the others. Do not write any prose for the essay."`
- Read / check: Verify Claude asks 5 questions one at a time (not all at once); confirm the questions push for specificity (sensory details, what changed, who was present, what was said) rather than more narrative summary; check that the hinge identification names the specific moment and gives a 2-3 sentence explanation of WHY it is the hinge (not just which one).
- Human supplies (Claude can't): The writer's actual literacy and memories — Claude cannot invent these. For the video, use a concrete literacy (learning to code Python, learning to read music notation) with 5 pre-written fragmentary memories to demonstrate the question sequence at video pace.
- Output medium: screen-recording mp4 (screen-rec of interactive session: literacy and 5 memories input, then Claude's 5 questions appearing with typed answers between each, then the hinge identification appearing — the specific moment highlighted from the 5 memories)
- The change: After the hinge is identified, run a second prompt: "What are 3 things to include in the hinge scene to make it concrete for a reader who wasn't there — not what to write, but what material to gather (a specific object, a piece of dialogue, a sensory detail)?" This transitions from finding the moment to knowing what to put in the scene.
- Teardown angle: The hinge is not the story's climax. It is the moment before which the writer was one person, after which a different person. Most writers know which memories they have. They don't know which one is the hinge until Claude asks the 5 questions that make the transformation visible.
- Exclusions: Full literacy narrative structure beyond the hinge; the cultural/identity dimension of literacy (ch03 covers this); the genre conventions of personal narrative vs. academic essay; revision after drafting.
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 06 — Build the Digital World Credibility Audit with Claude (LLM Exercise, Ch 2)

- Source: writing-guide-with-llms/chapters/02-the-digital-world-building-on-what-you-already-know-to-respond-critically.md + LLM Exercise
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: The digital world is built on what you already know — the skills of critical reading you learned in print apply online, but the failure modes are different. An LLM can produce a confident-sounding claim with a plausible-looking citation that doesn't exist. The credibility audit is the same as it has always been — and harder than it has ever been.
- The artifact: A CLI Python script (~25 lines) that takes a digital artifact (a social media post, an online article, a screenshot of an LLM output) as text input and runs a 4-criterion credibility audit: (1) who published this and what are their incentives? (2) what is the primary evidence cited — is it specific and checkable? (3) does the text use confident language for uncertain claims ("studies show," "experts agree")? (4) what would you find if you searched for the primary source this references? Output: a credibility scorecard with one "next search" recommendation.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Audit the following digital text for credibility. Apply 4 criteria: (1) PUBLISHER: who published this and what incentive do they have to present information this way? (2) EVIDENCE: what is the primary evidence cited — is it named (author, study, institution) or gestured at vaguely? Rate: specific/partial/vague. (3) CONFIDENCE LANGUAGE: does the text use confident language for uncertain or contested claims? Flag phrases like 'studies show', 'experts agree', 'it is well known', 'research confirms'. (4) TRACE-BACK: what search term would you use to find the primary source this text references? Provide the exact search query. Output: scorecard with PASS/WARN/FAIL per criterion and one 'next search' recommendation. Text: [INPUT]"`
- Read / check: Verify the publisher incentive analysis is specific (not just "they have a bias" but what type of bias and why); confirm the confidence-language flag actually identifies the specific problematic phrases from the text; check that the "trace-back" search query is specific and realistic (not "search for [topic]" but an actual search query).
- Human supplies (Claude can't): A real digital text to audit — the human must supply a social media post, article, or LLM output. For the video, use an LLM-generated text on a factual topic that includes 2 confident-but-vague citations and 1 invented statistic — demonstrating all 4 failure modes.
- Output medium: screen-recording mp4 (screen-rec of terminal: digital text pasted in, then 4-criterion scorecard printing — FAIL rows in red, the confidence-language phrases quoted from the original text, the search query appearing last as the actionable next step)
- The change: After the audit, run the search query in a real search engine (show the screen-rec of the search) — demonstrating whether the primary source the text claimed to reference actually exists. The audit predicted a confidence issue; the search confirms or refutes it.
- Teardown angle: The digital world is built on what you already know — and what you already know is that every text has an author, an audience, a purpose, and a context. The audit applies that knowledge to a new terrain where the confidence language is optimized to bypass it.
- Exclusions: Full media literacy curriculum; algorithmic amplification and filter bubbles; source bias ratings (Media Bias/Fact Check); the political dimensions of online misinformation.
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 07 — Build the Language Identity Mirror with Claude (LLM Exercise, Ch 3)

- Source: writing-guide-with-llms/chapters/03-language-identity-and-culture-exploring-employing-embracing.md + LLM Exercise
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: Language is not just communication — it is identity. The way you write in academic English may feel like a different self than the way you write in the language of your community, your family, your neighborhood. Claude can hold a conversation in both registers and reflect back what it notices — but the interpretation is always yours.
- The artifact: A CLI Python interactive session (~20 lines) where Claude holds a parallel conversation: the writer describes the same event (anything — a meal, a commute, a sports moment) twice, once in formal academic register and once in the language/register of their community. Claude reflects back what it notices about the two versions — what each says about identity, what each loses, what each preserves — without judging one as better. Output: a 150-word reflection on register as identity.
- Prompt seed: `claude "I am going to describe the same event twice. Version 1: in formal academic English (the register of academic writing). Version 2: in the language or register of my community, family, or a specific social context. After both versions, reflect back what you notice: (1) what does each version prioritize? (2) what does each version lose? (3) what does the difference between the two versions say about the relationship between language and identity for this writer? Do not judge one as better than the other. Do not translate between them. Give me a 150-word reflection on register as identity. Event: [EVENT]. Version 1 (academic): [TEXT]. Version 2 (community/family register): [TEXT]."`
- Read / check: Verify Claude does not judge one register as more correct or better (it should treat both as legitimate); confirm the reflection names specific elements of each version (not generic "one is formal, one is informal"); check that the 150-word reflection is actually 150 words and constitutes a usable piece of writing the student could incorporate into a longer essay.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Both version descriptions — the writer must produce the two register versions. For the video, use a pre-written pair (same event described in academic English and in a specific regional or community register) to demonstrate the register contrast and the reflection.
- Output medium: screen-recording mp4 (screen-rec of terminal: event description and both versions pasted in, then Claude's reflection appearing — the 150 words appearing as a typed output, specific elements from both versions quoted in the reflection)
- The change: After the reflection, ask Claude: "Which register — academic or community — sounds more like who I am? And what would it cost me to always write in the other one?" This produces the identity-cost question that the ch03 discussion of code-switching centers on.
- Teardown angle: Employing, exploring, and embracing language means recognizing that the register you use is never neutral. The reflection makes the difference visible — not to resolve it, but to give the writer a name for what they are navigating when they write in academic English.
- Exclusions: Full sociolinguistics of code-switching; standard language ideology critique; multilingual writing pedagogy; the politics of academic English as a gatekeeper.

## Candidate 08 — Build the Memoir Lesson Excavator with Claude (LLM Exercise, Ch 6)

- Source: writing-guide-with-llms/chapters/06-memoir-or-personal-narrative-learning-lessons-from-the-personal.md + LLM Exercise
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: The memoir is not a report on what happened — it is an argument about what it meant. The lesson that emerges from a personal experience is not the first lesson you think of. It is the one that survives the question: "Is that actually what this experience taught, or is that what I wanted it to have taught?" Claude asks the question that makes the difference visible.
- The artifact: A CLI Python interactive session (~20 lines) where Claude interrogates a draft memoir passage (100-200 words) on 3 lesson-related questions: (1) Is the stated lesson the one the narrative actually earns — does the story contain the evidence for this lesson, or is the lesson imported from outside the experience? (2) What is the hardest version of the opposite lesson — what would someone who had the exact same experience conclude differently? (3) Is there a smaller, less comfortable lesson hiding under the stated one? Output: 3 revision prompts the writer can use to deepen the draft.
- Prompt seed: `claude "I am writing a memoir for a college writing class. A memoir requires both the story and the lesson — what the experience taught, in retrospect. Here is a draft passage of ~150 words followed by my stated lesson. Interrogate the lesson on 3 questions: (1) Does the narrative contain evidence for this lesson — does the story earn it, or is the lesson imported from somewhere else? Be specific about what evidence is there and what is missing. (2) State the hardest version of the opposite lesson — what would someone who had the identical experience conclude differently, and why is that version plausible? (3) Is there a smaller, less comfortable lesson hiding under the stated one — something the writer might prefer not to admit but that the story suggests? Give me 3 revision prompts based on your interrogation. Do not rewrite the passage. Draft: [TEXT]. My stated lesson: [LESSON]."`
- Read / check: Verify that question 1 identifies specific evidence in the draft (quotes specific sentences or moments that do or don't support the lesson); confirm question 2 produces a genuinely opposite lesson (not a weakened version of the same lesson); check that question 3 identifies something uncomfortable that is actually suggested by the draft (not invented).
- Human supplies (Claude can't): A draft memoir passage and a stated lesson — the writer must bring real material. For the video, use a synthetic 150-word draft about a sports loss or a family conflict where the stated lesson is too comfortable ("I learned resilience") and the deeper lesson is more specific and uncomfortable.
- Output medium: screen-recording mp4 (screen-rec of terminal: draft and stated lesson pasted in, then 3 interrogation questions appearing with their answers, then 3 revision prompts appearing — the uncomfortable lesson identified in question 3 highlighted)
- The change: After the 3 interrogation questions, ask Claude to produce a "scene to add" — one specific moment or piece of dialogue that, if added to the draft, would make the stated lesson more earned. The writer decides whether to add it.
- Teardown angle: The lesson is not what the experience meant. It is what the writer can argue the experience meant, supported by what is actually in the draft. The gap between the lesson you want and the lesson you earned is where the revision lives.
- Exclusions: Full memoir genre conventions (time structure, interiority, scene vs. summary); the ethics of writing about real people; literary memoir vs. academic personal essay; the literacy narrative subgenre (ch04).
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 10 — Build the Evaluation Genre Checker with Claude (LLM Exercise, Ch 9)

- Source: writing-guide-with-llms/chapters/09-evaluation-or-review-would-you-recommend-it.md + LLM Exercise
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: The evaluation requires criteria. Not "this is good" (an opinion) but "this achieves X, fails at Y, and is recommended for audience Z who values X over Y" (a judgment with explicit criteria). Most student evaluations have hidden criteria — the writer knows what they think but hasn't named the standard against which they're measuring. Claude names the standard.
- The artifact: A CLI Python script (~25 lines) that takes a draft evaluation (a restaurant review, a film review, a product review — 200-300 words) and runs a 3-step criteria audit: (1) identify the implicit criteria the writer is using — what standards are embedded in the judgment even though they're not stated? (2) check whether the criteria are appropriate for the audience and purpose — are these the criteria the target reader cares about? (3) test whether the judgment follows from the criteria — if the stated criteria are X and Y, does the recommendation actually follow? Output: a 3-criterion scorecard with a "state your criteria" revision prompt.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Audit this draft evaluation for hidden criteria. The evaluation genre requires explicit criteria — the standards against which the subject is measured. Run 3 steps: (1) CRITERIA IDENTIFICATION: list the 3-4 implicit criteria this writer is using — what standards are embedded in the judgment even though they're never explicitly stated? Quote specific sentences that reveal each criterion. (2) CRITERIA-AUDIENCE FIT: for each identified criterion, assess whether it matches the needs of the stated or implied audience — is this what the target reader cares about? (3) JUDGMENT-CRITERIA FIT: does the final recommendation actually follow from the identified criteria, or does the conclusion pull in a different direction than the criteria would support? ## Candidate 11 — Build the Image Analysis Annotation with Claude (LLM Exercise, Ch 20)

- Source: writing-guide-with-llms/chapters/20-image-analysis-what-you-see.md + LLM Exercise
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: What you see in an image is not what is in the image — it is your interpretation of what is in the image. The image analysis genre requires separating observation (what is in the frame) from interpretation (what it means). Most writers jump to interpretation before completing observation. Claude can run the separation for you.
- The artifact: A CLI Python script (~25 lines) that takes an image URL or description (text description of the image for non-multimodal CLIs) and runs a 3-step analysis: (1) observation layer — describe only what is literally in the image (no interpretation); (2) composition analysis — describe the formal choices (framing, color, light, focal point, foreground/background); (3) interpretation layer — what does the composition suggest about meaning, intention, or cultural context? Output: 3 labeled paragraphs that together constitute an image analysis structure.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Analyze the following image (or description) using a 3-layer structure that separates observation from interpretation. Layer 1 — OBSERVATION: describe only what is literally present in the image. Do not interpret — do not say 'the person looks sad'; say 'the person's eyes are directed downward and the corners of their mouth are turned down.' Layer 2 — COMPOSITION: describe the formal choices made by the photographer or designer: framing (what is included/excluded), focal point, use of light and shadow, color palette, foreground/background relationship, rule of thirds or other compositional strategies. - Teardown angle: What you see in an image is not what is in the image. The observation layer is discipline — forcing the writer to stay in the frame before they interpret what the frame means. ## Candidate 12 — Build the Public Forum Script Checker with Claude (LLM Exercise, Ch 22)

- Source: writing-guide-with-llms/chapters/22-scripting-for-the-public-forum-writing-to-speak.md + LLM Exercise
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: Writing to speak is a different genre than writing to read. The sentence that reads clearly on the page may be impossible to follow when heard aloud. The script that sounds like an essay will lose its audience by paragraph 2. Claude reads the script aloud — in its way — and identifies the sentences that will break at the seam.
- The artifact: A CLI Python script (~20 lines) that takes a public forum script (a speech, a podcast intro, a scripted YouTube explainer — 150-300 words) and runs a 4-criterion speakability audit: (1) sentence length — flags sentences over 25 words as too long to deliver in one breath; (2) syntactic complexity — flags subordinate-clause-heavy sentences that require re-reading to parse; (3) transition clarity — checks whether each transition is explicitly spoken (not implied); (4) audience address — checks whether the script directly addresses the audience at least once per 100 words. Output: per-sentence flags with a revised version of each flagged sentence.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Audit the following public forum script for speakability — the quality of being performable aloud for a live or recorded audience. Apply 4 criteria and flag problem sentences: (1) SENTENCE LENGTH: flag any sentence over 25 words (too long to deliver in one breath); (2) SYNTACTIC COMPLEXITY: flag any sentence with more than 2 subordinate clauses — give a simpler rewrite; (3) TRANSITION CLARITY: identify any transition that is implied but not spoken aloud (e.g., a paragraph break that would be signaled by pausing, but the script doesn't contain a verbal signal); (4) AUDIENCE ADDRESS: flag any 100-word block that does not contain a direct address to the audience ('you', 'we', 'consider this'). For each flagged item, provide a one-sentence rewrite. Script: [TEXT]"`
- Read / check: Verify the sentence length audit correctly counts words (not characters); confirm the subordinate clause flag identifies genuinely complex sentences (not just long ones); check that the transition clarity flag is meaningful (not every transition needs to be explicit — only those where the relationship would be unclear without a verbal signal); verify the rewrites are shorter and simpler, not just paraphrases.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): A real speech or script to audit — human must supply. For the video, use a synthetic 200-word scripted explainer that deliberately includes 2 sentences over 25 words, 1 complex clause structure, 1 missing transition, and 1 audience-free 100-word block.
- Output medium: screen-recording mp4 (screen-rec of terminal: script pasted in, then per-sentence flags appearing with problem sentences highlighted — flagged items in red with the rewrite suggestions below each, the overall speakability score appearing at the end)
- The change: After the speakability audit, take the flagged sentences and their rewrites and ask Claude: "Read the original version aloud (approximately) and the rewrite aloud. Which sounds more like a real person speaking? What specific word or phrase in the rewrite makes the difference?" This produces a principle about oral prose that transcends the specific example.
- Teardown angle: Writing to read is not writing to speak. The seam is the sentence that makes sense on the page and loses the audience at the second subordinate clause. The speakability audit finds the seam before the microphone does.
- Exclusions: Full public speaking technique (delivery, presence, eye contact); podcast production; scripting for video specifically vs. live speech; the rhetorical analysis of a speech (ch11 covers this).
