## Candidate 01 — Build a Volatility Drag Visualizer with Claude Code
- Source: mba-computational-finance/chapters/02-returns-and-risk-measurement.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: A stock goes up 50% one year, then down 50% the next. Most people say you break even. You don't — you're down 25%. Volatility itself costs you money, even when your average return is zero. This card makes that invisible cost visible.
- The artifact: A Python script that simulates two portfolios over 20 periods — one with stable returns (arithmetic mean R), one with a volatile return path (same arithmetic mean, higher variance) — computes the geometric mean for each, and animates both wealth paths as growing curves on the same axes; the gap between them at period 20 is the volatility drag, labeled explicitly. Output: a Manim animation of both wealth curves diverging over time, with the drag gap labeled in red at the final period.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Write a Python script that compares two portfolios over 20 periods. Portfolio A: constant return of 10% per period. Portfolio B: alternating returns of +50% and -30% (same arithmetic mean as A: (0.50 + (-0.30))/2 = 10%, but higher variance). For each portfolio: compute wealth at each period starting from $100, compute the geometric mean return, compute the total wealth at period 20. Plot both wealth paths on the same axes with matplotlib, label the gap at period 20 as 'Volatility Drag = $X'. Add a third panel showing the formula: geometric_mean ≈ arithmetic_mean - (sigma^2)/2."`
- Read / check: Verify Portfolio A: wealth at period 20 = $100 × (1.10)^20 ≈ $672.75; verify Portfolio B: wealth alternates (1.50, 0.70, 1.50, 0.70...) — after 20 periods = $100 × (1.50 × 0.70)^10 = $100 × (1.05)^10 ≈ $162.9 — confirming substantial drag; check that the geometric mean approximation formula is stated correctly (geo ≈ arith - σ²/2); confirm the drag is labeled as the dollar difference between A and B at period 20.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Nothing — fully synthetic. The simulation is entirely algorithmic. No real market data required.
- Output medium: Manim (two wealth curves animating simultaneously period by period; Portfolio A in blue, Portfolio B in orange; vertical red arrow at period 20 labeled "Volatility Drag = $X"; the approximation formula appearing below the chart)
- The change: Add a third portfolio — Portfolio C with ±70% swings and the same 10% arithmetic mean — and show how the drag scales with variance. Animate all three curves and ask Claude to compute the drag for each using the approximation formula, then compare to the simulated result.
- Teardown angle: The arithmetic mean is the right number to report to a human asking "what was the average return?" The geometric mean is the right number for anyone asking "what did I actually end up with?" High-volatility assets must earn a higher arithmetic mean just to break even on a compounded basis — which is why volatility is a real cost, not just an accounting choice.
- Exclusions: Options pricing from volatility (Black-Scholes — different chapter); implied volatility vs. realized volatility; GARCH models; high-frequency intraday volatility patterns.
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 02 — Build a Portfolio Diversification Simulator with Claude Code
- Source: mba-computational-finance/chapters/08-the-diversification-miracle.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: A parent who has worked at the same company for 31 years asks: how does owning more things I don't know about make me safer? The math gives a specific answer. At correlation = +1, no diversification benefit whatsoever. At correlation = 0, risk drops by 30% just by splitting the bet in two. This card runs the numbers live.
- The artifact: A Python portfolio risk simulator: inputs are two assets with individual volatilities (σ_A, σ_B) and weights (w_A, w_B), plus a user-supplied correlation (ρ); outputs are portfolio variance, portfolio standard deviation, and a plot of portfolio σ as a function of ρ sweeping from -1 to +1; animated as a Manim curve sweep showing how portfolio risk changes as correlation moves from perfect negative (-1) to perfect positive (+1), with the current ρ marked as a moving dot.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Write a Python portfolio risk calculator. Function signature: portfolio_risk(sigma_a, sigma_b, w_a, w_b, rho). Formula: portfolio_var = w_a^2 * sigma_a^2 + w_b^2 * sigma_b^2 + 2 * w_a * w_b * rho * sigma_a * sigma_b. Plot portfolio_sigma vs. rho for rho in [-1, 1] with 200 points. Mark three points: rho=-1 (minimum risk), rho=0 (independent), rho=+1 (no benefit). Test with: sigma_a=0.20, sigma_b=0.30, w_a=0.60, w_b=0.40. Output: numerical results at the three marked rho values, plus the plot."`
- Read / check: Verify at ρ=+1: portfolio_σ = w_a×σ_a + w_b×σ_b = 0.60×0.20 + 0.40×0.30 = 0.12 + 0.12 = 0.24 (weighted average, no benefit); verify at ρ=0: portfolio_σ = √(0.36×0.04 + 0.16×0.09) = √(0.0144 + 0.0144) = √0.0288 ≈ 0.1697 (meaningful reduction below 0.24); verify at ρ=-1: portfolio_σ reaches its minimum (may reach 0 depending on weights and σ values); confirm the plot is a smooth concave-downward curve.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Nothing — fully synthetic. The simulation is entirely algorithmic. For the revision beat, the viewer can substitute real stock correlation data from Yahoo Finance — state this in the NEXT STEPS beat.
- Output medium: Manim (smooth curve sweeping across the rho axis; a moving dot animates from ρ=-1 to ρ=+1; the portfolio σ value updates as the dot moves; three landmark values labeled with callout annotations)
- The change: Extend to a 10-asset equal-weight portfolio: show how portfolio σ falls as assets are added (from n=1 to n=10), assuming all pairwise correlations = 0. Plot risk vs. n and mark the "diversification plateau" — the point at which adding more uncorrelated assets produces diminishing returns.
- Teardown angle: The variance of the average is less than the average of the variances — and this one sentence restructures every investment decision. A high-volatility stock that is uncorrelated with everything else in a portfolio is less dangerous than a low-volatility stock that moves perfectly with everything you already own. Standalone volatility is the wrong metric. Portfolio contribution to variance is the right one.
- Exclusions: Efficient frontier optimization (next chapter topic — separate card); options as a hedging instrument; correlation breakdown during market crises; Markowitz mean-variance optimization full solution.
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 03 — Build a CAPM Beta Estimator with Claude Code
- Source: mba-computational-finance/chapters/11-asset-pricing-models.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: NVDA returned 240% last year. The S&P 500 returned 24%. Was NVDA exceptional, or just lucky? To answer that, you need a baseline — and the baseline is the CAPM expected return, which requires estimating beta from historical data. This card runs the regression live.
- The artifact: A Python CAPM beta estimator: fetches or accepts monthly returns for a stock and a market index, runs an OLS regression (stock returns on market returns), outputs the slope (beta), intercept (alpha), R², the security market line (SML) as a formula, and the CAPM expected return given a risk-free rate and equity risk premium; plotted as a scatter plot of actual monthly returns vs. market returns with the regression line drawn through them.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Write a Python CAPM beta estimator. Input: two lists of monthly returns — stock_returns and market_returns (same length, at least 36 months). Use numpy to run OLS regression: beta = Cov(stock, market)/Var(market), alpha = mean(stock) - beta × mean(market). Output: beta, alpha, R^2, the CAPM expected return formula E(R) = Rf + beta × ERP where Rf=4.2% and ERP=6.0%, and the numerical expected return. Plot scatter of market_returns (x) vs. stock_returns (y) with the regression line. Generate synthetic test data: market returns drawn from Normal(0.008, 0.04), stock returns = 1.5 × market_returns + Normal(0, 0.06) — this should produce beta ≈ 1.5 and R^2 ≈ 0.6."`
- Read / check: Verify that the OLS formula is implemented correctly — beta = Cov(R_stock, R_market) / Var(R_market); confirm the expected return formula is E(R) = 0.042 + beta × 0.06; verify that synthetic data with true beta=1.5 produces an estimated beta between 1.3 and 1.7 (sampling variation); confirm R² ≈ 0.6 for this DGP; check the scatter plot shows the regression line and axis labels (market return, stock return).
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Real historical stock and market return data. For the video, synthetic data is used. For the NEXT STEPS beat, direct viewers to Yahoo Finance (yfinance Python library) or FRED to pull real monthly returns. State explicitly that real beta estimates should use 36–60 months of monthly data.
- Output medium: Manim (scatter plot appearing point by point; regression line drawing across the scatter; beta value appearing as an annotation on the line; CAPM expected return formula appearing below the plot)
- The change: Run the estimator with three different lookback windows (24 months, 36 months, 60 months) on the same synthetic data and show how beta estimates differ. Annotate the range — "beta instability" — and ask Claude why this matters for WACC calculations.
- Teardown angle: Beta is not a fact — it is an estimate with a standard error. The same company can produce betas ranging from 1.1 to 1.8 depending on the window length and market proxy chosen, and neither estimate is "wrong." The lesson is not to trust any single beta figure — it is to understand the assumptions baked into the number someone else computed.
- Exclusions: Fama-French three-factor model (interesting but adds complexity that obscures the CAPM lesson); rolling beta over time; implied beta from options markets; Damodaran's industry beta approach.
- Score: 9/10

The "unlimited upside, limited downside" shape of a long call is the visual signature of a specific asymmetric risk trade. ## Candidate 05 — Build a Time Value of Money Compounding Engine with Claude Code
- Source: mba-computational-finance/chapters/05-time-value-of-money-and-discounted-cash-flows.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: If you invest $10,000 today at 7% annually, how much will you have in 40 years? Most people guess too low by a factor of three. The answer is $149,744. Compounding is the one math result that genuinely surprises every person who runs it for the first time.
- The artifact: A Python TVM engine: computes future value, present value, and internal rate of return for an arbitrary cash flow stream; also runs a "Rule of 72" comparison (approximate doubling time vs. exact); plotted as a Manim animated bar chart of wealth at each period, with the compound growth curve drawn on top.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Write a Python TVM engine with four functions: (1) future_value(pv, rate, n) = pv × (1+rate)^n. (2) present_value(fv, rate, n) = fv / (1+rate)^n. (3) npv(cashflows, rate) — list of cash flows at t=0,1,...,n. (4) irr(cashflows) — use numpy.irr or scipy to solve for the rate that makes NPV=0. Also: rule_of_72(rate) = 72/rate (years to double). Test: PV=$10,000, rate=7%, n=40. Output: FV, NPV of the same cash flow stream at different discount rates (5%, 7%, 10%), and the Rule of 72 doubling time vs. exact doubling time. Plot wealth over 40 years."`
- Read / check: Verify FV at 7%, 40 years: $10,000 × (1.07)^40 = $10,000 × 14.974 ≈ $149,745; verify Rule of 72: 72/7 = 10.3 years to double vs. exact doubling time ln(2)/ln(1.07) ≈ 10.24 years (Rule of 72 is an excellent approximation); verify the NPV function correctly discounts each cash flow at its period-specific discount factor; confirm the IRR function produces a rate that makes NPV=0 for a test cash flow series.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Nothing — fully synthetic. TVM is entirely analytical.
- Output medium: Manim (bar chart of wealth growing period by period over 40 years; compound growth curve drawn on top of the bars; the final bar labeled with the exact future value; Rule of 72 doubling markers appearing as horizontal dashed lines)
- The change: Run the same engine comparing three scenarios: (1) invest $10K today at 7% for 40 years; (2) invest $10K ten years from now at 7% for 30 years; (3) invest $10K per year for 40 years at 7%. Show the three FV values and animate the difference — the "cost of waiting" visual.
- Teardown angle: The compounding curve is exponential — it starts flat and ends steep. This is why the last ten years of a 40-year investment contribute more growth than the first twenty. Waiting ten years to start does not lose a quarter of the benefit; it loses more than half. The math is counterintuitive until you run it.
- Exclusions: Bond duration and convexity (separate chapter); mortgage amortization (different application); real vs. nominal rates (inflation adjustment — different card); perpetuity and annuity formulas.
- Score: 8/10

- Teardown angle: Short selling has a mathematical asymmetry that most investors understand abstractly and very few understand viscerally. A long position's maximum loss is 100% of investment. A short position's maximum loss is unlimited — the price can theoretically rise forever. ## Candidate 07 — Build a Bond Duration and Price Sensitivity Calculator with Claude Code
- Source: mba-computational-finance/chapters/03-equity-and-fixed-income.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: Interest rates rise 1%. A 10-year bond falls 8%. A 2-year bond falls 2%. Duration tells you exactly how sensitive a bond is to rate moves — and most bond investors can't compute it. This card does.
- The artifact: A Python bond duration calculator: inputs are coupon rate, face value, maturity, and yield-to-maturity; computes Macaulay duration and modified duration; outputs a price-vs-yield curve and a price-change prediction for a 1% rate move (using modified duration as the first-order approximation); animated as a Manim price-vs-yield curve with the current yield marked and the 1% shift showing the predicted vs. actual price change.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Write a Python bond duration calculator. Inputs: face=1000, coupon_rate (annual), maturity (years), ytm (yield-to-maturity). Compute: (1) bond price = PV of coupons + PV of face; (2) Macaulay duration = sum(t × PV(CF_t)) / price; (3) modified duration = Macaulay duration / (1 + ytm/2) for semi-annual; (4) price change estimate for +1% rate move = -modified_duration × 0.01 × price. Plot price vs. yield (yield from 1% to 15%). Test: face=1000, coupon=5%, maturity=10 years, ytm=5%."`
- Read / check: Verify bond price at coupon=5%, ytm=5%, 10 years = $1,000 (par bond — coupon equals yield, so price equals face); verify Macaulay duration for a 10-year 5% par bond ≈ 7.72 years; verify modified duration ≈ 7.72/(1.025) ≈ 7.53; verify predicted price change for +1% = -7.53% × $1,000 ≈ -$75.3; confirm the price-vs-yield curve is convex (bowed toward the origin) — this is convexity, and the approximation slightly underestimates the actual price fall for large rate moves.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Nothing — fully synthetic. Bond pricing is entirely analytical.
- Output medium: Manim (price-vs-yield curve drawing from left to right; current yield marked as a dot; the 1% shift shown as a horizontal arrow to the right; the predicted price change appearing as a vertical drop with the approximation labeled)
- The change: Compare duration for three bonds — 2-year, 10-year, and 30-year — all with the same 5% coupon, and show how duration scales with maturity. Then simulate a 2% rate rise and show the price impact on each — making the argument for why long-duration bonds are "interest rate risk" in a rising-rate environment.
- Teardown angle: Modified duration is the first-order approximation to price sensitivity. Convexity is the second-order correction — bonds fall less than duration predicts when rates rise (and gain more than duration predicts when rates fall). The approximation is useful; knowing its limits is what separates a sophisticated bond investor from a naive one.
- Exclusions: Credit risk and bond ratings (different risk dimension); yield spread analysis; Treasury vs. corporate bond pricing; TIPS inflation linkage.

## Candidate 09 — Build a Capital Allocation Efficient Frontier Plotter with Claude Code
- Source: mba-computational-finance/chapters/10-capital-allocation-and-diversification.md + chapters/09-portfolio-construction.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: Every possible combination of two risky assets traces a curve in return-vs-risk space. The upper portion of that curve is the efficient frontier — the set of portfolios where you can't get more return without taking more risk. Above the curve is impossible. Below it is wasteful. This card plots the curve.
- The artifact: A Python efficient frontier plotter for two risky assets: inputs are expected returns, standard deviations, and correlation; sweeps all possible weight combinations (w_A from 0 to 1, w_B = 1 - w_A), computes portfolio return and portfolio σ for each combination, plots the opportunity set; identifies the minimum-variance portfolio (MVP) and the tangency portfolio (highest Sharpe ratio) given a risk-free rate; animated as a Manim sweep of the frontier curve appearing point by point.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Write a Python two-asset efficient frontier plotter. Inputs: mu_a=0.12, sigma_a=0.20, mu_b=0.08, sigma_b=0.12, rho=-0.30, rf=0.04. Sweep w_a from 0 to 1 in 100 steps. For each: portfolio_return = w_a×mu_a + (1-w_a)×mu_b; portfolio_var = w_a^2×sigma_a^2 + (1-w_a)^2×sigma_b^2 + 2×w_a×(1-w_a)×rho×sigma_a×sigma_b; portfolio_sigma = sqrt(portfolio_var). Identify: minimum-variance portfolio (MVP) = weight with lowest sigma; tangency portfolio = weight with highest Sharpe ratio = (return-rf)/sigma. Plot all 100 (sigma, return) pairs as a curve, mark MVP and tangency portfolio with distinct markers."`
- Read / check: Verify that at ρ=-0.30, the frontier curves back to the left before the MVP (this is the signature of negative correlation — the "banana" shape); confirm the MVP produces the lowest sigma along the sweep; confirm the tangency portfolio has the highest (return-rf)/sigma; verify that at w_a=0, the point is (sigma_b=0.12, return_b=0.08) and at w_a=1, the point is (sigma_a=0.20, return_a=0.12).
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Nothing — fully synthetic. For the NEXT STEPS beat, direct viewers to Yahoo Finance or FRED to pull real asset return estimates and correlations.
- Output medium: Manim (frontier curve sweeping point by point from the bottom-right; MVP appearing as a gold dot with a label; tangency portfolio appearing as a blue dot; the capital market line (from risk-free rate through tangency) appearing as a dashed line extending to the upper right)
- The change: Add a third asset and show how the efficient frontier shifts — the new frontier dominates (lies above) the two-asset frontier at every level of risk. Ask Claude to run a 3-asset optimization using scipy.optimize.minimize to find the true minimum-variance frontier, and compare it to the naive two-asset result.
- Teardown angle: The efficient frontier is not a description of what investors do — it is a description of what rational investors with correct beliefs should do. Most real portfolios sit inside the frontier (wasteful) or below the capital market line (not taking enough equity risk for their return target). The diagram is a judgment about your current portfolio, not just a theory.
- Exclusions: Black-Litterman model; full Markowitz optimization for N assets; factor models (Fama-French); rebalancing mechanics and transaction costs.
- Score: 8/10
