# Chapter 1 — Introduction to Finance

**Suggested titles**
1. What Finance Actually Studies
2. The Trade-off That Runs Underneath Everything
3. Money, Risk, and the Three Decisions Every Manager Makes

**TL;DR.** Finance is the study of how cash moves between people and firms over time, and how to make consistently good decisions about that movement when the future is uncertain. Underneath every topic in this book is one trade-off — taking on more risk should pay you more on average, and figuring out *how much more* is what the field is for.

## The puzzle of Bacon Signs

In 1978, a regional sign company in the Midwest had a problem most family businesses would consider a miracle. Bacon Signs, founded in 1901, had spent eight decades doing solid, unspectacular work — manufacturing and installing commercial signage for clients in its region. Then the owner noticed something. The custom signs his shop was building were better than the signs his shop was *installing* on behalf of national franchise companies. Different machinery, different craftsmen, different result. He could see it. The national companies, presumably, could see it too.

So the question was straightforward: could Bacon Signs sell that quality difference to the larger national sign companies, by manufacturing their midsize orders for them on subcontract? The companies said yes. Bacon promised on-time and under-budget delivery. Reputation grew. Demand grew faster than reputation.

And here is where the chapter begins, because the next sentence is the one most accounts of "successful family business" leave out. **Demand exceeded what Bacon's existing capital could finance.** Skilled welders, neon-bending equipment, bigger work bays, longer accounts-receivable cycles when the national companies took ninety days to pay their invoices — these things had to be paid for *before* the cash from the national companies came back through the door. A firm that cannot pre-fund its growth cannot accept its growth.

Bacon Signs got past that constraint because the owner, the banker, and the firm's vice president of finance built a financing plan in advance. The plan named when capital would be needed, how much, what kind, and from whom. The bank could see the plan. The owner knew which lever to pull when. Growth, when it arrived, did not embarrass the company.[^1]

That is what this chapter — and most of this book — is about. Not signs. Not the Midwest. The general thing. **Finance is the study of how cash moves between people and firms over time, and how to make consistently good and repeatable decisions about that movement when the future is uncertain.** Bacon Signs would have been a good business with no financial planning at all. With financial planning, it became a much bigger good business. Same shop, same craftsmen, same signs. Different financial machinery underneath.

[^1]: Dun & Bradstreet, "Bacon Signs, Inc.", D&B Business Directory.

## Concept 1 — What "finance" actually means (three jobs in one word)

Open any introductory finance text and the first definition will sound something like *finance is the study of the management, movement, and raising of money*. This is true. It is also unhelpful, because the sentence does not tell you that the word "finance" is doing at least three different jobs at once. A student who learns the definition without learning the split will be confused for the next thirteen weeks.

Here are the three jobs.

**1. Business finance** — what a manager inside a firm does with the firm's money. Should we buy the new machine? Borrow the money or sell stock? Extend credit to this customer or insist on cash up front? Pay a dividend or reinvest? These are decisions made by people whose paycheck comes from the firm and whose job description includes the phrase "maximize the value of the firm." The field assumes those people work for the shareholders. We will examine that assumption — it is doing more work than a single sentence can carry — in Chapter 2.

**2. Investments** — what a person or institution does with money they already have, when they want to grow it. Buying stocks, buying bonds, building portfolios, choosing between a 401(k) and an IRA, deciding whether to put money in a money market fund or in real estate. Glamour-adjacent — *Billions*, *Wall Street*, the breathless cable-news ticker — but most of the actual work is patient analysis of cash flows under uncertainty.

**3. Financial markets and institutions** — the firms and regulators that let the first two jobs happen. The New York Stock Exchange and the NASDAQ are markets. The Securities and Exchange Commission is a regulator. JPMorgan Chase is an institution that does several jobs at once — taking deposits, making loans, underwriting securities, managing money. Without this third layer, a Bacon Signs cannot meet its banker, an investor cannot buy a share of stock, and a Treasury cannot fund itself.

The three areas overlap. A bank earns its keep in area three but makes business-finance decisions about its own balance sheet in area one and offers investment products to its customers in area two. A retiree picking a bond fund touches all three at once. The split is not a wall — it is a way of telling which kind of question you are asking.

### Specification, before we go further

Three terms in the sentences above are doing more work than they look like they are doing. We pull them apart now, because every chapter that follows assumes the work is done.

**Money.** In day-to-day conversation, "money" means cash. In finance, "money" usually means *purchasing power transferred over time* — the ability to claim resources now or later. A dollar in your hand is money. A government bond promising you a dollar a year from now is also money in this sense — same purchasing power, different timing, with risk attached. Most of this book is about translating between the two.

**Risk.** Not danger. Not even bad outcomes. **Uncertainty about the size and timing of future cash flows, including outcomes better than expected as well as worse.** A stock with returns ranging from –30% to +50% in a year is *more* risky than a Treasury bill with returns of exactly 2% — because the uncertainty is wider, in both directions. The discipline of finance, when it is doing its job, makes risk a *measurable thing*, not a vibe.

**Value.** At least three meanings, depending on which kind of finance person is talking.
- **Book value** — what the accountants record on the balance sheet, based on historical cost adjusted for things like depreciation. Backward-looking.
- **Market value** — what the asset trades for right now in a market. Forward-looking, because the price reflects what other people expect.
- **Economic value** — what someone would actually be willing to pay if you cornered them honestly. Often higher than market value, because markets aggregate willingness-to-pay and most buyers get a slight discount on what they would have agreed to.

When a financial argument seems contradictory, check first whether two different "values" are getting mashed together in the same sentence. The contradiction usually dissolves.

### The three decisions inside business finance

Inside the first job — business finance — a manager (often a Chief Financial Officer, with a Vice President of Finance, a treasurer, and a controller working under them) makes three kinds of decisions, and only three.

**Working capital management.** Short-term assets and short-term liabilities. Should we extend net-30 credit to this customer or net-60? When a supplier offers us a discount for paying within ten days, do we take it? How much cash should we keep on hand versus invested? The accounting department implements; the finance department sets the policy.

**Capital budgeting.** Long-term assets. Should we acquire the new factory? Replace the truck fleet? Launch the new product line? These are the high-stakes decisions, because the investments are large and reversal is expensive. We will spend Chapter 16 entirely on the question *what is this long-term project actually worth*.

**Capital structure.** Long-term financing — the right side of the balance sheet, below the working-capital line. Debt or equity? Issue bonds or sell stock? Reinvest earnings or pay them out? The choice changes who owns the firm, who has a claim on its cash flows, and how much of the firm's risk gets absorbed by which class of investor. Chapter 17 lives here.

The cleanest way to remember this: *working capital lives in the top of the balance sheet, capital budgeting lives on the left side under long-term assets, and capital structure lives on the right side under long-term financing.* Three decisions, three regions. Every job inside a corporate finance function eventually maps to one of them.

### The trade-off (concept 1)

All three decisions trade **liquidity for return**. Holding more cash is safer and more flexible — you can pay any bill that lands on the desk tomorrow — but cash earns nothing, or close to it. Investing the same money in a long-term project earns more on average but locks it up. Taking on debt amplifies returns when things go well and amplifies losses when they don't. There is no decision in business finance that does not look, when you squint at it, like a trade between *being able to react quickly* and *earning more over time*.

### Worked example — Bacon Signs in three decisions

Pull the case apart with the three buckets.

- **Working capital.** When Bacon Signs accepted ninety-day payment terms from national franchise companies, it tied up cash in receivables for three months at a time. The firm needed a line of credit big enough to fund payroll and materials during that gap. *Working-capital management.*
- **Capital budgeting.** Adding a second work bay and the additional welding equipment was a long-term asset purchase. The owner had to estimate the cash flows the new capacity would produce, compared with the cost of the equipment, before committing. *Capital budgeting.*
- **Capital structure.** The financing plan presented to the bank was a mix of short-term credit (working capital) and longer-term loans (capital structure). It also implicitly chose *not* to bring in outside equity investors — keeping the family's ownership intact at the cost of higher leverage. *Capital structure.*

The same firm, the same growth, three different finance decisions, each in a different region of the balance sheet.

### Common misconceptions

- *"Finance is mostly about investing."* No. Investing (area 2) is the visible part — it has the TV shows. Business finance (area 1) is what most graduates with finance degrees actually do, most days, for most of their careers.
- *"The CFO does everything."* The CFO sets policy and signs off on big decisions. The treasurer raises and manages cash. The controller reports what happened. A small firm collapses these into one or two people; a large firm spreads them across hundreds.
- *"Finance and accounting are the same thing."* Accounting tracks and reports what already happened. Finance plans what to do next. They share data and often share offices. They are different jobs.

## Concept 2 — Risk and expected return (the trade-off underneath everything)

Here is the sentence the entire field of finance is built on, and we are going to take it apart slowly because it is constantly misquoted.

> Investments with greater risk should, *on average and over time*, deliver greater expected returns.

Notice the words that get dropped when this sentence is repeated.

- **"On average."** A risky investment can lose money in any given year. The claim is about long-run averages across many investments or many years, not about any one outcome.
- **"Over time."** Risk premiums need decades to show up reliably. Five years is not enough time to "prove" that stocks beat bonds, even though across nearly a century they have.
- **"Should."** This is the prediction the theory makes. It is *not* always true. Asset prices can wander away from the trade-off for long stretches. The claim is what *should* happen if investors are pricing assets rationally and getting paid for the risk they take. When the data and the claim disagree, finance treats that as a puzzle to investigate, not as a sign the claim is wrong.

### Why the trade-off has to exist

Suppose, hypothetically, that two investments offered identical expected returns — say, 6% per year on average — but one had wildly variable outcomes (sometimes –20%, sometimes +35%) and the other was a steady 6% every year. Which would you choose?

Almost everyone picks the steady one. **Risk-averse** is the technical term for this preference, and it is the empirical fact that makes the trade-off work. If risk-averse investors won't take a risky bet for the same expected return as a safe one, then risky investments have to *offer more* expected return to attract any buyers at all. The size of that "more" — the **risk premium** — is what assets compete on.

That is why the trade-off exists. Not because the universe rewards bravery. Because the price of risky things has to fall until risk-averse investors are willing to hold them, and a lower price means a higher expected return going forward.

### What risk is made of

The word "risk" hides several different sources of uncertainty. The split matters because some types of risk can be reduced by holding many investments together, and some cannot.

**Default risk** — the issuer fails to make the promised payment. A corporate bond can default. A homeowner can stop paying the mortgage. A startup can go to zero. The probability of default is part of why two bonds of identical maturity can offer different yields.

**Inflation risk** — your cash flows arrive on schedule, but their purchasing power has shrunk. Lock yourself into a 3% bond when inflation runs at 5%, and you are losing real wealth even though no one defaulted. Chapter 3 will spend serious time here.

**Diversifiable (unsystematic) risk** — the part of an individual stock's variability that is specific to that company. A new CEO, a botched product launch, a lawsuit. Hold one stock and you bear it all. Hold a hundred stocks and the company-specific shocks largely cancel out — the good ones and the bad ones average toward zero across the portfolio.

**Non-diversifiable (systematic) risk** — the part of variability that *cannot* be diversified away because it affects the whole market. Recessions, financial crises, broad shifts in interest rates. The standard measure for an individual stock's exposure to systematic risk is **beta**, which we will build from scratch in Chapter 14. The crucial point for now: diversifiable risk should not be priced (you can avoid it for free, by holding a portfolio), but systematic risk should be — investors deserve compensation for risk they cannot escape.

**Political risk** — the rules of the game change. Tax policy, regulation, expropriation, zoning. Hard to model with the same equations as the others; named separately because it can dominate everything else in some regions and industries.

### Worked example — what does a 6% expected return *mean*?

Suppose you have $10,000 to invest for thirty years, and you have two choices.

- **Option A** — A US Treasury inflation-protected bond yielding 2% real, guaranteed by the federal government.
- **Option B** — A diversified equity index fund with an expected real return of 6% per year, but with a standard deviation of returns around 17%.

Run the arithmetic on Option A. After thirty years, your $10,000 grows to:

$$10{,}000 \times (1.02)^{30} \approx \$18{,}114$$

Predictable, modest, more than doubling but only just.

Run the central case for Option B. With an average real return of 6%:

$$10{,}000 \times (1.06)^{30} \approx \$57{,}435$$

Three times Option A. That is the risk premium, *if it shows up.* But Option B's standard deviation tells you it might not show up cleanly. In any given year, Option B can be down 25% or up 40%; over a thirty-year period, you can land anywhere from roughly $25,000 to $130,000 with 80% probability — assuming returns behave the way the historical data suggest.

The trade-off is real, but the trade-off is *probabilistic*. You give up the certainty of $18,114 to get an *expected value* of $57,435 with a wide range of possible outcomes. Whether that's a good trade depends on your time horizon, your other resources, and your tolerance for variance. Finance gives you tools to make the trade quantitatively. It does not make the trade for you.

### Common misconceptions

- *"Higher risk means higher return."* No — higher *expected* return, on average, over time. Any given high-risk investment can lose money badly. The trade-off is a long-run property of the asset class, not a guarantee about your personal portfolio next year.
- *"Risk-free means risk-free."* US Treasury bills are called "risk-free" in finance models, meaning default-risk-free in nominal terms. They still carry inflation risk, reinvestment risk, and interest-rate risk for longer maturities. The label is shorthand, not the whole story.
- *"Diversification eliminates risk."* It eliminates the *diversifiable* part. Systematic risk remains by construction.

## Concept 3 — Where finance happens (markets, instruments, players)

The first two concepts told you what finance studies and what trade-off it is built on. The third concept tells you where finance physically happens — the markets, the instruments traded in them, and the players who facilitate the trades. Without this layer, a CFO's plan and an investor's portfolio cannot meet.

### Primary versus secondary markets

The cleanest distinction. **Primary market** transactions are sales of *new* securities — the issuing firm or government receives the cash. **Secondary market** transactions are sales of *previously issued* securities between investors — the issuer is no longer involved.

The car-market analogy works as far as it goes. New cars at a dealership: primary market. A 2018 Honda Civic on Craigslist: secondary market. The crucial thing the analogy does *not* capture: secondary-market trading prices feed information back into the primary market by signaling what investors think the underlying assets are worth, which changes the prices at which firms can issue new securities. Trading a share of Amazon between two investors does not send cash to Amazon, but it does change the price at which Amazon could issue more stock tomorrow. Markets are information machines as much as they are exchange machines.

When a firm goes public for the first time, that is an **initial public offering (IPO)** — primary market, large, well-publicized. When a public firm issues additional shares later, that is a **seasoned equity offering (SEO)** — also primary, usually less dramatic. Every trade after the initial transaction is secondary.

The Treasury Department runs primary auctions of new T-bills, T-notes, and T-bonds nearly every week. As of recent data, those auctions raise hundreds of billions of dollars annually, refinancing maturing debt and funding the federal government's ongoing borrowing needs.[^2]

### Money markets and capital markets

Maturity-based split.

**Money markets** trade short-term, low-risk, highly liquid debt instruments — maturities of one year or less, often much shorter. The instruments include:
- **Treasury bills (T-bills)** — short-term federal debt, default-risk-free in nominal terms, exempt from state and local income tax. Auctioned weekly.
- **Commercial paper** — short-term unsecured debt issued by large corporations to fund inventory and receivables. Maturities of 1–270 days. Default rates are normally tiny but spiked into double digits during the 2008 financial crisis.
- **Negotiable certificates of deposit (NCDs, "jumbo CDs")** — large CDs (usually $1 million minimum) issued by banks, redeemable only at maturity but tradeable in a secondary market.
- **Federal funds** — overnight loans of reserves between banks. The Federal Reserve targets the *federal funds rate* as one of its primary monetary-policy tools; many other rates in the economy float off it.

**Capital markets** trade longer-term instruments — bonds and stocks — where maturities run from years to decades (and stocks have no maturity at all). Default risk varies more, liquidity is less uniform, instruments are less homogeneous. You will spend Chapters 10 and 11 living in this market.

### Players — broker, dealer, intermediary

Three different things that look similar at a glance.

**A broker** brings buyers and sellers together and earns a commission on the transaction. The broker does not own the security; they facilitate. Discount brokers (Robinhood, E*Trade, Fidelity's basic platform) execute trades cheaply and offer little advice. Full-service brokers (Morgan Stanley, the wealth-management arms of major banks) charge more and bundle in advice, planning, portfolio management.

**A dealer** owns inventory of the security and stands ready to buy from you at a *bid* price or sell to you at an *ask* price. The dealer earns the **spread** — the difference between bid and ask — multiplied by trading volume. Dealers "make markets" — their willingness to quote both sides at all times is what makes a security liquid. The SEC regulates dealer behavior because the asymmetric position (dealer has inventory and information; you have neither) creates room for abuse.

**A financial intermediary** transforms one kind of financial claim into another. A commercial bank takes your deposits (one kind of claim — short-term, insured, liquid) and uses them to make mortgage loans (a different kind of claim — long-term, secured, illiquid). A mutual fund takes your money (a claim against the fund) and buys a portfolio of stocks and bonds (claims against the underlying companies). The intermediary is *not* just facilitating someone else's trade; it is creating a new financial product.

The distinction matters. If a borrower defaults on a mortgage held by your bank, the bank absorbs the loss; your deposit is unaffected (and is insured by the FDIC up to applicable limits). If you bought a mortgage-backed security directly, you would absorb the loss yourself. Intermediation moves risk around, and where it is moved to is one of the most important questions in finance.

### Worked example — buying a share of Apple

Trace what actually happens when you tap "Buy 1 share of AAPL" on a brokerage app.

1. Your brokerage receives the order. The brokerage is acting as your **broker** — it does not own the share you are about to buy. (It might also be a dealer for some securities, but for this trade assume pure broker behavior.)
2. The broker routes your order to an exchange (NASDAQ, in Apple's case) or to a market-making firm.
3. A market-making **dealer** holds inventory of AAPL shares and quotes a bid (say, $189.50) and an ask (say, $189.52). You buy at the ask. The dealer earns the $0.02 spread.
4. The exchange records the trade. The clearinghouse settles it (delivering the share to your account, the cash to the dealer's). This is **secondary market** activity — Apple receives nothing.
5. Two days later, the share appears in your brokerage account. Apple's outstanding share count is unchanged. Your brokerage's records, the dealer's inventory, and the clearinghouse's books have all updated. The market price of AAPL might have nudged by a fraction of a cent based on your order being part of the day's order flow.

If, instead, you had bought a share at Apple's original 1980 IPO from the issuing syndicate, the cash would have flowed to Apple itself — primary market. Today's trade flows the cash from you to whoever sold the share (via the dealer), and gives Apple no new cash, but contributes a tiny piece of price-discovery information to the market.

### The trade-off (concept 3)

Markets trade **liquidity for cost** in a different way than firms do. A more liquid market — many buyers, many sellers, narrow bid-ask spreads — is cheaper to trade in, but the firms that make markets earn less per trade and so demand other compensation (volume, fees, sometimes advantages from order-flow information). Less liquid markets (small-company stocks, exotic bonds, real estate) have wider spreads — you pay more to enter and exit — but the underlying assets often offer higher expected returns to compensate. The *liquidity premium* is one of the more reliable patterns in financial data.

### Common misconceptions

- *"The stock market is the economy."* It is a forward-looking indicator that *includes guesses about* the economy. The stock market and the economy can move in opposite directions for long stretches.
- *"Brokers and dealers are interchangeable."* They are different jobs with different incentives and different regulatory regimes. Confusing them produces wrong answers about who profits from what.
- *"Financial intermediation just adds cost."* It also adds liquidity, credit assessment, scale, and risk transformation. Disintermediation episodes — when households bypass banks to buy securities directly — have repeatedly produced market disruptions, including the savings-and-loan turmoil of the late 1970s.

## Synthesis — finance as a way of seeing

Three concepts, one underlying shape.

**Finance is the study of trade-offs over time under uncertainty.** Every chapter that follows is an instance of this. Time value of money (Chapters 7–9) gives you the arithmetic for trading cash now against cash later. Bond and stock valuation (Chapters 10–11) applies that arithmetic to specific instruments. Statistics and regression (Chapters 13–14) build the tools for measuring uncertainty. Capital budgeting (Chapters 15–16) is the trade-off applied to long-term investment decisions. Capital structure (Chapter 17) is the trade-off applied to financing choices. Forecasting and working capital (Chapters 18–19) apply the trade-off to short-term planning. Risk management (Chapter 20) is the discipline of choosing *which* trade-offs to take and which to hedge.

Underneath each chapter is the same skeleton: cash flows happen at different times, with different probabilities, and we need a way to make those flows commensurable so we can decide. Finance is the field that decided to take that problem seriously.

The other thing to notice — because this book is *Principles of Finance with LLMs* — is that large language models like Claude are now a real part of how this work gets done. A junior financial analyst building a discounted cash-flow model in 2026 is not building it in a vacuum. They are asking Claude to sanity-check their assumptions, to draft alternative scenarios, to translate accounting language into plain English, to spot inconsistencies between sections of a 10-K. The finance is still finance. The tools are different. Chapter 00 — *Claude Basics* — is about how to use these tools well, which mostly means knowing where they help, where they hurt, and how to tell the difference. Every chapter in this book ends with one practical exercise that uses Claude on the chapter's material. None of them substitute Claude for the thinking. All of them assume you will check Claude's output against the numbers on the page.

## Chapter summary

- **Finance** is the study of how cash moves between people and firms over time, and how to make consistently good decisions about that movement when the future is uncertain.
- The field divides into three areas: **business finance** (decisions inside a firm), **investments** (decisions for an investor managing wealth), and **financial markets and institutions** (the infrastructure that lets the first two happen).
- Inside business finance, three decisions: **working-capital management** (short-term assets and liabilities), **capital budgeting** (long-term investments), and **capital structure** (long-term financing mix). They map cleanly to three regions of the balance sheet.
- The central trade-off of the field: **higher risk should, on average, over time, deliver higher expected return.** Risk decomposes into default, inflation, diversifiable, non-diversifiable, and political. Diversifiable risk is not priced, because investors can eliminate it for free.
- Markets split into **primary** (new securities, cash to issuer) and **secondary** (existing securities, cash between investors), and into **money** (short-term, low-risk) and **capital** (long-term, varied-risk).
- Three player types: **brokers** (commission, no inventory), **dealers** (inventory, earn the spread), and **intermediaries** (transform one financial claim into another).

---

## What would change my mind

The chapter argues finance is best framed as the study of trade-offs over time under uncertainty, with the risk-return trade-off as the central organizing claim. The reading would have to revise if the empirical risk-return relationship turned out to be reliably backwards — that is, if low-risk asset classes systematically outperformed high-risk ones over multi-decade periods across multiple economies. Some narrow reversals exist (low-volatility anomalies, Japan's lost decades), and the chapter acknowledges them. A wholesale reversal would mean the central claim is wrong in a deep way, and the field would need a new organizing frame.

## Still puzzling

The fuzziest piece of this chapter, to me, is the boundary between *risk* (uncertainty about cash flow distributions) and *uncertainty* in the deeper sense Frank Knight named — situations where we cannot even put a probability distribution on outcomes. The textbook frames everything as risk because the math works on risk. Knightian uncertainty is what financial models stop being able to handle. The 2008 crisis, the COVID-era market dislocations of 2020, the LTCM blowup of 1998 — at the moments that matter most, the assumption that we have a probability distribution starts looking like the assumption that does the most damage. I don't have a clean way to teach this in a first chapter without spooking the reader. I've left it for the synthesis chapter at the end of the book, but I notice it nagging.
