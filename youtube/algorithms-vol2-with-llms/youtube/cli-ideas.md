## Candidate 01 — "Set Up Your Decision Diary Claude Project with Claude"
- Source: algorithms-vol2-with-llms/chapters/01-bayes-rule-and-bayesian-inference.md   LLM Exercise
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: A decision journal that doesn't record the prior before the outcome is revealed is a rationalization diary, not a decision diary — you can always construct a prior that makes your decision look good in hindsight.
- The artifact: a Claude Project named "Decision Diary" with a populated system prompt (viewer's role, domain, decision style, and the explicit instruction to push back on post-hoc rationalization) plus a completed first diary entry — a real decision the viewer is currently facing, worked through under Bayes' rule with explicit probability statements, likelihood ratios, and a populated posterior — formatted as an 8-section markdown document ready to archive.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Chapter 1 entry — Bayes' Rule. The decision I'm thinking through: [name it in one or two sentences, including timeframe]. Help me work through this as a Bayesian inference problem: (1) State my prior as a probability — push back if it feels like post-hoc rationalization. (2) Name the likelihood: P(evidence|hypothesis) and P(evidence|NOT hypothesis). (3) Compute or estimate the posterior with explicit arithmetic. (4) Check the base rate — what's the prior probability of this type of decision turning out well? (5) Surface the misconception I'm at risk of. Output: 8-section diary entry with headings: The decision / Prior / Likelihood / Posterior / Base-rate check / What I'll do next / What would change my mind / Outcome (to fill in later)."`
- Read / check: Verify the prior is stated as a number (not "I'm leaning toward yes"), check that Claude runs the posterior arithmetic explicitly, confirm the base-rate check references a real comparison class (not "this is unique"), verify "What would change my mind" names specific observable evidence — not "if things don't work out."
- Human supplies: A real decision the viewer is currently facing — this is the mandatory human input. The entire exercise is meaningless without it. Claude cannot supply the decision, the prior beliefs, or the domain context. The human must also return to the entry six months later to fill in the Outcome section — that is the calibration mechanism.
- Output medium: Manim — a Bayes tree animates: the prior splits into two branches (H / ¬H); each branch produces a likelihood label; the branches recombine to form the marginal; the posterior is computed in the final node and highlighted; the ratio between the naive intuition and the correct posterior is annotated.
- The change: Add a second piece of evidence that arrives after the initial analysis and update the posterior — sequential Bayesian updating, showing how the diary entry evolves with new information.
- Teardown angle: The prior you don't write down is the prior you can change retroactively. The design lesson: write the prior before seeing the outcome — that is the only way the diary calibrates your judgment over time.
- Exclusions: Bayesian networks, MCMC, hierarchical models — this is the single-decision, single-update case.
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 02 — "Research Your Explore/Exploit Decision with Claude"
- Source: algorithms-vol2-with-llms/chapters/03-explore-vs-exploit-multiarmed-bandits.md   LLM Exercise
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: A/B testing and multi-armed bandits are not interchangeable — A/B testing commits to a fixed exploration budget before seeing the data; bandits adapt. If one option is clearly worse after 100 pulls, A/B testing keeps pulling it; bandits don't.
- The artifact: a structured diary entry on one real allocation decision the viewer is currently managing — which marketing channels to fund, which research threads to keep alive, which ad creatives to run — worked through as a multi-armed bandit problem with: arms named and defined, reward signal characterized (clean/delayed/noisy), trial budget specified, bandit strategy selected (epsilon-greedy/UCB/Thompson) with justification, and the A/B testing misconception addressed explicitly.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Chapter 3 entry — Multi-Armed Bandits. The decision I'm thinking through: [name a real allocation problem with multiple options, uncertain payoffs, and a finite trial budget]. Help me: (1) Define the arms concretely. (2) Characterize the reward signal — is it clean/delayed/noisy? (3) Specify the trial budget — how many pulls can I afford? (4) Select a bandit strategy (epsilon-greedy/UCB/Thompson sampling) and justify why the others don't fit. (5) Identify whether I've been running A/B tests where a bandit would have saved exploration budget. (6) Name the failure mode I should watch for (non-stationary arms, exploration traps, reward delay)."`
- Read / check: Verify the arms are concretely named (not "various options"), confirm the trial budget is a specific number (not "indefinite"), check that the selected strategy has a justification that references the problem's reward signal and stationarity, verify the A/B testing comparison is accurate (Claude should not claim bandits are always better — they require a real-time reward signal that A/B tests don't).
- Human supplies: A real allocation decision with multiple options and a finite trial budget — mandatory human input. The reward signal and trial budget require domain knowledge only the viewer has. Claude can name strategies but cannot know which arms are relevant or what counts as a "pull" in the viewer's context.
- Output medium: Manim — three regret curves (epsilon-greedy, UCB, Thompson sampling) animate simultaneously over 1000 rounds on a 5-arm Bernoulli bandit; cumulative regret is shown; Thompson sampling's Bayesian update advantage appears as it converges fastest; the "A/B testing" flat line appears as a fourth comparison, stopping exploration at round 500.
- The change: Add a stationarity violation at round 500 (the best arm's reward probability drops by 0.3) and watch which strategy adapts fastest — the viewer sees that Thompson sampling adapts through resampling, UCB is slower, and A/B testing is completely blind.
- Teardown angle: A/B testing is a two-arm bandit run for a fixed duration — which commits the exploration budget before seeing the data. The design lesson: if your reward signal is real-time and your arms are stationary, a bandit saves exploration cost. If your reward is delayed or your arms are seasonal, use A/B testing.
- Exclusions: Contextual bandits, Bayesian optimization, reinforcement learning beyond simple bandits.
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 03 — "Research Your Game-Theoretic Decision with Claude"
- Source: algorithms-vol2-with-llms/chapters/04-game-theory.md   LLM Exercise
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: If your best move depends on what the other player does, and their best move depends on what you do, you're in a game — and the rational outcome may be worse for everyone than any single player intended.
- The artifact: a structured diary entry on one real multi-player decision where the viewer's optimal action depends on another party's action — a pricing decision, a negotiation, a hiring timeline, a platform strategy — worked through as a normal-form game: players named, strategies enumerated, payoff matrix constructed, Nash equilibria found, and the question answered: "is the Nash equilibrium where we'll land, and if so, is it good?"
- Prompt seed: `claude "Chapter 4 entry — Game Theory. The decision I'm thinking through: [name a real situation where your best move depends on what another party does]. Help me: (1) Name the players and their available strategies. (2) Sketch a payoff matrix — what do I get in each strategy combination? (3) Find any dominant strategies. (4) Identify the Nash equilibrium (pure or mixed). (5) Determine whether the Nash equilibrium is Pareto-optimal or whether there's a jointly better outcome neither player will reach unilaterally. (6) Identify what mechanism — contract, precommitment, communication — could move us from the Nash equilibrium to the Pareto-superior outcome."`
- Read / check: Verify the payoff matrix is actually a matrix (not a prose description of preferences), confirm the Nash equilibrium check is correct — both players' best responses are to the other's equilibrium strategy, verify the Pareto comparison identifies whether there is a jointly better outcome, confirm the mechanism section proposes a specific operational fix (not "cooperate more").
- Human supplies: A real multi-player decision where the outcome depends on another party's choices — mandatory human input. The payoff matrix requires the viewer's actual estimates of how they and the other party value outcomes. Claude cannot know the viewer's payoffs or the other player's preferences.
- Output medium: Manim — a 2×2 payoff matrix animates: cells fill in with the payoff pairs; dominant strategies are highlighted with arrows; the Nash equilibrium cell pulses; a second frame shows the Pareto frontier with the Nash equilibrium marked as suboptimal; the gap is labeled.
- The change: Add a repeated-game extension — what happens if the same interaction occurs 10 times? Does the Nash equilibrium hold, or does cooperation emerge? The viewer watches the one-shot game vs. repeated-game equilibrium comparison.
- Teardown angle: Nash equilibrium is a fixed point of mutual best-responses — it is where rational agents land, not where they aim. The design lesson: if the Nash equilibrium is bad for everyone, the design problem is the payoff matrix — change the stakes, the outside options, or add a binding commitment mechanism.
- Exclusions: Extensive-form games, mechanism design proofs, auction theory, coalition games.
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 04 — "Research Your Stable-Matching Decision with Claude"
- Source: algorithms-vol2-with-llms/chapters/06-stable-matching-and-galeshapley.md   LLM Exercise
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: Stable matching is not fair — who proposes determines who benefits. The proposer side gets its best stable outcome; the receiver side gets its worst. Most real two-sided markets assign the proposer role by tradition, not by design.
- The artifact: a structured diary entry on one real two-sided pairing decision — a hiring round, project-engineer assignment, mentor-mentee matching, co-founder search — worked through as a stable-matching problem: both sides identified, preference lists sketched, current proposer side identified, strategy-proofness analyzed, and unraveling signals diagnosed.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Chapter 6 entry — Stable Matching. The decision I'm thinking through: [name a real two-sided pairing decision — hiring round, project assignment, mentorship matching]. Help me: (1) Identify the two sides being matched. (2) Sketch preference lists for each side — real preferences, not just 'everyone wants the top option.' (3) Identify who currently proposes — which side makes the first commitment move? (4) Analyze strategy-proofness: is it in either side's interest to misrepresent their preferences? (5) Diagnose unraveling signals: are commitments being made before formal evaluation? Are exploding offers being used? Are side channels reshaping the formal match? (6) Propose whether to change who proposes and why."`
- Read / check: Verify that two distinct sides are named, confirm the preference lists are specific (not "everyone ranked by prestige"), check that the strategy-proofness analysis is correct for the market structure (applicant-proposing DA is strategy-proof for applicants; program-proposing is not strategy-proof for programs in many-to-one cases), verify the unraveling diagnosis names specific mechanisms (early offers, exploding deadlines, side channels).
- Human supplies: A real two-sided matching situation the viewer is participating in or designing — mandatory human input. The preference lists require domain knowledge and actual relationship context. Claude can analyze the mechanism but cannot know the viewer's actual preferences or the other side's preferences.
- Output medium: Manim — an animated bipartite graph: proposers on left, receivers on right; proposal arrows animate, deferred acceptances appear as tentative matches, rejections retract; final stable matching highlighted green; a second frame shows the receiver-optimal matching with different pairings — the difference between the two outcomes is annotated.
- The change: Swap the proposer side and re-run Gale-Shapley — the viewer watches the matching change and identifies who gained and who lost from the swap.
- Teardown angle: The stable matching algorithm is strategy-proof for the proposing side — which means whoever proposes should submit true preferences. The design lesson: identify who proposes before choosing your strategy. If you're the receiver in a market where programs propose, you have strategic room; if programs receive, you should be truthful.
- Exclusions: Many-to-many matching, capacitated market extensions, matching with money.
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 05 — "Build the Thompson Sampling Decision Tracker with Claude Code"
- Source: algorithms-vol2-with-llms/chapters/03-explore-vs-exploit-multiarmed-bandits.md   LLM Exercise
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: Thompson sampling updates a Beta distribution after each outcome — and you can watch the uncertainty collapse in real time as more pulls arrive. Build a live tracker that shows you which arm to pull next and how confident you are.
- The artifact: a Thompson sampling tracker for a 3-to-5 arm Bernoulli bandit with: Beta-Bernoulli conjugate posterior for each arm, a visualization showing the posterior distributions for all arms at each round, a "recommended arm" output per round, and a simulation mode that runs 500 rounds and plots how the posterior certainty evolves from flat Beta(1,1) to a tight distribution around the true best arm.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Build a Thompson sampling tracker. Initialize each of 5 arms with Beta(1,1). Each round: sample theta_i ~ Beta(alpha_i, beta_i) for each arm; select arm with highest sample; on reward: update alpha_i += 1; on no reward: update beta_i += 1. Implement plot_posteriors(arms, round) that shows current Beta distributions for all 5 arms on the same axes. Simulate 500 rounds with true arm probabilities [0.3, 0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.7]. Plot: posterior distributions at rounds 1, 50, 100, 250, 500. Report: how many rounds before the best arm (0.7) is identified with 95% posterior probability of being best."`
- Read / check: CODE — confirm Beta updates are correct (alpha_i += reward, beta_i += 1-reward, not both), confirm posterior sampling uses scipy.stats.beta.rvs correctly. OUTPUT — by round 250 the arm with true probability 0.7 should be identified as best with high posterior probability; the posterior distributions should visibly narrow from round 1 to round 500; the 95% identification round should be in the range 100-300 for these arm probabilities.
- Human supplies: Nothing — fully synthetic. True arm probabilities are configured in the prompt.
- Output medium: Manim — five Beta distribution curves animate on the same axes, one per arm, color-coded; as rounds advance (1 → 50 → 100 → 250 → 500), the distributions narrow and separate; the arm with true probability 0.7 becomes a sharp spike; the others stay broad; a "rounds to 95% identification" counter is displayed.
- The change: Add a non-stationary arm — at round 250, the arm with probability 0.7 drops to 0.3 — and watch how long Thompson sampling takes to detect the change and switch to the new best arm.
- Teardown angle: The Beta distribution is the uncertainty; the width of the distribution is how much exploration is still needed. The design lesson: Thompson sampling's uncertainty is visible — you can read the posterior to know how confident you should be, not just which arm to pull.
- Exclusions: Gaussian bandits (continuous rewards), contextual Thompson sampling, Bayesian optimization.
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 06 — "Build the Bayesian Update Calculator with Claude Code"
- Source: algorithms-vol2-with-llms/chapters/01-bayes-rule-and-bayesian-inference.md   LLM Exercise
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: Most people compute the posterior wrong — they anchor on the likelihood and ignore the prior, or they anchor on the prior and ignore the evidence. Build a tool that forces the arithmetic and shows the inverse fallacy in action.
- The artifact: a Bayesian update calculator — given a prior, a likelihood ratio (sensitivity and specificity), and a base rate, compute the posterior using Bayes' rule with a natural frequency visualization (the Gigerenzen tree diagram showing true positives, false positives, and the 1-in-11 surprise); plus a slider interface showing how the posterior changes as the prior varies from 0.001 to 0.5, demonstrating that the test's accuracy alone does not determine the posterior.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Build a Bayesian update calculator. Given: prior_prob (float), sensitivity P(+|D), specificity (1 - false_positive_rate). Compute: true_positives = prior * sensitivity; false_positives = (1-prior) * (1-specificity); marginal = true_positives + false_positives; posterior = true_positives / marginal. Implement natural_frequency_tree(prior, sensitivity, specificity, population=10000) that prints a tree showing n_disease, n_test_positive_given_disease, n_healthy, n_test_positive_given_healthy, n_posterior. Run on: disease prior 0.001, sensitivity 0.99, specificity 0.99. Then sweep prior from 0.001 to 0.5 at fixed sensitivity/specificity and plot posterior vs prior."`
- Read / check: CODE — confirm the natural frequency tree uses integer counts (not floating-point probabilities), confirm the posterior formula is P(TP) / (P(TP) + P(FP)) not the incorrect P(sensitivity). OUTPUT — the medical screening example should produce a posterior of approximately 0.09 (9%) — verify this matches. The sweep plot should show the posterior is nearly 0 for priors below 0.01 even with 99% accurate tests, rising sharply past 0.05.
- Human supplies: Nothing — fully synthetic. The medical screening parameters are configured in the prompt.
- Output medium: Manim — the natural frequency tree animates: 10000 people appear as a dot matrix, 10 disease cases separate, 9.9 true positives appear (rounded to 10), 99.9 false positives appear (rounded to 100), the posterior fraction 10/110 ≈ 9% is annotated; the surprise "99% accurate but only 9% posterior" is highlighted.
- The change: Vary the sensitivity from 0.80 to 0.99 while fixing the base rate and specificity — the viewer watches how much the posterior changes with test quality when the base rate is low.
- Teardown angle: The posterior is determined by the prior, the sensitivity, and the specificity together — no single parameter dominates. The design lesson: a test with 99% sensitivity is not a test with 99% posterior probability of disease when the prior is low. Name the prior before interpreting the test.
- Exclusions: Sequential testing, hierarchical priors, beta-binomial models — this is the single-test, single-update case.
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 07 — "Build the Scheduling Optimizer with Claude Code"
- Source: algorithms-vol2-with-llms/chapters/05-scheduling.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: Every system that runs more than one thing has already made a scheduling decision. Most of them made it by sorting by priority — which is optimal for exactly one of the five most common scheduling objectives and wrong for the other four.
- The artifact: four scheduling algorithms (SJF for minimum total completion time, EDF for minimum maximum lateness, weighted job scheduling DP for maximum weight under deadlines, list scheduling LPT for parallel machines), an adversarial counterexample showing "sort by priority" failing vs. SJF succeeding on the same instance, and an empirical comparison of all four on 1000 random job sets with the objective specified correctly for each.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Build a scheduling module. Implement sjf(jobs) for minimum total completion time (sort by processing time). Implement edf_preemptive(jobs, deadlines) for minimum maximum lateness. Implement weighted_job_scheduling_dp(jobs, weights, deadlines) returning max weight and job set. Implement lpt_parallel(jobs, m_machines) for approximate minimum makespan on m machines. For each algorithm, verify via exchange-argument adversarial inputs that 'sort by priority' fails. Run 1000 random instances per objective and report average and worst-case suboptimality of priority-sort vs the correct algorithm."`
- Read / check: CODE — SJF must sort by processing time (ascending), EDF must use preemptive scheduling (not just ordering), LPT must assign the longest remaining job to the lightest machine at each step. OUTPUT — adversarial inputs must show priority-sort failing with a gap > 10% on at least 50% of instances; weighted DP must match brute-force on small instances (n ≤ 8); EDF must succeed (produce a feasible schedule) on all instances where a feasible schedule exists.
- Human supplies: Nothing — fully synthetic.
- Output medium: Manim — animated Gantt chart comparison: two identical job sets, two scheduling policies; SJF Gantt chart on the right shows jobs finishing earlier; total completion time labels appear below; the gap is annotated in red.
- The change: Add a scheduling instance from a real build system (provide a dependency graph of 10 tasks with processing times) and find the minimum makespan on 2 parallel machines using LPT — the viewer watches the algorithm work on a real engineering artifact.
- Teardown angle: The scheduling objective determines the optimal algorithm — and the four most common objectives require four different algorithms. The design lesson: before choosing a scheduling policy, write down the objective function explicitly.
- Exclusions: Job shop scheduling (NP-hard for 3+ machines), online scheduling with unknown jobs, preemption on multiple machines.
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 08 — "Build the Social Network Centrality and Influence Suite with Claude Code"
- Source: algorithms-vol2-with-llms/chapters/08-social-networks.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: The 5 most connected people in a network are almost never the best people to seed a marketing campaign. Greedy influence maximization — which uses submodularity to get a (1-1/e) approximation — picks a different and better set.
- The artifact: four centrality measures (degree, eigenvector, betweenness via BFS, PageRank), greedy influence maximization with the independent cascade model for k=5 seed nodes, a community detection algorithm, and a demonstration on a synthetic 3-cluster network showing that betweenness centrality identifies bridges, degree centrality identifies hubs, and influence maximization identifies a spread-optimizing set that is neither.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Social network analysis suite. Implement: degree_centrality(G), eigenvector_centrality(G) (power iteration, 20 steps), betweenness_centrality(G) (exact, BFS), pagerank(G, d=0.85, iterations=50). Greedy influence maximization: for k=5 seeds, use independent cascade model (each edge transmits with probability 0.1), greedy selection by marginal gain, average over 100 simulations per candidate. Community detection: girvan_newman(G) removing highest-betweenness edge iteratively. Demo: 3 dense clusters + 2 bridges, show betweenness identifies bridges, degree identifies hubs, influence maximization selects across clusters."`
- Read / check: CODE — PageRank must include teleportation (probability 1-d jump to uniform random), betweenness centrality must normalize correctly (divide by (n-1)(n-2) for directed). OUTPUT — in the 3-cluster demo, betweenness centrality's top 5 nodes should include the 2 bridge nodes; degree centrality's top 5 should be hub nodes within clusters; influence maximization's 5 seeds should be spread across clusters (not all in one cluster).
- Human supplies: Nothing — fully synthetic by default. If the viewer has a real network edge list (email communication, academic citations, social follows), they can substitute it for the synthetic demo — but the algorithms run on synthetic by default.
- Output medium: Manim — the 3-cluster demo: a graph animates; centrality scores appear as node sizes; top-5 degree nodes highlight in blue (all within clusters); top-5 betweenness nodes highlight in orange (includes bridges); influence maximization seeds highlight in green (spread across clusters); influence spread simulates as a contagion animation.
- The change: Compare the influence spread from the degree-centrality top-5 vs. the greedy influence maximization top-5 on 1000 simulation runs — show the expected spread gap as a histogram.
- Teardown angle: Centrality answers a specific question — "who is most connected" or "who is the broker" — and influence maximization answers a different question: "who seeds the most spread." The design lesson: choose the centrality measure by the question, not by computation convenience.
- Exclusions: Temporal networks, signed networks, real-world social media data collection.
- Score: 8/10
