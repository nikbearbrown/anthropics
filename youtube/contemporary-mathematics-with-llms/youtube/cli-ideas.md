## Candidate 01 — Build a Compound Interest Simulator: Visualize the Exponential Cliff with Claude
- Source: contemporary-mathematics-with-llms/chapters/06-money-management.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: A $1,000 investment at 7% annual interest. After 10 years: $1,967. After 30 years: $7,612. After 50 years: $29,457. The curve is not a line — it bends upward like a cliff. The bend only becomes visible when you plot it. Most people never plot it.
- The artifact: A Python script that generates compound interest curves for three scenarios: same principal, three compounding frequencies (annual / monthly / daily); same principal, three interest rates (4% / 7% / 10%); and a "credit card vs. savings" comparison (18% APR debt growing vs. 7% savings growing). Output is a data table with year-by-year values. Manim animation shows the three curves drawing left to right simultaneously, with the doubling points marked and a final annotation showing the ratio of 50-year to 10-year value (~15×).
- Prompt seed: `claude "Write a Python script that: (1) computes compound interest using A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt) for P=1000, r=0.07, and three compounding frequencies n = [1, 12, 365] across t = [0, 5, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50] years; (2) computes a second set for r = [0.04, 0.07, 0.10] with n=12; (3) computes a credit-card debt series at 18% APR monthly compounding growing alongside a savings series at 7%; (4) prints three formatted tables with year, frequency/rate, and accumulated value. No external libraries except math."`
- Read / check: Verify the formula uses (1 + r/n)^(nt) correctly — r is annual rate, n is compounding periods per year, t is years. Confirm the credit-card series grows faster than the savings series at every year. Check that the 50-year value at 7% monthly compounding is approximately $29,500 (not $29,457, since monthly compounds slightly more).
- Human supplies: Nothing — fully synthetic. The formula, the principal, and the rate are all given in the chapter. No external data sources needed.
- Output medium: Manim (animated) — three compound-interest curves animate drawing from left to right simultaneously on one axis; each curve's doubling point is marked with a vertical dashed line and a label ("doubles at ~10 years @ 7%"); a second panel shows the credit-card vs. savings divergence growing wider each decade.
- The change: Add a "rule of 72" annotation — at each rate, draw a horizontal marker at the predicted doubling time (72 / rate%) and compare it to the actual curve's crossing of 2P. Show the rule is an approximation and measure its error at each rate.
- Teardown angle: The bend in the curve is not a math trick — it is why starting at 25 beats starting at 35, every time, even with lower contributions. The simulation makes visible what the formula hides: the last 20 years of a 50-year investment account for more growth than the first 30.
- Exclusions: Continuous compounding (e^rt), bond pricing, full APR vs. APY distinction, tax treatment of investment returns.
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 02 — Build a Law of Large Numbers Demonstrator: Watch Empirical Probability Converge with Claude
- Source: contemporary-mathematics-with-llms/chapters/07-probability.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: Flip a fair coin 10 times: you might get 3 heads (30%). Flip it 1,000 times: you'll get close to 500 (50%). Flip it 100,000 times: you'll get within 1% of 50%. The Law of Large Numbers is not a platitude — it has a rate of convergence, and you can watch it happen.
- The artifact: A Python script that simulates coin flips (or die rolls, or roulette spins) for n = [10, 100, 1000, 10000, 100000] trials, computing the running empirical probability at each step and the deviation from the theoretical probability. Output is a table of n, empirical probability, deviation from theoretical, and a flag if deviation > 5%. Manim animation shows the empirical probability curve oscillating wildly at small n and converging to a flat line near 0.5 as n grows, with a shaded "±5% band" that the curve eventually stays within.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Write a Python script that: (1) simulates coin flips using random.random() < 0.5 for n in [10, 100, 1000, 10000, 100000] trials; (2) for each n, runs 5 independent simulations and records the empirical P(heads) for each; (3) prints a table: n, min empirical P, max empirical P, mean empirical P, mean deviation from 0.5; (4) also runs a die-roll simulation (P(roll=6) = 1/6) for the same n values; (5) prints both tables. No external libraries except random."`
- Read / check: Verify the coin flip uses random.random() < 0.5 or random.randint(0,1), not a biased generator. Confirm the die-roll probability converges to approximately 1/6 ≈ 0.1667 at large n. Check that the deviation column shrinks monotonically with n in the mean (though individual runs may not be monotone).
- Human supplies: Nothing — fully synthetic. The random module is standard library.
- Output medium: Manim (animated) — a running empirical probability curve animates across the x-axis (n from 1 to 100,000 on log scale); the theoretical probability (0.5) is a horizontal dashed line; a shaded ±5% band shrinks as n grows; five independent simulation runs are plotted as thin transparent lines with the mean as a bold line.
- The change: Replace the coin flip with a casino bet (roulette: P(red) = 18/38 ≈ 47.4%). Show that the Law of Large Numbers guarantees the house edge — at 100,000 spins, the empirical win rate converges reliably to 47.4%, not 50%. The casino's advantage is not luck; it is the Law of Large Numbers working against the player.
- Teardown angle: The Law of Large Numbers is the reason casino profits are predictable and individual gambler outcomes are volatile. At small n, anything happens. At large n, the expected value rules. The house sets expected value negative and then waits for large n.
- Exclusions: Central Limit Theorem, confidence intervals, formal proof of LLN, Bernoulli vs. binomial distribution distinction.
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 03 — Build an Arrow's Impossibility Demonstrator: Find the Voting Paradox in Real Data with Claude
- Source: contemporary-mathematics-with-llms/chapters/11-voting-and-apportionment.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: Arrow's Impossibility Theorem (1951): no voting system with more than 2 candidates can simultaneously satisfy unanimity, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship. Every voting method fails at least one of Arrow's criteria. Can we generate a concrete election where Plurality, Borda Count, and Instant-Runoff produce three different winners from the same ballots?
- The artifact: A Python script that generates a synthetic preference election (3 candidates, ~50 voters, with a Condorcet-cycle preference profile) and runs Plurality, Borda Count, Instant-Runoff, and Condorcet (pairwise majority) on the same ballot set. Output is a table: Method | Winner | Criteria Violated. Manim animation shows the same ballot set yielding four different winners depending on the method, with the violated Arrow criterion highlighted for each.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Write a Python script that: (1) generates a preference election with 3 candidates (A, B, C) and 60 voters split into three preference groups of 20: Group 1 prefers A>B>C, Group 2 prefers B>C>A, Group 3 prefers C>A>B (a classic Condorcet cycle); (2) computes the winner under four methods: Plurality (most first-place votes), Borda Count (3 pts for 1st, 2 for 2nd, 1 for 3rd), Instant-Runoff (eliminate lowest first-place vote getter, redistribute), and Condorcet (pairwise majority — A beats B if more voters prefer A over B); (3) prints a table: Method, Winner, Arrow Criterion Violated (IIA / Monotonicity / Condorcet). No external libraries."`
- Read / check: Verify the Condorcet cycle is genuine: A beats B pairwise, B beats C pairwise, C beats A pairwise — no Condorcet winner exists. Confirm Plurality picks A (20 first-place votes each, then tiebreak), Borda picks B (2×20+1×20 = 60 pts vs. A's 3×20 = 60 — check arithmetic), and that the outputs differ. Check that the Arrow criterion column cites the correct failure for each method.
- Human supplies: Nothing — fully synthetic. The preference profile is constructed analytically, not from real election data.
- Output medium: Manim (animated) — four method boxes animate in sequence; each shows the ballot tally, elimination steps (for IRV), or pairwise table (for Condorcet); each box's winner is highlighted; a final frame lines up the four different winners from the same ballots with the violated Arrow criterion labeled on each.
- The change: Add the Alabama paradox: an apportionment scenario (per Chapter 11) where increasing the House size by one seat causes a state to lose a seat. Use the Hamilton method on a synthetic population table and show the paradox firing.
- Teardown angle: Arrow's theorem is not a curiosity — it is a proof that every democratic voting system embeds an arbitrary design choice that can change the outcome. The impossibility is not a bug to fix; it is a trade-off to make explicitly. The same logic applies to any multi-objective optimization: the theorem is a portable warning about systems optimizing multiple fairness criteria simultaneously.
- Exclusions: Full social choice theory, Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem, complete Balinski-Young proof, comparison of real electoral systems.
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 04 — Build a Königsberg Bridge Solver: Test Euler's Degree Theorem on Any Graph with Claude
- Source: contemporary-mathematics-with-llms/chapters/12-graph-theory.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: In 1736, Euler proved you cannot cross all seven Königsberg bridges exactly once — not by trying every path, but by proving it in one theorem: an Euler circuit exists if and only if every vertex has even degree. The theorem converts a search problem into a parity check. Can you build a tool that tests any bridge/street network for the existence of an Euler circuit in O(n) time?
- The artifact: A Python script that takes a graph as an adjacency list and: (1) computes the degree of each vertex; (2) checks whether all degrees are even (Euler circuit exists) or exactly two are odd (Euler path exists, not circuit); (3) prints the verdict and the degree table; (4) if a circuit exists, finds it using Hierholzer's algorithm. Output includes a degree table and the circuit sequence. Manim animation shows the Königsberg graph as four land-mass nodes with seven bridge edges, each degree label appearing on the node, and the parity check firing as nodes turn red (odd degree) or green (even degree).
- Prompt seed: `claude "Write a Python script that: (1) accepts a graph as a Python dict adjacency list (e.g., {'A': ['B', 'B', 'C', 'D'], 'B': ['A', 'A', 'C'], 'C': ['A', 'B', 'D'], 'D': ['A', 'C']} for Königsberg); (2) computes the degree of each vertex; (3) prints a degree table and a verdict: 'Euler circuit exists' (all degrees even), 'Euler path exists' (exactly 2 odd degrees), or 'No Euler path or circuit' (>2 odd degrees); (4) if an Euler circuit exists, finds one using Hierholzer's algorithm and prints the circuit sequence. Handle multigraphs (parallel edges). No external libraries."`
- Read / check: Verify Königsberg returns "No Euler path or circuit" — all four land masses have odd degree (3 or 5). Confirm Hierholzer's algorithm correctly backtracks and splices paths. Test a simple cycle (A-B-C-A) returns "Euler circuit exists" with circuit A→B→C→A. Check that a graph with exactly 2 odd-degree vertices returns "Euler path exists."
- Human supplies: Nothing — fully synthetic. The Königsberg graph is a known fixed example. Optional: the viewer supplies a street map of their neighborhood as an adjacency list.
- Output medium: Manim (animated) — the Königsberg graph appears as four land-mass nodes (North, South, East, West bank) with seven bridge edges; each node's degree label appears and colors red (odd) or green (even); a parity verdict banner appears; then a second example (a cycle or grid) shows the circuit being traced as an animated path.
- The change: Add a second graph: a city block grid (where every intersection has degree 4 — all even). Show the Euler circuit being traced through the grid, then ask: what is the minimum number of bridges to add to Königsberg to make an Euler circuit possible? Answer: add one bridge between any two odd-degree land masses.
- Teardown angle: Euler's theorem converts an exponential search (try every possible path) into a linear check (count the degrees). The lesson is not about bridges — it is about the power of invariants: a property that either holds or doesn't, independent of which path you choose, that decides the answer before the search begins.
- Exclusions: Hamiltonian circuits (NP-hard), graph coloring, planar graphs, full proof of Euler's theorem.
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 05 — Build a Number Base Converter: Show Why Place Value Is the Greatest Engineering Trick with Claude
- Source: contemporary-mathematics-with-llms/chapters/04-number-representation-and-calculation.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: The year 1492 in Roman numerals: MCDXCII. Try multiplying MCDXCII by XLII. You can't — you have to translate, compute, retranslate. The Hindu-Arabic place-value system does not just record numbers more compactly; it makes arithmetic possible. The proof is converting the same number across bases and watching the pattern hold.
- The artifact: A Python script that converts any integer to base 2 (binary), base 8 (octal), base 16 (hex), and base 60 (Babylonian) representations, with expanded form showing each digit's positional contribution. Output is a table: number, base, representation, expanded form (digit × base^position for each digit). Manim animation shows the number 738 decomposed into its positional contributions simultaneously in base 10, base 2, and base 16 as color-coded digit columns stacking up to the total.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Write a Python script that: (1) accepts an integer n (default 738); (2) converts it to base 2, 8, 10, 16, and 60, using only arithmetic (no Python bin/hex builtins — compute digit by digit using n mod base and n // base); (3) for each base, prints: the representation as a list of digits, and the expanded form (digit_k × base^k + ... = n); (4) also accepts a base-b numeral string and converts it back to base 10 using positional expansion. No external libraries."`
- Read / check: Verify the base-2 representation of 738 is 1011100010 (check: 512+128+64+32+2 = 738). Confirm the base-16 representation is 2E2 (2×256 + 14×16 + 2 = 512+224+2 = 738). Check that the expanded form prints correct power annotations. Verify the reverse-conversion (base-b string to decimal) correctly applies positional weighting.
- Human supplies: Nothing — fully synthetic. The conversion algorithm is pure arithmetic.
- Output medium: Manim (animated) — a three-column layout shows the number 738 decomposing in base 10, base 2, and base 16 simultaneously; each digit's column stacks up like a bar chart showing its contribution (512 as a tall teal bar in base 2, 128 as a shorter bar, etc.); the bars sum to 738 in all three bases; a final annotation labels "same number, different representations."
- The change: Show the Roman numeral problem directly: ask Claude to attempt MCMXCII × XLII (1992 × 42) in Roman numerals, then solve it in Arabic notation. Measure the number of steps. Conclude with: the representation system determines what arithmetic is possible.
- Teardown angle: Zero is the invention that made computation possible — not as a quantity, but as a placeholder that lets position carry meaning. Without zero, 1009 becomes indistinguishable from 19. The deepest idea in this chapter is not a number; it is the symbol for the absence of a number.
- Exclusions: Floating-point representation (IEEE 754), two's complement, full binary arithmetic, modular arithmetic applications.
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 06 — Build a Sampling Bias Demonstrator: Replicate the 1936 Literary Digest Disaster with Claude
- Source: contemporary-mathematics-with-llms/chapters/08-statistics.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: The 1936 Literary Digest poll sent 10 million ballots and received 2.4 million responses, the largest poll ever conducted. It predicted Alf Landon would beat FDR by a landslide. FDR won by the largest Electoral College margin since 1820. The sample was huge. The sample was biased. Bigger is not better when the selection is wrong.
- The artifact: A Python script that simulates a biased vs. unbiased poll on a synthetic voter population. The population is 60% Democrat / 40% Republican. A biased sample (selecting from telephone and magazine-subscriber lists, which skew wealthy and Republican) vs. a simple random sample — both of size n=2400 — produce two different predicted winners. Output is a table: sample type, n, predicted winner, predicted margin, actual population margin. Manim animation shows the two sampling processes side by side: random sampling (dots drawn evenly) vs. biased sampling (dots drawn disproportionately from the wealthy segment), with the resulting prediction bars diverging from reality.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Write a Python script that: (1) generates a synthetic voter population of 10,000 voters: 6,000 Democrat (D), 4,000 Republican (R), split into two groups — 3,000 wealthy (2,000 R, 1,000 D) and 7,000 non-wealthy (2,000 R, 5,000 D); (2) simulates a biased sample of 2,400 voters by sampling 70% from the wealthy group and 30% from non-wealthy; (3) simulates an SRS (simple random sample) of 2,400 voters from the full population; (4) for each sample, computes the predicted winner and predicted margin; (5) prints a comparison table: sample type, predicted D%, predicted R%, predicted winner, actual population D%. No external libraries except random."`
- Read / check: Verify the biased sample consistently predicts R as winner (wealthy group skews Republican 2:1). Confirm the SRS consistently predicts D as winner (reflects the 60/40 population split). Check that the biased sample size (2,400) is large but the bias dominates — larger n does not fix selection bias.
- Human supplies: Nothing — fully synthetic. The population parameters are analytically defined.
- Output medium: Manim (animated) — two panels animate simultaneously: left panel draws random dots from the full population (each dot colored by party affiliation), right panel draws disproportionately from the wealthy segment; bar charts below each panel show the predicted vote share updating in real time as dots are drawn; a final frame shows both predictions alongside the actual 60/40 truth.
- The change: Add a third condition: a 2.4-million-response biased sample (still 70/30 wealthy). Show that the Literary Digest's actual mistake — a 2.4M response biased sample — is even more confidently wrong than a 2,400-response biased sample. More responses amplify the bias, not correct it.
- Teardown angle: The Literary Digest had 2.4 million responses and was catastrophically wrong. George Gallup had 50,000 responses and was right. The question is not how many people you ask but whether the people you ask are representative of the people you're predicting. This is why AI training sets matter more than training set size.
- Exclusions: Full survey methodology (weighting, stratification), confidence interval calculation, formal definition of sampling frame, Gallup's quota sampling method.
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 07 — Build a Metric Conversion Auditor: Catch the Mars Climate Orbiter Error Before Launch with Claude
- Source: contemporary-mathematics-with-llms/chapters/09-metric-measurement.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: September 23, 1999. NASA loses a $325 million Mars orbiter because one engineering team used pounds-force, another used newtons, and no one labeled the units at the interface. The conversion factor between them is not secret: 1 lbf = 4.44822 N. The error was findable. It was not found. A unit-checking tool at the API boundary would have caught it.
- The artifact: A Python script that implements a simple unit-checking validator: accepts numerical values with explicit unit labels and validates that operations between them are dimensionally consistent (N × m = J; lbf must be converted to N before mixing; m/s² is not m/s). Output is a validation report: operation, left operand (value + unit), right operand (value + unit), result (value + unit), consistency verdict (valid / unit mismatch). Manim animation shows the Mars Climate Orbiter interface as a pipeline: thrust values in lbf pass into a system expecting N, the conversion factor is missing, and the trajectory error accumulates across 9 months.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Write a Python script that: (1) defines a unit conversion table for force (N, lbf, dyne), length (m, km, ft, mi), mass (kg, g, lb), time (s, min, hr), and pressure (Pa, psi, atm); (2) defines a DimensionalValue class with value and unit attributes; (3) implements an add() function that raises UnitMismatchError if units are not compatible (not the same dimension) and auto-converts before adding if they are compatible; (4) runs the Mars Climate Orbiter scenario: a force in lbf·s is passed to a system expecting N·s, and the function catches the mismatch; (5) prints a validation report. No external libraries."`
- Read / check: Verify the conversion factor 1 lbf = 4.44822 N is used correctly. Confirm UnitMismatchError fires when lbf is added to N without conversion. Check that the trajectory error accumulates correctly if the conversion is silently dropped (lbf treated as N — a ~4.45× error). Verify the report prints both operand units clearly.
- Human supplies: Nothing — fully synthetic. The NASA scenario parameters are documented public record.
- Output medium: Manim (animated) — a pipeline diagram shows the thruster output (lbf·s) passing through an interface to the navigation system (expecting N·s); a red "unit mismatch" gate appears at the interface; below, a trajectory divergence chart shows what happens when the gate is absent (the orbiter's actual fate) vs. present (the corrected flight path).
- The change: Add a second scenario: a medication dosage in mg/kg body weight being entered into a system that expects mg total dose for a 70 kg patient. Show the same unit-mismatch pattern occurring in a clinical context — the NASA mistake has a medical cousin that causes adverse drug events.
- Teardown angle: The metric system's power is not compactness — it is traceability. Every unit relates to every other through powers of ten, which means every conversion has a formula. The orbiter died not because the formula was unknown but because nobody enforced the label. A labeled interface makes the formula visible and the error catchable.
- Exclusions: Full SI base unit definitions, dimensional analysis in fluid mechanics, full NASA accident report, non-SI units in science.
- Score: 7/10

## Candidate 08 — Build a Prime Factorization Timer: Demonstrate the Asymmetry That Encrypts the Internet with Claude
- Source: contemporary-mathematics-with-llms/chapters/03-real-number-systems-and-number-theory.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: Multiply two 10-digit primes: your laptop does it in microseconds. Factor the 20-digit product: trial division would take hours. This one-way asymmetry — multiplication is O(n²), factorization is exponential — is the foundation of RSA encryption. Every HTTPS connection you make today rents this asymmetry.
- The artifact: A Python script that: (1) multiplies pairs of primes of increasing size (3-digit × 3-digit, 6-digit × 6-digit, 9-digit × 9-digit) and records multiplication time; (2) runs trial-division factorization on each product and records factorization time; (3) prints a table: prime pair size, product, multiply time (μs), factor time (ms/s), ratio (factor/multiply). Manim animation shows the ratio growing exponentially as prime size increases — a bar chart where multiply bars are constant-height teal slivers and factor bars grow until they leave the frame.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Write a Python script that: (1) uses the sympy.isprime() function to generate prime pairs of sizes 3, 5, 7, 9 digits; (2) for each pair, records the wall-clock time to multiply them using time.perf_counter(); (3) runs trial division factorization on the product (trial divide by all integers from 2 up to sqrt(product), stop at first factor) and records factorization time; (4) prints a table: prime sizes, product (first 20 digits if large), multiply time (μs), factor time (ms or s), ratio. Use sympy for primality checking; no sympy factorization functions — trial division only."`
- Read / check: Verify trial division is implemented as a loop, not sympy.factorint. Confirm multiply time is measured separately from factor time using perf_counter. Check that the ratio column grows by roughly 10× to 100× for each step up in prime size. Note: for 9-digit × 9-digit products, trial division may take several seconds — that is the demonstration.
- Human supplies: Nothing — fully synthetic. Primes are generated by the script. Allow ~60 seconds for the script to run (the 9-digit factorization is intentionally slow).
- Output medium: Manim (animated) — a two-bar chart per prime-size pair: left bar (teal, short) = multiply time; right bar (amber, growing) = factor time; the ratio label animates in above each pair; as prime size increases, the amber bar grows so tall it exits the frame; a final annotation reads "this is RSA."
- The change: Add a fourth row with a 12-digit × 12-digit prime pair and project the trial-division time (rather than running it — it would take centuries). Show how the extrapolated bar would be larger than the frame by a factor of 10^20. The projection is the lesson.
- Teardown angle: The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic guarantees that factorization is always possible. It says nothing about how long it takes. The guarantee of existence and the computational difficulty of discovery live in the same theorem — and that gap is what RSA exploits.
- Exclusions: RSA algorithm details, modular exponentiation, Shor's algorithm, elliptic curve cryptography, full computational complexity theory.
- Score: 7/10
