## Candidate 01 — The Gap Between Legal and Ethical Is Where Business Ethics Lives
- Source: `mba-business-ethics/chapters/01-why-ethics-matter.md`
- Topic: BUSINESS ETHICS
- Hook: Three Equifax executives sold $2 million of stock while knowing about a breach — and it was completely legal.
- Key case: Equifax insider stock sales, July 2017: legal under then-current insider-trading rules, universally judged unethical. The legal-and-unethical cell of the 2x2 is the cell no one names aloud — yet it is where almost every real business-ethics decision lives.
- The Question: If the law is already the floor, what fills the space above it — and why does almost every business scandal happen in that gap?
- Core idea: Law and ethics are two perpendicular axes, not a single line; a four-cell diagram places most interesting business decisions in the legal-but-unethical quadrant. The firm that treats legal compliance as its ethical ceiling will systematically choose the short-horizon option until the long-horizon cost arrives.
- Visual object: A 2×2 matrix with "Legal / Illegal" on one axis and "Ethical / Unethical" on the other, with a dot moving through each cell and landing in the legal-unethical quadrant as the key case arrives
- Manim move: split → transform (dot migrates to the named cell as case narrows)
- Example seed: Equifax breach disclosed internally May 2017, stock sold in August, public told September 7. Share price falls 14%. CEO resigns within three weeks. The legal route was also the catastrophic long-run route — and the law still permitted the sale.
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: c2v (Equifax headquarters exterior), raster (2×2 matrix)
- Prerequisites: basic awareness that laws exist; no prior ethics vocabulary needed
- Exclusions: no full three-lens framework, no Faris case, no detailed timeline of litigation costs
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 02 — Productivity Grew 74%. Wages Grew 12%. Where Did the Other 62% Go?
- Source: `mba-business-ethics/chapters/06-what-employers-owe-employees.md`
- Topic: BUSINESS ETHICS
- Hook: Between 1973 and 2016, U.S. workers produced 74% more output per hour — and received 12% more pay.
- Key case: EPI / BLS data on the productivity-pay decoupling since 1973. CEO-to-worker pay ratio: 20-to-1 in 1965, approximately 300-to-1 by 2014. The Gravity Payments $70K minimum salary experiment: 5,000 applications in one week, 90%+ retention, revenue growth.
- The Question: If workers produce six times more output per dollar of wages than they did in 1973, where did the difference go — and is the firm that captured it operating efficiently or exploiting a legal floor?
- Core idea: The post-1973 decoupling of productivity from compensation is a structural ethical failure, not a policy accident; it is maintained by the legal minimum being lower than the moral minimum. Firms that moved above the floor — Costco, Gravity Payments — consistently outperform sector comparables on retention, quality, and long-run returns.
- Visual object: Two diverging lines on a shared axis from 1973 to 2016 — productivity (steep rise) and median worker compensation (nearly flat) — with the widening gap labeled as the ethical space the book is asking about
- Manim move: accumulate → spread (lines diverge frame by frame)
- Example seed: Dan Price, Gravity Payments CEO, learns an employee cannot live on $35,000 in Seattle. He cuts his own salary from $1M to $70K, raises the firm floor to match. Critics predict collapse. Five thousand applications arrive in a week.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: geo (Seattle, Gravity Payments office), c2v (FRED chart source), raster (diverging lines)
- Prerequisites: what wages and productivity mean; no economics background needed
- Exclusions: no minimum-wage legislation history, no gender pay gap detail, no CEO pay formula, no Icelandic equal-pay standard
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 03 — Integrity Erodes Exception by Exception — Until the Call Is Ordinary
- Source: `mba-business-ethics/chapters/01-why-ethics-matter.md`, `mba-business-ethics/chapters/11-epilogue-why-ethics-still-matter.md`
- Topic: BUSINESS ETHICS
- Hook: Rajat Gupta — former McKinsey global managing director, Goldman Sachs board member — tipped a hedge fund and personally received nothing. Why?
- Key case: Rajat Gupta (Goldman tip, 2009) and Mark Faris (Cisco SMARTnet fraud, 2001–2002). Both actors started from a position where they would have refused the final action if asked cold. The mechanism: small accommodations, each individually defensible, compound until the catastrophic choice feels like one more step in an ordinary sequence.
- The Question: If a person with Gupta's career would refuse a bribe on Day One, how does the same person make a federal-felony phone call forty years later — and what is the mechanism of the drift?
- Core idea: Integrity is not a fixed asset; it is a running average of decisions, maintained or eroded by daily habit. Each small exception updates the baseline for what feels normal; by the time the large exception arrives, the baseline has moved to accommodate it. Virtue ethics calls this character formation — it works in both directions.
- Visual object: A single horizontal line labeled "ethical baseline" with small downward notches accumulating — each labeled with a defensible-sounding excuse — until the line is so far down that the final action (the Gupta call) sits at what is now the new baseline
- Manim move: decay (baseline drifts down notch by notch)
- Example seed: Mark Faris in 2001: revenue is down 30%, so he files one false SMARTnet claim. Then another. 140 claims later, he is convicted of mail fraud, wire fraud, and money laundering. He tells audiences: "I would have refused a $100,000 bribe in 2000." He is right. He would have.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: c2v (Gupta courtroom sketch reference), raster (baseline erosion diagram)
- Prerequisites: what ethics means; no prior philosophy
- Exclusions: no full Enron case, no three-lens framework in detail, no Swanson plagiarism case
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 04 — The Tragedy of the Commons: Why Rational Actors Destroy What They Share
- Source: `mba-business-ethics/chapters/04-three-special-stakeholders-society-the-environment-and-government.md`
- Topic: BUSINESS ETHICS
- Hook: Cruise ships discharge approximately one billion gallons of sewage into the ocean every year — and it is entirely legal.
- Key case: Cruise-ship nighttime wastewater discharge into international waters. Each ship's operators are acting within the law and within rational self-interest. The ocean, the marine ecosystem, and downstream fishing communities bear the cost they never agreed to pay.
- The Question: When individually rational decisions by many actors destroy a shared resource that no single actor owns, who is responsible — and what mechanism could fix it?
- Core idea: A commons (ocean, atmosphere, groundwater) is non-excludable and rival in consumption; when individual users bear no price for extraction or discharge, they use it past exhaustion. The tragedy is structural — no bad actor required. Three instruments address it from different directions: cap-and-trade (quantity ceiling), carbon tax (price signal), and standards (floor).
- Visual object: A shared pool (labeled "commons") with multiple pipes drawing from it — each pipe small, individually rational — and the pool's level falling frame by frame. Then three valve icons (cap, tax, standard) appearing as potential regulators.
- Manim move: accumulate (pool level falls) → split (three instrument branches appear)
- Example seed: The Probo Koala, 2006: a Trafigura ship dumps hundreds of tons of toxic slurry in Abidjan, Côte d'Ivoire, at $3/ton. European processing would cost $3,000/ton. One hundred thousand residents seek medical attention. Fifteen die. Each decision in the chain was individually cost-optimal.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: geo (Abidjan, Côte d'Ivoire satellite view), c2v (cruise ship at sea), raster (commons pool diagram)
- Prerequisites: what a shared resource is; no economics background needed
- Exclusions: no earth jurisprudence detail, no Whanganui River, no Ecuador constitution, no full Trafigura litigation history
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 05 — A $500,000 Safety Valve That Would Have Prevented a $65 Billion Disaster
- Source: `mba-business-ethics/chapters/04-three-special-stakeholders-society-the-environment-and-government.md`
- Topic: BUSINESS ETHICS
- Hook: Norway required an acoustic shutoff valve on every offshore oil well. The U.S. did not. One well cost sixty-five billion dollars to clean up.
- Key case: Deepwater Horizon, 2010. The acoustic shutoff valve costs approximately $500,000 per well. The Minerals Management Service considered requiring it, received industry lobbying against it, and declined. BP's total Deepwater Horizon liability exceeded $65 billion. The ratio is 130,000-to-1.
- The Question: When a regulator skips a $500K safety requirement after industry lobbying — and a $65B disaster follows — is that a regulator failure, a corporate failure, or a structural failure that has a name?
- Core idea: Regulatory capture occurs when a regulator's access, information, and career incentives are dominated by the industry it regulates, shifting its effective loyalty from the public interest to industry interest. The three structural pressures — access asymmetry, information asymmetry, revolving door — are predictable in advance and distinguishable from mere collaboration.
- Visual object: A single bar chart with two bars — "$500K valve (not installed)" and "$65B total liability (paid)" — at logarithmically different heights, with the revolving-door mechanism illustrated as a rotating arrow between regulator and industry boxes
- Manim move: compare (two bars appear side by side) → rotate (revolving-door arrow spins)
- Example seed: The MMS inspector socialized with the firms she regulated. Required audits were signed without site visits. The Norwegian Petroleum Safety Authority — which did require the valve — has a per-barrel incident rate roughly one-tenth that of U.S. Gulf operations. The difference is institutional, not cultural.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: geo (Gulf of Mexico, Macondo well coordinates), c2v (Deepwater Horizon fire photo), raster (bar chart)
- Prerequisites: what a regulator is; what offshore drilling is
- Exclusions: no MMS reorganization into three agencies detail, no public-private partnership section, no full Abidjan / society section
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 06 — The Whistleblower Won $104 Million — and Never Worked in Finance Again
- Source: `mba-business-ethics/chapters/07-what-employees-owe-employers.md`
- Topic: BUSINESS ETHICS
- Hook: Bradley Birkenfeld exposed UBS's tax-evasion scheme, received a $104 million IRS award — and served prison time before collecting it.
- Key case: Sherron Watkins (Enron, 2001): internal memo to CEO, no financial reward, career in corporate finance effectively ended. Bradley Birkenfeld (UBS, 2007): False Claims Act disclosure, $104M award, also served prison time for his own involvement. Sallie Krawcheck (Smith Barney / Bank of America): internal reporting worked when the CEO backed her — and she was reorganized out anyway under the next CEO.
- The Question: If whistleblowing is legally protected, financially rewarded, and socially celebrated — why does the research consistently show that even successful whistleblowers rarely return to comparable careers?
- Core idea: The whistleblower legal regime — approximately twenty separate statutes, no comprehensive federal law — is structurally inadequate: protections are real but patchwork. The career consequence is nearly universal regardless of outcome or award because internal channels take their cue from organizational loyalty norms that the legal regime cannot override. The correct sequence (internal first, documented, then external) is not about courtesy — it is the evidentiary foundation that legal protections require.
- Visual object: A decision-tree flowchart: discover wrongdoing → internal report → document response → outcome branches (resolved / not resolved → external disclosure → legal outcome / career outcome). The career-outcome node is always "cost."
- Manim move: trace (path through the decision tree)
- Example seed: Sherron Watkins, August 2001: she reads the Enron accounting documents and writes a memo to Chairman Lay warning the company will "implode in a wave of accounting scandals." Lay convenes an investigation by the same law firm that structured the questioned transactions. The investigation concludes the transactions are defensible. By December, Enron files for bankruptcy. Watkins later says she would never hold a job in corporate America again.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: c2v (Watkins congressional testimony reference), raster (decision-tree flowchart)
- Prerequisites: what a publicly traded company is; what the SEC is
- Exclusions: no full insider-trading framework, no FCPA detail, no gift-policy discussion
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 07 — One Bystander Video Turned Ten Million People Into Stakeholders in Four Hours
- Source: `mba-business-ethics/chapters/03-defining-and-prioritizing-stakeholders.md`
- Topic: BUSINESS ETHICS
- Hook: Two men were arrested in a Starbucks. Starbucks closed 8,000 stores and trained 175,000 employees. The math only makes sense if you understand who Starbucks was actually responding to.
- Key case: Starbucks Philadelphia, April 2018. Two men arrested; video posted; 10 million views same day. Starbucks' response dwarfed the incident's direct victims because the actual stakeholder demanding response was a diffused public — millions of nonpublic stakeholders who became an active public in one afternoon.
- The Question: Why did a two-person arrest force an eight-thousand-store response — and what does that gap reveal about who a company's real stakeholders are?
- Core idea: A diffused stakeholder (anyone with a phone and a social-media account) can be a nonpublic one morning and an active organized public by afternoon; the firm that has only mapped its enabling, normative, and functional stakeholders has not mapped its risk. The Grunig-Hunt awareness taxonomy (nonpublic → latent → aware → active) describes the transition mechanism; it is not theoretical — it is the mechanism behind every large corporate crisis of the last decade.
- Visual object: Four concentric rings (enabling, normative, functional, diffused). Then a single bystander-phone icon in the diffused ring triggers a "spread" animation where the outer ring lights up from a single node, filling the ring in seconds.
- Manim move: spread (activation propagates through the diffused ring from one point)
- Example seed: The manager at 18th and Spruce was not thinking about diffused stakeholders. She was thinking about a store policy. The bystander was a diffused stakeholder who was a nonpublic until she pressed record. Ten million views later, she was the mechanism that forced the largest single diversity-training event in U.S. retail history.
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: geo (18th and Spruce, Philadelphia), c2v (Starbucks exterior), raster (concentric-ring diagram)
- Prerequisites: what a stakeholder is; no prior business vocabulary
- Exclusions: no power-interest matrix, no triple bottom line, no Strider case, no greenwashing concept
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 08 — Every Major Ethical Tradition Agrees on Five Things. Only Five.
- Source: `mba-business-ethics/chapters/05-the-impact-of-culture-and-time-on-business-ethics.md`
- Topic: BUSINESS ETHICS
- Hook: Four philosophical traditions and six major religions arrived at the same five business-ethics commitments — from completely different starting assumptions.
- Key case: The thin universal core (honesty, non-coercion, fair dealing, stewardship, treating persons as ends) vs. thick local norms (what counts as fair, how commitments are signaled, gift norms, time allocation). The Petrobras / Lava Jato case: the cultural defense (*jeitinho*) attempted to cover multi-billion-dollar kickbacks. The Manhattan 1626 case: two parties each acting in good faith, each with a coherent ethical framework, each believing the deal honored their obligations.
- The Question: If cultures genuinely disagree about ethics, how do you know when "that's how things are done here" is legitimate cultural sensitivity — and when it is a cover for violating the floor that no culture actually sanctions?
- Core idea: There is a thin universal core that travels across every serious ethical tradition, and a thick local layer that specifies how the core is operationalized in a particular culture. The error mode in one direction is imposing the home culture as universal (Pieter Minuit, 1626). The error mode in the other direction is treating local norms as suspending the thin core (Petrobras, 2014). The discipline is to hold the boundary between the two.
- Visual object: A Venn diagram with overlapping circles for each major tradition; the intersection zone (the thin core) labeled with the five items; the non-overlapping zones labeled "thick local"
- Manim move: morph (circles assemble and overlap, revealing the intersection)
- Example seed: Judaism calls it *zhōngxìn*. Christianity prohibits false witness. Islam codifies *amana* (trustworthiness). Kant calls truth-telling a categorical duty. Bentham calls it a precondition of any utility calculation. Rawls derives it from the original position. Every tradition, from a different direction, arrives at: don't lie in a transaction. The form varies. The prohibition is constant.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: raster (Venn diagram), c2v (Manhattan island satellite / historical illustration)
- Prerequisites: awareness that different cultures have different norms; no philosophy background
- Exclusions: no Hofstede cultural dimensions, no Jillian Ramadan case, no full mercantile-to-information era timeline
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 09 — The Personality That Built the Company Became the Structure That Nearly Killed It
- Source: `mba-business-ethics/chapters/09-professions-under-the-microscope.md`
- Topic: BUSINESS ETHICS
- Hook: Susan Fowler's blog post appeared February 19. Travis Kalanick was gone by June. Four months. Uber's HR department had the same information for two years and moved zero distance.
- Key case: Uber / Susan Fowler 2017. Fowler's internal reports: filed, documented, retroactively downgraded. Fowler's external post: one blog, millions of reads, CEO removed in four months. The founder-culture paradox: the intensity that produced early success became the culture that absorbed every complaint, because both were expressions of the same personality.
- The Question: Why did Uber's formal HR apparatus — which existed, which received the reports, which produced documentation — fail to do what one blog post accomplished in four months?
- Core idea: In early-stage firms, the founder's personality is the culture; bureaucracy layers on top of it but cannot override it because the founder's authority runs through informal channels below the formal structure. The correction mechanism is external pressure, not internal process, because internal process answers to the founder. The firms that install genuine override capacity before the external pressure arrives — by empowering boards or professional management with real authority — produce smoother corrections.
- Visual object: Two parallel vertical tracks — "formal HR channel" (wide, labeled, documented, slow) and "external pressure" (narrow at first, then exploding to wide) — with a timeline showing the internal track stopped after two years and the external track completed in four months
- Manim move: compare (two parallel tracks animate simultaneously from the same start date)
- Example seed: Fowler's manager propositioned her on her first day. She reported it. HR told her he was a high performer and it was a "one-time mistake." She continued to report. Her own performance reviews were retroactively downgraded. By the time she left, the engineering org had gone from 25% women to 6%. One post changed everything the apparatus had not.
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: c2v (Uber HQ San Francisco), raster (two-track timeline diagram)
- Prerequisites: what HR is; what a startup founder is
- Exclusions: no advertising section, no insurance/healthcare section, no Weber bureaucracy detail
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 10 — The Firm That Recognized the Right Before the Supreme Court Did Won the Decade
- Source: `mba-business-ethics/chapters/08-recognizing-and-respecting-the-rights-of-all.md`
- Topic: BUSINESS ETHICS
- Hook: North Carolina passed a law. PayPal canceled a 400-person expansion. Deutsche Bank halted a 250-person expansion. The NBA moved its All-Star Game. The law was partially repealed in twelve months.
- Key case: North Carolina H.B. 2, 2016: corporate withdrawal produced estimated $3.7B economic loss within one year, partial repeal within twelve months. *Bostock v. Clayton County*, 2020: Supreme Court extends Title VII to sexual orientation and gender identity — seven years after Aimee Stephens was fired for being transgender. McKinsey Diversity Matters (2015, 2018, 2020, 2023): top-quartile diversity firms 15–35% more likely to exceed industry median financial performance.
- The Question: Aimee Stephens's case gave legal protection to millions of workers she would never meet — but she died before the ruling. The firms that had already built that protection didn't need the Court to tell them. What is the mechanism that lets some firms see the next rights recognition before it is legally required?
- Core idea: Workplace rights follow a consistent pattern: specific people absorb years of personal cost to force recognition that eventually becomes law; firms that anticipate the recognition before the ruling incur a short-run cost and capture a long-run asset (retention, talent, brand); firms that wait for the ruling incur a larger, later cost. The pattern is structural and has repeated across disability accommodation, gender pay equity, LGBTQ+ rights, and minimum-wage floors. The mechanism for early recognition: treat rights as expanding rather than fixed.
- Visual object: A timeline of rights expansions (disability, equal pay, LGBTQ+, minimum wage) with two curves — "firms that led" (cost concentrated early, durable long-run gain) and "firms that trailed" (low short-run cost, high long-run cost) — diverging rightward from each expansion date
- Manim move: trace (timeline progresses left to right, two curves diverge from each event marker)
- Example seed: Stephens wrote the letter in spring 2013. She was fired two weeks later. Her case moved through the EEOC, the federal courts, and the Supreme Court for seven years. She died in May 2020, weeks before the ruling. The firms that had already written that protection into their operative culture before June 2020 did not need the Court. They were already there.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: geo (Detroit, Harris Funeral Homes area), c2v (Supreme Court exterior), raster (two-curve divergence chart)
- Prerequisites: awareness of Title VII / employment discrimination; no legal background needed
- Exclusions: no animal rights section, no NJ/CA equal pay statute detail, no Fight for $15 legislative history
- Score: 8/10
