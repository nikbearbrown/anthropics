## The puzzle of international politics being different

Domestic politics has a final authority. Inside a state, when two parties disagree, there is — eventually — some entity that can resolve the dispute: a court, a legislature, a president, an election. The dispute may be slow and ugly, but there's an arbiter.

International politics doesn't have this. There is no world government. No supreme international court whose rulings are reliably enforced against major powers. No global police. The closest equivalents — the UN Security Council, the International Criminal Court, the WTO dispute resolution body — operate by consent of the states they're meant to govern. A state that disagrees with an international ruling can usually ignore it.

This condition is called **anarchy** in international relations theory. Not anarchy in the sense of chaos — many international interactions are structured, predictable, and law-governed. Anarchy in the technical sense: the absence of a higher authority that states are subject to.

Anarchy has consequences that don't apply inside states.

**The security dilemma.** A state that builds up its military to defend itself can't perfectly signal "this is purely defensive." Other states have to assume the buildup might be offensive and respond by building up themselves. Both states end up worse off — more spent on militaries, more risk of war — even though neither wanted that outcome.

**Self-help.** A state under attack can't reliably call the police; it has to defend itself or build alliances. The need for self-help shapes how states allocate resources and choose partners.

**Cooperation as choice.** Cooperation between states happens, but it has to be sustained — by repeated interaction, by mutual interest, by formal agreements that include enforcement mechanisms (often weak), by hegemonic leadership. International cooperation is harder to maintain than domestic cooperation precisely because there's no overarching enforcer if it breaks down.

These features explain why international relations needs theoretical frameworks that don't apply (or apply differently) inside states.

## Why Part 5 is the hardest

A confession that's worth making explicit: the international scale is harder to study than the domestic scale, for several reasons.

**Smaller-N.** There are about 200 states; there are about 8 billion individuals. Statistical methods that work for individual-level analysis (large samples) can't work the same way for state-level analysis (small samples).

**Each case has weight.** When you study the US 2020 election, your sample is one election, but the within-election data is rich (millions of voters, hundreds of thousands of precincts). When you study the Russia-Ukraine war, your sample is one war, and the within-war data is murky and contested. State-level analysis often relies on small numbers of cases studied in depth, with all the limitations that implies.

**Strategic interaction matters more.** Inside a state, most actors take the institutional environment as given and act within it. Internationally, actors are continuously trying to shape the environment, anticipate each other's moves, and signal credibility. Game theory becomes essential — and so do its limits, since real actors aren't always rational.

**Identity and norms shift over time.** What counts as a "great power" varies. What counts as legitimate behavior changes. The post-1945 international order is being renegotiated in real time. Theory has to track these shifts.

These difficulties are real but not paralyzing. The frameworks in Part 5 are the field's best efforts to make sense of an irreducibly complex domain. They illuminate enough to be useful, while remaining honest about their limits.

## Three concepts that thread through Part 5

### 1. Sovereignty

The Westphalian system (named after the 1648 Peace of Westphalia) is built on **sovereignty** — the principle that each state has supreme authority within its territory and that no external authority can interfere with its internal affairs.

Sovereignty is a legal fiction more than a fact. Many "sovereign" states have limited control over their own territory (failed states), or are subject to substantial external influence through trade, finance, military presence, or international organizations. But the fiction is consequential. When the US imposes sanctions on Russia, the formal claim is that Russia remains sovereign — the sanctions are about cross-border interactions. When China cracks down in Hong Kong, the legal claim is that Hong Kong is part of China. Sovereignty as legal principle constrains what international actors can openly demand.

The principle is also under strain. Climate change, pandemics, financial flows, and information networks all cross borders in ways that traditional sovereignty doesn't accommodate well. Part of contemporary IR is the negotiation between Westphalian sovereignty and these cross-cutting flows.

### 2. The international system

The arrangement of states relative to each other constitutes the **international system**. Some systems are **unipolar** (one dominant state — like the US in the 1990s). Some are **bipolar** (two roughly equal poles — Cold War US/USSR). Some are **multipolar** (several roughly equal great powers — pre-WWI Europe).

System polarity shapes behavior. In bipolarity, alliances tend to be tight and stable (you're with one side or the other). In multipolarity, alliances tend to be flexible (yesterday's enemy is tomorrow's ally). In unipolarity, lesser powers can free-ride on the hegemon or hedge.

The current international system is contested terrain. Unipolar US dominance from 1991 has eroded. Whether the emerging system is bipolar (US/China), multipolar (US/China/EU/India/Russia), or "non-polar" (no clear hierarchy) is a live debate in IR theory.

### 3. Cooperation under anarchy

Despite anarchy, states cooperate constantly — on trade, climate, health, narcotics, financial flows, refugee management. Why?

Three mechanisms.

**Mutual interest.** When cooperation makes both parties better off and the gains are stable, cooperation persists without much external structure. Trade between democracies is mostly like this.

**Repeated interaction.** When states interact repeatedly, the prospect of future cooperation makes current defection less attractive. Tit-for-tat behavior in trade and diplomacy stabilizes cooperation even without enforcement.

**Institutional structure.** Treaties, organizations, and procedures lower the cost of monitoring compliance and raise the cost of defection. The WTO's dispute resolution body can't force a state to comply, but it can authorize retaliation that makes non-compliance costly. The UN can't force a state to follow Security Council resolutions, but the diplomatic costs of being branded a violator are real.

The puzzle of cooperation under anarchy is the central engine of liberal IR theory and one of the most-studied questions in the field. Chapters 19 and 20 explore it in depth.

## What you should bring to Part 5

Three habits.

**1. Take theoretical frameworks seriously without committing to one.** The realist, liberal, and constructivist traditions each illuminate different phenomena. A skilled analyst applies the right framework to the right question, not a single framework to everything.

**2. Distinguish what states say from what they do.** International behavior is full of strategic communication. Sanctions get announced for domestic audiences while their bite gets calibrated to maintain economic relationships. Treaties get signed and then ignored when convenient. Read the policy and the action separately.

**3. Track the gap between what international institutions can do and what they're authorized to do.** Many international bodies have formal authority but limited capacity. Others have real capacity but formal restraints. The interesting cases are usually in this gap.
