## The puzzle of your phone

If you own a smartphone, you are involved in politics, and politics is involved with you. This isn't metaphor.

Whether Facebook works on your phone depends on where you live. China bans it. So does Iran. Russia restricts it. Whether your phone works at all depends on government decisions, too. Before Uganda's 2021 election, the government simply turned off the country's internet for several days.[^1] In India, the government uses phones as the primary delivery mechanism for social benefits — direct transfers tied to a national identity number.[^2] In the United States, the FCC sets rules for what radio frequencies your phone can use, the FDA sets rules for what radiation it can emit, and the FTC sets rules for what data the apps on it can collect about you.

[^1]: "Uganda Blocks Social Media Ahead of Tense Election," *Africanews*, January 12, 2021.
[^2]: Alan Elb, Anit Mukherjee, and Brian Webster, *Delivering Social Assistance during COVID with a "Digital-First" Approach: Lessons from India* (Center for Global Development, July 22, 2021).

Your phone also enables you to do politics. You can use it to call your representative, read about a policy debate, organize a campaign through WhatsApp, share videos of police interactions, or move money to a political cause through Venmo. The same device that the political system regulates is the device through which you participate.

This dual character — politics acts on you; you act through politics — is what makes the topic worth studying. The phone in your pocket is a piece of compressed political history: who decided that you could buy this device, who decided what it could do, who decided what you can use it for, who decided what happens when you use it badly. Chapter 2 — and most of this book — is about how decisions like that get made, and by whom.

## Concept 1 — What politics is

The discipline's most-quoted working definition came from Harold Lasswell in 1950: politics is **"who gets what, when, how."**[^3] Five words. They cover an enormous amount of ground.

[^3]: Harold D. Lasswell, *Politics: Who Gets What, When, How* (P. Smith, 1950). The book had been published in 1936; the 1950 edition is the one usually cited.

**Who** — different actors compete for outcomes (individuals, groups, parties, regions, nations).
**Gets what** — there are scarce things and contested values being allocated.
**When** — timing matters; an outcome delivered too late may not count.
**How** — the rules and processes that determine the allocation.

Politics, in this view, is wherever people interact to make decisions that affect them collectively. It includes governments and elections, but also extends to families, workplaces, religious communities, and any other setting where collective decisions get made. When a family decides where to live, who attends which school, what to spend money on — politics. When a workplace decides who gets promoted, what hours to keep, who gets the corner office — politics. The discipline of *political science* tends to focus more narrowly on government-centered politics, but the underlying activity is broader.

Aristotle, twenty-three centuries before Lasswell, said something compatible: humans are "political animals," and we reach our highest potential through political activity.[^4] Lasswell's definition is more cynical — it foregrounds the conflict over scarce resources rather than the human flourishing — but the underlying claim is similar: politics is fundamental to how humans live together.

[^4]: Aristotle, *Politics* (4th c. BCE), Book I. See Fred Miller, "Aristotle's Political Theory," *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* (Winter 2017).

### The three elements: rules, reality, choices

Every political event has three components. Get all three on the table and the event becomes analyzable.

**Rules.** The instructions that say what behaviors are required, forbidden, or allowed. Rules can be **de jure** (formally written down — laws, constitutions, regulations) or **de facto** (the rules that actually govern behavior, even if they aren't formally written). The two are often different.

A simple example: until recently, the US Air Force required pilots to be between 5'4" and 6'5". The de jure rule was a height range; the de facto effect was that 40 percent of American women — too short to qualify — were excluded from being pilots, regardless of their other qualifications.[^5] The rule was changed; the gap between formal and actual effects had been there for decades before anyone noticed it as a problem worth fixing.

[^5]: "Air Force Pilot Height Qualifications and Waivers," US Air Force, AETC, November 5, 2019.

Constitutions are the most consequential rules. They vary enormously: Monaco's constitution runs under 4,000 words; India's runs about 150,000.[^6] Some countries (Canada, New Zealand, the United Kingdom) don't have a single written constitution at all — instead, foundational rules are scattered across multiple documents, statutes, and conventions. Some countries' "basic law" (Germany, Oman, Saudi Arabia) functions as a constitution under a different name.

[^6]: Sophy Owuor, "Which Country Has the Shortest Written Constitution?" World Atlas, December 21, 2018.

**Reality.** What actually exists — the verifiable facts of the situation. The number of votes cast in an election. The amount of money in a candidate's campaign account. Who is currently in office. What has just happened in the news cycle. Reality is the input the political actors are operating on.

In a chess game, the rules are constant and the reality (board state) changes as the game progresses. In politics, both can change at once: actors play within rules, but they can also try to change the rules mid-game. The 2021 redistricting cycle in the US was not just a fight within rules but a fight over what the rules would be.

**Choices.** Given the rules and the current reality, political actors make strategic decisions. To run or not run. To compromise or hold out. To vote for or against. Choices are where individual agency meets the structural constraints set by rules and reality.

Outcomes follow from the interaction of all three. They aren't predictable with certainty — humans aren't deterministic — but they're often reasonably predictable when you account for all three at once. "Who is likely to win the next US presidential election?" is a question an analyst can answer probabilistically by examining: rules (Electoral College, primary system), reality (current approval, economy, candidates), and choices (each candidate's strategy, each major actor's position).

For the running project, the rules-reality-choices framework returns repeatedly. When you analyze any political situation in your project — a policy fight, an election, a regime transition — diagnosing it well means having all three on the table.

### Common misconceptions

- *"Politics is just bargaining."* Bargaining is one mechanism. Coercion, persuasion, public mobilization, and sheer institutional friction are others. We'll meet all of them.
- *"Politics is a zero-sum game."* Often it is — when one side wins, the other loses. But politics also produces positive-sum outcomes: trade agreements that benefit both parties, public goods that benefit everyone. The distinction matters.

## Concept 2 — Power, authority, legitimacy, sovereignty

To analyze politics you need a small core vocabulary. Four terms do most of the work.

### Power

**Power** is the ability to compel someone to do something they would not otherwise choose to do.[^7] That's the canonical definition, from Robert Dahl in 1957. It's deceptively simple.

[^7]: Robert A. Dahl, "The Concept of Power," *Behavioral Science* 2, no. 3 (1957): 201–215.

Three properties make power tricky to study.

**Power is invisible.** You cannot weigh it, photograph it, or count it directly. You can only infer it from observed compliance. When someone does what someone else wants, power may be operating, or compliance may be coincidence, or the compliant party may have wanted to do it anyway.

**Power is not constant.** It increases and decreases over time as circumstances change. A US president has substantial power on Inauguration Day; less six months later as approval drops; less still in the final year of a second term. Same person, same office, different power.

**Power is partly a matter of belief.** If you believe someone has power over you, they often effectively do — the belief itself produces compliance. If everyone simultaneously decided that a particular ruler had no power, the ruler would lose much of their power immediately. Authoritarian regimes invest heavily in maintaining the *appearance* of power because the appearance and the reality are partly the same thing.

### Authority and legitimacy

**Authority** is power that's recognized as legitimate by those subject to it. The police officer pulling you over for speeding has authority. The criminal threatening you on the street has power without authority — you might comply, but you don't recognize the right.

**Legitimacy** is the further property: authority is being used in ways consistent with the institution's rules and purposes. A legitimate police officer pulls people over for actual traffic violations under proper procedures. An officer who pulls people over for "driving while Black" is exercising the same formal authority but is not exercising it legitimately.[^8]

[^8]: David A. Harris, "The Stories, the Statistics, and the Law: Why Driving While Black Matters," *Minnesota Law Review* 84 (1999): 265.

The distinction matters because legitimacy is what makes political systems function over time. A government that consistently exercises authority illegitimately erodes its own foundation — citizens stop complying voluntarily, enforcement costs rise, the system breaks down. Most political conflict is, at some level, a conflict over what counts as legitimate.

### Sovereignty

**Sovereignty** is the highest form of power: ultimate authority within a territory, with no other entity having the right to override. A sovereign state can pass laws, raise armies, sign treaties, and recognize other states. By definition, no external entity has the right to interfere with these decisions — though external powers often do anyway, through influence, sanctions, or force.

In monarchical systems, sovereignty rests with the monarch. In democratic systems, the people are sovereign — they elect leaders, can remove them through elections or impeachment, and ultimately authorize what the government does.

The concept matters for international relations. We'll come back to it in Chapter 17 and Chapter 19, when sovereignty becomes the organizing principle of the international system.

### Country, state, nation, nation-state

These four terms get used loosely in everyday speech. They have specific technical meanings in political science.

**Country** — a populated geographic territory with a sovereign government. About 190 of these in the world today. Some cases are disputed (Taiwan, Palestine, Western Sahara) — they have territory and population, but their sovereign status isn't universally recognized.

**State** — generally a synonym for country in political-science usage ("nation-state," "state actors"). In US usage, "state" also means a sub-national political unit (California, Texas), and context decides which meaning applies. India is a *state* in the country sense; California is a *state* in the sub-national sense.

**Nation** — a population united by some combination of shared history, culture, language, religion, or political identity, occupying a particular territory. The Kurdish people are a nation — about 30 million people sharing language and culture — but they don't have their own country.[^9] Their territory ("Kurdistan") spans parts of Turkey, Iraq, Iran, and Syria.

[^9]: Erin Blakemore, "Today, the Kurds Are Spread Across Four Nations," *National Geographic*, August 16, 2019.

**Nation-state** — a country whose population is dominantly one nation. Pakistan, France, Japan, Germany are nation-states (roughly). The United States, Canada, India, and most countries in the world are *not* pure nation-states — they're multi-national countries where multiple nations share a single political system.

The distinction matters. Multi-national countries have specific political tensions (regional separatism, language politics, federal-national conflict) that nation-states usually don't. Many of the 20th and 21st century's most consequential political conflicts have been about whether a particular nation deserves its own state.

### Government and the legitimate use of force

A specific point worth making explicit: in modern political systems, the **government** is the only institution with the legal, legitimate authority to use coercive power within its territory.

A family can punish rule-breaking; only government imprisons people. A church can ask for money; only government compels taxes through legal process. A business can fire employees; only government can require military service or jury duty. Other institutions exercise various forms of power — economic, social, religious — but only government holds the monopoly on legitimate coercion.

This is what makes government different from other social institutions. It's also what makes politics — the contest over how government uses that monopoly — so consequential.

### Common misconceptions

- *"Power and authority are the same thing."* Authority is power that has been legitimated. Power without authority can produce compliance through fear; authority produces compliance through recognition.
- *"All states are nation-states."* Most aren't. Multi-national countries are the rule, not the exception.

## Concept 3 — What political science does

So far we've talked about politics. Political science is the systematic study of politics. The "systematic" matters — it distinguishes the discipline from punditry, opinion-writing, and political activism.

### Empirical and normative

Political science divides into two main modes.

**Empirical political science** asks: *what is happening, and why?* Its tools are data, hypotheses, statistics, comparison, and observation. Its claims are testable in principle. "Voter turnout among college-educated Americans is higher than turnout among Americans without a college degree" is an empirical claim — you can verify or refute it with data.

**Normative political science** (also called political theory or political philosophy) asks: *what should we do, and why?* Its tools are logic, argument, conceptual analysis, and moral reasoning. Its claims aren't testable the same way. "Voting should be compulsory" is a normative claim — there's a defensible argument for it and a defensible argument against, and which view a person holds depends on their underlying values.

Both modes are legitimate. Most working political scientists do empirical work; theorists handle the normative side. Both modes interact: empirical findings often inform normative conclusions ("if compulsory voting in fact increases turnout without harming democracy, that's relevant to whether we should adopt it"), and normative commitments often shape empirical work ("we study turnout because we think turnout matters").

### What kind of science is it?

A reasonable critique: political "science" doesn't look like physics. There are no universal laws like F = ma. Predictions fail. The same conditions can produce different outcomes.

The response: science doesn't require universal laws. What it requires is the **scientific method** — a way of learning about the world by:

1. Forming testable hypotheses.
2. Systematically collecting data.
3. Making hypotheses and data available for challenge or verification.
4. Updating beliefs based on what survives.

By that standard, political science is science. It just deals with phenomena that are **probabilistic**, not **deterministic**.

A deterministic claim says: *if X, then Y*, always. The sun rises in the east. F = ma.

A probabilistic claim says: *if X, then Y is more likely.* Educated voters are more likely to turn out. Established democracies are more likely to peacefully transition power. Major-party incumbents are more likely than challengers to win re-election.[^10] None of these is "always." All are useful generalizations that survive systematic testing.

[^10]: Jamie L. Carson et al., "Nationalization and the Incumbency Advantage," *Political Research Quarterly* 73, no. 1 (March 2020): 156–168.

The discipline gets in trouble when it forgets the probabilistic nature of its claims and treats generalizations as if they were laws. Predictions fail; surprised pundits act as if the field has been embarrassed; the next generalization gets stated more cautiously. This is normal science under conditions of irreducible uncertainty.

### Generalizations and stereotypes

Political science generalizes about groups: voters of one demographic, members of one party, citizens of one regime type. These generalizations are useful — they describe patterns that hold on average — but they're easy to misuse.

**Generalization (legitimate):** "On average, voters with college degrees are more likely to vote than voters without."

**Stereotype (illegitimate):** "Any specific person without a college degree is unlikely to vote, and any specific college graduate is likely to vote."

The first is a probabilistic statement about averages. The second uses the average to predict individuals. The leap from one to the other is where many political analyses (and many social judgments more broadly) go wrong.

A reader who can hold this distinction firmly — averages describe groups, not individuals — is better equipped to think politically than one who can't. Through this book, when you encounter empirical claims about parties, regions, demographics, or countries, hold them as group averages, not individual predictions.

### Motivated reasoning — the discipline studying itself

A specific finding political science has to apply to its own field: humans engage in **motivated reasoning** — they decide what's true first (often based on what they want to be true), then find supporting evidence, then reject contradictory data.[^11] The Big Tobacco industry's decades-long denial of nicotine's addictiveness, despite mounting evidence, is the classic case.[^12]

[^11]: Joseph M. Pierre, "The Psychology of Guns: Risk, Fear, and Motivated Reasoning," *Palgrave Communications* 5, no. 1 (December 2019): 1–7.
[^12]: Stanton A. Glantz, *The Cigarette Papers* (UC Press, 1996).

Political scientists are not immune. The empirical research that survives review survives partly because the discipline forces explicit confrontation with disconfirming evidence. The discipline also has its blind spots — areas where dominant assumptions go unexamined. Reading political science well means reading critically, including reading critical accounts of the field's own biases.

### Common misconceptions

- *"Political science is just opinion dressed up as research."* Some popular political commentary is. Peer-reviewed political science isn't — its claims are explicitly testable, and bad claims get refuted.
- *"If political science can't predict elections accurately, it's failed."* Many political-science predictions are calibrated probabilities, not point predictions. A prediction that says "Candidate A has a 60% chance of winning" is not falsified by Candidate B winning — it's only falsified if the prediction was systematically miscalibrated across many such cases.

## Synthesis — what this chapter installed

Three concepts to carry forward.

**1. Politics is "who gets what, when, how" — the activity of resolving disputes about scarce resources and contested values, ideally without violence.** The rules-reality-choices framework lets you analyze any political event by asking what rules govern it, what reality the actors are responding to, and what choices they're making.

**2. Power, authority, legitimacy, and sovereignty are the core vocabulary.** Power compels. Authority is recognized power. Legitimacy is authority used consistently with the institution's rules. Sovereignty is the highest form of authority — ultimate within a territory.

**3. Political science is the systematic study of politics — partly empirical (what is), partly normative (what should be), mostly probabilistic (not deterministic).** Generalizations describe groups, not individuals. Motivated reasoning is real and applies to the discipline itself.

The rest of the book builds on this vocabulary. Part 2 examines how individuals engage with politics. Part 3 examines how groups operate. Part 4 examines institutions. Part 5 examines states and the international system.

## Chapter summary

- **Politics** is "who gets what, when, how" — the activity of resolving disputes about scarce resources and contested values.
- Every political event has three elements: **rules** (de jure and de facto), **reality** (verifiable facts), **choices** (strategic decisions).
- **Power** is the ability to compel; **authority** is recognized power; **legitimacy** is authority used consistently with the rules; **sovereignty** is the highest form of authority.
- **Country, state, nation, nation-state** mean different things. Many countries are not nation-states.
- **Political science** is the systematic study of politics, dividing into **empirical** (what is) and **normative** (what should be) modes.
- Political-science claims are mostly **probabilistic**, not deterministic. Generalizations describe groups, not individuals.

## What would change my mind

This chapter argues that politics is best understood through the rules-reality-choices framework and that political science's probabilistic mode is real science. The reading would have to revise if (a) a competing framework consistently outperformed rules-reality-choices in explaining specific political phenomena (some scholars argue cultural or structural frameworks do better in certain contexts; the principles-course consensus remains rules-reality-choices), or (b) probabilistic political-science predictions failed to outperform random guessing in calibrated long-run testing (they don't, by a substantial margin, but the gap is smaller than many practitioners admit).

## Still puzzling

The genuinely hard question this chapter sets up is *whether the line between empirical and normative political science can be held cleanly in practice.* The textbook position is that it can — empirical claims are testable, normative claims aren't. But empirical work is shaped by normative commitments (which questions get studied?), and normative arguments often draw on empirical findings (what would the policy actually do?). The cleaner the line, the easier to teach. The truth is messier. Honest political scientists name their normative commitments rather than pretend their empirical work is value-free.
