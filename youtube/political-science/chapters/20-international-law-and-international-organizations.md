**TL;DR.** International law is a peculiar kind of law — it has no global police force to enforce it, yet states usually comply with it. International organizations (the UN, WTO, IMF, ICC, EU) are the institutions that try to make the rules work. Both exist because states found that binding themselves to common rules can serve their interests better than pure self-help. The system is fragile, partial, and remarkably consequential.

---

In December 2017, the U.S. Trade Representative announced that the United States would no longer participate in the appointment of judges to the World Trade Organization's Appellate Body — the panel of judges that hears appeals in trade disputes between WTO members. Without new appointments, the Appellate Body would, by attrition, lose its quorum and stop being able to issue rulings.

The argument for the U.S. position was that the Appellate Body had been overreaching — interpreting WTO rules in ways the U.S. believed went beyond what member states had agreed to. The argument against was that the WTO dispute settlement system, of which the Appellate Body was the second tier, was the largest and most successful experiment in binding international adjudication in history. Hundreds of trade disputes had been settled through it since 1995. Without the Appellate Body, the system was effectively broken.

By December 2019, the Appellate Body had only one judge remaining. It could no longer hear appeals. Trade disputes that reached the appellate stage were left in legal limbo — countries could file appeals "into the void," knowing no ruling would issue. Five years later, despite intermittent negotiations, the system has not been restored.

This is one episode in a longer story. International law and international organizations are at once enormously consequential and structurally fragile. They depend on the consent of the states that participate in them. When a powerful state withdraws cooperation, the institution can be paralyzed. And yet the institutions persist, evolve, and shape state behavior in ways that pure realism would not predict. Understanding why requires understanding what international law actually is, where it comes from, and what international organizations do.

## Concept 1 — What international law is and where it comes from

International law is the body of legal rules that governs relations among states (and increasingly among states and other actors — international organizations, individuals in some contexts). It differs from domestic law in three big ways: there is no global legislature to make it, no global executive to enforce it, and no compulsory global court to interpret it. Every state can choose whether to participate in any given rule. And yet the body of rules exists and most states comply with most rules most of the time.

The Statute of the International Court of Justice (Article 38) lists four sources of international law:

**Treaties.** Written agreements between states. The UN Charter, the Geneva Conventions, the WTO agreements, the Paris Climate Agreement, bilateral trade and defense treaties — thousands of them. A treaty binds the states that are party to it. Most international law in any specific area is treaty-based. Interpretation of treaties follows specific principles laid out in the 1969 Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties.

**Customary international law.** Rules that emerge from consistent state practice combined with *opinio juris* — the belief that the practice is legally required, not just politically convenient. Diplomatic immunity is customary law; the prohibition on torture has become customary law; the freedom of the high seas is customary law. The standard is high — a rule has to be widely practiced and widely accepted as legal — but customary law genuinely binds states even without a treaty.

**General principles of law.** Principles common to most domestic legal systems — good faith, due process, prohibition on retroactive criminal law. These fill gaps where treaty and custom are silent.

**Judicial decisions and the writings of qualified scholars.** Listed by the ICJ as "subsidiary means" — they are evidence of what the law is, not sources of law in themselves.

The relative weight of these sources varies by area. Trade law is heavily treaty-based (the WTO agreements). Diplomatic law is heavily customary. Human rights law is a mix (the major treaties — ICCPR, ICESCR, CAT, CRC — overlap with customary protections of fundamental rights). The lawyer's craft is partly knowing which sources govern which questions.

### *Jus ad bellum* and *jus in bello*

A specific area worth highlighting: the law on the use of force. Two main bodies of rules.

***Jus ad bellum*** — the law on when force can be used. The UN Charter (Article 2(4)) prohibits the threat or use of force against the territorial integrity or political independence of any state. The exceptions: self-defense (Article 51) and Security Council authorization (Chapter VII). Everything else is, in principle, illegal. Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine, Iraq's 1990 invasion of Kuwait, the U.S. 2003 invasion of Iraq (the legal justification for which was contested) — these are the cases where *jus ad bellum* gets argued.

***Jus in bello*** (international humanitarian law / law of armed conflict) — the law on how force can be used once a conflict is underway. The Geneva Conventions of 1949 and their Additional Protocols establish core protections: civilians cannot be deliberately targeted; prisoners of war must be treated humanely; certain weapons (chemical, biological, anti-personnel mines in some regions) are prohibited. *Jus in bello* applies regardless of who started the war or who is fighting justly; both sides in a conflict are bound by these rules.

The two bodies of law are analytically distinct. A war can be lawful under *jus ad bellum* (legitimate self-defense) but conducted unlawfully under *jus in bello* (war crimes). It can also be unlawful under *jus ad bellum* (illegal aggression) but conducted within *jus in bello* limits (no war crimes). Mixing the two produces sloppy analysis.

> ### Dig Deeper — The 2003 Iraq War's legal status
> The 2003 U.S.-led invasion of Iraq was justified by some governments as enforcement of prior UN Security Council resolutions; others argued it was illegal under the UN Charter. Ask Claude: "Walk me through the legal arguments for and against the 2003 invasion's compliance with international law. What did the UN Secretary-General Kofi Annan say? What did major international lawyers argue? Where does the consensus sit now?" The exercise illuminates how international law gets argued in real cases.

### The compliance puzzle

The big puzzle of international law: why do states comply with it as often as they do? There is no global police force. The ICJ can issue judgments but cannot enforce them. Sanctions for violations are inconsistent. And yet most treaties are observed most of the time. Diplomats are not arrested. Trade rules are followed. Most international law is, surprisingly, obeyed.

Several explanations:

**Reputation.** A state that violates international law signals to other states that it is an unreliable partner. Future cooperation suffers. The cost of violation is reputational.

**Reciprocity.** Most international law is mutual. If you violate the rules on diplomatic immunity, your diplomats face the same risks. The bilateral and multilateral character of the rules creates self-enforcing dynamics.

**Domestic incorporation.** Many states incorporate international obligations into domestic law. Treaty obligations are then enforced by domestic courts, domestic regulators, domestic legislatures. The "international" law operates partly through domestic channels.

**Internalization.** Over time, international rules become part of the legitimate normative framework that states and their officials accept. Compliance becomes automatic rather than calculated.

**Power asymmetry.** Powerful states sometimes violate the rules and pay no cost; weaker states are constrained. The system is not equally binding.

The empirical literature on compliance (Chayes and Chayes 1995; Goldsmith and Posner 2005; Simmons 2009) is large. Most scholars now agree that international law has real effects on state behavior — not because of central enforcement but through the combined operation of these mechanisms.

### Common misconceptions

**"International law isn't really law because there's no enforcement."** It is law in the sense that courts apply it, states cite it as binding, and most rules are followed. The lack of central enforcement makes it different from domestic law, not less of a law.

**"States only follow international law when it benefits them."** Partly true — but states often benefit from rules they did not specifically choose, and the mechanism of long-term cooperation often requires honoring rules that are inconvenient in specific cases. Pure interest-based accounts under-explain the compliance pattern.

**"International law is just whatever the powerful want."** Powerful states do shape the rules, and they often violate the rules with limited consequences. But powerful states are also constrained by the rules in important ways — the U.S. has lost WTO disputes and complied; major powers have changed policies in response to ICJ rulings; even great powers find it costly to be seen as serial law-violators.

## Concept 2 — The major international organizations

International organizations are the institutional vehicles through which much international law is made, applied, and enforced (such enforcement as exists). The major ones:

**The United Nations.** Founded 1945, membership now 193 states. The UN Charter is the constitutional document; the General Assembly (one state, one vote) and the Security Council (15 members, including 5 permanent with vetoes) are the main political bodies; the Secretariat is the bureaucracy; the International Court of Justice is the judicial body; specialized agencies (WHO, UNESCO, ILO, others) handle specific issue areas.

The UN's central function is the maintenance of international peace and security. The Security Council can authorize sanctions, peacekeeping, and (rarely) the use of force against states that threaten international peace. The veto-power structure (P5: U.S., UK, France, Russia, China) reflects the power configuration of 1945 and is the source of much of the system's frustration — when great powers disagree, the Security Council is often paralyzed.

The UN's record: real successes in peacekeeping (Mozambique, Liberia, Sierra Leone in some cases), in coordinating responses to humanitarian crises, in establishing global development goals, in normative leadership on issues like decolonization and human rights. Real failures in preventing genocide (Rwanda, Bosnia), in addressing major-power conflicts, in reforming itself to reflect post-1945 power shifts.

**The World Trade Organization.** Founded 1995 (succeeding the GATT system that began in 1947). Membership 164 states. Sets rules for international trade and provides a dispute settlement system for trade conflicts. The WTO's Appellate Body crisis (described in the opening) has weakened the system since 2019.

**The International Monetary Fund and World Bank.** Founded 1944 at Bretton Woods. The IMF lends to states facing balance-of-payments crises (often with conditions attached); the World Bank funds development projects. Both have been criticized — by the Global South for imposing conditions that reflect Northern economic priorities, by free-marketers for distorting markets, by progressives for under-attention to inequality and environmental costs.

**The International Criminal Court.** Founded 2002 by the Rome Statute. Prosecutes individuals (not states) for genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, and (since 2018) the crime of aggression. Membership 124 states. The U.S., China, Russia, and India are not parties. The ICC has issued arrest warrants for sitting heads of state (Bashir of Sudan, Putin of Russia) but has limited ability to actually apprehend them.

**Regional organizations.** The European Union (a much deeper integration than other regional bodies, with its own legislature, executive, court, and currency for most members). The African Union. ASEAN in Southeast Asia. Mercosur in South America. NATO as a security alliance. Each has different functions, different depths of integration, and different relationships with the global UN system.

### What international organizations do

International organizations do several things that bilateral diplomacy cannot do as easily.

**Reduce uncertainty.** They produce reports, monitor compliance, share information. States know more about each other's behavior than they would in their absence.

**Provide forums.** Negotiations happen in institutional settings with established procedures. The procedures themselves matter — the UN's one-state-one-vote vs. the IMF's weighted voting produces different outcomes.

**Generate norms.** The UN General Assembly's resolutions are not legally binding but contribute to customary international law over time. The treaties states sign through international organizations become part of the normative environment.

**Lock in commitments.** Once states have built institutions around a commitment, withdrawal is costly. The institutional embedding is part of what makes commitments credible.

**Coordinate solutions to collective action problems.** Climate change, pandemic response, financial stability, refugee crises — all require coordinated action. International organizations are the imperfect institutional vehicle for that coordination.

> ### Dig Deeper — UN Security Council reform
> The Security Council has been a subject of reform debate for decades. Ask Claude: "Walk me through the major Security Council reform proposals of the last 30 years. Who has been pushing for what? Why has reform been so hard? What would a reformed Council look like, and what would the trade-offs be?" The exercise reveals how institutional reform interacts with great-power politics.

### The trade-off

International organizations face a fundamental tension. To be effective, they need to constrain state behavior. To win state participation, they have to respect state sovereignty. Every IO sits somewhere on a spectrum between these poles, and the location matters.

**Highly state-respecting** (UN General Assembly): broad participation, weak enforcement.
**Moderately state-binding** (WTO, certain treaty bodies): substantive rules, formal dispute settlement, but participation is voluntary and exit is possible.
**Strongly binding** (EU, ICC for parties): substantive rule-making with real consequences, including the possibility of fines and (in the EU) judgments that supersede national law.

The trade-off: more state-binding institutions are more effective but harder to build and easier for powerful states to disrupt. Less state-binding institutions get broader participation but achieve less.

### Common misconceptions

**"The UN is a world government."** It is not. It has no taxing power, no military of its own (peacekeepers are contributed by member states), no police, no executive authority over states.

**"International organizations are just talk."** Some are; many are not. The WTO has settled hundreds of trade disputes. The IMF has restructured economies. The EU has built the deepest economic integration project in history. Treating all IOs as ineffective talk-shops misses the variation.

**"International organizations are tools of the West / the Global South."** Different organizations serve different coalitions. The UN General Assembly is a Global South majority body; the IMF/World Bank are dominated by their largest contributors (the U.S. and Europe historically); the WTO has more balanced membership; the ICC has been a frequent target of Global South complaint about selective prosecution. The systemic claim "the IOs are biased against X" usually requires more specification than is given.

## Concept 3 — The system's fragility and durability

The post-1945 international institutional system was the most ambitious experiment in international governance in history. It survived the Cold War, the post-Cold War unipolar moment, the rise of China, and recurring crises. It is now under pressure that has not been seen since its founding. Understanding what is durable and what is at risk is the central question for the field.

### What is durable

Several features have proven remarkably durable.

**The norm against territorial conquest.** Since 1945, conquest has been treated as illegitimate in a way it was not before. Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine was condemned by the UN General Assembly with very large majorities; Russia's annexations have been treated as illegal by most states. This is a genuine normative shift since the pre-1945 era when conquest was a regular feature of international politics.

**Diplomatic immunity and the basic infrastructure of relations.** Embassies operate; diplomats are protected; treaties on consular relations are followed. The everyday machinery of state-to-state relations works.

**Treaty-based cooperation in technical areas.** Aviation safety, postal services, telecommunications, weather forecasting — these are governed by international agreements that operate quietly and effectively across geopolitical divides.

**The trade rules' core commitments.** Even with the WTO's troubles, most international trade still follows MFN and national-treatment rules. Trade flows have continued through periods of geopolitical tension.

**Human rights as a normative framework.** Even when honored in the breach, human rights norms shape what states say about themselves and each other. The vocabulary of legitimate political discourse globally is shaped by post-1945 human rights instruments.

### What is at risk

Other features are under pressure.

**The Security Council's effectiveness.** Russia's veto on Ukraine, China's veto on Hong Kong, ongoing P5 disagreements have reduced what the Council can do. Without major reform — which is politically improbable — the Council will continue to be paralyzed on issues where great powers disagree.

**The WTO dispute settlement system.** The Appellate Body crisis has not been resolved. The dispute settlement mechanism that made the WTO different from earlier trade regimes is significantly weakened.

**The ICC's reach.** Major powers (U.S., China, Russia, India) are not parties. The Court's ability to enforce its rulings against non-parties is essentially nil. Its prosecutions remain disproportionately focused on African cases, fueling charges of selective enforcement.

**Climate cooperation.** The Paris Agreement's structure — voluntary national commitments — produces emissions trajectories well above what the science indicates is needed. Whether this counts as the system "working" or "failing" depends on what you measure.

**The post-1945 order's broad trajectory.** The center of economic and demographic gravity is shifting toward Asia. The institutions designed for a 1945 world reflect a different power configuration. Whether the institutions adapt or are replaced is a central question.

> ### Dig Deeper — Climate as a test of international cooperation
> Climate change is the largest collective action problem in international politics. Ask Claude: "Walk me through the evolution of international climate cooperation — the UNFCCC, Kyoto, Paris, the post-Paris national commitments. Why has each iteration been the institutional shape it has? What does the trajectory tell us about the limits of what international cooperation can achieve on a problem of this scale?"

### The trade-off — sovereignty vs. coordination

The deep tension underlying all of this: states want to coordinate on issues that cross borders, *and* they want to retain control over their own affairs. Every international institution sits on this spectrum, and the position is contested.

A more federalist approach (the EU's deeper integration, proposals for binding climate enforcement, the ICC's universal jurisdiction claims) buys more effective coordination at the cost of more constrained sovereignty.

A more sovereigntist approach (the UN General Assembly's non-binding resolutions, voluntary climate commitments, the WTO's exit provisions) preserves sovereignty at the cost of weaker coordination on shared problems.

Most actual institutions are mixed and contested. The argument about how much sovereignty to trade for coordination is not going to be resolved; it is the recurring negotiation that shapes the international system.

### Common misconceptions

**"International institutions are inevitable."** They are not. The League of Nations failed. The post-1945 system was a deliberate American-led construction that could have been built differently or not built at all. The system in 2050 will look very different from the system in 2025; what shape it takes is being decided now.

**"International law is mostly aspirational."** Some of it is. Much of it is operational and consequential — extradition treaties, trade rules, investment agreements, environmental protocols. Treating international law as universally aspirational misses how much of it actually shapes state behavior.

**"The U.S. is the indispensable upholder of the international order."** The U.S. built much of the post-1945 system and has selectively complied with parts of it. Other states' commitment to the system has often been firmer than U.S. commitment in some areas (climate, ICC). The "indispensable" framing reflects a specific U.S. self-understanding more than the empirical record.

## Synthesis — what the system can and cannot do

The honest reading of international law and international organizations: they do real work that bilateral diplomacy cannot do, and they have real limits that pure institutional optimism does not capture.

What the system can do: provide forums for negotiation, generate and codify norms, reduce uncertainty about state behavior, lock in commitments, coordinate technical cooperation, raise the cost of egregious violations, and over time shape what behaviors are seen as legitimate. The post-1945 international system has done all of these in ways the pre-1945 system did not.

What the system cannot do: enforce rules against great powers determined to violate them, coordinate effectively on issues where great powers disagree, override domestic political constraints when those constraints are strong, replace national governments as primary decision-makers on most issues. The gap between what the system can deliver and what advocates sometimes claim it can deliver is a persistent source of disappointment.

The serious student of international institutions holds three things together. First, they matter — more than realist skeptics predict and less than liberal optimists hope. Second, their effectiveness varies enormously by issue area, by membership, and by historical moment. Third, their long-run survival depends on continued state cooperation, which is not guaranteed.

The post-1945 system is being remade in real time. The 2020s are a transitional decade, and the institutional architecture of 2050 will reflect choices being made now about which existing institutions to defend, which to reform, which to replace, and what new ones to build. The political-science literature is racing to keep up with the reality. The serious citizen and the serious student of politics will be doing the same.

## Chapter summary

International law is the body of legal rules governing relations among states, derived from treaties, customary practice, general principles, and judicial-scholarly elaboration. It exists without a global sovereign and yet is largely complied with, through reputation, reciprocity, domestic incorporation, internalization, and selective use of power. International organizations are the institutional vehicles through which much international law is made and applied — the UN, WTO, IMF, ICC, regional bodies. The system has real successes (the norm against territorial conquest, the trade rules, technical cooperation) and real limits (great-power disputes paralyze the Security Council, exit options weaken commitments, sovereignty constrains what institutions can do). The post-1945 system is being remade; what survives, what is reformed, and what is replaced is the central question for the next several decades.

## What would change my mind

If a sustained period of international cooperation succeeded in addressing climate change at the scale science indicates is needed, I would update my reading of the system's capacity for collective action on hard problems. The current trajectory shows iterated cooperation falling short of what the science requires; that gap is, for me, the strongest evidence that the international institutional architecture is significantly under-powered relative to the challenges it faces. A clear reversal would change that.

## Still puzzling

I do not have a confident view on the long-run trajectory of the post-1945 institutional order. Possible outcomes range from gradual reform and adaptation, to slow erosion with selective survival of useful pieces, to dramatic restructuring with new institutions replacing old ones. Each scenario has supporters in the literature; the evidence for any one over the others is not conclusive. The answer will depend on choices made in the next two decades that have not yet been made.
