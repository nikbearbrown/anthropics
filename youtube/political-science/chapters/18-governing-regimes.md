**TL;DR.** A *regime* is the set of rules — formal and informal — that determines how political power is acquired and exercised. Two big families dominate the world: democracies and autocracies. The interesting and analytically tricky cases are the hybrids in between, and the recent literature on "democratic backsliding" is mostly about how regimes move from one type to another. Classifying regimes well is harder than it looks and matters more than it seems.

---

In 1989, a political scientist named Francis Fukuyama published an essay called "The End of History?" in the journal *The National Interest*. The argument, simplified: liberal democracy had won the long ideological contest of the twentieth century. Communism was collapsing. Authoritarianism had no remaining philosophical defense. The future would be a world of liberal democracies, with disagreements about details but no serious challenge to the basic regime form.

It was 1989. The Berlin Wall would fall that November. The Soviet Union would dissolve two years later. South Africa would end apartheid in 1994. Country after country in Eastern Europe, Latin America, and Asia was transitioning to multi-party elections. The argument, in 1989, did not look crazy.

In 2025, the argument looks very different. Several long-running democracy indices show the global stock of democracy declining or stagnating since the early 2000s. Hungary, Turkey, Venezuela, Nicaragua, the Philippines under Duterte, India under Modi (in some readings) — democracies that backslid toward autocratic features without a single coup or fully closed-fist takeover. Russia returned to authoritarianism after a brief democratic interlude. China, the world's largest country, did not democratize as the modernization-theory literature had predicted. The "third wave" of democratization that swept the world from 1974 onward seems to have stalled or partially reversed.

So what happened? The answer requires going back and thinking carefully about what kind of object a regime actually is, what makes a regime democratic or autocratic, and how regimes change. This is the work of comparative politics, and it is the subject of this chapter.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

4. Identify hybrid regimes (competitive authoritarianism, illiberal democracy, electoral autocracy) and the diagnostic features that place a country in this gray zone.
5. Diagnose democratic backsliding — the gradual erosion of democratic institutions through formally legal means — and identify the warning signs.

## Concept 1 — What "regime" means and what makes one democratic

The term "regime" gets used loosely. In this chapter, it means the set of formal and informal rules determining how political power is acquired, exercised, and transferred in a given country. A regime is more enduring than a particular government (the Conservative Party government of the U.K. in 2024 vs. the Labour Party government in 2025 — same regime, different government); a regime is more specific than a state (the United States is a state; the United States has had broadly the same democratic regime since 1789, with the major modifications of post-Civil-War amendments, women's suffrage, civil rights legislation, and various reforms).

Regime change is what we call moving from one regime type to another — democracy to dictatorship, monarchy to republic, authoritarian to democratic. Regimes can change without regime change: democracies can have major reforms (the U.S. transition from indirect to direct election of senators in 1913) without becoming non-democracies.

What makes a regime democratic? The minimal definition, due to Joseph Schumpeter and refined by later writers like Robert Dahl, has roughly these features:

1. **Competitive elections.** Multiple parties or candidates, free to compete, with reasonable opportunity to win.
2. **Universal (or near-universal) adult suffrage.** Most adult citizens can vote.
3. **Civil liberties.** Freedom of speech, association, assembly, press — without these, "competitive elections" don't mean what they're supposed to mean.
4. **Rule of law.** Laws apply to everyone, including the powerful; courts can hold government accountable.
5. **Peaceful transfer of power.** When the opposition wins an election, they actually take office; incumbents leave.

Each item is necessary; none is sufficient alone. A country with elections but no civil liberties (Iran's elections among regime-vetted candidates, for instance) is not democratic. A country with civil liberties but a rigged electoral process (Russia in some periods, Hungary in some readings) is not fully democratic. Democracy is the joint configuration of all five features, not any one of them.

### The trade-off — minimal vs. maximal definitions

The Schumpeter / Dahl minimal definition is one camp. A more demanding definition, common among "deliberative democrats," adds requirements: substantive equality, robust public deliberation, responsive policy outcomes, freedom from severe economic coercion. The richer definition makes very few real-world regimes count as democratic.

Each definition has trade-offs. The minimal definition gives you a clean comparative tool — you can rank countries by elections-civil-liberties-rule-of-law metrics. The richer definition captures more of what democracy is supposed to be for, but at the cost of making the category fuzzy and contested.

Most empirical research uses the minimal definition or close variants (Polity scores, V-Dem, Freedom House). Most political theory uses richer definitions. The two communities sometimes talk past each other; the student should know both.

> ### Dig Deeper — Look at the indices
> Three major democracy indices are V-Dem (varieties of democracy, run from the University of Gothenburg), Freedom House (a U.S.-based NGO), and Polity (an academic project). Ask Claude: "What are the methodological differences among V-Dem, Freedom House, and Polity? Where do they agree on country classifications, and where do they disagree? ### Common misconceptions

**"Elections make a country democratic."** Not by themselves. North Korea has elections. Russia has elections. Iran has elections. The question is whether the elections are competitive, free, fair, and consequential — whether they could result in the incumbent losing power. By that test, many "elections" are theater.

**"Democracy is binary — a country either is one or isn't."** It is closer to a continuum. Most countries are partial democracies, partial autocracies, or somewhere in between. The clean-binary view is a useful simplification but obscures the gray zones where most contested regime classification happens.

**"Western democracies are the model."** They are *a* model. Indian democracy, Japanese democracy, Brazilian democracy operate with different institutional features and cultural assumptions. Treating Westminster or Madisonian democracy as the only legitimate template misreads the diversity of working democratic regimes.

## Concept 2 — Types of autocracy

If democracy is one regime type, "non-democracy" covers many. Different autocracies operate by very different logics, and lumping them together obscures more than it reveals.

The classical typology, due to Barbara Geddes and refined by Geddes, Wright, and Frantz, distinguishes four main autocratic types.

**Personalist regimes.** Power concentrates in a single ruler. The ruler personally controls the security forces, the patronage system, and major policy decisions. There may be a ruling party, but it is a vehicle for the ruler rather than an independent source of authority. Examples: North Korea under the Kim family, Equatorial Guinea under Obiang, Belarus under Lukashenko, Russia in some readings of Putin's later years.

**Single-party regimes.** Power resides in a ruling party, with internal mechanisms for leadership succession and policy decision. The leader rules through and within the party, not above it. Examples: China since 1949, Vietnam, Cuba, Mexico under the PRI (1929–2000).

**Military regimes.** Power resides in the military as an institution, with senior officers running the government either directly or through a junta. Examples: Brazil 1964–1985, Argentina 1976–1983, Egypt under several governments, Burma/Myanmar in various periods.

**Monarchic regimes.** Power resides in a hereditary monarch with substantial ruling authority. Constitutional monarchies (UK, Spain, Sweden) are democracies with a ceremonial monarch; absolute monarchies (Saudi Arabia, Brunei, Eswatini) are genuinely autocratic regimes. Examples: Saudi Arabia, Oman, Bhutan in earlier periods, Brunei.

These types matter because they predict different patterns of behavior. Personalist regimes tend toward erratic policy, succession crises, and instability after the leader's death. Single-party regimes tend toward more institutionalized policy-making, smoother succession, and longer durability. Military regimes tend to produce predictable forms of repression and economic management, with characteristic exit problems (the military often returns to barracks under pressure but maintains tutelary power afterward). Monarchic regimes have specific legitimacy resources (tradition, religion, dynastic continuity) but also specific vulnerabilities (succession disputes, modernization pressure).

### The trade-off — institutionalization vs. flexibility in autocracy

Within autocracies, more institutionalized regimes (single-party, monarchies with established procedures) are more durable and less prone to crisis but slower to change direction. Less institutionalized regimes (personalist, some military) can adapt faster but are more vulnerable to leader-specific shocks (death, illness, factional fight).

Most autocracies face a version of the same problem democracies face: how to balance stability against responsiveness. Their solutions look different and produce different failure modes.

> ### Dig Deeper — Why China's authoritarianism has lasted
> China's regime type has confounded the modernization-theory prediction that economic development produces democratization. Ask Claude: "What does the political-science literature say about why China's authoritarianism has been durable through dramatic economic transformation? Look at Pei, Nathan, Bell, Shirk, and others. What features of Chinese governance has the literature pointed to? Where is there serious empirical disagreement?"

### Common misconceptions

**"Autocracies are unified."** They are not. Most autocracies have factions, internal disputes, and politics. The politics happen behind closed doors rather than in public, but they are real and consequential. Reading an autocracy as a unitary actor misses most of what happens inside it.

**"Autocracies can't make good policy."** They can, in some areas, when the incentives align. Singapore (a competitive authoritarian regime in some readings, hybrid in others) has produced exceptional economic policy. China has lifted hundreds of millions out of poverty under a non-democratic regime. Whether autocracies produce good policy in the long run on hard problems (especially environmental, redistributive, or politically threatening to the ruling group) is more contested.

**"Autocracies are fragile."** Many last for decades or generations. The Soviet Union lasted 74 years. The Mexican PRI ruled for 71 years. The Saudi monarchy is approaching its centennial. Autocratic durability is its own analytical challenge.

## Concept 3 — Hybrid regimes and democratic backsliding

The interesting analytical territory is in the gray zone — regimes that are neither cleanly democratic nor cleanly autocratic. The literature has developed several terms for these.

**Competitive authoritarianism** (Levitsky and Way, 2002, 2010). Regimes that hold real elections — the opposition could in principle win, and sometimes does — but where the playing field is sufficiently tilted (state media bias, intimidation of opposition, manipulation of registration, pressure on judiciary) that the elections do not meet democratic standards. The term captures regimes like Putin's Russia, Erdogan's Turkey, Orbán's Hungary, Maduro's Venezuela.

**Electoral autocracy** (Schedler 2006; many V-Dem analyses). Regimes that hold elections but where competitive features are largely absent — incumbents face no real challenge, opposition is marginalized or banned, results are systematically manipulated. Examples: Belarus, Cambodia under Hun Sen.

**Illiberal democracy** (Zakaria 1997; revived in different forms). Regimes that hold competitive elections but suppress civil liberties — restricted press, attacks on opposition figures, weakened courts, attacks on civil society. The term emphasizes the tension between procedural democracy (elections) and substantive democracy (rights and rule of law).

These categories overlap and the boundaries are contested. Hungary under Orbán is described as competitive authoritarian by some scholars, illiberal democracy by others, and a flawed democracy by Freedom House. The disagreements are not merely terminological — they reflect different empirical readings of how rigged the playing field actually is.

### Democratic backsliding

The major analytical innovation of the last fifteen years is the literature on **democratic backsliding** — the process by which a democracy gradually erodes into something less democratic, through formally legal means rather than through coups or revolutions.

The classic backsliding sequence (developed by scholars including Levitsky, Ziblatt, Bermeo, Waldner, Lust):

1. An elected leader is given or acquires a strong popular mandate.
2. The leader weakens institutional checks — packing or capturing courts, restricting press freedom, intimidating opposition, redrawing electoral districts, changing constitutional rules.
3. Each individual step is plausibly legal under existing rules. The cumulative effect is that the leader becomes substantially harder to remove through electoral competition.
4. Elections continue, often increasingly tilted, until at some point the regime crosses into electoral autocracy or remains as competitive authoritarianism.

The key feature: no single moment is a clear "coup." There is no tank in the streets, no suspension of the constitution, no obvious break with democracy. The country slides, by degrees, sometimes over many years, sometimes faster.

Hungary 2010-present is the canonical case. Orbán's Fidesz won a supermajority that allowed it to rewrite the constitution, restructure the judiciary, redraw electoral districts, capture or eliminate independent media, and harass civil society — all through formally constitutional means. Hungary holds elections; the elections are not free and fair by V-Dem and similar metrics; Orbán continues to win.

Other cases under varying degrees of debate: Turkey post-2013, Venezuela post-2002, Poland 2015–2023 (where the trajectory partially reversed in 2023), the United States during 2017–2021 (more contested, with serious scholars on different sides).

### The trade-off

Democratic backsliding is hard to stop because, by design, it works through ostensibly democratic means. The leader campaigns on resentment of elites, wins elections, and then uses the elected mandate to weaken the institutions that would constrain them. Opposition parties calling out the backsliding can be portrayed as anti-democratic for opposing the elected leader.

The literature has identified several factors that make backsliding more or less likely.

**Resilience factors:** strong independent courts (especially constitutional courts with insulated tenure), a healthy independent press, a mobilized civil society, a tradition of military political non-interference, a supportive international environment (EU membership has been cited as a brake on backsliding in some cases).

**Vulnerability factors:** weakened parties, polarized electorate, captured or weak courts, concentrated economic power, social-media-driven information environment, gradualism (no single dramatic event triggering opposition).

The honest reading: backsliding is real, observable, and difficult to reverse. The literature does not have confident predictions about which countries will backslide, but it has reasonably good descriptions of how the process works once it begins.

### Common misconceptions

**"Democratic backsliding only happens in poor countries."** Hungary is in the EU. Turkey was a NATO member in good standing through much of its backsliding. Poland and Hungary are in the OECD. The earlier modernization-theory expectation that wealth produces democratic durability has been substantially weakened by recent evidence.

**"You'd know if democracy was failing."** That is the methodological point of the backsliding literature: by design, you often don't know in real time. Each step is small enough to seem like normal political conflict; the cumulative effect is only visible in retrospect or through comparative metrics.

**"Backsliding can always be reversed."** Sometimes it can — Poland's 2023 election reversed several years of backsliding under PiS. Sometimes it can't — Venezuela has not reversed in twenty years; Russia has been on a sustained autocratizing trajectory for two decades. The reversibility of backsliding is itself an empirical question, not a normative assumption.

## Synthesis — reading regimes honestly

A serious student of regimes holds several ideas at once.

First, regime classification is an empirical exercise that depends on coding choices. The major indices (V-Dem, Freedom House, Polity) sometimes agree and sometimes disagree on individual countries; their disagreements are informative about the difficulty of classification, not just about methodological flaws.

Second, regimes are continua more than categories. Hungary, Turkey, India, the Philippines, Brazil under Bolsonaro — each is a flawed democracy by some readings, a competitive authoritarian regime by others. The classification depends on how you weight elections (which they hold) against civil liberties (which they have weakened) against rule of law (which has been compromised).

Third, regimes change slowly and then sometimes quickly. Backsliding takes years, sometimes a decade or more. Reversal can also take years. Sudden regime change (revolutions, coups) does happen but is rarer than the news cycle suggests.

Fourth, the formal institutions tell only part of the story. A regime with formally democratic institutions and informally autocratic practices is functionally different from one with the same formal institutions and democratic practices. Reading countries from constitutions alone systematically misclassifies them.

Fifth, normative judgments matter but should not crowd out empirical analysis. Whether backsliding is bad is a normative question; whether it is happening is an empirical one. The empirical work has progressed faster than political consensus on what to do about it.

The world in 2025 has fewer fully democratic regimes than at the high-water mark of the third wave around 2005. It has more competitive authoritarian and electoral autocratic regimes. The "end of history" prediction was wrong on the trajectory; whether it was wrong on the destination remains to be seen. The serious student should hold both possibilities open.

## Chapter summary

A regime is the configuration of formal and informal rules determining how political power is acquired, exercised, and transferred. Two big families dominate the world — democracies and autocracies — and most analytical interest concentrates in the gray zones between them. Democracy requires competitive elections, civil liberties, rule of law, and peaceful power transfer; absence of any of these features moves a country toward the autocratic end of the continuum. Autocracies vary by type (personalist, single-party, military, monarchic), with each producing different patterns of stability, repression, and policy. Hybrid regimes (competitive authoritarianism, illiberal democracy, electoral autocracy) occupy the middle, and democratic backsliding — the gradual erosion of democracy through formally legal means — is the analytical territory of the past two decades. Reading regimes honestly requires holding formal institutions and informal practices in view simultaneously and acknowledging the empirical contestability of many specific classifications.

## What would change my mind

If a sustained reversal of the recent backsliding trend produced a clear net gain in democracies globally over a decade or more, I would update my reading of the post-2005 stagnation. So far the indices show stagnation or modest decline; a clear reversal is possible but is not what the data show currently.

## Still puzzling

I do not have a confident view on the relationship between economic development and regime type. The modernization-theory claim was that wealth produces democracy. The 21st-century evidence is mixed — China, Singapore, the Gulf monarchies all show that significant wealth and stable autocracy can coexist. But the long-run dynamics of these regimes are still playing out; whether they eventually democratize, evolve into something new, or persist as wealthy autocracies is genuinely uncertain.
