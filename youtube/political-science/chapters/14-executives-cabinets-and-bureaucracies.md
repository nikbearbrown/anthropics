**TL;DR.** The executive branch in any modern state is three things at once — a chief executive (president, prime minister, monarch), a cabinet of department heads, and a vast bureaucracy of career civil servants. The chief gets the news coverage; the bureaucracy does most of the actual governing; the cabinet sits in the middle. Understanding executive power requires holding all three in view.

---

In 2008, when Barack Obama became president of the United States, he inherited an executive branch with about 2.7 million civilian employees, a defense department that employed roughly another 700,000 civilians and 1.4 million active-duty service members, and a federal budget approaching $3 trillion. He inherited 15 cabinet departments, dozens of independent agencies, hundreds of advisory boards, thousands of pages of regulations being drafted in any given week, and a workforce of career professionals — many of whom had served under several presidents from both parties — running everything from air traffic control to nuclear weapons safety to the food inspection system that decided whether your morning bacon was safe to eat.

He also inherited the news cycle. Every weekday for eight years, the question "what is the President doing?" would be answered by cameras and a scrum of reporters at the South Lawn or the Rose Garden. The image of the executive branch in the public mind — and in most political coverage — would be one person standing at a podium.

Both pictures are accurate. Both are also dangerously incomplete on their own. The president is not the federal government. The federal government is also not running on autopilot independent of the president. Executive power, in any modern state, is the joint product of an elected chief, a small political team, a cabinet of department heads, and a much larger career bureaucracy. Understanding what the government actually does requires understanding all of them.

This chapter is about that machinery. Three concepts organize it: what kind of chief executive a country has (presidential, parliamentary, semi-presidential), how the cabinet relates to the chief, and what the bureaucracy is and what it does. Each shapes the others. None can be understood in isolation.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

2. Explain the principal-agent problem in executive politics — why the chief executive cannot simply order the bureaucracy and have it happen.
3. Describe the role of the cabinet in different systems — symbolic, administrative, deliberative — and identify which a given cabinet is performing.
4. Compare the U.S. civil service system with European Westminster and continental systems and identify their structural differences.
5. Diagnose Max Weber's account of bureaucracy — what it does well, what it does badly, and why both are features of the same machinery.
6. Read an executive action — a presidential order, a regulatory rule, an agency budget decision — as a move in a larger game involving the chief, the cabinet, the bureaucracy, the legislature, and the courts.

## Concept 1 — Three kinds of chief executive

Three main institutional designs separate the world's democracies. Each puts the chief executive in a different relationship to the legislature.

**Presidential systems.** The head of government is directly elected (or nearly so) by the people for a fixed term, separately from the legislature. The president cannot easily be removed mid-term except through extraordinary procedures (impeachment in the U.S.; recall in some Latin American constitutions). The president and the legislature can be from different parties — divided government — and frequently are. United States, Mexico, Brazil, most of Latin America, the Philippines, South Korea, Indonesia.

**Parliamentary systems.** The head of government — the prime minister — is selected by the legislature, usually as the leader of the majority party or coalition. The PM serves as long as they retain the confidence of the legislature; a no-confidence vote brings down the government and triggers either a new government or new elections. The head of state (a monarch in the U.K., a ceremonial president in Germany or India) is separate from the head of government. United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, Germany, India, Japan, Israel, most of Europe.

**Semi-presidential systems.** A directly elected president shares executive power with a prime minister chosen by the legislature. The president usually controls foreign policy and defense; the PM usually runs domestic policy. When president and PM are from the same party, the system functions roughly like a presidential one with a cabinet. When they are from different parties — *cohabitation* in French parlance — it functions roughly like a parliamentary system with an unusually empowered head of state. France, Russia, Portugal, Romania, several post-Communist states.

### The trade-off

The Linzian critique. Juan Linz, a political scientist writing in the 1990s, argued that presidential systems carry a structural risk that parliamentary systems do not: when the president and the legislature are from different parties and cannot work together, there is no constitutional mechanism for breaking the deadlock except waiting for the next scheduled election. The president cannot be removed for political failure; the legislature cannot be dissolved early; the country has to limp through whatever crisis emerges with a deadlocked government.

In a parliamentary system, by contrast, a no-confidence vote triggers either a new government or new elections — a crisis is forced to resolution.

Linz argued that this design feature contributed to democratic breakdowns in Latin American presidential systems through the 20th century. Other scholars (Scott Mainwaring, Matthew Shugart) have pushed back: presidential systems can be stable when party systems are not too fragmented, and parliamentary systems can fail too (Italy, Israel, the Weimar Republic). The honest reading is that institutional design interacts with political-cultural context; presidentialism is a risk factor, not a doom.

The compensating advantage of presidentialism is direct accountability — voters elect the chief executive, knowing what they are getting. Parliamentary systems sometimes produce prime ministers no one voted for as PM (Theresa May replacing David Cameron, John Major replacing Margaret Thatcher, several Italian PMs through the 1990s).

### Parliamentary discipline

In a Westminster parliamentary system, the prime minister has powers a U.S. president would envy. The PM controls the cabinet, sets the legislative agenda, and — through party discipline — can usually command a working majority on most votes. When you read about the UK government "introducing" a bill, that government has, in advance, ensured the votes. Major bills almost always pass.

The flip side: party discipline runs through the PM. A backbench rebellion can topple a government. Margaret Thatcher was forced from office in 1990 by her own party. Tony Blair's government was constrained for years by Labour rebellions on Iraq, public services, and welfare reform. The PM who cannot hold the party loses the office; the PM who holds the party governs almost like an elected monarch.

### Common misconceptions

**"Presidential systems are more democratic."** The president is directly elected, but the legislature is too. In parliamentary systems the legislature elects the PM, but the legislature itself was directly elected. Both designs derive authority from elections; neither is intrinsically more democratic.

**"Parliamentary PMs have less power than presidents."** Often the opposite is true within their domestic governments. A British PM with a working majority faces fewer veto points than a U.S. president facing a hostile Senate.

**"Coalition governments are weak."** They can be — but they can also be remarkably stable (Germany's grand coalitions; the Dutch tradition). Their weakness, when it appears, comes from coalition arithmetic, not from coalition status as such.

## Concept 2 — The cabinet: not what you think

In American civics-class lore, the cabinet is "the President's advisors." This is technically true and substantively misleading. The cabinet does very different work depending on the country.

**U.S. cabinet.** Department secretaries (Treasury, Defense, State, etc.) appointed by the president and confirmed by the Senate. They run their respective departments and are technically the President's chief advisors on the policy areas they oversee. In practice, the U.S. cabinet rarely meets as a deliberative body. The President consults individual secretaries as needed and rarely convenes the full cabinet for policy decisions. Major decisions usually flow through the National Security Council, the White House Chief of Staff, or smaller policy councils — not through cabinet meetings.

The U.S. cabinet is mostly an *administrative* structure (each secretary running a department) rather than a *deliberative* one (the cabinet as a body deciding policy together).

**Westminster cabinet.** The cabinet is the engine of the British government. The PM and 20-or-so cabinet ministers meet weekly to decide policy collectively. The convention of *cabinet collective responsibility* requires every cabinet minister to publicly support every cabinet decision, regardless of private dissent (or to resign). The cabinet is genuinely a deliberative body where major policy is hashed out, sometimes through bruising fights, before being announced as government policy.

**Continental European cabinets.** Coalition cabinets in Germany, the Netherlands, Sweden function differently again. The cabinet is the working level of the coalition agreement: each portfolio is allocated to a coalition partner, and inter-cabinet bargaining is essentially inter-party bargaining. The cabinet meeting is where the coalition's day-to-day governance happens.

The lesson: "the cabinet" is doing very different work in different countries. The U.S. cabinet is largely administrative; Westminster cabinets are genuinely deliberative; continental cabinets are coalition-management vehicles. Trying to read U.S. executive politics through Westminster intuitions, or vice versa, produces systematic errors.

### The trade-off

The U.S. structure — strong individual secretaries, weak collective cabinet — privileges *executive flexibility* (the President can ignore the cabinet and rely on whatever advisors fit the moment) at the cost of *coordination* (departments often pursue conflicting agendas because no body is forcing them to negotiate with each other).

The Westminster structure — strong collective cabinet, weaker individual ministers — privileges *coordination* (cabinet collective responsibility forces inter-departmental fights to resolve before the policy is public) at the cost of *flexibility* (the PM has to negotiate the cabinet, not just direct it).

### The kitchen cabinet problem

Every president, every PM, every leader has a kitchen cabinet — the smaller, informal group of trusted advisors who actually shape decisions. In the U.S., this is the Chief of Staff, the National Security Advisor, sometimes the President's personal lawyer or political consultant. In the U.K., it is the small group of senior ministers and special advisors ("SpAds") whose offices are physically near the PM in 10 Downing Street. In coalition systems, it is the leaders of the coalition parties, who meet bilaterally outside the formal cabinet structure.

The kitchen cabinet often matters more than the formal cabinet. This is not a corruption — it is a recurring institutional pattern. Decision-making concentrates around the people the leader personally trusts, regardless of the constitutional or conventional structure.

### Common misconceptions

**"The President's cabinet meeting is where policy is decided."** Not in the U.S. The cabinet meeting is largely a photo opportunity and a coordination mechanism; major decisions happen in smaller, function-specific bodies.

**"Cabinet ministers in the UK are independent powers."** They are bound by collective responsibility. A minister who publicly disagrees with cabinet policy must resign. The trade-off is real influence within cabinet at the cost of public independence.

**"Coalition cabinets are paralyzed by negotiation."** Often the opposite — the coalition agreement specifies in advance what the government will and won't do, and the cabinet executes within that framework. Day-to-day negotiation is rarer than headlines suggest.

## Concept 3 — The bureaucracy: the part you don't see

Most of what governments actually do, day to day, is done by career civil servants — people who were not elected, who do not change with administrations (mostly), and who spend their working lives implementing policy rather than making it. This is the bureaucracy. It is the part of government most invisible in news coverage and most consequential in citizens' lives.

The Social Security Administration sends out roughly 67 million benefit checks per month. The IRS processes about 165 million tax returns per year. The FAA controls about 45,000 flights per day. The CDC tracks disease outbreaks; the FDA approves drugs; the EPA writes air-quality standards. Hundreds of thousands of teachers, soldiers, postal workers, foresters, veterans-affairs counselors, food inspectors, weather forecasters do the work of the federal state every day. Almost none of them are politically appointed. Almost none of them change when a new administration takes office.

### Weber on bureaucracy

Max Weber, writing in the early 20th century, gave the canonical account of what bureaucracy *is*. His description was meant as analysis, not endorsement, but it has held up.

A modern bureaucracy, in Weber's account, has six characteristics: (1) hierarchy of offices, (2) specialized division of labor, (3) written rules and recordkeeping, (4) impersonal application of rules (the same rules apply to everyone, regardless of personal relationship to the official), (5) qualified expertise (officials are selected by training and credential, not by patronage), and (6) full-time, salaried career employment.

Each of these features, Weber argued, is what makes bureaucracy enormously powerful — and enormously frustrating to deal with.

The power: a Weberian bureaucracy can do work at a scale and consistency that no patronage or charismatic system can match. You can administer Social Security to 67 million people because you have written rules, hierarchy, specialization, and impersonal application. You cannot do that through personal favor.

The frustration: the same features produce rigidity. The rules are the rules even when they shouldn't apply; specialization means no one sees the whole problem; hierarchy means decisions take forever to climb the organizational chart. The qualities that make bureaucracy work also make it grind.

This is not a defect in particular bureaucracies. It is a structural feature of bureaucracy as a form. You can reduce some frictions; you cannot eliminate them without eliminating the features that make the system work.

### The principal-agent problem

The chief executive ("the principal") sits at the top of the bureaucracy. The bureaucracy ("the agent") is supposed to implement the chief's policies. But the agent has its own knowledge, its own incentives, its own cultural memory of past policy directions, and the chief cannot perfectly observe what the agent is actually doing.

This is the **principal-agent problem**, and it is the central problem of executive control. A new president comes in wanting to redirect the EPA toward stricter enforcement; the EPA's career staff include both supporters and opponents of the new direction; some staff drag their feet, others go enthusiastically beyond what was asked, and the President cannot personally inspect every regulatory action. The agency moves, but not exactly where the President pointed.

This is not corruption or insubordination. It is the structural reality of trying to direct a large organization with diffuse authority over technical subject matter. Every chief executive in every modern state lives with this problem. Strategies for managing it — political appointees at the top of agencies, executive orders, performance metrics, legislative oversight — partially work and partially don't.

### The trade-off

A bureaucracy with strong civil-service protections — career employees who cannot be fired easily, professional standards independent of political control — is more *competent* and more *resistant to corruption* (you can't easily install patronage cronies; expertise persists across administrations). It is also more *resistant to democratic direction* (the agency may continue old policy after voters have elected someone with a different mandate).

A bureaucracy with weak civil-service protections — large numbers of political appointees, easy firing — is more *responsive to elected leadership* (the new administration can rapidly redirect agencies) but more *vulnerable to corruption*, *less competent on average*, and *less able to maintain expertise across administrations*.

Most democracies have settled on something close to the strong-civil-service end (the U.S. since the 1883 Pendleton Act; the U.K. since the Northcote-Trevelyan reforms of 1854; most European systems with longer civil-service traditions). The historical reason: 19th-century patronage systems produced corruption, incompetence, and chaos with each administration change. The civil-service reforms were a response to that.

The contemporary debate, especially in the U.S., is whether the balance has tipped too far toward unaccountable bureaucracy — and whether more political control is needed, or whether weakening civil service protections will reproduce the 19th-century problems.

### Regulation and the administrative state

A specific feature of modern bureaucracy: many agencies are empowered by Congress to make *regulations* with the force of law. Congress passes the Clean Air Act in 1970 and 1990; the EPA writes thousands of pages of regulations interpreting and implementing it. These regulations are how the law actually meets the world.

This raises the question: how much regulatory authority can Congress legitimately delegate to executive agencies? Too little and the law cannot adapt to changing technical conditions. Too much and unelected agency officials are effectively writing the rules that bind citizens.

The U.S. Supreme Court has wrestled with this for a century. The 1984 *Chevron v. NRDC* decision held that courts should defer to reasonable agency interpretations of ambiguous statutes — making the agencies effectively the primary interpreters of the laws they administer. The 2024 *Loper Bright v. Raimondo* decision overturned *Chevron*, returning more interpretive authority to courts. The full consequences are still being worked out, but the case marks a significant shift in the balance among Congress, agencies, and courts in shaping the administrative state.

### Common misconceptions

**"Bureaucracies are inefficient by nature."** They are inefficient at certain things — innovation, rapid response, individual cases that don't fit the rules — and remarkably efficient at others — running large-scale standardized operations consistently and at lower per-unit cost than private alternatives. The Social Security Administration's overhead is roughly 0.6% of benefits paid; private retirement-product overhead is typically several percent.

**"Civil servants are politically neutral."** They are required to be neutral in their official duties; they are not blank slates as people. Career staff have professional commitments, agency cultures, and individual views. The norm is that those views do not override their duty to implement the elected administration's lawful directions; the practice is that the boundary is sometimes fuzzy.

**"You could just shrink the bureaucracy by cutting regulations."** Some regulations could be cut with little cost; many produce real public benefits (clean air, drug safety, financial stability). Reducing regulation reduces some costs and removes some benefits. Empirical work on specific regulations gives a better answer than ideological priors.

## Synthesis — the executive as a system

The chief executive, the cabinet, and the bureaucracy operate as a system. None can be understood in isolation. Three illustrations:

A new administration comes in promising rapid policy change. The chief executive can issue executive orders, fire and replace political appointees, set priorities. They cannot, mostly, fire career civil servants, who have legal protection and institutional memory. So the new administration's actual reach into policy depends on how well it manages its political appointees, how the appointees relate to career staff, and what the bureaucratic culture in each agency tolerates.

A coalition cabinet announces a major policy. The cabinet has agreed; the agency tasked with implementation now has to write the regulations. The agency has its own institutional view of the policy area; some career staff are enthusiastic, some skeptical. The implementation will diverge from the cabinet's intent in detailed but consequential ways. The next election may turn on whether voters perceive the policy as having worked, even though the policy as enacted is the joint product of cabinet decision and bureaucratic implementation.

A piece of major legislation is passed by parliament. The PM's office now has to ensure that the responsible minister's department drafts the regulations, hires the staff, and stands up the program. If the program fails, the minister gets blamed publicly; if the bureaucracy is the actual point of failure, the minister cannot easily say so without violating cabinet collective responsibility.

In each case, executive performance is a joint product. Holding any one of the three actors fully responsible for outcomes — the chief, the cabinet, the bureaucracy — produces a misleading picture. Effective executive analysis follows the policy through all three layers and asks where each contributed.

## Chapter summary

The executive branch in any modern state operates as a three-layer system: a chief executive (president, PM, or hybrid), a cabinet that ranges from administrative to deliberative depending on country, and a large career bureaucracy that does most of the actual implementation work. Institutional design — presidential vs. parliamentary, strong vs. weak cabinet, strong vs. weak civil service — produces predictable patterns of stability, responsiveness, and competence, with each design optimizing for different things. The principal-agent problem is the central tension: the chief cannot fully control the bureaucracy that implements policy, and effective executive performance requires managing the gap rather than wishing it away.

## What would change my mind

If a careful study of presidential vs. parliamentary systems, controlling for party-system fragmentation and economic context, showed that presidentialism does not in fact carry elevated risk of democratic breakdown, I would weaken Linz's argument in my own reading. The literature is large and mixed; my current view is that presidentialism is a moderate risk factor in fragmented systems and not in non-fragmented ones, but the comparative empirical work is genuinely contested.

## Still puzzling

I do not have a confident view on the *Chevron* / *Loper Bright* shift. Returning interpretive authority from agencies to courts addresses a real concern (unelected agency officials shaping the law's meaning) but creates a new one (unelected federal judges shaping technical regulatory areas they do not specialize in). Both designs have failure modes; I do not know which produces better policy outcomes in expectation. The empirical evidence is too new, and the question depends on which kinds of cases dominate the docket in coming years.
