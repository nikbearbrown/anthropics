**TL;DR.** The media is the institution that decides what citizens see of their government, their world, and each other. Three things shape what gets reported: who owns the outlets, what the audience clicks on, and what reporters can actually find out. Each has changed dramatically in the last twenty years, and the changes have reshaped politics in ways the political-science literature is still catching up to.

---

In 1960, three U.S. television networks — CBS, NBC, and ABC — collectively decided what most Americans saw on the news each evening. The audience for the network evening news in a typical 1960s broadcast: roughly 75% of households with a television in use. The reporters were largely interchangeable in editorial outlook; the news was largely the same across the three networks; the "news cycle" was an actual cycle, with morning papers in the morning and evening news at six.

In 2024, no single news outlet reaches anything close to that share. The most-watched cable news show in any given month reaches a few million viewers in a country of 330 million. The largest social-media platforms are accessed by hundreds of millions of Americans daily, but no two users see the same feed. The "news cycle" is continuous and personalized. The reporters writing what most Americans actually read are spread across thousands of outlets, ranging from the New York Times to anonymous Twitter accounts to AI-generated aggregators.

This is not a small change. It is a fundamental restructuring of the institution that mediates between citizens and political life. The political effects — polarization, declining trust in institutions, the rise of "alternative facts," the difficulty of agreed-upon evidence in policy debates — are partly downstream of this restructuring. Understanding contemporary politics requires understanding what happened to the media and what it became.

This chapter is about that institution. Three concepts organize it: what "the media" actually is and how it has changed, the gatekeeping function and how new media has redistributed it, and the relationship between media and democratic politics — both the case that media makes democracy possible and the case that media is currently making it harder.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

1. Distinguish four media eras — partisan press, professional press, broadcast, and platform — and identify what each optimized for.
2. Explain the gatekeeping function in journalism — what it does, why it exists, and what happens when it weakens.
3. Diagnose the political consequences of social-media platforms as news distribution: algorithmic amplification, filter bubbles, virality, the attention economy.
4. Compare different media systems internationally — strong public broadcasters (BBC, ARD/ZDF), market-driven systems (U.S.), state-controlled systems (China, Russia) — and predict what each produces.
5. Read a piece of political coverage as a structured artifact — what the framing emphasizes, what it downplays, what it omits, and how the choice of frame shapes citizen response.
6. Hold both the case for and against contemporary media simultaneously: media is what makes democracy possible *and* media in its current configuration is producing problems no one designed.

## Concept 1 — Four media eras

The U.S. media (and most other countries' media in roughly parallel ways) has gone through four major institutional configurations. Each had different incentives, different gatekeepers, different relationships to political parties.

**The partisan press (roughly 1789–1880s).** Newspapers were openly affiliated with political parties. The Federalist papers, the Jeffersonian papers, then the Whig and Democratic papers, then the Republican and Democratic papers. Reporters did not pretend to neutrality; they argued for their party's positions. Readers chose which paper to subscribe to based on partisan preference. The model was sustainable because parties subsidized papers (printing contracts, postal advantages, jobs for partisan editors).

**The professional press (roughly 1880s–1960s).** Mass-circulation newspapers built around advertising revenue, not party subsidy. To maximize circulation, papers softened partisan affiliation and adopted the norms of "objectivity" — separating news from opinion, training reporters in fact-finding standards, professionalizing journalism through schools of journalism. The model was sustainable because advertising revenue scaled with circulation, and circulation scaled with broad appeal across partisan lines.

**The broadcast era (roughly 1950s–1990s).** Television added a third layer: a small number of national networks reaching nearly everyone, with a regulatory structure (the FCC's "fairness doctrine," for instance, until 1987) that pushed broadcast journalism toward perceived neutrality. Walter Cronkite of CBS was, for a time, "the most trusted man in America." The political effect was a degree of national consensus on what "the facts" were on most major issues. This consensus was always partial — civil rights, Vietnam, Watergate were never agreed upon — but the shared informational baseline was stronger than what came before or after.

**The platform era (roughly 2005–present).** Social media platforms — Facebook, YouTube, Twitter/X, TikTok, Instagram — became major distributors of news. They distribute news algorithmically, optimizing for engagement (clicks, time-on-site, shares, comments). They do not employ the journalists who produce the content; they distribute it. They have no editorial responsibility for accuracy in the way that traditional editors do. The economic consequence: advertising revenue moved from newspapers to platforms, hollowing out professional journalism. The political consequence: the gatekeeping function was redistributed and partially destroyed.

### The trade-off — gatekeeping vs. open access

Each era trades different things off.

**Partisan press.** Democratic accountability through clear party-paper alignment; informational diversity (each party has its outlets); reduced trust in any single source; weaponized misinformation as standard practice.

**Professional press.** A shared informational baseline across partisan lines; reduced "spin"; concentration of editorial power in a small number of newsrooms; the editorial filter as a chokepoint that could be captured by elite interests.

**Broadcast era.** Mass national audience, shared reference points; strong professional norms enforced by regulation; the "fairness doctrine" pushed toward consensus framings; suppression of more partisan or fringe perspectives.

**Platform era.** Open access (anyone can publish); distributional reach (audiences for niche content); algorithmic amplification of engagement-maximizing content; weakened editorial standards; partial collapse of the shared baseline; rise of viral misinformation and partisan-sorted realities.

The point of the comparison is not to nominate a winner. The professional/broadcast era had genuine virtues that the platform era has destroyed. It also had genuine vices (newsroom homogeneity, suppressed perspectives, gatekeeping captured by elite consensus) that the platform era has corrected. The trade-off is real.

### Common misconceptions

**"Older media was unbiased."** No. The professional/broadcast era's "objectivity" was a norm produced by specific institutional pressures (advertising revenue, broadcast regulation, professional schools), and it had its own biases — toward elite consensus, toward "both sides" framings even on questions with clear empirical answers, toward stories that fit existing news values. It was not neutral; it was differently biased.

**"Social media gave voice to the voiceless."** It did, partially — marginalized perspectives, citizen journalism, organizing platforms. It also gave equal amplification to misinformation, harassment, foreign-government influence operations, and engagement-bait that crowded out higher-quality reporting. Both effects are real.

**"The mainstream media is uniformly liberal/conservative."** Both claims are made; both oversimplify. Mainstream U.S. journalism tends socially liberal in newsroom culture; it is also institutionally cautious, deferential to official sources on national-security questions, and historically slow to challenge consensus framings. Reading a media outlet as a single ideology obscures its internal contradictions.

## Concept 2 — Gatekeeping and what it does

A **gatekeeper**, in media-studies terminology, is the actor or institution that decides what content reaches the audience. In the broadcast era, editors at the New York Times, the AP, and the network news desks made these decisions for most Americans. They decided which stories to cover, how prominently, with what framing, what to leave out.

The gatekeeping function was, and is, simultaneously the best and worst feature of a media institution.

The best: it filters. With unlimited information available, citizens cannot evaluate every claim individually. Gatekeepers — when they perform their function well — separate signal from noise, fact from rumor, important stories from trivial ones. The professional norms of journalism (verify sources, get the other side, distinguish news from opinion) are the gatekeeper's craft.

The worst: it filters. The same gatekeepers can suppress important stories, amplify trivial ones, frame coverage in ways that serve elite consensus, and exclude perspectives the gatekeepers find inconvenient or incomprehensible. The Vietnam War's early coverage; the run-up to the 2003 Iraq War; the dot-com bubble's coverage; the 2008 financial crisis's pre-history — all involve cases where mainstream gatekeepers failed to challenge official framings, and citizens paid the cost.

The platform era has redistributed gatekeeping rather than eliminating it. The algorithms of Facebook, YouTube, TikTok, and X are gatekeepers — they decide what reaches users. The gatekeeping is just performed differently: by engagement metrics rather than editorial judgment, by automated systems trained on user behavior rather than by professional reporters.

### The trade-off

Algorithmic gatekeeping vs. editorial gatekeeping is one of the central institutional tensions of contemporary media.

**Editorial gatekeeping** (a human editor at a major paper deciding what's news) optimizes for what the editor thinks is important, with all the editor's expertise, biases, and elite-consensus blind spots.

**Algorithmic gatekeeping** (a feed-ranking system deciding what shows up in your feed) optimizes for what produces engagement — clicks, shares, comments, time-on-site. The system has no view about what is important; it has a metric.

Both have failure modes. Editorial gatekeeping fails by missing stories or framing them in ways that serve incumbent interests. Algorithmic gatekeeping fails by amplifying outrage, misinformation, and emotionally charged content — because those are what produce engagement, regardless of accuracy.

The empirical evidence on what algorithmic feeds actually produce is large and contested. Some findings: politically extreme content does outperform moderate content on engagement metrics. Outraged emotional reactions outperform calm ones. False claims about politics travel faster than true ones, on average, on Twitter (one widely-cited 2018 MIT study by Vosoughi and colleagues). Filter bubbles exist but are not as severe as early commentary feared (most users are exposed to multiple political perspectives; the issue is more often within-perspective extremity than cross-perspective isolation).

### Misinformation and disinformation

Two terms worth distinguishing.

**Misinformation** — false information shared without intent to deceive. Someone reads a wrong story, believes it, shares it. Most of the false information circulating online is misinformation in this sense.

**Disinformation** — false information deliberately created to deceive. Foreign-government influence operations, partisan fabrications, scam content. Disinformation is a specific subset of misinformation, distinguished by intent.

The distinction matters because solutions differ. Misinformation responds to better information ecosystems (fact-checking, source evaluation, media literacy education). Disinformation responds to harder interventions (platform takedowns, sanctions on producers, intelligence-agency action). Treating all false information as the same problem produces bad policy.

The empirical scale: the most rigorous studies (Allcott and Gentzkow 2017; Guess et al. 2019, 2020; Grinberg et al. 2019) find that exposure to false news on social media is concentrated in a small subset of users (the most politically active, the oldest, the most extreme) and that the *aggregate* informational diet of most users is dominated by mainstream news. This is not a defense of the misinformation problem — exposure of even small subsets matters because those subsets are politically active. It is a check on the broader claim that "fake news" dominates the average user's feed.

### Common misconceptions

**"Social media made polarization."** It contributed; it did not create. Polarization was rising in U.S. politics before social media's rise (most of the trend lines start in the 1980s or 1990s). Social media has likely accelerated certain kinds of polarization (especially affective polarization — how people feel about the other party). It is one factor among several; "social media did it" is a single-factor explanation for a multi-factor phenomenon.

**"Algorithms have a political bias."** Empirically, the bias claims are made in both directions and are not well supported in either direction by careful research. The bias of platforms is toward engagement, which interacts with political content unevenly across topics and times. Strong claims of systematic political bias usually do not survive careful empirical scrutiny.

**"Platforms can solve misinformation by removing more content."** They can reduce some forms of misinformation; they cannot eliminate it without making content moderation choices that themselves become politically contentious. The trade-off between letting misinformation circulate and giving platforms editorial power they may misuse has no clean solution.

## Concept 3 — Media and democracy

The standard liberal-democratic argument is that a free press is essential to democracy. The argument has several layers:

**Information.** Citizens need information to make political choices. The press provides it.

**Accountability.** The press investigates power. Without investigative journalism, the executive branches, the courts, the corporate sector operate with reduced scrutiny.

**Deliberation.** A democratic public square requires shared reference points — agreed-upon facts about what is happening — even when interpretations differ.

**Representation.** The press carries diverse perspectives into the public conversation, including perspectives that don't get representation through formal political institutions.

This case is mostly right. It is also incomplete. A free press in itself does not guarantee any of these functions. A free press optimized for partisan engagement, or for advertiser-friendly content, or for sensationalism, can fail at information, accountability, deliberation, and representation simultaneously while remaining nominally free.

The political-science literature has been working through this. Some findings:

**Investigative journalism produces measurable accountability effects.** Studies of local newspaper closures show that municipal governments raise borrowing costs (suggesting reduced oversight), corruption indictments fall, and voter turnout in local elections drops. The press as accountability mechanism is real, and its decline has measurable consequences.

**The shared-baseline function has weakened.** Surveys of partisan news exposure show that Republican and Democratic voters, on average, draw their political information from substantially non-overlapping sources. The "two countries" framing of contemporary American politics — though overstated in popular commentary — has measurable empirical content.

**Trust in media has collapsed.** Gallup's tracking polls show U.S. trust in mass media at near-record lows, with steep partisan asymmetry (Democrats trust mass media more than Republicans, with the gap widening over the last fifteen years). The decline is not unique to the U.S.; many democracies have seen similar erosion.

### The trade-off

A media system optimized for democratic deliberation might look like this: well-funded public broadcasters with strong professional norms; healthy local journalism providing accountability for state and municipal government; algorithms that prioritize accuracy and depth over engagement; a citizenry educated to evaluate sources critically.

A media system optimized for commercial profit might look like this: advertiser-funded outlets selecting for content that produces engagement; consolidation around a few dominant platforms; emphasis on national over local coverage (because the audience scales nationally); algorithms that maximize attention-time.

The U.S. has moved closer to the second model and away from the first. Many other democracies have public broadcasters (the BBC, Germany's ARD/ZDF, Japan's NHK, Sweden's SVT) that anchor a more deliberation-friendly media ecosystem alongside commercial outlets. The empirical evidence (Curran and colleagues' comparative work; the World Values Survey on media trust) suggests that countries with stronger public broadcasting have higher media trust and more shared informational baselines, though the causality is complex and the comparison is sensitive to confounders.

### State-controlled vs. market vs. public-broadcast media

A comparative point worth making: not all media systems are commercial-private.

**Market-private** (U.S. dominant model). Advertising-funded, owned by private corporations, regulated lightly. Optimizes for engagement and profit. Strong investigative journalism in the strongest outlets; weak coverage outside major cities and major markets.

**Public-broadcast-strong** (UK, Germany, Sweden, Australia ABC, Japan NHK). Significant public broadcasters funded by license fees or general taxation, alongside commercial press. Optimizes for public-interest content, with strong accountability norms. Vulnerable to government pressure where public broadcasting is funded through political processes; can ossify.

**State-controlled** (China, Russia, North Korea, others). Media operates under government direction, dissent is suppressed, alternative outlets are restricted or forbidden. Optimizes for regime maintenance. Produces predictable failures of accountability (corruption goes unreported; policy failures are hidden; foreign reporting is curated).

These are not pure types either. The BBC has been accused (from both partisan directions) of bias; PBS in the U.S. has weak funding compared to ARD/ZDF in Germany; the Chinese media system is far more sophisticated than crude propaganda models would suggest. The lesson: media systems are institutional designs, with predictable patterns and failure modes.

### Common misconceptions

**"A free press is sufficient for democracy."** Necessary, not sufficient. A free press in a polarized, fragmented information environment with collapsed local journalism is producing observable democratic dysfunction. Press freedom is one institutional feature among several.

**"Public broadcasters are state-controlled propaganda."** In well-designed systems (BBC, ARD/ZDF), the public broadcaster operates under independent governance, professional editorial standards, and reasonably arms-length relationships with elected officials. Critics of public broadcasting often conflate it with state-controlled media; the institutional differences are large and consequential.

**"Local news is dying because people don't want it."** Survey research shows people consistently say they value local news. They don't pay for it (or don't pay enough) at scale. The collapse is partly about willingness-to-pay, partly about the platform-driven hollowing of the advertising revenue that local journalism relied on. "People don't care" is a misreading of what people say they want.

## Synthesis — what kind of citizen does this institution produce?

Three pictures of how media interacts with politics:

The first picture is the small-town newspaper from 1965. A reader subscribes. The paper covers the school board, the city council, the local crime, the high school football team, the state legislature's actions affecting the town. The reader does not love every editorial position, but the paper is a shared reference point in town. Civic life partly organizes around what the paper covers.

The second picture is the cable-news viewer from 2005. The viewer chooses a network — Fox, MSNBC, CNN — based on partisan affinity. The network reports primarily national politics, framed through that partisan lens. The viewer rarely encounters the framing of the other partisan camp. Local government coverage is largely absent. The viewer's political identity becomes increasingly tied to the network's framing.

The third picture is the social-media user from 2024. The user opens a feed assembled by an algorithm trained on prior engagement. The feed includes some traditional journalism, some viral political commentary, some outrage-bait, some misinformation, some ads, some videos from acquaintances and strangers. The user spends two hours a day in this feed. They cannot easily characterize what kind of political environment they are inhabiting; the algorithm is opaque, the content is mixed, and the boundary between reporting and opinion has dissolved.

Each picture produces a different kind of political citizen. The first reads, considers, sometimes attends meetings — a citizen of a place. The second consumes partisan framings, becomes more reliable as a partisan voter, less reliable as an evaluator of cross-partisan claims. The third is harder to characterize — affectively engaged, often poorly informed about specific facts, vulnerable to viral misinformation, deeply attached to certain political identities, often disengaged from local civic life.

These pictures are simplifications. Real citizens move among them; the same person might read local news and watch cable and scroll feeds. But the institutional gravity has shifted, and the median media-encounter for the median citizen is more like the third picture than the first or second. The political effects — collapsed shared baseline, affective polarization, weakened local accountability, vulnerability to misinformation — are not separate problems. They are downstream of the same institutional shift.

The honest reading: media institutions matter for democracy in ways the political-science literature is still trying to characterize. The current configuration is producing measurable problems. Reform is hard because the underlying economics (advertising moving from journalism to platforms, attention as the scarce resource, platform business models structured around engagement) does not respond to wishful thinking. The path forward will require institutional creativity that has not yet been demonstrated at scale — and the demonstrations that exist (public broadcasters, philanthropic-funded journalism, platform-fee proposals) are partial answers, not solutions.

## Chapter summary

The media is the institution that mediates between citizens and political life. It has gone through four major institutional configurations — partisan press, professional press, broadcast era, platform era — and each has produced different relationships among citizens, governments, and informational baselines. The gatekeeping function (deciding what reaches the audience) is performed differently in each era, and the platform-era redistribution of gatekeeping from human editors to algorithmic feeds has produced measurable effects on what citizens see and believe. The institutional design of media systems matters for democratic outcomes: countries with strong public broadcasters and healthy local journalism produce more shared informational baselines than countries dominated by engagement-driven platforms. Reform is hard because the underlying economic dynamics resist easy intervention.

## What would change my mind

If careful experimental and observational work showed that platform-era media is producing equivalent democratic outcomes to the broadcast era — equivalent shared baselines, equivalent accountability, equivalent civic engagement — I would substantially revise my reading of contemporary media's political effects. The evidence so far points the other direction; multiple measurable indicators of healthy democratic media (trust, local journalism employment, shared factual baseline) have all declined together in roughly the period of platform dominance. But the causal claim is harder than the correlation; longer-running comparative work might surprise me.

## Still puzzling

I do not have a confident view on what to do about platform-driven media's effects. The interventions that have been tried at scale (Section 230 reform proposals, EU's Digital Services Act, public-interest journalism funds) are small relative to the size of the problem; the interventions that would be large enough to matter (platform breakups, mandatory chronological feeds, public funding for journalism at the scale needed to replace lost advertising revenue) face large political and constitutional obstacles in the U.S. context. I do not know what the realistic path forward is.

## Connections forward

This chapter closes Part 4 (Institutions). Part 5, beginning with Chapter 17, opens onto the international level — states, sovereignty, the relationships among nations. Media's role in international relations is large (war coverage shapes public support; diplomatic crises are partly performed through media; transnational platforms operate across jurisdictional boundaries) and increasingly contested.
