## The puzzle of why turnout varies so much

In 2020, US voter turnout was 66% — the highest since 1980. In 2016, it was 60%. In 2014, a midterm year, 36%. Same country. Same eligible population, more or less. Different turnout by margins of 30 percentage points.

Look across countries and the variation is wider. Sweden, South Korea, and Turkey routinely produce voter turnout around 80%. The US ranks 30th of 35 OECD countries.[^1] Australia and Belgium have *compulsory* voting; turnout there is over 90%.

[^1]: OECD comparative turnout data; see also Pew Research Center cross-national comparisons. `[verify]` for current vintage.

The variation isn't random. It's shaped by:
- Institutional rules (compulsory vs. voluntary voting, automatic registration, frequency of elections)
- Individual characteristics (age, income, education, civic socialization)
- Specific election features (perceived stakes, candidate appeal, mobilization effort)

Studying the variation is one of the discipline's main empirical activities. Predicting it is much harder, as the 2016 and 2020 US elections made famous.

This chapter installs the framework political science uses for participation and opinion measurement.

## Concept 1 — Modes of participation

Voting is the most-studied mode of participation, but it's not the only one. The full picture includes:

**Voting.** The most direct form of self-government. The Pew Research Center's 14-country study found voting in elections to be the most common form of participation across countries — typically 60-90% of eligible voters in election years.

**Contacting elected officials.** Letters, calls, emails, in-person meetings. In the US, the First Amendment's right to petition government formalizes this. About 27% (median across the Pew sample) of citizens have contacted an elected official in the past year.

**Volunteering.** Door-to-door canvassing, phone banking, voter registration. Volunteer activity has measurable effects on turnout, especially in low-turnout (off-year) elections. Door-knocking by trained volunteers can increase turnout by several percentage points in the targeted households.

**Donating.** About 25% of total US campaign money in 2020 came from small donors (under $200). Bernie Sanders's repeat-donor model — supporters giving $3 increments multiple times — typifies the small-dollar small-frequent approach. Larger donors fund the rest.

**Protesting.** Marches, sit-ins, civil disobedience. The 2019 protest activity globally was, by some measures, the largest in modern history.[^2] The 2020 Black Lives Matter protests in the US drew tens of millions. Hong Kong's 2019-2020 Umbrella Movement; the Arab Spring of 2011; the Yellow Vests in France; numerous others. The frequency of mass protest has increased in many countries, with mixed effects on policy.

[^2]: Center for Strategic and International Studies, *Mass Protests Track Record* (CSIS, 2020). `[verify]` for specific data.

**Online activism.** Social media engagement, e-petitions, hashtag campaigns. Especially common among younger citizens — among Americans aged 18-29, about 48% get political news primarily from social media.[^3] The effects are contested: some online campaigns produce policy change (the Sandy Hook gun-policy petition led to direct White House engagement); others produce visibility without action.

[^3]: Pew Research Center, *Social Media and the News*. `[verify]`.

**Running for office, working on campaigns, organizing communities.** Higher-effort forms of participation that fewer citizens engage in but that disproportionately shape outcomes.

### Predictors of participation

Five factors consistently predict who participates more.

**Age.** Older voters vote more than younger voters, by a wide margin. In US presidential elections, voters 65+ turn out at rates 30-40 percentage points higher than voters 18-29. The gap reflects life-stage effects (younger people move more, are less established in communities), policy stakes (older voters have more to lose from changes to Social Security and Medicare), and habit (voting becomes a routine for those who do it consistently).

**Income.** Higher-income citizens vote at higher rates than lower-income citizens. The gap has structural causes — lower-income workers have less flexible schedules, less ability to take time off, more difficulty obtaining identification — and motivational ones (less perceived efficacy, less integration into political networks).

**Education.** College-educated citizens vote more, donate more, contact officials more, and follow politics more closely than those without college education. Whether this is a causal effect of education itself or a correlation with other characteristics (childhood civic socialization, parental education) is debated, but the empirical association is strong.

**Race.** Historically in the US, White voters turned out at substantially higher rates than Black, Latino, and Asian American voters. The gap has narrowed and in some cases reversed: Black voter turnout exceeded White turnout in 2008 and 2012 (Obama elections); Asian American turnout in Georgia surged 63% from 2016 to 2020. Race effects on turnout are heavily mediated by mobilization — when targeted voter outreach reaches a group, that group turns out more.

**Gender.** US women have outvoted men since 1964; the gap is now several percentage points. This holds across most age and education groups.

### Institutional factors

Beyond individual characteristics, **institutions** shape turnout:

- **Registration requirements.** Same-day registration, automatic voter registration, and online registration all increase turnout. The seven US states (and DC) with automatic voter registration have higher registration rates than other states.
- **Voter ID laws.** Stricter ID requirements correlate with lower turnout, especially among lower-income and minority voters who are less likely to have qualifying ID.
- **Gerrymandering.** When district boundaries are drawn to make outcomes preordained, voter motivation drops. Why turn out when the result is known?
- **Voter fatigue.** Frequent elections (US has elections in many odd-numbered years; some other countries have one large election per 4-5 years) can depress turnout.
- **Compulsory voting.** Australia, Belgium, Brazil, Argentina, and several others legally require voting. Turnout consistently exceeds 85%. The trade-off: some forced voters cast votes they don't think carefully about.

For your project, when analyzing any country's turnout patterns, both individual characteristics and institutional rules matter. The US is unusual in the OECD in combining voluntary voting with relatively burdensome registration requirements, which produces low turnout by international standards.

### Common misconceptions

- *"Low turnout means voters don't care."* Sometimes — but it can also mean voters don't believe their vote matters (low political efficacy), or that registration barriers prevent them from voting. Disentangling motivation from access is essential.
- *"Online activism replaces traditional participation."* Studies are mixed. Some find online engagement crowds out offline action; others find it amplifies offline networks. The relationship is complicated.

## Concept 2 — Public opinion and how it's measured

**Public opinion** is the aggregation of individual political views at a given moment. It matters because democracies, in their self-justifying logic, derive legitimacy from public consent. If you can't measure public opinion, you can't tell whether government decisions reflect it.

### What shapes opinion

Walter Lippmann's *Public Opinion* (1922) is the classic statement: people don't experience politics directly; they experience it through media, opinion leaders, and social networks. The "pictures in our heads" come from second-hand sources, and those sources can systematically distort.

Five major factors shape individual opinion formation:

**Demographics.** Age, race, gender, income, education, region, religion. These correlate strongly with opinion patterns, though within any demographic group there's substantial variation.

**Social groups.** Family, friends, religious communities, workplaces, voluntary associations. Most people's political views align closely with their immediate social circles.

**Ideology.** The frameworks from Chapter 5. People filter new information through the ideological lens they bring to it.

**Opinion leaders.** Public figures whose views shape others'. Could be presidents (popular ones move public opinion); could be religious leaders, business figures, celebrities, or "near-by influencers" (in John Stuart Mill's phrase) — local people whose judgment is trusted.

**Media framing.** How an issue is presented affects how it's understood. Frame an environmental regulation as "job-killing red tape" and opinion shifts one way; frame the same regulation as "protecting our children's future" and opinion shifts the other. Media framing is particularly powerful on foreign affairs (where direct experience is rare) and on novel issues without prior partisan signaling.

### How polling works

The standard tool for measuring public opinion is the **survey** or **poll** — asking a sample of citizens questions and aggregating their answers.

**Probability sampling** is the foundation. The principle: select respondents randomly from a defined population (e.g., adult US citizens) so each member has a known probability of being selected. With a sample of about 1,500 randomly selected respondents, you can estimate population opinion to within roughly ±2.6 percentage points (margin of error) at a 95% confidence level.

**Sample size and margin of error.** Larger samples give smaller margins of error, but with diminishing returns. Going from a sample of 500 to 1,500 reduces margin of error from about 4.4% to 2.6%. Going from 1,500 to 5,000 only reduces it to about 1.4%. For most purposes, 1,000-1,500 is the practical sweet spot.

**Sampling methods.** For decades, random digit dialing (RDD) of landline phones was the standard. Now most pollsters use mixed-mode sampling — landlines, cell phones, online panels, sometimes face-to-face interviews. The shift reflects:
- Declining landline coverage (only about 40% of US households still had landlines as of 2022).
- Cell-only households (over 60% of US adults under 35).
- Declining response rates (5-10% for most polls today, vs. 30%+ in earlier decades).

**Non-probability methods.** Some pollsters use opt-in online panels rather than random selection. These are cheaper and faster but require sophisticated weighting to approximate population representativeness. Quality varies widely.

### What can go wrong

Five major sources of error in polling:

**Sampling error.** Random variation due to surveying a sample rather than the whole population. Captured by margin of error.

**Selection bias.** When the sampling frame systematically excludes some part of the population. RDD landline polls in 2008 famously underweighted Obama supporters (younger, more cell-only). Modern polls weight to compensate, but the weighting itself introduces error.

**Question wording effects.** "Global warming" produces lower agreement than "climate change" on the same underlying claim. "Welfare" polls worse than "assistance to the poor." Even small wording changes can shift answers by 5-10 percentage points.

**Interviewer bias.** Respondents give different answers to interviewers of different races, genders, or accents. Particularly important for racially charged questions.

**Social desirability bias.** Respondents give the answer they think they "should" give rather than their honest view. Classic case: the **Bradley effect** in 1982 California. Tom Bradley, a Black candidate for governor, polled ahead but lost. Researchers concluded some White voters told pollsters they'd vote for Bradley but didn't. Whether the Bradley effect persists in modern elections is debated; some 2008 and 2012 polls showed slight underpolling of Black candidates, though others didn't.

### The 2016 and 2020 polling failures

In 2016, most national polls had Hillary Clinton ahead by 3-4 percentage points; she won the popular vote by 2.1, but lost the Electoral College. The state-level polls in key swing states understated Trump support meaningfully — some by 8-10 points.

In 2020, polls again overstated Biden's margin, by about 3-4 percentage points nationally and considerably more in some states.

The post-election analyses identified several culprits:

- **Underweighting non-college voters**, especially White men without college degrees — a group that swung heavily to Trump. Polls couldn't easily reach them; weighting algorithms didn't fully correct.
- **Differential response rates** between Trump and Biden supporters. Some hypothesis: Trump supporters who distrusted media and pollsters were less likely to respond to polls, causing the responses to skew away from them.
- **Late-deciding voters**. Polls have trouble capturing voters who decide in the final week.

The polling industry is still working through what these failures mean.[^4] Some analysts argue current methodology is structurally broken; others argue 2016 and 2020 reflect specific political conditions that may be self-correcting. The 2024 polls were, by some measures, more accurate, suggesting some adjustment.

[^4]: For ongoing debate, see Nate Silver's analyses at FiveThirtyEight and the American Association for Public Opinion Research's post-election reports.

### Common misconceptions

- *"Polls are useless because they got 2016 wrong."* The 2016 polls were not far outside their stated margins of error in most cases — they suggested Clinton was likely to win, and she was, but "likely" doesn't mean "certain." The bigger lesson is reading polls as probabilities, not predictions.
- *"A poll with 1,000 respondents tells you what 300 million Americans think."* Within stated error bars, yes — for the question asked, at the time asked. The gaps between what polls measure and what citizens actually do (vote, protest, change their minds) are substantial.

## Concept 3 — Politics as hobby vs. genuine engagement

A specific distinction worth installing, drawn from political scientist Eitan Hersh's *Politics Is for Power* (2020).[^5]

[^5]: Eitan Hersh, *Politics Is for Power: How We Can Truly Cope with Lying Politicians, Tweeting Demagogues, and Voter Suppression* (Scribner, 2020).

Hersh distinguishes two kinds of political activity.

**Genuine political engagement** is action aimed at producing political outcomes: voting, contacting officials, organizing volunteers, working on campaigns, building coalitions, bargaining with adversaries, sustaining political relationships over time. It's effortful, slow, and often frustrating. Successes are partial. The participants spend years working on specific causes.

**Political hobbyism** is action that *feels* political but doesn't produce political outcomes: doom-scrolling political news, sharing partisan content on social media, engaging in online arguments with strangers, signing online petitions, expressing strong opinions among like-minded friends. It can be intellectually engaging or emotionally satisfying, but its connection to actual political change is weak.

The distinction matters because hobbyism feels like engagement and substitutes for it. A citizen who spends an hour a day reading political news, posting opinions, and arguing online may believe they're highly politically active. By participation measures (volunteering, contacting officials, organizing, donating, voting in low-turnout elections), they may not be. Their attention has been captured by political content without producing political action.

Hersh's broader argument: hobbyism is structurally rewarded by media and platforms, which monetize attention regardless of what citizens do with it. Genuine engagement is structurally underprovided. The result is a gap between perceived political activity and actual political activity, with consequences for which causes get represented.

The implication for your project — and your own political life — is to ask routinely: *is this activity changing anything, or am I just consuming political content?* Both can be valuable, but they're different.

### Comparative perspective

The hobbyism critique applies less in countries with stronger participatory traditions. Scandinavian and Western European democracies have higher rates of party membership, union membership, and organized civic participation than the US. The trade-off: stronger political organizations also produce less individual flexibility. Each model has costs and benefits.

### Common misconceptions

- *"Hobbyism is bad."* It's not bad; it's just not engagement. People who do it instead of engagement are confused; people who do it in addition to engagement (with realistic awareness) are fine.
- *"Online activism is always hobbyism."* Some online activism is the modern form of genuine organizing. The distinction is whether the activity produces real-world results, not whether it happens online.

## Synthesis

Political participation and public opinion are the two sides of how citizens connect to government in a democracy.

**Participation** ranges from voting to protest to online activism. Different forms have different costs, reach different segments of the population, and produce different effects. Turnout varies widely by country and election; both individual characteristics and institutional rules shape it.

**Public opinion** is measurable but not perfectly so. Polls provide useful information within stated error bars; they fail when sampling, weighting, or response patterns introduce systematic bias. The 2016 and 2020 US polling experiences are the most-discussed recent cases.

**The hobbyism distinction** matters because attention without action is increasingly common and is sometimes mistaken for engagement.

For the running project: when analyzing any political situation, you'll want to ask both *what are people doing* (participation) and *what are people thinking* (opinion). The two together tell a richer story than either alone.
