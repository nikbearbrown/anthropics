**TL;DR.** International relations is the study of politics between states in a world where there is no global government. Three big theoretical schools — realism, liberalism, constructivism — answer the central question (why do states do what they do?) in different ways, and most contemporary IR work draws on more than one. Power, interests, institutions, and ideas all matter; the disagreement is about how much each contributes.

---

In August 1914, the major powers of Europe went to war. The proximate cause was the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria-Hungary by a Bosnian Serb nationalist in Sarajevo. The actual mechanism was a cascade of mutual-defense alliances: Austria-Hungary moved against Serbia; Russia mobilized to defend Serbia; Germany declared war on Russia and France; Britain declared war on Germany over the German invasion of Belgium. Within weeks, four empires were at war. By 1918, when the fighting stopped, around 17 million people had died, four empires (Russian, Austro-Hungarian, Ottoman, German) had collapsed, and the political map of Europe had been rewritten.

Almost no one had wanted this war in the form it took. The Kaiser of Germany did not want to fight Britain. The British Foreign Office did not want a continental war. The Russian Tsar warned Germany against escalation in his telegrams of late July. The French government inherited rather than chose the war. And yet they all marched in, because each calculated that not marching in would be worse — that the alliance system, once activated, made participation more rational than abstention.

This is the puzzle that haunts international relations as an academic field. States, often run by reasonable people with reasonable interests, regularly do things that are catastrophic for everyone — including themselves. Why? What is it about international politics that produces these outcomes? And what, if anything, can be done about it?

The field has produced three main answers, and most working IR scholarship draws on two or all three. This chapter is about that.

## Concept 1 — Anarchy and the security dilemma

The fundamental structural fact of international politics, in the IR mainstream, is **anarchy**. The term does not mean chaos. It means the absence of a higher authority above states. Within a state, you can call the police if someone attacks you; the police are an authority above you and the attacker. Among states, there is no equivalent authority. The United Nations is not a global government. International courts depend on state cooperation for enforcement. The international system is structured by the absence of a sovereign above the states.

This is a simple-sounding observation with complicated consequences. Among them, the most famous is the **security dilemma**, formulated in different versions by John Herz (1950) and Robert Jervis (1978).

The mechanism: in a system without a higher authority, a state's security depends on its own capabilities. A state acquires military capability for defensive reasons. A neighboring state, observing the buildup, cannot perfectly distinguish defensive from offensive intent — the same tank, missile, or aircraft carrier could be used either way. The neighbor, anxious about being attacked, builds up its own military. The first state, observing the neighbor's buildup, builds further. Both end up more militarized, less secure, and at greater risk of war than they would have been without the cycle.

The security dilemma is not a problem of bad intentions. It can occur — and historically often has — between states with no aggressive plans against each other. The structure of the system produces the dynamic.

The empirical evidence: arms races have repeatedly preceded major wars (Anglo-German naval competition before WWI; U.S.-Soviet arms competition during the Cold War; current dynamics in East Asia between China and its neighbors). Whether the security dilemma causes the wars or just accompanies them is debated. The mechanism is real; its policy implications are contested.

### The trade-off — how much does anarchy explain?

The realist view: anarchy is the master variable. State behavior is explained primarily by the structure of the system and the distribution of capabilities within it. Domestic politics, ideology, leaders' personalities matter at the margin but do not override the system-level pressures.

The liberal view: anarchy is real but its effects are mediated by institutions, interdependence, and domestic politics. International institutions (the UN, the WTO, NATO, the EU) reduce uncertainty and the security dilemma. Economic interdependence raises the costs of conflict. Democratic regimes (democratic peace theory) tend not to fight each other. Anarchy is a constraint, not a determinant.

The constructivist view: anarchy is what states make of it (Alexander Wendt's famous formulation). The same structural condition can produce a Hobbesian war-of-all-against-all, a Lockean rivalrous-but-stable system, or a Kantian community of cooperating democracies — depending on the identities, norms, and shared understandings the states have built up. Structure and agency are mutually constitutive.

These are not mutually exclusive. Most working IR scholarship combines elements of more than one. The interesting empirical question is how much each contributes in any given case.

> ### Dig Deeper — Test the security dilemma
> Pick a current bilateral arms competition (U.S.-China, India-Pakistan, North Korea-South Korea, Israel-Iran). Ask Claude: "Apply the security dilemma framework to this case. Is each side's military buildup defensive or offensive? Can the other side tell? What confidence-building measures might mitigate the dilemma, and which have been tried? Where is the literature most uncertain?" The exercise reveals how much the framework illuminates and where it doesn't.

### Common misconceptions

**"Anarchy means lawlessness."** It means the absence of higher authority, not the absence of order. International law exists, treaties are mostly honored, diplomacy works most of the time. The system is anarchic in structure but ordered in practice — sometimes.

**"Realism says states are always at war."** It says states must always be prepared for the possibility of war and behave accordingly. Most realists distinguish offensive realism (Mearsheimer — states must seek dominance) from defensive realism (Waltz — states need only enough power to be secure). The latter is much closer to mainstream realism.

**"International institutions don't matter."** Realists have been making this argument for decades, and it has been losing empirical ground. The post-1945 institutions (UN, IMF, World Bank, GATT/WTO, NATO, EU) have shaped state behavior in measurable ways. They are not all-powerful, but the strong claim "institutions don't matter" is hard to sustain against the historical record.

## Concept 2 — Realism, liberalism, constructivism

Three big theoretical traditions organize the IR field. Most actual analysis combines them; the pure types are useful for clarity.

### Realism

The oldest tradition, traceable through Thucydides, Machiavelli, Hobbes, and into the 20th century with Hans Morgenthau (*Politics Among Nations*, 1948), Kenneth Waltz (*Theory of International Politics*, 1979), and John Mearsheimer (*The Tragedy of Great Power Politics*, 2001).

Core claims:

1. **States are the central actors.** Other actors (international organizations, NGOs, corporations) matter less than states.
2. **States seek power.** Either as an end in itself (classical realism) or as a means to security (defensive realism) or as a way to ensure dominance (offensive realism).
3. **Anarchy structures behavior.** The international system has no higher authority; states must self-help.
4. **Domestic politics doesn't matter much.** What matters is the state's external position — relative power, geographic location, alliance options.
5. **Cooperation is hard.** States worry about relative gains (will my neighbor benefit more than I do?) and about being abandoned by allies.

Realism predicts: balancing against rising powers, periodic great-power conflict, the limits of international institutions, the persistence of hard power as a determinant of outcomes.

Realism's wins: predicted (in some readings) the failure of the League of Nations, the Cold War's bipolar stability, the Russian-Western tensions after NATO's eastward expansion, the rise-of-China challenge.

Realism's losses: under-predicted the persistence of post-1945 institutions, the absence of major-power war for 80 years, the spread of democracy, the role of identity and ideology in conflict.

### Liberalism

The tradition of Immanuel Kant, Adam Smith, Woodrow Wilson, and in the modern academic field, Robert Keohane, Joseph Nye (*Power and Interdependence*, 1977), Michael Doyle, and many others.

Core claims:

1. **Multiple actors matter.** States, but also international organizations, corporations, civil society, individual leaders.
2. **Cooperation is possible and frequent.** Common interests can produce stable cooperation, especially when institutions reduce uncertainty.
3. **Interdependence raises the cost of conflict.** Trade, financial flows, shared infrastructure create vested interests in stability.
4. **Domestic politics matters.** Democracies behave differently from autocracies; interest groups inside states shape foreign policy.
5. **International institutions reshape state behavior.** They are not just epiphenomenal; they create rules, generate information, and lock in commitments.

Liberalism predicts: the deepening of post-1945 international institutions, the democratic peace, the role of trade in conflict reduction, the importance of multilateral problem-solving on issues that cross borders.

Liberalism's wins: the post-1945 international order, the democratic peace empirical pattern, the rise of multilateral cooperation on issues like trade, environment, and human rights, the EU as a long peace project.

Liberalism's losses: under-predicted backsliding of democracies, the vulnerability of liberal international order to revisionist powers, the gap between formal commitments and actual compliance on issues like climate.

### Constructivism

A more recent tradition, associated with Alexander Wendt (*Social Theory of International Politics*, 1999), Martha Finnemore, Kathryn Sikkink, and others.

Core claims:

1. **Identities and norms matter.** States behave according to who they think they are and what norms they consider legitimate, not just according to material interests.
2. **Material structures matter through their meanings.** A nuclear weapon in British hands means something different (to Americans) than a nuclear weapon in North Korean hands.
3. **Anarchy is socially constructed.** The same structural condition can produce different patterns of state behavior depending on the dominant identities and norms.
4. **Change is possible.** Norms shift; identities evolve; what was unthinkable becomes thinkable. Slavery was internationally legitimate two centuries ago; now it is universally condemned. This is not material change but normative change.
5. **Institutions and discourse shape interests.** What states want is not given exogenously; it is shaped by the social context they are embedded in.

Constructivism predicts: the rise and decline of specific norms (sovereignty, human rights, humanitarian intervention, prohibition on territorial conquest), the importance of soft power and discourse, the role of national identity in foreign policy.

Constructivism's wins: explained the spread of human rights norms, the changing meaning of sovereignty, the importance of norm entrepreneurs (transnational advocacy networks), why some weapons (chemical, biological) are taboo while others aren't.

Constructivism's losses: harder to test rigorously; some constructivist claims (about discourse, about identity) are difficult to operationalize empirically; sometimes accused of being closer to interpretation than science.

### How they fit together

Most working IR analysis is theoretically eclectic. A serious analysis of the U.S.-China relationship draws on realist insights about power transitions and the security dilemma, liberal insights about economic interdependence and international institutions, and constructivist insights about identity, ideology, and the meaning of "rivalry." The pure types are useful in seminars; the eclectic combinations are useful in actual analysis.

> ### Dig Deeper — Three lenses on one event
> Pick a major international event — Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine, the U.S. exit from Afghanistan in 2021, China's South China Sea claims, Brexit. Ask Claude: "Walk me through how a realist, a liberal, and a constructivist would each interpret this event. What does each lens highlight, and what does each miss? Which lens (or combination) seems best supported by the evidence?" The exercise sharpens your sense of what each tradition actually contributes.

### Common misconceptions

**"Realists are warmongers, liberals are naive."** Both characterizations are unfair. Realists like Morgenthau, Kennan, Waltz, and Mearsheimer have generally counseled restraint in foreign policy; many of them opposed the Iraq War, opposed Vietnam, opposed NATO expansion. Liberals are not naive about power but argue that institutions, interdependence, and democracy moderate power's effects. The caricatures obscure the actual debates.

**"Constructivism just means everything is socially constructed."** Constructivists do argue that identity and norms shape state behavior, but careful constructivists (Wendt, Finnemore) acknowledge material constraints. The slogan is a lazy version of the position.

**"You have to pick one school."** You don't. The interesting empirical question is which school best illuminates which questions. Most working IR scholars draw on more than one.

## Concept 3 — The democratic peace and its puzzles

A specific empirical claim deserves its own concept: the **democratic peace**.

The observation: democracies very rarely (in some readings, never) go to war with each other. Democracies fight wars — colonial wars, wars against autocracies, civil wars — but war between fully democratic states is exceptionally rare in the historical record. The pattern was first noted by Immanuel Kant (*Perpetual Peace*, 1795); developed empirically by Michael Doyle (1983) and Bruce Russett (*Grasping the Democratic Peace*, 1993); and is one of the more robust empirical findings in the IR literature.

This is a striking claim because it suggests that regime type — a domestic feature — has predictable effects on international behavior. It cuts against the realist view that the structural conditions of anarchy override domestic differences.

### What might explain it?

Several mechanisms have been proposed.

**Institutional constraints.** Democratic leaders face checks (legislatures, courts, opposition, media, voters) that make it harder to launch wars. They have to mobilize public support, justify costs, and accept electoral consequences.

**Normative socialization.** Citizens of democracies internalize norms of peaceful conflict resolution; they apply these norms to relations with other democracies (which they recognize as fellow democracies) but not necessarily to autocracies.

**Information.** Democracies have more transparent decision-making, which makes their intentions easier to read. Lower uncertainty, lower security dilemma, lower risk of war.

**Audience costs.** Democratic leaders who back down from public commitments face domestic political costs; this allows them to make credible commitments in negotiations, reducing the need for war to settle disputes.

**Selection effects.** Democracies pick their fights carefully, attacking only states they expect to defeat decisively; they avoid fighting other democracies because the strategic calculation is harder.

The empirical literature has not converged on which mechanism is doing the work. Some scholars argue the democratic peace is mainly an artifact of post-1945 American hegemony — democracies don't fight each other partly because the United States has structured the post-war system. Some argue it is overstated and that the historical record contains more borderline cases than usually acknowledged.

### Where the claim is contested

Two main areas of contestation:

**Scope.** Does democratic peace apply to all wars, or just to interstate wars between full democracies? It clearly does not apply to colonial wars, covert interventions, or wars where one party is a partial democracy. Defining "war between democracies" carefully is itself part of the analytical work.

**Stability.** What happens if a democracy backslides? If the democratic peace is real, then democratic backsliding makes the world more dangerous. Some recent literature has tried to test whether the pacific effects survive partial autocratization; the evidence is mixed.

> ### Dig Deeper — Borderline cases
> The democratic peace claim has been contested through specific case analysis. Ask Claude: "Walk me through the borderline cases — the War of 1812 (US-UK), the Spanish-American War, the Boer War, the Indo-Pakistani wars, NATO actions against Serbia. For each, was at least one party a full democracy at the time? How does the resolution affect the empirical claim?" The exercise sharpens your sense of how robust the empirical pattern is.

### Common misconceptions

**"Democratic peace means democracies are peaceful."** It does not. Democracies fight wars; they just rarely fight each other. The claim is dyadic, not monadic.

**"Democratic peace proves liberalism right."** It supports a specific liberal hypothesis, but realists have responses (the democratic peace might be an artifact of U.S. hegemony, of geographic concentration, of selection effects). The empirical pattern is robust; its theoretical interpretation is contested.

**"Promoting democracy will produce peace."** This is a policy implication some have drawn, but it is contested. Democratization processes are themselves often violent (Mansfield and Snyder argued that *transitions* to democracy are particularly war-prone, even if mature democracies are not). Whether democracy promotion produces peace depends on the trajectory and the conditions.

## Synthesis — power, interests, ideas, institutions

The serious student of IR holds four things together when reading any state behavior:

**Power.** The distribution of capabilities — military, economic, technological — across states. Realism's central variable. It matters; states with more power can do more, and weaker states adjust accordingly.

**Interests.** What states want — security, wealth, status, influence, particular policy outcomes. Often shaped by domestic politics, sometimes by structural position. Liberal and realist analyses disagree about how interests form, but both emphasize their importance.

**Ideas.** What states believe — about themselves, about other states, about norms of legitimate behavior, about historical analogies. Constructivism's central variable. Ideas can change rapidly under pressure or persist across generations; either can matter.

**Institutions.** The rules, organizations, and routines that structure state interaction. The UN, the WTO, NATO, the IMF. They matter more than realism predicted, less than the most ambitious liberal claims suggest.

A foreign policy decision is the joint product of all four. The U.S. response to Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine, for instance, can be partly explained by power (U.S. and NATO's military superiority over Russia), partly by interests (concern about a peer competitor's revanchism, energy markets, alliance credibility), partly by ideas (post-Cold-War norms against territorial conquest, Ukrainian identity, U.S. self-image as a liberal hegemon), and partly by institutions (NATO commitments, the UN system that delegitimized the invasion, EU unity). Trying to explain it through any one of these alone misses too much.

This is not a counsel of theoretical mush. It is a counsel of analytical seriousness. The interesting work is figuring out how much each variable contributed in a given case, what the alternative trajectories would have been, and what it tells us about the system's structural pressures and the room for agency within them.

## Chapter summary

International relations studies politics among states in a system without a higher authority. Anarchy as a structural condition produces predictable dynamics like the security dilemma. Three big theoretical traditions — realism, liberalism, constructivism — explain state behavior with different emphases on power, institutions, and ideas. Most actual analysis combines them. The democratic peace is one of the more robust empirical findings, suggesting domestic regime type shapes international behavior in ways pure realism doesn't predict, but the mechanism remains contested. Reading state behavior honestly requires holding power, interests, ideas, and institutions in view simultaneously and asking how each contributes to the case at hand.

## What would change my mind

If a careful study showed that democratic peace evaporates outside the post-1945 American-hegemonic period — that democracies in earlier centuries fought each other at rates comparable to mixed-regime pairs — I would substantially revise my reading of the democratic peace claim. The evidence so far supports the claim across centuries, but the post-1945 period dominates the data, and a robust pre-1945 test would sharpen the conclusion.

## Still puzzling

I do not have a confident view on whether the post-1945 international order is durable. Realists predicted its erosion; liberals predicted its endurance. The post-2008 evidence is mixed — institutions are weaker than they were, but they have not collapsed; great-power competition is back, but major-power war has not occurred. I am genuinely uncertain about the next twenty years.
