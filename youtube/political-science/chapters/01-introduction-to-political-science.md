**TL;DR.** Political science is the systematic study of political activity — how power is acquired, exercised, contested, and constrained. It is *not* the practice of politics, and it does not tell you what positions to hold. What it does is build frameworks for understanding why political systems behave the way they do, why some institutions deliver good outcomes and others don't, and why citizens make the choices they make. ## The puzzle of why this is a separate field

A reasonable first question, before reading 400 pages of political science, is: why is this a separate field at all?

History tells you what happened. Economics tells you how resources get allocated. Sociology tells you how groups form and behave. Psychology tells you why individuals decide as they do. Law tells you what the rules say.

Political science sits at the intersection of all of these and adds a specific question on top: **how is power organized, and why does it work the way it does?** That question is what makes the field cohere. The Russian invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 was a historical event, an economic shock, a sociological story about national identity, a psychological story about a leader's risk tolerance, a legal violation of the UN Charter — and it was all of those at once because politics is the discipline of competing claims on collective decision-making, and collective decisions touch every other domain.

The discipline studies political activity systematically. That word — *systematically* — is doing important work. A pundit gives an opinion. A historian narrates an episode. A political scientist tries to identify patterns: across many elections, what predicts turnout? Across many countries, what makes democracies stable? Across many wars, what conditions let combatants negotiate peace?

Sometimes the patterns hold. Sometimes they don't. The field has spent a century learning where its tools work well (electoral system effects, regime classification, voter behavior) and where they work poorly (predicting specific events, accounting for charismatic individuals, mapping rapid changes). One of the things this book teaches you is the boundary — what political science can answer and what it can't.

For the running project that threads this book together, the field's tools become practical instruments. By the end of Chapter 21, you'll have used political-science frameworks to produce a substantive analysis of a real political question. The project will be selected after Chapter 00 introduces the LLM workflow.

## What political science actually does

The discipline divides into four main subfields.

**American politics** studies the politics of one country (the United States) in detail. The subfield is large in the US for both intellectual and institutional reasons — many US political scientists are American, the country is consequential, and US political institutions are well-documented enough to support quantitative work. American politics specialists study Congress, the presidency, courts, parties, voting, public opinion, and federalism.

**Comparative politics** studies politics across many countries — comparing the way different systems handle similar problems. Why do some countries have parliamentary systems and others presidential? What makes some democracies stable and others fragile? How do authoritarian regimes maintain power? Comparative politics is where most cross-national theorizing happens.

**International relations (IR)** studies relations between states (and increasingly between states and non-state actors — corporations, NGOs, terrorist groups, international organizations). War and peace, trade and sanctions, alliances and treaties. IR has its own theoretical traditions (realism, liberalism, constructivism) that we'll meet in Chapter 19.

**Political theory** studies the normative and conceptual foundations of politics — questions about justice, legitimacy, freedom, authority, equality. It's the subfield closest to philosophy. The theorists ask "what *should* we do," not just "what *is* happening." We touch theory in Chapter 5 (political ideology) but don't make it a separate strand.

Most working political scientists specialize in one subfield while reading across all four. Real-world political questions — the rise of populism, climate policy, the future of NATO — almost always touch multiple subfields. Specialization is the entry point; integration is the goal.

### Methodologically, the field uses many tools

A century ago, political science was mostly historical narrative and legal analysis. Today the field is methodologically diverse:

- **Quantitative methods** — statistics, regression, large-N comparative datasets. Especially common in American politics and comparative politics.
- **Qualitative methods** — case studies, process tracing, archival work, interviews, ethnography. Especially common in comparative politics, IR, and political theory.
- **Game theory and formal modeling** — mathematical models of strategic interaction. Especially common in IR and some American politics.
- **Experimental methods** — survey experiments, field experiments. Increasingly common across subfields.

The methodological divides are real and sometimes contentious. A behaviorist quantitative scholar and an interpretivist qualitative scholar may disagree on what counts as evidence. The book introduces methods as they become useful for specific topics, rather than making methodology its own chapter.

## How this book is organized

Twenty-one chapters in five Parts. The Parts move outward from the individual to the global system.

**Part 1 — Foundations (Chapters 1-2).** What political science studies; what politics is. You're reading Part 1 right now.

**Part 2 — Individuals (Chapters 3-7).** How individuals engage with politics. Political behavior, ideology, civil liberties, participation, public opinion.

**Part 3 — Groups (Chapters 8-11).** How individuals coordinate to act politically. Group political activity, civil rights, parties, interest groups, elections.

**Part 4 — Institutions (Chapters 12-16).** The structures that translate group activity into governance. Legislatures, executives and bureaucracies, courts, media.

**Part 5 — States and International Relations (Chapters 17-21).** How states relate to each other. Regime types, IR theory, international law and organizations, international political economy.

The order matters. Individuals come first because they're the basic unit. Groups follow because political action is mostly group action. Institutions follow because institutions translate group preferences into outcomes. States and the international system come last because they're the largest scale.

A reader in a hurry can read Parts 2-5 in any order. A reader who wants the full theoretical arc should read straight through.

## A note on the running project and the LLM layer

This book is *Introduction to Political Science with LLMs*. The "with LLMs" part is not decorative. Every chapter ends with a structured exercise that uses Claude (or another LLM) to advance a running project. By the end of the book, those exercises produce a substantial deliverable — a policy analysis, a regime comparison, an election tracker, or similar — depending on the project you select after reading Chapter 00.

The LLM layer is also not a replacement for the analytical work the chapters teach. Claude can summarize a 300-page government report in seconds. Claude cannot tell you which 300-page report matters or what the report's claims would imply for a specific policy question. The first task is text-handling; the second is political-science reasoning. The book teaches the second.

## A note on contested politics

Political science is the study of politics. It is not the practice of politics. The book does not tell you which party to support, which policies to favor, or which countries are good or bad. Where the field has empirical findings, the book reports them. Where the field studies contested values — what should the welfare state do? what's the right balance between liberty and security? — the book presents the major positions charitably and helps you think about them, without taking sides.

This even-handedness is a discipline, not a dodge. A textbook that took partisan sides would teach less than one that helps students reason through political questions independently. The book trusts you to form your own views once you have the field's tools.

You'll meet political concepts where reasonable people genuinely disagree. The book treats those disagreements as features of the topic, not problems to resolve in either direction.

## What this book does not cover

A few omissions worth flagging:

- **Detailed methodology.** The book introduces methods as they become useful but doesn't teach statistics, game theory, or qualitative research design as standalone strands. A second course in political science methods would do this work.
- **Deep area studies.** The book draws cases from many countries but doesn't do deep coverage of any one beyond the United States. Country-specific courses (US politics, comparative politics of Europe, Middle East politics) would.
- **Normative political theory beyond Chapter 5.** The treatment of ideology in Chapter 5 introduces the major modern frameworks. A standalone political theory course would cover Plato through Rawls in depth.
- **Public administration and policy detail.** The book covers institutions and policy at a conceptual level. Detailed policy analysis (health, education, environmental, defense) would require subject-matter courses.

The book's ambition is breadth — give a student the field's basic tools and a sense of how they fit together. Depth comes from later courses or the running project.

## LLM Exercise — Chapter 01: Introduction to Political Science (Election Tracker Project)

**Project:** Election Tracker.
**What you're building this chapter:** the central political question the election is deciding — framed in political-science terms.
**Tool:** **Claude Project**.

---

**The Prompt:**

```
Chapter 1 of my Election Tracker project. The Election Tracker
Charter is in this Claude Project. Chapter 1 introduced political
science as the systematic study of politics — distinct from
journalism (which describes), advocacy (which urges), and common
sense (which assumes); the major subfields (American politics,
comparative politics, international relations, political theory);
the empirical-theoretical distinction.

Write the brief's "Central Political Question" section in 400-600
words.

1. **The campaign-press question.** What is the question the
   campaign-press treats as central? (Often: who's winning?)

2. **The political-science question.** What is the deeper
   structural question a political scientist would identify? Often
   different from the campaign-press question. Examples:
   - "Is this election the moment a realigning coalition emerges?"
   - "Is this election a referendum on incumbent performance, or
     a choice between visions of governance?"
   - "Will this election test institutional legitimacy under
     stress?"
   - "What does this election reveal about the median voter's
     drift over the past decade?"

3. **The four-subfield contributions.**
   - American politics (or country-specific politics): what does
     this election reveal about that country's political dynamics?
   - Comparative politics: what cross-national comparison is
     informative?
   - International relations: what international consequences?
   - Political theory: what normative questions does this
     election raise (about democracy, representation, justice)?

4. **The empirical-vs-theoretical distinction.** State what
   empirical questions you'll need data to answer (turnout by
   demographic, polling accuracy, vote share, etc.) and what
   theoretical questions you'll need argument to answer (what
   counts as a "free and fair" election; what makes a coalition
   "realigning"; etc.).

End with: state your initial hypothesis about the election's
outcome and significance. Be honest — most students arrive with
a guess. Documenting it now makes it possible to learn from
how the analysis updates the guess.
```

---

**What this produces:** A 400-600 word section on the political-science framing. The "campaign-press vs. political-science question" distinction is the discipline this chapter trains.

**Connection to previous chapters:** Ch 00's Charter set the verification discipline; Ch 1 makes the analytical-framing discipline visible.

**Preview of next chapter:** Chapter 2 covers what politics IS (Easton's "authoritative allocation of values"). You'll apply it to identify what's being authoritatively allocated by your election.
