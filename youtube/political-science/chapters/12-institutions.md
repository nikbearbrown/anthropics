## The puzzle of why same preferences produce different outcomes

Two countries, both democracies. Both have populations with similar median preferences on, say, environmental policy. One country adopts strong climate legislation; the other doesn't.

Why?

The standard explanation reaches for differences in public opinion, party strength, or interest-group power. These matter, but they don't explain the puzzle if we've stipulated similar preferences. The deeper answer is **institutions** — the rules that structure how preferences get aggregated into decisions.

Consider US legislation. The US has a strong status-quo bias built into its institutions: the bicameral legislature requires both chambers to agree; the Senate's filibuster requires 60 of 100 votes for most major legislation; the president can veto bills; courts can strike them down. A 51% majority in the country may not be enough to enact a 51%-supported policy. The institutional friction is by design — the framers wanted change to be hard.

In a parliamentary system with a unicameral legislature and majority rule, a 51% voting bloc can enact major legislation in days. The same preferences produce dramatically different outcomes.

This is the central insight of **institutionalism** in political science: institutions are not just venues where politics happens. They are causal forces in their own right. Parts 2 and 3 took preferences (individuals, groups) as the starting point. Part 4 takes institutions as the starting point and asks how they shape what gets done.

## Three concepts to install before Part 4

### 1. Formal vs. informal institutions

**Formal institutions** are the rules written down — constitutions, laws, parliamentary procedures, court rules. They're explicit and enforceable through some mechanism (police, courts, sanctions).

**Informal institutions** are the rules everyone follows but no one wrote — political norms, conventions, customs. The US president's tradition of yielding to subpoenas, the British monarch's tradition of not interfering in policy, the convention that legislators of opposite parties shake hands after debates. Informal institutions are real — they shape behavior — but they're not enforced through the formal system. They're enforced through reputation, social pressure, and the threat of formal change if violated.

Some political systems work because their informal institutions are strong even when the formal rules would permit very different behavior. Other systems formally look fine but have weak informal norms, with the result that formal authority gets used in ways the writers didn't intend.

The 2020-2024 period in the US has been an active stress test of informal institutions — many norms (peaceful transfer of power, presidential restraint in attacking specific judges, congressional deference to ethics processes) faced unusual pressure. Whether the formal institutions are robust enough to function when informal norms erode is a live question in contemporary American politics.

### 2. Veto players

A **veto player** is an actor whose agreement is required for policy change. The more veto players a system has, the more status quo bias it has — change requires getting all of them to agree.

The US has many veto players: House majority, Senate majority (or 60 votes), President, Supreme Court (effectively, through judicial review). A unitary parliamentary system might have just one veto player: the parliamentary majority. The same set of voter preferences, run through these two systems, produces different rates of policy change.

George Tsebelis's *Veto Players* (2002) made this framework central in comparative politics. We'll use it implicitly throughout Part 4 — different institutional configurations produce different numbers of veto players, and that count is a good first-pass predictor of how reformist or conservative a system will be.

### 3. Path dependence

**Path dependence** means that a system's current state depends on the order in which past events happened, not just on current conditions. Once a path is taken, the costs of switching to a different path rise — sometimes prohibitively.

The US two-party system is path dependent: the structure of single-member-district elections (institutional feature) plus a century of party-organization investment makes a successful third party very expensive to build, even though many voters might prefer one. Switching electoral systems would be costly enough that no major actor wants to bear the cost.

Path dependence is why reform is hard. The current institution is suboptimal in many ways, but everyone has built their strategies around it. Switching to a better institution requires coordinated action by people whose advantages depend on the current setup.

Part 4's chapters apply path dependence to specific institutions — why some legislatures stay structured the way they are even when the structure produces poor outcomes, why some judiciaries persist in particular methods of selection.

## What you should bring to Part 4

Three habits.

**1. Treat the rules as causal.** When a political outcome puzzles you, ask: what institutional configuration produced this? The answer is often more illuminating than asking what voters wanted.

**2. Look for veto players.** When change is slow or absent, count the veto players. When change is fast or surprising, ask which veto players were bypassed or persuaded.

**3. Distinguish the formal from the informal.** When formal institutions seem unchanged but behavior shifts dramatically, the change is usually in informal institutions — norms eroding, conventions weakening. The reverse is also true: behavior can stay stable while formal rules change, when the informal norms remain.
