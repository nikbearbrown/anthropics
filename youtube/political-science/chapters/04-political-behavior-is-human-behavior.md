**TL;DR.** Political behavior follows the same logic — and the same limits — as other human decision-making. People aren't fully rational, fully selfish, or fully strategic. They're a mix of rational-when-it-matters, intuitive-when-it's-cheaper, expressive when identity is on the line, and motivated-reasoning when the truth is uncomfortable. The result is *patterned irrationality* — systematic enough to predict at the group level, idiosyncratic enough to surprise at the individual level. This chapter installs the basic framework.

## The puzzle of the unfair offer

Sit two strangers at a table. Hand $10 to one of them — call her A. Tell her: "Offer some portion of this $10 to B. If B accepts, you both keep your shares. If B rejects, both of you get nothing."

A standard rational-choice model predicts a clean answer. A keeps $9, offers $1 to B. B reasons: $1 is more than $0, so accept. Both walk away — A with $9, B with $1.

The actual experiment, run hundreds of times across cultures, almost never produces that result. A typically offers $4 or $5. When A offers $1 or $2, B usually rejects. Both walk away with nothing — angry, but both with nothing.

This is the **ultimatum game**, and it doesn't fit the rational-choice model. The "rational" play (A offers $1, B accepts) is unstable in practice because *humans value fairness enough to punish unfairness even at personal cost.* B walks away from $1 to deny A the $9. A, anticipating this, offers $4 or $5 to forestall the rejection.

The ultimatum game is a microcosm of much of political behavior. The simple model (rational, self-interested actors maximizing material gain) explains some things and fails on others. The richer model — where people are partly rational, partly intuitive, partly emotional, partly identity-driven — explains more, at the cost of being less crisp.

This chapter lays out that richer model. Five propositions:

1. People use both fast (intuitive) and slow (deliberative) thinking.
2. Rationality is a useful fiction for skilled actors but breaks for ordinary voters.
3. Motivated reasoning shapes how people process political information.
4. Identity often outweighs material interest.
5. Context determines behavior more than abstract preferences.

## Concept 1 — Two ways of thinking, two ways of voting

Daniel Kahneman's *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011) crystallized a distinction that political psychologists had been working with for decades: humans navigate the world using two cognitive systems.[^1]

[^1]: Daniel Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 2011).

**Fast thinking** is intuitive, automatic, pattern-matching. It runs on autopilot most of the time. When you walk into a familiar room and feel the temperature is wrong before you check the thermostat, that's fast thinking. It's cheap to use — minimal cognitive effort — and it works well for situations the brain has seen before.

**Slow thinking** is deliberative, careful, analytical. It runs when fast thinking flags something as unusual. When you have to do long division by hand, weigh competing options for a major decision, or learn a new game, you're using slow thinking. It's expensive — cognitively demanding — and it works well for situations that don't pattern-match cleanly.

The two systems work together. Fast thinking handles most of the day. Slow thinking takes over when fast thinking fails. The catch: slow thinking is so expensive that humans use it as little as they can get away with.

### Why this matters for politics

Political decisions are mostly fast-thinking territory.

**Heuristics.** Voters rely on cognitive shortcuts. The most common political heuristic is **partisan signaling**: "I'll vote for the candidate from my party because that's a reliable proxy for someone I'd agree with." Partisan signaling is fast, cheap, and usually right enough for the voter's purposes — checking each candidate's record on every issue would take days. The shortcut produces predictable patterns at the population level even though individual voters are barely thinking about specific candidates.

Other political heuristics: voting for the more familiar name, voting for the candidate whose advertising you've seen, voting for the candidate who matches your demographic, voting against the incumbent if the economy feels bad. Each is a fast-thinking shortcut. Each substitutes for what slow thinking would do (research, comparison, deliberation) at much lower cost.

**The rationality-of-ignorance.** Anthony Downs's classic argument: a single voter's vote almost never determines an election outcome, so the rational amount of effort to put into being well-informed is almost zero.[^2] This is *rational ignorance*: the conscious decision not to invest in political information because the personal payoff is too low. Most voters operate this way without thinking about it.

[^2]: Anthony Downs, *An Economic Theory of Democracy* (Harper, 1957).

**Skilled actors think slow.** Politicians, lobbyists, journalists, and political scientists all use slow thinking on political questions because their jobs depend on it. The ordinary voter, whose job doesn't depend on it, mostly doesn't.

This produces a structural feature of democracy: the people doing politics professionally use careful analytical thinking, while the people *being governed* use heuristics. The two groups end up speaking different languages — one careful and contingent, the other quick and confident. Most political miscommunication has roots here.

### Common misconceptions

- *"Fast thinking is bad."* It isn't. It's how humans handle the volume of decisions modern life requires. The problem is when fast thinking processes inputs (advertising, partisan media) designed to exploit it.
- *"Better-educated voters use slow thinking on politics."* Education helps — but the rational-ignorance argument applies to everyone. Even highly educated voters use heuristics for most political questions; they just have access to more sophisticated ones.

## Concept 2 — When rationality breaks

The simplest model of political decision-making is the **rational-choice model**: actors are self-interested, calculating, and strategic. Given preferences and information, they maximize expected utility.

This model works well in some contexts and badly in others. Knowing where the line falls is part of the job.

### Where rational choice works

- **Skilled actors.** Politicians, lobbyists, party strategists, interest-group leaders mostly behave the way rational-choice theory predicts. They have clear objectives, sustained attention, and feedback loops that punish poor strategy.
- **Repeated, high-stakes interactions.** When the same actors face the same decisions repeatedly with significant consequences (legislative bargaining, international diplomacy), rational-choice models track behavior reasonably well.
- **Aggregate-level patterns.** Even when individuals are "irrational," populations of millions often produce behavior that looks rational on average, because the irrationalities cancel out.

### Where rational choice breaks

- **Single-shot voting.** A voter's choice is largely consequence-free at the individual level (your one vote almost never decides). The rational-choice apparatus designed for high-stakes repeated interactions doesn't fit.
- **Identity-driven politics.** Voting for a candidate because they share your identity isn't strategic — it's expressive. We'll come back to this.
- **Fairness-driven behavior.** As the ultimatum game showed: people reject offers that are profitable but unfair. This isn't self-interest; it's something else.
- **Motivated reasoning.** When evidence contradicts what someone wants to believe, they often reject the evidence. Rational-choice theory predicts updating beliefs based on evidence; humans often do the opposite.

### Predictably irrational

Dan Ariely popularized the term "predictably irrational" to describe behaviors that violate rational-choice predictions but in *systematic*, *foreseeable* ways.[^3] If everyone in the ultimatum game offered randomly between $0 and $10, that would be irrationality with no pattern. The actual finding — most people offer $4 or $5, most reject offers below $2 — is a systematic deviation from the rational-choice prediction. Predictable, just not in the direction theory expects.

[^3]: Dan Ariely, *Predictably Irrational* (HarperCollins, 2008).

For political analysis, "predictably irrational" is the right working assumption. Voters don't behave as the simple model predicts, but they behave in ways that follow patterns. The patterns are the data the discipline studies.

### Common misconceptions

- *"Rational-choice theory is wrong."* It's right for some uses, wrong for others. The skill is knowing which.
- *"If voters were better educated, rationality would prevail."* Some of the predictable-irrationality findings hold for highly educated subjects too. Education changes the *content* of beliefs, not the *cognitive mechanisms* that produce them.

## Concept 3 — Motivated reasoning, identity, and expressive behavior

Three related phenomena reshape how political behavior actually works.

### Motivated reasoning

**Motivated reasoning** is the tendency to embrace beliefs that fit one's existing preferences and reject beliefs that don't, regardless of the evidence.[^4] It is not deliberate dishonesty. It's a cognitive process that operates below conscious awareness — the brain unconsciously weighting confirming evidence more heavily than disconfirming evidence.

[^4]: Toby Bolsen, James N. Druckman, and Fay Lomax Cook, "The Influence of Partisan Motivated Reasoning on Public Opinion," *Political Behavior* 36, no. 2 (2014): 235–262.

Motivated reasoning shapes how partisans process political information. A study finding that policies favored by one party produce good outcomes will be accepted readily by that party's voters and resisted by the other party's voters — *even when the study is methodologically identical.* The belief comes first; the evaluation of evidence follows.

A specific finding worth flagging: each side typically views the other side as the side with motivated reasoning. Republicans see Democrats as ideologically blinded; Democrats see Republicans as ideologically blinded. Both are partly right and partly wrong. The motivated-reasoning literature suggests that the cognitive mechanism operates roughly equally across the political spectrum, with content varying.[^5]

[^5]: Thomas J. Leeper and Rune Slothuus, "Political Parties, Motivated Reasoning, and Public Opinion Formation," *Advances in Political Psychology* 35, S1 (2014): 129–156.

### Identity over material interest

A common observation in political analysis: voters often vote against their material interests. A working-class voter votes for a candidate whose tax policies favor the wealthy. A wealthy voter supports redistribution that would cost them money. By the rational-choice model, these are puzzles.

By the **expressive model**, they aren't. Voting for a candidate isn't (only) about predicted material outcomes. It's also about *expressing who you are.* Party identification, ideology, religion, cultural identity — these all give voters a stable identity that voting expresses. The candidate matches the identity; the vote follows.

Specific cases:

- **Trump's "Make America Great Again."** The slogan promised intangible national prestige rather than specific material benefits. Many voters found the intangible appealing in a way the rational-choice model can't easily price.
- **Evo Morales (Bolivia, 2006-2019).** Indigenous-identity politics — pride and recognition — drove a significant share of Morales's support, alongside material policies.
- **Black Lives Matter (2020 onward).** Movement support combined strategic policy goals (police reform) with expressive identity claims (recognition, dignity).

The ratio of expressive to strategic in any specific political action varies. Most political action has both. The rational-choice analyst who underweights the expressive component will be repeatedly surprised.

### Strategic vs. expressive

To install the distinction cleanly:

**Strategic** behavior is action chosen to produce a desired outcome. A senator votes against legislation because she believes the legislation will have bad consequences. A campaign donor gives money to influence which candidate wins.

**Expressive** behavior is action chosen to communicate identity, values, or solidarity, regardless of consequence. A protester carries a sign at a march that won't change policy. A rally attendee wears a hat that signals belonging. Voting in a non-competitive district where the outcome is foregone.

Most political action mixes both. The discipline of distinguishing them is what lets you analyze political behavior accurately.

### Common misconceptions

- *"People who vote against their material interests are voting incorrectly."* By a rational-choice metric, maybe. By an expressive-identity metric, they're voting correctly — for the candidate who matches who they are.
- *"Motivated reasoning is something the other side does."* It's something *everyone* does. Recognizing it in yourself is harder than recognizing it in others.

## Synthesis — patterned irrationality as the working assumption

Three concepts, one framework.

Political behavior isn't fully rational, fully selfish, or fully strategic. It's *patterned irrationality*: systematic enough to study, idiosyncratic enough to surprise. The patterns include:

- Heavy reliance on heuristics (especially partisan signaling)
- Rational ignorance about most political questions
- Fairness-driven behavior that overrides material self-interest
- Motivated reasoning when evidence contradicts preferences
- Expressive action that's identity-driven rather than instrumental
- Context-dependence — behavior shifts with relationship, repetition, stakes

For the running project, this matters because every political phenomenon you analyze will involve actors whose decisions don't fit the simple rational model. A campaign strategy. A protest movement. A regime transition. An election upset. Each will be partly rational, partly something else. The skilled analyst names both components.
