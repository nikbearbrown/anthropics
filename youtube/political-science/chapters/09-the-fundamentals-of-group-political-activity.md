## The puzzle of why everyone agrees and nothing happens

In 2015, 197 countries signed the Paris Climate Accord, pledging to reduce emissions enough to limit warming to well below 2°C. The agreement was unanimous on the goal. Each signing country recognized that climate change would damage them — directly through extreme weather, sea-level rise, agricultural disruption; indirectly through migration pressures and economic shocks.

In 2022, an analysis of national progress found that almost no major emitter had actually met its commitments. The US, China, India, the EU, Russia — the countries responsible for over half of global emissions — had each missed their targets. Why?

The answer is that climate cooperation is a *collective action problem*. Each country, individually, would benefit if it could free-ride on others' emissions cuts. The atmospheric warming reduction comes whether or not any one country participates; the economic costs of cutting emissions are borne entirely by the country that cuts. Each country's self-interest argues for promising cuts loudly while delivering them quietly. When all countries reason this way at once, the result is what we observe: lots of pledges, little action.

This isn't unique to climate. The same logic applies to:
- Why individual citizens vote rarely even when their group's interests are at stake
- Why labor unions struggle to organize without legal protection
- Why interest groups for diffuse interests (consumers, taxpayers) are weaker than for concentrated ones (specific industries)
- Why arms races escalate even when both sides would prefer disarmament
- Why most fisheries collapse if not actively managed

Mancur Olson's *The Logic of Collective Action* (1965) is the canonical analysis of this phenomenon.[^1] This chapter installs his framework and the major mechanisms groups use to overcome it.

[^1]: Mancur Olson, *The Logic of Collective Action* (Harvard University Press, 1965).

## Concept 1 — Political socialization and political culture

Before getting to the collective action problem proper, install two preliminary concepts.

### Political socialization

**Political socialization** is the gradual process by which individuals develop political identities — ideologies, party identifications, attitudes about government, beliefs about civic duty.

The major influences, in rough order of strength:

**Family.** The single strongest predictor of political identity in most societies. Estimates vary, but roughly 60-70% of US teens adopt their parents' partisan identification. This isn't because parents indoctrinate children; it's because family environment shapes the political information children receive, the values they internalize, and the social networks they're part of.

**Peers.** Friends, schoolmates, online communities. Peer influence rises as children become teenagers and young adults, and increasingly is mediated by social media — which creates self-selecting "echo chambers" where peer influence skews further from family influence.

**Ethnicity, religion, gender.** These shape political identity in ways that often crosscut family. A Mexican-American Catholic woman in California faces a different political environment than her Anglo-Protestant male neighbor, regardless of whether the families had similar political views.

**Schools.** Modest independent effect. Schools mostly reinforce family and ethnic patterns — a private religious school amplifies religious identity; a diverse public school exposes students to alternative views — but rarely produce independent political effects of their own.

**Major events.** Generation-defining events — the Great Depression, World War II, Vietnam, 9/11, the 2008 financial crisis, COVID — shape the cohort that experienced them at formative ages.

For your project, when analyzing why a particular movement gathered support among certain demographics, the political-socialization framework tells you to ask: what shared formative experiences did these people have? What family backgrounds? What media environment? Movements rarely build broad coalitions across very different socialization patterns; understanding the socialization patterns predicts the movement's likely reach.

### Political culture

**Political culture** is the set of shared attitudes, values, and beliefs about politics that characterize a group. It operates at three levels:

**Elite culture.** The political values and assumptions held by the wealthy and powerful. These shape what proposals get serious consideration, what's framed as "responsible" or "reasonable," and what gets dismissed. Elite culture is often more cosmopolitan, more institutionally trusting, and more centrist than mass culture.

**Mass culture.** The broadest views of the bulk population. This is what surveys typically measure. Mass culture has been weakening in many countries as media fragments — there's no longer a "common conversation" the way three TV networks once produced.

**Minority cultures.** Subgroups with distinctive political beliefs. Religious traditions that diverge from the mainstream, regional identities, racial/ethnic groups, ideological subcultures (libertarians, democratic socialists, religious-right activists). Minority cultures shape coalition possibilities — they define which groups can be allies, which are antagonists, and which are simply uninterested.

A specific empirical finding: countries vary enormously in political culture along the individualism-collectivism axis. Surveys show about 60% of Americans prioritize personal freedom over state action, vs. about 80% of Lithuanians and others in former Soviet states preferring active state involvement.[^2] These differences correlate with policy preferences — the US tolerates more inequality and provides less universal welfare than European democracies — but the cultural attitude is causally upstream of the policy.

[^2]: Pew Research Center comparative cultural data; `[verify]` for current vintage.

The reverse-causation argument also matters: culture shapes politics, but politics also shapes culture. Decades of US elite messaging about "small government" reinforced individualist cultural values. Decades of Scandinavian welfare-state experience reinforced collectivist values. Culture and policy reinforce each other over time.

### Common misconceptions

- *"People form their own political views."* In a sense, yes — adults can change their minds. But the formation process is heavily shaped by family, peers, and culture. Someone who insists they "thought through" their politics is usually unaware of how much they absorbed it before they could think through it.
- *"Political culture is destiny."* It isn't. Cultures change — sometimes slowly (decades), sometimes rapidly (during crises). The relationship between culture and politics is mutual, not one-way.

## Concept 2 — The collective action problem in three forms

Now to the central material. Olson's basic insight: individual rationality and group rationality often diverge. Three forms of the problem are worth installing distinctly.

### Tragedy of the commons

A shared resource that no one owns. Each individual user has an incentive to take as much as possible, since they capture the full benefit of their use while spreading the cost across all users. When many users reason this way, the resource is depleted.

Garrett Hardin's classic 1968 framing: a shared pasture where individual herders have an incentive to graze more cattle than the pasture can sustain. Each herder gains the full benefit of their additional cattle but bears only a fraction of the cost (depleted grass). Result: overgrazing.[^3]

[^3]: Garrett Hardin, "The Tragedy of the Commons," *Science* 162 (1968): 1243-1248.

Real-world cases:

- **Atlantic bluefin tuna.** Commercial fishing depleted Atlantic bluefin populations by about 80% from the 1970s to 2000s. Individual fishing nations and fleets had incentives to take as much as they could get; recovery required international quota agreements (ICCAT). Bluefin populations have partially recovered as quotas tightened.
- **Black rhinos.** Hunted near to extinction in southern Africa during the 20th century. Saving the species required heavy-handed government action: international trade bans (CITES), protected areas, and sometimes military protection of remaining herds.
- **Climate.** The most consequential commons in the modern world. The atmosphere is shared; emissions cause warming; benefits of emissions accrue locally to the emitter while costs are global. China, the US, the EU, and India together produce over half of global CO₂. None has a unilateral incentive to bear the cost of major reductions.
- **Moa bird (New Zealand).** Hunted to extinction by Maori within a few hundred years of human arrival. A clean, terrible case.

### Free-riding

In a group whose members would all benefit from coordinated action, each member has an incentive to let others bear the cost of action while still receiving the benefit.

Public broadcasting in countries that fund it through voluntary donations is a classic case. Listeners benefit whether or not they donate. About 80% of listeners don't donate. The 20% who do bear the cost of providing the service for everyone.

Voting is partly a free-riding problem. Your one vote almost never decides an election. If others turn out, the outcome happens whether or not you vote. So why bother? Many people don't. Voter turnout below 100% is, in part, free-riding.

Public goods generally face this problem: clean air, national defense, scientific research, public broadcasting. Each citizen prefers high levels of provision but has individual incentives to underprovide. The standard solution is government — taxation forces contributions from everyone, eliminating the free-riding option.

### Prisoner's dilemma

Two parties would each benefit from cooperating but each has individual incentives to defect. When both reason this way, both defect, and both end up worse than if they had cooperated.

The classic illustration: two suspects in separate interrogation rooms. Each is offered: confess and get a reduced sentence (informing on the other) vs. stay silent (and risk the other confessing). The "rational" individual choice is to confess regardless of what the other does. When both confess, both serve longer sentences than if both had stayed silent. The cooperative outcome (both stay silent) is preferred by both — but neither can trust the other to choose it, so neither does.

Real-world political cases:

- **Arms races.** Both countries would prefer mutual disarmament to mutual armament. But each fears the other will arm if they don't, so both arm. The Cold War US-USSR arms buildup, the current US-China nuclear modernization, the India-Pakistan nuclear standoff — all have prisoner's-dilemma structure.
- **Negative campaign advertising.** Both candidates would prefer to run positive ads (cheaper, more dignified). But each fears the other will go negative, so both go negative. US presidential campaigns have escalated in this direction over decades.
- **Tax compliance with neighbors.** Each of you would prefer everyone (including yourself) to pay taxes honestly. But you each have an incentive to underreport while hoping others pay. Tax-compliance cultures vary enormously across countries; the variation reflects how successfully each has overcome the prisoner's-dilemma structure.

### Common misconceptions

- *"Collective action problems mean groups are dysfunctional."* They mean groups *can* fail to coordinate even when their members agree on goals. Groups that succeed have found ways to overcome the structural problem.
- *"The free rider is acting badly."* The free rider is acting *rationally* in the narrow sense. The problem is that individual rationality produces collective irrationality. Solving it requires changing the incentive structure, not lecturing the free riders.

## Concept 3 — How groups solve the problem

Three classes of mechanism. Each works for different sizes and types of groups.

### Coercion

The simplest solution: make non-cooperation illegal or costly. Government taxation is the canonical case — you don't get to decide whether to fund national defense; the IRS will collect.

Coercion works best for clear, measurable contributions (paying taxes, military service when conscripted) and worst for ambiguous ones (how much should someone "care" about politics?). Most modern states use coercion for the most important public goods and rely on other mechanisms for everything else.

The 2021 G-7 agreement on a global minimum corporate tax (15%) is a recent example of coercion solving a free-rider problem at the international level. Without coordination, countries had incentives to compete for corporate domiciles by lowering taxes, depriving everyone of revenue. The agreement, if enforced, sets a floor below which no signatory will go.

### Selective incentives

Olson's most-cited solution: provide individual benefits available only to group members.

Labor unions in countries with closed-shop laws operate this way: union dues are mandatory for workers in unionized workplaces, but in exchange members receive collective bargaining and individual workplace representation. The incentive to join (or rather, not to defect) is the access to bargaining benefits.

Political parties offer selective incentives: campaign volunteers get insider access, professional connections, eventual job opportunities. Voluntary associations (AARP, NRA) offer member services (insurance, magazines, discounts) tied to membership.

Selective incentives work because they make the individual cost-benefit calculation favor cooperation. Without the selective incentive, the rational individual free-rides; with it, the rational individual joins.

### Trust, repetition, and small numbers

For small groups, the collective action problem can be solved through informal mechanisms: repeated interaction, mutual monitoring, social pressure.

Elinor Ostrom (Nobel laureate in economics, 2009) documented this extensively. Small communities managing shared resources (Japanese village irrigation, Spanish *huerta* irrigation in Valencia, Philippine *zanjera* networks) developed elaborate informal rules that worked because everyone could see whether everyone else was complying.[^4] Repeated face-to-face interaction made defection costly socially even when it was rational economically.

[^4]: Elinor Ostrom, *Governing the Commons* (Cambridge University Press, 1990).

The mechanism breaks down at scale. Modern climate cooperation requires billions of actors; you can't monitor each other or build trust through repeated face-to-face interaction across countries. International institutions try to substitute (treaty bodies, monitoring regimes, sanctions for non-compliance), with mixed success.

### Framing and cultural mobilization

A subtler mechanism: change how individuals perceive the situation, so that what *was* free-riding becomes morally costly.

Political and social movements rely heavily on framing. The civil-rights movement's framing of segregation as a moral abomination, not just a policy disagreement, changed how individual citizens perceived their own complicity in tolerating it. The environmental movement's framing of pollution as harming "your children's future" similarly raises the moral stakes of individual action.

Effective framing doesn't change the underlying incentives — it changes the psychic costs and benefits each individual perceives. Free-riding becomes shameful; cooperation becomes virtuous. This works to the extent that the framing is widely accepted and that participants identify with the movement's values.

### Worked example — Why the Arab Spring failed (mostly)

The 2010-2012 Arab Spring uprisings illustrate both the power and the limits of collective action.

The protests succeeded in the immediate term in several countries: Tunisia's regime fell in January 2011, Egypt's in February, Libya's by August. Mass mobilization had overcome the standard authoritarian defenses. Social media and Al Jazeera provided coordination mechanisms that bypassed traditional state-controlled media. Trust networks among activists built rapidly through repeated face-to-face interaction in Tahrir Square and elsewhere.

The protests largely failed in the longer term. Egypt returned to military rule under General el-Sisi by 2013. Libya descended into civil war and prolonged instability. Syria dissolved into one of the 21st century's most catastrophic civil wars. Tunisia is the closest thing to a partial success, with a democratic transition that held until further deterioration in the 2020s.

Why the divergent outcomes? Several factors, but a collective-action perspective suggests:

- Sustained mobilization to *build* democratic institutions is harder than mobilization to *topple* a regime. The first phase relies on the energy of opposition; the second requires the slow, costly work of building. Free-riding rises sharply as the existential urgency fades.
- New regimes face their own collective action problems — making constitutional bargains, sharing power, paying for services. Pre-existing trust networks among activists don't automatically translate to trust between previously hostile groups.
- External actors (foreign governments, international institutions, transnational movements) can support new democracies but can't solve their internal coordination problems.

The Arab Spring's mixed record shouldn't be read as proof that mass mobilization doesn't work. It does work — for specific, concrete demands (regime removal). It works less reliably for the harder ongoing work of governing.

### Common misconceptions

- *"Selective incentives are bribes."* They're solutions to a structural problem. Without them, large groups with shared interests often can't act on those interests, even when doing so would benefit everyone.
- *"If only people were less selfish, collective action problems would disappear."* Even people who genuinely care about the collective face the structural problem. The IT engineer who wants climate action still rationally drives a car if individual transit choices have little effect on global emissions. The structure produces the problem; changing individuals' values is partial at best.

## Synthesis

Group political activity rests on a paradox: individuals acting rationally for themselves often produce outcomes that harm everyone. Olson's framework names the problem; Ostrom's research shows the solutions; political socialization and culture set the substrate on which all of this operates.

For the running project, this chapter gives you the tools to diagnose any political mobilization:

- *What socialization patterns produced this group's identity?* What family, peer, and cultural influences brought participants into political life?
- *What collective action problem does the group face?* Is it tragedy of the commons (shared resource), free-riding (concentrated benefits, dispersed contribution), or prisoner's dilemma (mutual cooperation desired, defection rewarded)?
- *What mechanism is the group using to solve the problem?* Coercion, selective incentives, trust networks, cultural framing, or some combination?
- *Why has the group succeeded or failed?* The answer usually has structural components (the mechanism worked or didn't) as well as contingent ones (luck, leadership, external events).
