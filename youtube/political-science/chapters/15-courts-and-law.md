**TL;DR.** Courts are the institution that takes a written rule — a statute, a constitution, a treaty — and applies it to a specific dispute about specific people. The work sounds mechanical and isn't: every interesting case involves a rule that doesn't quite fit the facts, and the judge's job is to figure out, with reasons, what the rule should mean here. Different legal traditions answer that question differently. The differences matter.

---

In 1803, the U.S. Supreme Court decided a case about a missing piece of paper. A man named William Marbury had been appointed a justice of the peace in the District of Columbia in the final days of John Adams's presidency. The commission — the formal document making him a justice — had been signed by Adams and sealed but not delivered before Adams left office. Thomas Jefferson, the new president, told his Secretary of State, James Madison, not to deliver it. Marbury sued.

The case looked small. It became *Marbury v. Madison*, and the question Chief Justice John Marshall had to answer was whether the Supreme Court could order Madison to deliver the commission. Marshall's opinion did three things at once. He held that Marbury was entitled to his commission. He held that the Court could not order Madison to deliver it because the law authorizing such orders was unconstitutional. And — this was the move that mattered — he held that the Court had the power to declare laws unconstitutional in the first place.

That power is not in the U.S. Constitution. The Constitution does not say "the Supreme Court may strike down acts of Congress that violate the Constitution." Marshall *inferred* the power from the structure of the constitutional system: if the Constitution is the supreme law, and judges decide cases under the law, then judges must say what the Constitution means when it conflicts with a statute. Otherwise the Constitution would be no different from any ordinary law that Congress could amend at will.

The reasoning was elegant. It was also a power grab — a power that no one had expressly given to the courts and that, once asserted, became the foundation of American constitutional politics. Two and a quarter centuries later, every U.S. policy fight over abortion, gun rights, voting access, free speech, presidential power, regulatory authority eventually runs through this single move.

This is what courts do. They take rules written for general cases and apply them to specific situations. They take ambiguous text and decide what it means. They are political institutions that pretend, often successfully, not to be political. The pretense is part of how they work, and understanding when the pretense holds and when it breaks is half of legal politics.

This chapter is about that. Three concepts organize it: what the law actually is (statute, common law, constitutional, customary), how courts decide what the law means, and where the courts sit in the political system.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

1. Distinguish four sources of law — statutes, constitutions, common law / case law, and customary or international law — and explain how courts use each.
2. Compare common-law and civil-law legal traditions and predict the kinds of legal reasoning each produces.
3. Explain judicial review — the power of courts to strike down government actions as unconstitutional — and identify which countries have it and how strongly.
4. Read a judicial opinion as a political act: identify the legal text, the chosen interpretive method, the reasoning, and the policy consequences.
5. Diagnose the trade-offs between different methods of constitutional interpretation (originalism, textualism, living constitutionalism, purposive interpretation).
6. Place courts in the larger political system — when they constrain elected branches, when they enable them, and when they are themselves constrained.

## Concept 1 — What "the law" actually is

When you say "it's against the law," you might mean any of four different things. Each has a different source, a different relationship to courts, and a different political logic.

**Statutes.** Written laws passed by legislatures. The U.S. tax code, the Clean Air Act, the Affordable Care Act. The laws of your state about traffic, contracts, and property. Most of the law that affects daily life is statutory. Statutes are the easy case for legal reasoning — there is a written text, and the question is what the text means.

**Constitutions.** A higher level of law, structuring how the government itself operates and protecting fundamental rights. The U.S. Constitution. The German Basic Law. The Japanese Constitution. The Indian Constitution. Most countries have written constitutions; the U.K. has an "unwritten" constitution that is actually a collection of statutes, conventions, and judicial decisions accumulated over centuries. Constitutions are usually harder to amend than ordinary statutes — that is part of what makes them constitutions.

**Common law / case law.** Legal rules developed by judges in the course of deciding specific cases, accumulated over centuries. The doctrine of negligence in tort law, the rules governing contract formation, the principles of property law — most of this is not in any statute. It exists because case after case worked out the principles. In England, the United States, Canada, Australia, India, Israel, and other countries with English-derived legal systems, common law is a major source of law. In civil-law countries (most of continental Europe, Latin America, Japan), this body of judge-made law is much smaller and has different status.

**Customary law and international law.** Rules that derive their authority from long practice (customary law) or from treaties and accepted international norms (international law). Courts sometimes apply these — the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations governs how diplomats are treated; customary international law shapes how countries treat foreigners on their territory. The status of customary and international law in domestic courts varies across countries.

The four sources interact. A statute might modify common-law principles. A constitution might invalidate a statute. A treaty might be implemented through a statute. The lawyer's craft is partly knowing which sources govern a given dispute and how they relate.

### The trade-off — written vs. accumulated law

Legal systems differ in how much they rely on written codes versus accumulated case decisions.

**Civil-law systems** — France, Germany, Japan, most of Latin America — write comprehensive codes covering contract, property, torts, family law, criminal law. The civil code is meant to be the primary source; judges apply the code rather than building doctrine. Case law exists but has weaker formal authority; a court is not bound by previous decisions in the same way.

**Common-law systems** — UK, U.S., Canada, Australia, India, much of the former British Empire — operate with thinner statutes and thicker accumulated case law. The doctrine of *stare decisis* (let the decision stand) means courts are generally bound by previous decisions on similar facts. Legal reasoning is more analogical (how does this case compare to past cases?) than deductive (what does the code require?).

Each system has trade-offs. Civil-law systems offer more predictability for the same type of case (the code says what the code says). Common-law systems offer more responsiveness to fact patterns the legislature didn't anticipate (the doctrine evolves). Civil-law systems can ossify when the code falls behind reality. Common-law systems can produce inconsistent doctrine when courts in different jurisdictions decide similar issues differently.

Most modern legal systems are mixed. Civil-law countries have growing case-law importance, especially in constitutional and administrative law. Common-law countries have growing statutory codification, especially in commercial and regulatory areas. The pure types are rarer than the textbooks suggest.

> ### Dig Deeper — Common-law and civil-law on the same problem
> Pick a legal question — say, what counts as breach of contract under exigent circumstances, or what duty does a homeowner owe to a trespasser. Ask Claude: "Walk me through how a common-law jurisdiction (say, England or California) and a civil-law jurisdiction (say, France or Germany) would approach this question. What sources of law do their judges consult? How does the reasoning differ?" The exercise reveals how legal traditions shape what counts as a good answer.

### Common misconceptions

**"The law is just written rules applied mechanically."** Almost never. Even in civil-law systems, applying a code to a specific case requires interpretation. In common-law systems, the case-law body is so vast that good legal reasoning is mostly about which precedents to apply.

**"Constitutional rights protect you from anything the government does."** Constitutional rights have boundaries, exceptions, and competing rights. Free speech does not protect fraud or true threats; due process has different requirements in different contexts; the right to bear arms in the U.S. is subject to "longstanding prohibitions" the Court has acknowledged. Reading constitutional text without reading the case law produces a misleading picture.

**"International law is not really law."** It is law in the sense that courts apply it, governments comply with it most of the time, and violations have consequences (diplomatic, economic, sometimes military). It is not law in the sense that there is no global police force enforcing it. Whether that makes it "real" law depends on how you define the term.

## Concept 2 — How judges decide

A judge has a case in front of them. There is a statute or a constitutional provision relevant to the case. The text doesn't quite settle the question. What does the judge do?

This is the question of **interpretive method**, and it is more contested than non-lawyers usually realize. Different methods are not just stylistic preferences; they often produce different outcomes from the same text.

**Textualism.** Read the words on the page. Look at what those words ordinarily mean (or meant when written). Apply that meaning to the case. The textualist's argument: the law is what the text says; reading anything else into it is the judge substituting their preferences for the legislature's choices. Justice Antonin Scalia was the most prominent recent textualist.

**Originalism.** Read the words as the people who wrote and ratified them would have understood them. For constitutional text, this often means asking what the words meant in 1787 (or 1791, or 1868 for the post-Civil-War amendments). Closely related to textualism but adds a temporal element: the meaning is fixed at the time of enactment. Major contemporary U.S. originalists include Justices Clarence Thomas and Samuel Alito.

**Purposive interpretation.** Read the words in light of what the legislature was trying to accomplish. If the literal reading would defeat the law's purpose, prefer the reading that serves the purpose. Common in many legal traditions and influential in U.S. statutory interpretation, especially for ambiguous regulatory schemes.

**Living constitutionalism.** Constitutional meaning evolves to meet changing social conditions. The Constitution's broad principles (due process, equal protection, free speech) take their content from contemporary understanding rather than 18th-century understanding. Justice William Brennan was a major mid-20th-century proponent; the current U.S. Supreme Court has fewer prominent practitioners.

**Pragmatism.** Decide based on what produces the best outcome under the circumstances, with attention to legal text but willing to weigh consequences. Justice Stephen Breyer was a notable pragmatist.

These methods can be combined. Most actual judges use textualism for routine statutes, lean originalist or living-constitutionalist for constitutional cases, and switch among methods depending on the issue. The pure types are debating positions more than actual practice.

### The trade-off — fixed meaning vs. evolving meaning

The deep argument behind these methods is about whether legal meaning is fixed at enactment or evolves over time.

**The fixed-meaning case.** Law is stable, predictable, and democratically legitimate when it means what it said when it was passed. If meaning evolves with judicial preferences, then judges become unelected legislators. The way to change the law is through democratic processes — passing new statutes, amending the constitution.

**The evolving-meaning case.** The law was written in 18th-century language for 18th-century circumstances. Applying the original meaning rigidly to modern conditions either renders the law useless or produces results no one would have wanted. The law has to evolve. Otherwise we'd still be applying 1787 understandings of "cruel and unusual punishment" to modern criminal procedure.

Each side has serious points. The empirical question — does originalism produce more predictable, less politicized law than living constitutionalism? — is contested. Proponents of each method argue that their method constrains judges; critics argue that each method gives judges plenty of room to reach the policy outcomes they prefer, just dressed in different doctrinal clothing.

The honest reading: every method allows some judicial discretion. The choice among methods is more a political-philosophical commitment about whose understanding (founders' or current generation's) should govern than it is a technical question about how to read text.

> ### Dig Deeper — Same case, different methods
> Pick a U.S. Supreme Court case where the majority and dissent disagreed sharply about interpretive method. *Bostock v. Clayton County* (2020) is a good one — the textualist majority opinion (by Justice Gorsuch) and the textualist dissent (by Justice Alito) reach opposite conclusions about the meaning of "sex" in Title VII of the Civil Rights Act. Ask Claude: "Walk me through how each opinion applied textualism and reached opposite conclusions. What does this tell us about whether interpretive methods actually constrain judges?"

### Stare decisis — when do courts overrule themselves?

A specific question within interpretive method: when, if ever, should a court overturn a previous decision? The doctrine of *stare decisis* counsels respect for precedent. But every jurisdiction acknowledges that some precedents should be overruled.

The factors courts say they consider:

- Was the original decision correctly reasoned?
- Has subsequent law or fact undermined its premises?
- How much reliance has built up around the precedent?
- Is the precedent workable in practice?

In recent years, the U.S. Supreme Court has overruled several major precedents — *Roe v. Wade* (1973) was overruled by *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization* (2022); the *Chevron* doctrine was overruled by *Loper Bright v. Raimondo* (2024). Critics argue this represents a more aggressive willingness to discard precedent than recent decades had practiced; defenders argue the overruled precedents were wrongly decided and *stare decisis* should not protect bad law indefinitely. The argument is, in effect, about whether stability or correctness is the higher legal virtue when they conflict — and observers' verdicts often track their views on whether the overruled precedents were correctly decided in the first place.

### Common misconceptions

**"Originalism eliminates judicial discretion."** It constrains some discretion and creates other discretion. The historical record is often ambiguous, contested, or silent on contemporary questions; judges still have to choose among historical understandings. Critics of originalism argue that the method's rhetoric of constraint is stronger than its practice.

**"Judges always reach the result their politics would predict."** They often do, but not always. Justice Scalia voted to invalidate flag-burning prohibitions on First Amendment grounds despite his personal views; Justice Ginsburg sided with prosecutors on some Fourth Amendment cases despite her general civil-libertarian instincts. Methodological commitments do produce results that surprise simple political predictions.

**"The U.S. Supreme Court is just an unelected legislature."** It has legislative-like effects, but it operates under significant constraints — it can only decide cases brought to it, it must produce written reasoning, it is bound (at least in theory) by precedent, and its decisions can be overridden by constitutional amendment, statutory override (for non-constitutional cases), or future Court decisions. Calling it a legislature obscures more than it reveals; calling it a non-political technical body obscures even more.

## Concept 3 — Where the courts sit in the political system

Courts are part of the political system, not above it. How much power they exercise depends on three institutional features.

**Judicial review power.** The strongest courts can strike down statutes and executive actions as violating the constitution. The U.S. Supreme Court (since *Marbury*), the German Constitutional Court (since 1949), the Indian Supreme Court, the South African Constitutional Court — all have strong judicial review. The U.K. has weaker judicial review (Parliament is technically supreme; courts can declare statutes incompatible with human rights but cannot strike them down). New Zealand similarly. France's Constitutional Council reviews statutes only before they take effect.

**Appointment process and tenure.** U.S. federal judges are appointed by the President, confirmed by the Senate, and serve for life. Many other countries have shorter terms (Germany's Constitutional Court justices serve 12-year non-renewable terms; many systems have mandatory retirement ages). Lifetime tenure protects independence and creates the high political stakes around individual appointments. Term limits protect against ideological calcification but introduce more political turnover.

**Legitimacy and compliance.** Courts have no army. They depend on the executive to enforce their orders and on the political culture to treat their decisions as binding. The U.S. has unusually strong norms of judicial compliance; many countries have weaker norms, with executives ignoring court rulings or stacking courts when politically convenient. The phrase "an independent judiciary" describes a political achievement that requires constant maintenance.

### The trade-off — judicial review

Strong judicial review gives courts substantial power to constrain elected branches. This produces predictable trade-offs.

**The case for.** Courts can enforce constitutional rights against majorities that would override them. *Brown v. Board of Education* desegregated U.S. public schools when Congress would not. Constitutional courts in post-authoritarian transitions (Germany after 1949, South Africa after 1994) protected fragile democracies against backsliding majorities. Independent courts are part of the answer to "what stops majorities from oppressing minorities?"

**The case against.** Strong judicial review is anti-democratic — unelected judges overriding the choices of elected legislatures. Decisions like *Lochner v. New York* (1905, striking down maximum-hours legislation) used constitutional rhetoric to entrench economic preferences against democratic reform. Whether contemporary judicial review serves democracy or constrains it depends on which decisions you focus on.

The honest reading: judicial review is a powerful tool that has been used both to protect minority rights and to entrench elite preferences. Its overall valence depends on the courts' composition, the constitutional text being interpreted, and the political moment. Believers in clean institutional design should be wary of ideologies — left or right — that uniformly endorse or condemn judicial review. The same institution that struck down anti-sodomy laws in 2003 also struck down major campaign-finance regulations in 2010.

> ### Dig Deeper — Judicial review without lifetime tenure
> Compare the U.S. Supreme Court (lifetime tenure) with Germany's Federal Constitutional Court (12-year non-renewable terms) or with U.S. state supreme courts that have term limits or retention elections. Ask Claude: "What does the empirical literature show about how tenure rules affect judicial behavior, court legitimacy, and political contestation around appointments? What might U.S. constitutional politics look like under 18-year staggered Supreme Court terms?" The exercise pushes past the assumption that the current U.S. design is the only viable one.

### Constitutional courts vs. supreme courts

A specific design feature: some countries have a single supreme court that handles ordinary appeals and constitutional questions (U.S., Canada, India, Japan). Others have separate constitutional courts that only decide constitutional questions (Germany, Italy, Spain, most former-Soviet states). The separated model concentrates constitutional expertise in a specialized body and isolates ordinary litigation from constitutional politics; the unified model integrates constitutional reasoning into ordinary appellate work but produces a single politically charged court.

There is no consensus answer about which is better. Different countries have made different choices for path-dependent reasons. The European post-1945 wave of constitutional courts (Germany, Italy, Spain after Franco, post-Communist states after 1989) reflects a deliberate institutional choice: separate the constitutional court from the politically vulnerable ordinary judiciary the prior regime had compromised.

### Common misconceptions

**"Courts are above politics."** They are part of politics. They produce decisions that shape political life. Their members are appointed through political processes. Their legitimacy depends on political norms. Pretending courts are non-political is a useful fiction; treating it as truth produces analytical error.

**"Originalism is conservative; living constitutionalism is liberal."** Roughly correlated, but the correlation has wobbled historically. Originalist arguments were used to support *Brown v. Board of Education* by supporters of school desegregation; living-constitutionalist arguments were used to support *Lochner*-era economic-substantive-due-process. The political valence of methods depends on which constitutional text and which political moment.

**"More judicial review is more democratic."** Sometimes — when courts protect rights against majorities. Sometimes the opposite — when courts strike down democratic legislation in the service of judicial preferences. The relationship between judicial review and democracy is contingent on which rulings the courts produce.

## Synthesis — courts as a political institution

Courts do real work. They settle disputes that without them would either go unresolved or be settled by force. They interpret legal texts that without their interpretation would have to be re-litigated case by case in the political arena. They provide a forum for rights claims that majorities don't want to honor. They constrain executive overreach. They maintain the rule of law as a working concept rather than a slogan.

They also do this work imperfectly. Their decisions reflect their composition; their composition reflects political processes; their political processes can be captured or distorted. The U.S. Supreme Court has reached very different conclusions about the same constitutional text in different decades, depending on who was on the Court. The German Constitutional Court has been more consistent — partly because of design (term limits, two-senate structure, deliberation norms), partly because of political consensus about post-1945 constitutional principles.

The institution that decides what the law means is itself a creature of politics. This is not a defect — it is the structural reality of legal systems anywhere. The question is not whether courts are political but how they are political and how their political character is constrained or unleashed.

The serious student of courts holds two ideas at once: that courts do work no other institution can do (settling disputes, interpreting law, protecting rights), and that they do that work in ways shaped by their composition, their methods, and their relationships to other branches. Reading court decisions as pure law misses the politics. Reading them as pure politics misses the law. Both readings are wrong. Holding both pictures simultaneously is the skill.

## Chapter summary

Courts take written rules — statutes, constitutions, treaties — and apply them to specific cases. The work involves four sources of law (statutes, constitutions, common law / case law, customary or international law), each with its own logic. Different legal traditions weight these sources differently — civil-law systems emphasize codified statutes; common-law systems give heavy weight to accumulated case decisions. Within any system, judges face the question of interpretive method, and methods (textualism, originalism, purposive interpretation, living constitutionalism, pragmatism) often produce different outcomes from the same text. Courts' political role depends on three institutional features: their judicial-review power, their appointment and tenure rules, and the legitimacy they hold within the political system. Strong judicial review can protect minority rights and entrench elite preferences; the valence depends on which decisions the courts produce.

## What would change my mind

If a careful empirical study showed that interpretive methods (originalism vs. living constitutionalism, for instance) reliably produced different policy outcomes from the same texts — that the method does the work rather than the judges' priors — I would update my view that methods are mostly post-hoc justifications for results judges would have reached anyway. The literature is mixed; my current reading is closer to "methods constrain judges modestly but do not predict votes well," but I am not confident.

## Still puzzling

I do not have a confident view on how to evaluate the recent U.S. Supreme Court's willingness to overrule precedents (*Roe*, *Chevron*, others). On the one hand, *stare decisis* is not absolute — wrongly decided cases should sometimes be overruled. On the other, the rapid succession of major reversals after a period of relative stability raises a real question about whether the Court is operating under more political-strategic logic than recent decades practiced. I am genuinely uncertain whether this represents a return to a more honest acknowledgment of judicial choice or a corrosive partisanization of the Court — and reasonable observers reach different verdicts.
