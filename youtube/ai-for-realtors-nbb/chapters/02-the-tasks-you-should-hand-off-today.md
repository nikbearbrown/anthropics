# Chapter 2 — The Tasks You Should Hand Off Today
*The first draft is not the work. The audit is the work.*

![A normal agent week laid out as a](images/02-the-tasks-you-should-hand-off-today-fig-01.png)
*Figure 2.1 — A normal agent week laid out as a*

Look at a normal agent week. Listing copy for the new property on Maple. A market-update email to the buyer prospect who has been watching the neighborhood for six months. A follow-up sequence for the three buyers who toured on Saturday. A transaction checklist for the file that closes in two weeks. CRM notes from the open house. A CMA formatted for the seller consultation on Thursday. Two social posts for Monday. Some of that work is judgment. Most of it is production — getting words on a page that fit a pattern the agent has executed dozens of times before.

AI is good at production. The first question is not whether AI can do any of this. It can do all of it. The first question is which of these tasks can produce an output that causes real damage if the output is wrong. Because the answer to that question determines not whether you hand the task off, but what you have to do before the output goes anywhere.

Here is the case that earns its place at the front of this chapter. An agent drafts a buyer follow-up sequence using an AI tool. The sequence is fluent, warm, personalized-sounding, and ready to load. She loads it into the CRM and activates it. Three days later, a buyer calls — not to schedule a showing, but to ask why the agent recommended a specific neighborhood. The recommendation language is in the second email of the sequence. The agent did not write it. She did not review it. She does not know why the tool wrote it or whether it constitutes a steering problem.

The tool did not fail. The workflow failed. The production step was done. The audit step was skipped. And what got skipped was the step that separates an agent's time savings from an agent's professional exposure.

---

## What the delegate list actually is

The delegate list is not a list of tasks AI can do. It is a list of tasks where AI production plus agent audit equals a defensible work product. Both parts are required. The list without the audit is not delegation — it is abdication.

With that structure in mind, the delegate list for a residential agent is substantial. Listing description drafts. Buyer guides. Market-update narratives. Offer cover letters. Inspection-summary writeups. Social copy. Transaction timeline summaries. First-pass CRM follow-up sequences. These are pattern-shaped tasks — the agent has done versions of them many times, the output follows a recognizable structure, and the raw material comes from source documents the agent already has: the MLS data, the property disclosures, the inspection report, the offer terms.

| Task | What AI produces | What the audit covers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| listing description (draft language from MLS facts | Fair Housing check, factual accuracy, MLS rule compliance | A concrete checkpoint for applying the chapter concept. |
| buyer follow-up email (personalized sequence from buyer profile | steering language, factual claims, opt-out compliance | A concrete checkpoint for applying the chapter concept. |
| market update (narrative from market data | source verification, statistic accuracy, local qualification | A concrete checkpoint for applying the chapter concept. |
| offer cover letter (narrative from offer terms | tone check, factual accuracy, nothing that could be construed as advice | A concrete checkpoint for applying the chapter concept. |
| inspection summary (plain-language narrative from report | factual accuracy, no legal interpretation, nothing omitted). Should function as a working starting template. | A concrete checkpoint for applying the chapter concept. |

The facts come from verified source material. The language gets drafted by AI. The audit confirms the language matches the facts, complies with applicable rules, and does not create exposure the agent has not reviewed. That sequence — source facts, AI draft, agent audit — is the architecture of every task on the delegate list.

![Three-step production architecture ](images/02-the-tasks-you-should-hand-off-today-fig-02.png)
*Figure 2.2 — Three-step production architecture *

What is not on the delegate list is pricing opinions, legal interpretations, Fair Housing calls, negotiation judgments, and anything that requires the agent's professional accountability rather than the agent's time. Those belong elsewhere, and the chapter that explains where is the one you just read.

## Why the review step is part of the task, not an addition to it

The most important reframe in this chapter is the simplest one: the review step is not something you do after the task. It is the second half of the task. The listing description is not done when it sounds good. It is done when the agent has confirmed that the facts are accurate, the language is Fair Housing compliant, the claims are defensible, and the output meets MLS rules. Before that confirmation, the output is a draft. After it, the output is a work product.

This distinction matters because it changes what "saving time with AI" actually means. It does not mean the task takes zero minutes. It means the production phase — the part that used to require the agent to sit down and write from scratch — takes minutes instead of tens of minutes. The audit phase still takes whatever time the audit takes. The net time savings are real but bounded, and the bound is different for different tasks.

A listing description audit takes five to ten minutes if the agent knows the property well and has the source documents in front of her. That is fast compared to writing the whole description from scratch. A CRM follow-up sequence audit takes longer, because the agent has to read every email for steering language, factual claims, compliance requirements, and tone — and the sequence may have six or eight emails. The time savings on production may be partially or fully offset by the time required for a careful audit. On some tasks, the net savings are large. On others, they are small. On a few, they are negative — the task is complex enough that doing it from scratch, with full attention, is faster and safer than auditing an AI draft that may require extensive revision.

The quality question that helps sort these cases is: if this output is wrong, what happens? If the answer is inconvenience — the email sounds generic, the listing description undersells the property — revise it and move on. If the answer is a complaint, a misrepresentation finding, a Fair Housing violation, a lost client relationship, or a license problem, the audit requirement escalates. Either the audit becomes more thorough, or the task moves out of the delegate list entirely.

| Task | If wrong | what happens? | Audit intensity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| social post (low: generic, off-brand | quick | A concrete checkpoint for applying the chapter concept. | A concrete checkpoint for applying the chapter concept. |
| market update (moderate: buyer loses trust | medium | A concrete checkpoint for applying the chapter concept. | A concrete checkpoint for applying the chapter concept. |
| listing description (serious: misrepresentation, Fair Housing, MLS violation | thorough | A concrete checkpoint for applying the chapter concept. | A concrete checkpoint for applying the chapter concept. |
| CRM follow-up sequence (moderate to serious: steering, implied recommendation | email-by-email | A concrete checkpoint for applying the chapter concept. | A concrete checkpoint for applying the chapter concept. |

## The Fair Housing problem in production work

Fair Housing is the compliance requirement that appears most often in residential agent production work, and it is the one where AI output requires the most careful attention.

The Fair Housing Act prohibits discrimination in the sale, rental, and financing of housing on the basis of race, color, national origin, religion, sex, familial status, and disability. The regulations that implement the Act address advertising specifically: language that expresses a preference, limitation, or discrimination — explicitly or by implication — is prohibited in property listings, marketing materials, and any communication directed at prospective buyers or renters.

The problem with AI-generated residential marketing copy is not that the tools are designed to produce discriminatory language. They are not. The problem is that the tools produce language based on patterns in their training data, and the training data contains decades of real estate marketing copy, some of which carries implied preferences that were never explicitly flagged as problematic. A description that emphasizes the "family-oriented neighborhood" or the "quiet streets" or the "established community" may sound like good marketing language. Some of those phrases have histories in housing discrimination that are not visible in the output.

| What to check | What to look for |
| --- | --- |
| protected-class language (any reference to race, national origin, religion, sex, familial status, disability | explicit or implied |
| neighborhood characterization (language that describes the character of the neighborhood in terms that could imply demographic preferences | A concrete checkpoint for applying the chapter concept. |
| physical description of buyers (any language suggesting the property is suited to a specific type of person rather than describing the property itself | A concrete checkpoint for applying the chapter concept. |
| disability-related language (any language about proximity to services in terms that imply a specific user profile | A concrete checkpoint for applying the chapter concept. |
| source accuracy (verify every fact against the MLS data, disclosure documents, or other verified source). The checklist should be specific enough to use, not just illustrative. | A concrete checkpoint for applying the chapter concept. |

The audit step for Fair Housing is not optional and it is not a quick scan. It requires the agent to read the output with specific attention to the categories that carry legal risk — not to confirm that nothing sounds obviously wrong, but to confirm that nothing carries the kind of implied preference or limitation that a complaint would identify. That is a different standard than "does this sound professional," and it requires different attention.

NAR Code of Ethics Article 12 requires agents to be honest and truthful in all real estate communications. NAR's 2025 Technology Survey shows AI adoption rising across the profession. Adoption without audit is not professional practice under those standards. The review step is not an extra burden the cautious agent adds. It is the step that makes the AI-assisted work product professionally appropriate.

## The CRM problem and the steering risk

The buyer follow-up sequence is the task that produced the opening case, and it is worth examining precisely because it illustrates a failure mode that is different from the Fair Housing problem and more subtle.

Steering is the practice of guiding a buyer toward or away from specific neighborhoods based on protected characteristics. It is illegal under Fair Housing regardless of whether it is explicit or implicit. A follow-up email that mentions a specific neighborhood in a way that associates it with demographic characteristics — without the agent having made a deliberate professional judgment about the match between this buyer's stated needs and this location — is a steering risk.

An AI tool generating a follow-up sequence has no mechanism to avoid this. The tool produces language based on patterns. It may infer from the buyer's profile — stated preferences, price range, family size — that certain neighborhoods are good matches. It may use language that sounds like a personalized recommendation. None of that inference was reviewed by the agent before it went into the buyer's inbox.

| Element | Risk category | What the audit checks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| neighborhood recommendations (steering | verify that any neighborhood mentioned was explicitly discussed with and agreed to by the buyer | A concrete checkpoint for applying the chapter concept. |
| buyer-profile language (Fair Housing | verify that language characterizing the buyer does not reference protected characteristics | A concrete checkpoint for applying the chapter concept. |
| factual claims about properties (misrepresentation | verify against MLS or disclosure source | A concrete checkpoint for applying the chapter concept. |
| tone and urgency (agency relationship | verify that language does not create urgency inconsistent with the buyer's actual situation and interests | A concrete checkpoint for applying the chapter concept. |
| opt-out and compliance language (CAN-SPAM and state law | verify that required disclosures and opt-out mechanisms are present and accurate). | A concrete checkpoint for applying the chapter concept. |

The fix is not to avoid using AI for follow-up sequences. The fix is to audit every email in the sequence before it is loaded, specifically for steering language and factual claims — and to document the approved version in the file. That documentation matters because it is the evidence, if a complaint is ever filed, that the agent reviewed the content before it reached the buyer.

The general principle is the one from the opening: delegate production, not responsibility. The tool can produce the language. The agent owns the professional judgment about whether the language is appropriate, accurate, and compliant. Those two things must not be confused.

## The quality question applied to each task

The framework for sorting production tasks is simple and the same across all of them: if this output is wrong, what happens?

For social posts, wrong usually means generic, off-brand, or slightly inaccurate about the property. The consequence is mild — the post gets less engagement, the tone is slightly off. The audit for social posts is short: check the facts, check the tone, confirm nothing implies a preference or limitation under Fair Housing. Five minutes. The time savings are real.

For market updates sent to a prospect list, wrong can mean a statistic is outdated, a trend is mischaracterized, or a claim about a specific submarket is not accurate for the segment of buyers receiving the email. The consequence is moderate — a buyer who catches the error loses trust. The audit for market updates is longer: check every statistic against its source, verify the source is current, confirm the local characterization is accurate for this submarket, check that any forward-looking language is appropriately qualified. Ten to fifteen minutes. The time savings are still real but smaller.

For listing descriptions that go on the MLS, wrong means a factual error in a public representation of the property, Fair Housing language that creates legal exposure, or a claim that cannot be supported by the property disclosures. The consequence is serious — a misrepresentation complaint, a Fair Housing complaint, or an MLS rules violation. The audit is thorough: check every fact against source documents, run the full Fair Housing checklist, confirm MLS compliance, document the approved version. Twenty to thirty minutes for a complex property. The time savings on production are still real — drafting from scratch takes longer than auditing — but the audit is not optional at any level of time pressure.

For the follow-up sequence, wrong can mean steering language, a factual claim about a property that was not verified, or language that creates an implied representation about the agent's recommendation. The consequence ranges from moderate to serious depending on what the language says and who receives it. The audit is email by email, specific to the failure modes above. The time savings depend on how many emails are in the sequence and how carefully the tool produced them. Some sequences audit quickly. Some require substantial revision. The agent who discovers mid-audit that half the sequence needs rewriting has not saved time — she has discovered, before the output went to clients, that the task belonged on the Delegate with Review list and warranted more attention from the start.

## What belongs on the guard list

The guard list is smaller than the delegate list but more consequential. These are the tasks where AI may assist — gathering information, producing a draft for the agent's eyes only — but where the output cannot become a client-facing work product without a level of professional judgment the audit step alone does not supply.

Pricing opinions belong on the guard list. An agent's price recommendation to a seller is a professional judgment that requires knowledge of the current buyer pool, the condition of the specific property relative to recent sales, the seller's timeline and flexibility, and the agent's reading of how the market is moving. An AVM-generated estimate is a starting point for that judgment, not a substitute for it. The agent who presents an AI-generated price to a seller without having made that judgment has not saved time — she has created exposure on the task that matters most.

Fair Housing calls belong on the guard list. When the agent is deciding how to describe a property, which neighborhoods to mention to a buyer, or how to characterize a location in a way that serves the client's needs without implying a preference — those decisions require professional judgment, not AI production. The audit step addresses the language AI produces. The guard list addresses the decision the agent makes before AI enters the process.

Legal advice belongs on the guard list. The agent is not a lawyer. Explaining the legal implications of a contract clause, advising a client on the consequences of a disclosure obligation, or interpreting a title issue are all beyond the scope of what an audit of AI output can make professionally appropriate. Those questions go to counsel.

| Task | Why the audit step is not enough |
| --- | --- |
| pricing opinion (requires current buyer pool judgment, seller-specific context, market-cycle reading that no AVM supplies | Use the chapter example as the concrete test case. |
| Fair Housing call (requires professional judgment about how to describe a property or characterize a location | audit checks language, guard list addresses the prior decision |
| legal interpretation (agent is not a lawyer | A concrete checkpoint for applying the chapter concept. |
| audit cannot make AI output legally appropriate for client reliance | A concrete checkpoint for applying the chapter concept. |
| negotiation judgment (requires counterparty reading, relationship context, client-specific risk tolerance | none of which is in any document). Should function as a clear demarcation, not a cautionary list. |

## What the delegate list buys you

The time savings from a disciplined delegate list are real and they compound. The listing description that used to take thirty minutes takes eight minutes of AI production and ten minutes of audit. The market update that used to take an hour takes fifteen minutes of AI production and fifteen minutes of audit. The buyer follow-up sequence that used to take forty-five minutes takes twenty minutes of AI production and twenty-five minutes of careful audit.

The hours saved in a week of production work are hours available for the tasks that require professional judgment — the seller consultation where the agent's pricing analysis and client knowledge determine the outcome, the buyer representation where the agent's market memory and relationship with the listing agent matter, the negotiation where no tool can replace what the agent knows about this specific transaction at this specific moment.

![Time allocation before and after disciplined AI delegation](images/02-the-tasks-you-should-hand-off-today-fig-03.png)
*Figure 2.3 — Time allocation before and after disciplined AI delegation*

That is the actual value proposition. Not that AI does the work. That AI does the production layer of the work fast enough that the agent can do more of the judgment layer — which is where the client relationship is won, where the professional value is demonstrated, and where no tool competes.

The delegate list is the mechanism. The audit is the protection. The judgment work is the point.

---

## What you should be able to do now

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to look at any production task in your current workflow and answer: is this task on the delegate list, the delegate-with-review list, or the guard list? If it is on the delegate list, what is the specific audit for this task — not "review it," but what specifically do you check, against what source, for what failure modes? If it is on the guard list, what is the professional judgment you have to supply before any tool output enters the process?

The agent in the opening case did not need a better AI tool. She needed an audit habit built into the workflow — specific enough to catch steering language, fast enough to survive deadline pressure, and documented enough to matter if a complaint was ever filed.

That habit is the chapter.

## LLM Exercises

**Apply:** Take one real but non-confidential production task from your current workflow — a listing description, a market update, a follow-up email. Run the AI production step, then perform the specific audit for that task type using the checklist from this chapter. Write down what changed between the AI output and the approved version, and why.

**Analyze:** Return to the CRM sequence case from this chapter. Identify the exact point in the workflow where the professional exposure appeared — was it in the tool's output, the agent's decision not to audit, or the workflow design that allowed unreviewed output to reach a client? Write a paragraph explaining where the failure occurred and what a single workflow change would have prevented it.

**Create:** Build the audit checklist for one production task you currently handle. The checklist should be specific to the failure modes of that task — not "check for errors" but the exact things to verify, against what source, for what risk category. The checklist should be short enough to complete in under fifteen minutes under deadline pressure.
