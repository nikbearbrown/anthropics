## Candidate 01 — "Audit Claude for Hidden Normative Framings in Sociology"
- Source: sociology/chapters/00-claude-basics.md
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: LLMs absorb the normative framings of their training data and reproduce them without flagging. In sociology, where the vocabulary itself carries ideological weight, this is the most dangerous failure mode. Can Claude expose its own framing?
- The artifact: A sourced 4-round research dialogue (screen-recording mp4): Round 1 — ask Claude a contested sociological question (e.g., the gender pay gap); Round 2 — ask "from which theoretical tradition is your analysis coming?"; Round 3 — ask "what would scholars from a competing tradition say?"; Round 4 — synthesize the disagreement into a structured comparison table. The table is the deliverable: two traditions, their core claims, their evidence, their key critiques of each other.
- Prompt seed: `claude "What does the sociological literature say about [contested question]? Then tell me: (1) from which theoretical tradition is your analysis primarily coming? (2) How would scholars from [competing tradition] respond differently? (3) What empirical evidence does each side cite? Output a comparison table: Tradition | Core claim | Key evidence | Critique of the other side."`
- Read / check: Verify Round 1 and Round 2 produce different outputs (Round 2 should be more explicit about framing). Confirm the comparison table contains at least 2 genuine points of theoretical disagreement (not just "they emphasize different things"). Cross-check cited scholars and evidence against primary sources before using.
- Human supplies: A contested sociological question from an active research debate. Verification of any specific scholars or studies Claude cites (hallucination risk is highest on named sources). A slate image of a sociology lecture or textbook for the intro beat.
- Output medium: screen-recording mp4 (terminal dialogue — 4 rounds shown with cursor, final comparison table rendered in terminal then zoomed in for readability)
- The change: Run the same 4-round sequence with two different LLMs (Claude + ChatGPT) — compare the comparison tables. Where they agree, the framing is likely dominant. Where they disagree, that's the live debate.
- Teardown angle: The LLM is not biased in the malicious sense. It reflects the structure of its training data. For sociological questions where the training corpus over-represents one tradition, the output will too. The 4-round protocol is the antidote — it forces the framing into the open before you absorb it.
- Exclusions: Skip the hallucination failure mode (that's Candidate 02's job). Skip the qualitative-ethnographic gap (Candidate 03). Focus on the normative framing detection protocol.
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 02 — "Build a Sociological Citation Verifier with Claude Code"
- Source: sociology/chapters/00-claude-basics.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: In sociology, LLMs reliably generate plausible-looking citations to ethnographies, scholars, and studies that don't exist. The DOI looks right. The author name fits the field. The book doesn't exist. Can a 5-minute verification workflow catch this before it's submitted?
- The artifact: A CLI that takes a list of up to 10 citations Claude generated, and for each: (1) searches Google Scholar API (or web scrape) for the title + author, (2) returns Found / Not Found / Partial Match (real author, wrong title), (3) flags any Not Found with "DO NOT CITE — hallucination risk." Output as an annotated citation list with traffic-light badges. Screen recording of running the verifier on a real LLM output.
- Prompt seed: (The Claude prompt is upstream — used to generate the citations: `claude "Cite 8 peer-reviewed sociology books or articles that establish the following claim: [claim]. Include author, title, publisher/journal, year."` Then the verifier CLI processes the output.)
- Read / check: Verify the verifier correctly identifies at least one fabricated citation from a real LLM output (test this before the video). Confirm Partial Match is handled correctly (don't mark a real author's real book as Found if the title is wrong). Verify the False Positive rate is low (don't mark found sources as Not Found due to query formatting issues).
- Human supplies: A real Claude or ChatGPT session output containing a citation list (the human must generate this from a genuine sociology question). Access to Google Scholar (or a library catalog) to verify the verifier's results manually for the video.
- Output medium: screen-recording mp4 (terminal: Claude citation output pasted in, verifier runs, badges appear — green Found, red Not Found, amber Partial Match; at least one red badge fires)
- The change: Run the verifier on a list where the citations are all from the human's own verified reading list — show all-green output. This proves the verifier works in both directions.
- Teardown angle: The fabrication is not hypothetical. It has destroyed undergraduate sociology work routinely since 2023. The discipline is simple: never use a citation you haven't found in the actual source. The verifier makes that discipline frictionless.
- Exclusions: Skip the normative framing failure mode. Skip the qualitative gap. Focus on the citation verification workflow and one real demonstration of a hallucinated source being caught.
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 04 — "Simulate the CARS Research Introduction Structure with Claude"
- Source: sociology/chapters/02-sociological-research.md (+ connection to research methods chapters)
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: Most student literature reviews are lists — "Smith found X. Garcia found Y. Chen found Z." The CARS model reveals why lists fail and how good introductions actually work. Can Claude build a CARS-compliant intro from a pile of sources?
- The artifact: A research synthesis document (Remotion slate): given 5 sociology paper abstracts, Claude generates a CARS-structured introduction: Move 1 (establish territory — what the field has done), Move 2 (establish niche — the specific gap), Move 3 (occupy niche — this study's question). Each move is labeled and color-coded. A separate panel shows the same 5 papers arranged as a summary list — demonstrating the difference between a list and a synthesis.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Here are 5 sociology paper abstracts: [paste abstracts]. Using John Swales' CARS model (Move 1: establish territory / Move 2: establish niche / Move 3: occupy niche), write a CARS-compliant introduction that synthesizes these papers. Label each move. In Move 2, identify the specific gap in this literature. In Move 3, propose a research question that the gap makes necessary. Keep each move to 2-3 sentences."`
- Read / check: Verify Move 2 names a specific gap (not "more research is needed"). Confirm the 5 papers are genuinely synthesized in Move 1 (not listed sequentially). Check that Move 3's research question logically follows from Move 2's gap. Verify the three moves connect — the gap in Move 2 must close in Move 3.
- Human supplies: 5 real sociology paper abstracts from the student's own reading list or a topic of interest. These should be genuine papers, not contrived examples — the synthesis is only authentic if the sources are real.
- Output medium: Remotion (two-panel animated comparison: left panel = sequential list version, right panel = CARS-structured synthesis with color-coded move labels appearing in sequence)
- The change: Ask Claude to rewrite Move 2 using each of Swales' 4 niche-establishing moves (counter-claim / gap-indicating / question-raising / tradition-continuing) — show 4 different versions of the same niche. This demonstrates that the niche is a choice, not a discovery.
- Teardown angle: The literature review is not evidence that you read. It is an argument about what the field knows and why the gap your study occupies is real. The CARS model makes that argument structure explicit — and the gap between a list and a synthesis is the gap between a credential display and a genuine contribution.
- Exclusions: Skip the quantitative/qualitative method distinction. Skip the ethnography vs. survey debate. Focus on the CARS model applied to any 5 sources.
- Score: 7/10

- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: The Montgomery bus boycott lasted 381 days. Most social movements fail within months. Sociology has identified the conditions that predict success — but which conditions are contested, and what does Claude get wrong?

A verification table shows which predictions are empirically supported and which are contested in the literature.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Apply four major social movement theories to [specific contemporary movement]: Resource Mobilization Theory, Political Opportunity Structure, Framing Theory, and New Social Movements theory. For each: (1) what does the theory predict about this movement's chances of success? (2) what empirical evidence from this movement's history supports the prediction? (3) what evidence challenges it? Output a comparison table."`
- Read / check: Verify the four frameworks are applied accurately (Resource Mobilization emphasizes material resources and organizations; POS emphasizes institutional openings; Framing emphasizes collective identity and meaning-making; NSMs emphasize identity rather than economic interests). - Human supplies: A specific contemporary movement with documented history (e.g., Black Lives Matter, climate strikes, #MeToo — choose one with enough public documentation to verify claims). Verification of specific historical claims Claude makes about the movement.
- Output medium: Remotion (4-framework animated comparison table: rows = frameworks, columns = prediction / supporting evidence / challenging evidence; each framework's row appears in sequence with a verdict badge)
- The change: Ask Claude to identify which framework best predicts the movement's outcomes given available evidence — and to name what additional data would change the answer. This adds an epistemic-humility beat.
- Teardown angle: Rosa Parks was not a tired seamstress. She was a longtime NAACP activist who had been preparing for years. The movements that look like spontaneous eruptions of grievance are almost always the product of sustained organization, political opportunity, and carefully crafted frames. - Exclusions: Skip the full typology of social change. Skip the comparison with historical movements (one contemporary movement is enough). Focus on the 4-framework comparison and the verification step.

## Candidate 08 — "Simulate a Sociological Methods Diagnosis with Claude"
- Source: sociology/chapters/02-sociological-research.md
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: A claim can be true at the association level and false at the intervention level. A study can be internally valid and externally meaningless. Can Claude diagnose the method level of a sociological claim before you cite it?
- The artifact: A 5-claim diagnostic table (Remotion slate): given 5 sociological claims from news articles or research summaries, Claude classifies each by (1) what research method generated it (survey / experiment / ethnography / historical-comparative), (2) what inferential level it occupies (descriptive / correlational / causal), (3) whether the language used to report the claim matches the method (overclaim / appropriate / underclaim), (4) what additional study would be needed to justify a stronger claim.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Diagnose these 5 sociological claims by method and inference level. For each claim: [claims]. Classify: (1) what research method likely generated this claim, (2) what inferential level the evidence supports (descriptive / correlational / causal), (3) whether the reporting language is appropriate, an overclaim, or an underclaim, (4) what additional research would justify a stronger inference. Output a table."`
- Read / check: Verify at least 2 claims are classified as overclaims (causal language in observational data is the most common). Confirm the "additional research" suggestions are feasible and specific (not just "run an experiment"). Check that the diagnostic correctly distinguishes correlation from causation in at least 3 cases.
- Human supplies: 5 real sociological claims from news articles, policy briefs, or research summaries — not contrived examples. The human should source these from actual current media coverage of social research.
- Output medium: Remotion (5-row animated diagnostic table: rows appear one at a time with method badge, inference badge, and overclaim flag; a summary counter at the end shows X of 5 are overclaims)
- The change: Take one overclaim and show how it would need to be restated to match the actual evidence — demonstrate that accuracy is not the same as weakness.
- Teardown angle: The methodological dividing line is not quantitative vs. qualitative. It is: what question is this method built to answer, and is this claim actually that question? Most undergraduate sociology errors are the overclaim — inferring more from the method than the method can support.
- Exclusions: Skip the full American Soldier / Middletown methodological contrast. Skip the reflexivity turn. Focus on the 5-claim diagnostic and the overclaim identification.
- Score: 6/10

## Candidate 10 — "Research Social Stratification Trends with Claude — Verify Everything"

- The change: Ask Claude to restate one smoothed claim with full specificity — measure, population, time period, data source — and show how the precise version is less dramatic but more defensible.
- Teardown angle: "Inequality is rising" is a claim that is simultaneously true, false, and contested depending on what you measure. The LLM defaults to the most dramatic version of the claim. The verification habit forces the specificity that makes a claim actually usable.
