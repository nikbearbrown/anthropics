In 1971, a sociologist named Stanley Cohen published a book based on his observations of British media coverage of conflicts between two youth subcultures (mods and rockers) at English seaside towns in the early 1960s. The book was called *Folk Devils and Moral Panics*. The argument: the actual conflicts had been minor — a few fights, some property damage, the kind of weekend disturbances that English seaside towns had seen for decades. The media coverage had transformed them into something else: a national crisis about youth, decline, and the state of the nation. The coverage had created the moral panic; the panic had then produced policy responses (more police, harsher sentences, sociologically inflected debates about youth culture) that far exceeded what the original events warranted.

Cohen's argument illuminated a feature of social control that had been hiding in plain sight: the response to deviance is not a simple function of the deviance itself. The same act can produce very different responses depending on who commits it, what social anxieties are active at the time, what political coalitions can be mobilized through the response, what the media coverage emphasizes. The "criminal" is not just a person who did a thing; the criminal is a person who did a thing *and was identified as such by social institutions whose response itself does sociological work*.

This is not to say criminal acts don't exist. Murder, theft, assault, and fraud are real acts with real victims regardless of how the social response is organized. The sociological insight is that the *pattern* of what gets prosecuted, who gets prosecuted, what counts as deviance in a society — these are products of social processes, not direct readings off the acts themselves. Understanding crime and deviance requires examining the act, the actor, *and* the response.

This chapter is about that. We will work through what deviance and crime mean sociologically, why people violate norms (the major theoretical frameworks), and what the American case of mass incarceration reveals about the social production of criminality.

## Concept 1 — What deviance and crime are, sociologically

The first move on deviance is to specify the categories.

**Deviance** is behavior that violates the social norms of a particular group, community, or society. The definition is relative: what is deviant in one context is not deviant in another. Wearing a swimsuit is deviant in a corporate boardroom, normal at a swimming pool. Speaking at a religious service is deviant in some traditions, expected in others. Drinking alcohol is normal in many cultures, deviant in others. The category is not given by the act but by the relationship between the act and its social context.

**Crime** is a specific type of deviance — behavior that violates law and is punishable by formal sanction. Not all deviance is crime (poor table manners are deviance but not crime); not all crime is sociologically significant deviance (jaywalking is crime but the social response is minimal). The relationship between crime and deviance varies: some crimes are widely committed without being widely treated as deviant (speeding); some deviance is treated more harshly than equivalent legal behavior (homosexuality in some societies and historical periods).

**Social control** is the set of mechanisms by which a society induces its members to follow its norms. Two main forms:

*Informal social control* — the everyday mechanisms operating through family, peer groups, communities. Disapproval, exclusion, gossip, reduced cooperation, social rejection. These are usually the strongest mechanisms because they operate continuously through the people one cares about. Most people most of the time follow norms not because they fear formal sanction but because they fear social-psychological consequences within their primary groups.

*Formal social control* — institutional mechanisms operating through law and bureaucratic apparatus. Police, courts, prisons, schools' disciplinary systems, regulatory agencies. Formal social control specializes in cases where informal control has failed or for behaviors the state has chosen to treat through formal mechanisms.

The two are deeply interrelated. Most norms are upheld primarily through informal control; formal control kicks in for serious violations or for groups where informal control has weakened. The contemporary American expansion of formal social control (Concept 4) partly reflects the weakening of certain informal-control structures and partly reflects political choices to treat social problems through criminal justice rather than other institutions.

### Durkheim on the function of crime

Émile Durkheim (Ch 1) made an argument in *The Division of Labor in Society* (1893) and *The Rules of Sociological Method* (1895) that has continued to provoke. Crime, he argued, is *normal* in any society. There is no society without crime; there has never been one; there will not be one. Even a society of saints would generate crime — by elevating standards so that the saintly equivalent of theft (perhaps minor breaches of monastic discipline) would be treated as serious deviance.

Crime, Durkheim argued, also has positive social functions:

**Boundary maintenance.** The community's response to deviance reaffirms what the community considers normal. Trials, punishments, public outrage — all communicate where the lines are.

**Social cohesion.** The shared response to crime can produce solidarity. The community becomes more cohesive in opposing wrongdoing, even as it processes the wrongdoing through formal institutions.

**Innovation.** Some deviance becomes innovation. Galileo was deviant in his time. Civil rights protesters violated existing law. Many social changes have begun as deviance that subsequently became accepted practice.

The argument doesn't justify crime; it observes a structural feature of social life. Crime is a recurring product of the way norms operate; the response to crime does work for the society. Recognizing this doesn't license cruelty or complacency, but it disciplines the assumption that with the right policy crime would simply disappear.

### The trade-off — what definitions buy and cost

The relativist definition of deviance ("whatever a society treats as deviant is deviant in that society") buys analytical clarity: it lets us study how categories are produced and contested. It costs moral grip: it can seem to imply that no behavior is intrinsically harmful, only contextually disapproved.

The absolutist counter ("some acts are intrinsically wrong, regardless of social classification") buys moral clarity. It costs analytical traction on cases where social classification is doing the work — where the same act is treated very differently across contexts, classes, races, time periods.

Most working sociologists adopt something like a hybrid: empirical relativism about how categories are produced, combined with explicit normative positions on serious harm (murder, sexual violence, fraud, exploitation) where context-dependent treatment is largely about scale and procedure rather than about whether the acts themselves are wrong.

### Common misconceptions

**"Deviance is just whatever society disapproves of."** It is more specific than that — deviance is behavior that violates *norms* (specific rules of conduct). Disapproval without normative violation is just disapproval. The norms can be contested; the norm-violation feature is what makes something deviance.

**"Crime is a fixed category."** It is not. What counts as crime varies enormously across societies and historical periods. Drug laws have shifted dramatically; sexuality laws have shifted dramatically; commercial laws have shifted with each new economic configuration.

**"Stricter punishment reduces crime."** The empirical evidence is mixed and complicated. Some forms of stricter punishment may modestly reduce some kinds of crime; others may have negligible or counterproductive effects. The relationship between punishment severity and crime rates is one of the most studied and contested areas of criminology.

## Concept 2 — Theoretical frameworks on deviance

Sociology has developed several major frameworks for explaining why people commit deviant or criminal acts and why some acts get classified as deviant in the first place.

### Strain theory (Merton)

Robert Merton (1910–2003) proposed in 1938 a theory that has been refined many times since. The core argument: deviance often emerges from a *gap* between socially defined goals and socially approved means of reaching them.

In American society, Merton observed, the goal of material success is widely promoted. The approved means — education, hard work, gradual career advancement — are not equally accessible to everyone. People for whom the approved means are blocked may continue to embrace the goal but turn to alternative means: theft, drug dealing, fraud, organized crime.

Merton identified five adaptations to the goal-means gap:

1. **Conformity** — accept both the goal and the legitimate means. The most common adaptation.
2. **Innovation** — accept the goal but use illegitimate means. The most common form of property crime in the framework.
3. **Ritualism** — abandon the goal but continue to follow the legitimate means. The bureaucrat who has given up on advancement but still goes through the motions.
4. **Retreatism** — reject both the goal and the means. Some forms of substance abuse, withdrawal from society.
5. **Rebellion** — reject the existing goals and means and substitute alternatives. Revolutionary movements, counter-cultural projects.

The framework has explanatory power. Property crime rates do correlate with the gap between aspirations and access; criminal involvement is more concentrated where economic opportunities are most blocked. The framework also has limits: it focuses on lower-class deviance and may be less useful for explaining white-collar crime, hate crimes, and forms of deviance not motivated by material aspiration.

### Labeling theory

A different approach: deviance is what gets *labeled* as deviance. The act in itself may be similar to many non-deviant acts; what makes it deviant is the social process that identifies and labels it.

Howard Becker's *Outsiders* (1963) is the classical statement. Becker argued: "Deviant behavior is behavior that people so label." The same act of marijuana use becomes "deviance" when noticed and labeled by relevant authorities; otherwise it is just behavior. The deviance isn't in the act; it's in the relationship between the act and the social response.

Edwin Lemert distinguished *primary deviance* (the original violation, often minor) from *secondary deviance* (the deviance produced *by* the labeling process). A young person who shoplifts once and is treated as a normal kid making a mistake may never engage in further deviance. A young person who shoplifts once and is processed through the criminal justice system, labeled as a delinquent, and treated accordingly may have their identity reshaped by the label and continue along a deviant career.

The framework illuminates how social institutions produce the deviance they claim to merely respond to. School suspensions that exclude students from regular education; criminal records that follow people through life; mental-health diagnoses that shape future treatment — each can produce secondary deviance through the labeling itself.

The framework's limit: not all deviance is produced by labeling. Some acts are clearly harmful regardless of social response; some people commit serious crimes without institutional contact preceding them. The framework explains some deviance well and other deviance not at all.

### Conflict theory of deviance

The view that what gets defined and prosecuted as crime reflects the interests of dominant groups. The classical statement is in Marxist criminology: crime is what threatens property, capital, and elite interests; behavior that harms ordinary people but serves elite interests goes uncriminalized.

The empirical pattern that the framework captures: white-collar crime, even when it produces enormous harm (corporate fraud causing billions of dollars in damage; corporate negligence killing or maiming workers and consumers), is much less aggressively prosecuted than street crime. The Sarbanes-Oxley reforms after Enron, the Dodd-Frank reforms after 2008, the relatively rare prosecution of executives during financial crises — all have struck observers as evidence that the legal system treats wealthy lawbreakers more leniently than poor lawbreakers.

The 1980s "war on drugs" is another canonical case. Possession of small quantities of crack cocaine was punished, until the 2010 Fair Sentencing Act, at 100 times the rate of equivalent powder cocaine — a discrepancy that disproportionately affected Black users. The disparity made little criminological sense but considerable political-economic sense within a conflict-theoretic reading.

The framework's limits: it can be applied too broadly, treating every disparity as evidence of deliberate elite manipulation. Some criminal-law decisions reflect genuine public-safety concerns; some "elite" interests align with broader social interests; some lower-class crime really does harm lower-class people most. The strongest version of conflict theory of deviance is precise about which mechanisms it identifies; the weakest version becomes unfalsifiable.

### Social control theory (Hirschi)

Travis Hirschi's *Causes of Delinquency* (1969) reversed the question. The standard sociology question had been: why do people commit deviant acts? Hirschi asked: why do most people *not* commit deviant acts, given how often the opportunity arises?

His answer: because they have **bonds to society** that constrain them. Hirschi identified four:

1. **Attachment** — emotional ties to others (parents, friends, teachers) whose disapproval one fears.
2. **Commitment** — investment in conventional success (jobs, education, reputation) that one risks losing through deviance.
3. **Involvement** — time and energy spent on conventional activities that crowds out time for deviance.
4. **Belief** — internalized commitment to the legitimacy of conventional norms.

Where these bonds are strong, deviance is constrained; where they weaken, deviance becomes more likely. The framework explains correlates of deviance — why religious involvement, employment, family attachment, school engagement all correlate with reduced rates of various kinds of deviance — without requiring complex psychological accounts.

The framework's limit: it's better at explaining why most people conform than at explaining the specific forms deviance takes when bonds weaken. It also tends toward an individualist account, less attentive to structural conditions that make bond-formation easy or difficult.

### Combining frameworks

These frameworks aren't mutually exclusive. Most contemporary criminology draws on multiple frameworks. Strain theory explains some deviance; labeling theory explains other deviance; conflict theory illuminates the political economy of how deviance gets defined and prosecuted; control theory illuminates why people do not engage in deviance more often than they do. The interesting empirical question for any specific phenomenon is which framework or combination best explains it.

> ### Dig Deeper — Multiple frameworks on one phenomenon
> Pick a specific category of deviance or crime — say, white-collar fraud, drug-related offenses, sexual violence, financial crime, hate crimes. Ask Claude: "Apply Merton's strain theory, labeling theory, conflict theory of deviance, and Hirschi's control theory to this phenomenon. What does each illuminate? Where do they converge and diverge?" The exercise tests whether you can hold multiple frameworks productively.

### Common misconceptions

**"There's a single best theory of deviance."** There isn't. Different frameworks illuminate different phenomena; the discipline draws on multiple frameworks pragmatically.

**"Deviance is caused by individual psychology."** Some deviance correlates with individual psychological factors; the most robust sociological findings concern structural and contextual factors. The "bad apple" framing misses most of what sociology has learned about deviance.

**"Tougher law enforcement reduces deviance."** Sometimes; often the relationship is weaker, more indirect, and offset by counterproductive effects of labeling and disrupted social bonds. The empirical literature is contested.

## Concept 3 — Mass incarceration as a sociological phenomenon

The contemporary American case deserves specific attention because it is a sociologically distinctive phenomenon — historically unusual, internationally exceptional, with consequences running through much of contemporary American social structure.

### The empirical picture

The United States imprisons people at rates dramatically higher than other comparable democracies. As of the mid-2020s, roughly 1.8 million people are incarcerated in U.S. federal, state, and local facilities, and the U.S. incarceration rate is roughly 5–7 times that of comparable Western European democracies, depending on the country. The U.S. has about 4% of the world's population and roughly 20% of the world's prisoners.

The pattern is recent. U.S. incarceration rates were similar to those of European countries in the 1960s and 1970s — around 100 per 100,000. They rose dramatically from the 1970s through the early 2000s, peaking at over 750 per 100,000 around 2008, before declining slightly to around 500–600 per 100,000 in the 2020s. The rise is one of the steepest changes in any social statistic in U.S. history.

The disparities by race are stark. Black Americans are incarcerated at roughly 5 times the rate of white Americans. Black men born in 2001 had, by some estimates, a 1-in-3 lifetime chance of incarceration; the rate for white men was about 1-in-17. The disparities have narrowed somewhat since 2010 but remain large.

The sources are well-documented. Bureau of Justice Statistics reports annually on prison populations, sentence lengths, and demographic patterns. The 2014 National Academies report *The Growth of Incarceration in the United States* synthesizes the major findings.

### What drove the rise?

Several mechanisms have been documented:

**Drug enforcement.** The "war on drugs," beginning in the 1970s and intensifying in the 1980s, dramatically increased drug-related arrests and sentences. Drug offenders went from a small share of federal prisoners to nearly half by the 1990s. The crack-vs.-powder cocaine disparity (Concept 2) particularly affected Black users.

**Sentencing policy.** Mandatory minimum sentences, "three strikes" laws (life imprisonment after a third felony), truth-in-sentencing requirements (limiting parole), and similar reforms increased the average time served per offense.

**Prosecutorial practices.** Increased use of plea bargaining, with pressure from severe charges to accept lesser pleas; aggressive charging in some jurisdictions and less so in others.

**Political dynamics.** "Tough on crime" became politically required across both parties for several decades. Politicians who appeared "soft on crime" lost elections. The Willie Horton ads in the 1988 campaign, the 1994 crime bill, the political dynamics around crime through the 1990s — all contributed.

**Police presence and arrests.** Differential policing concentration in certain neighborhoods produced differential arrest rates beyond any underlying differences in offending.

### Major frameworks for understanding mass incarceration

Several scholarly frameworks compete:

**Michelle Alexander, *The New Jim Crow* (2010).** Alexander argued that the war on drugs and mass incarceration constitute a system of racial control comparable to Jim Crow — a system that maintains racial hierarchy through formally race-neutral mechanisms (drug laws, criminal records, post-incarceration disenfranchisement) that disproportionately affect Black Americans. The book has been enormously influential in shaping public discourse.

**Bruce Western, *Punishment and Inequality in America* (2006).** Western's book traces how mass incarceration has reshaped American inequality — limiting the labor-market participation, marriage prospects, and political participation of incarcerated and formerly-incarcerated Americans, with disproportionate effects on Black men.

**John Pfaff, *Locked In* (2017).** Pfaff argued that the dominant explanation (drug enforcement) was overstated and that the rise of mass incarceration was driven mainly by aggressive prosecutorial practices on violent and property crime. Pfaff's account challenges some of the "decarceration" advocacy that focuses on drug offenses, since most people in state prison (where most U.S. incarceration is) are there for violent or property crimes.

**James Forman Jr., *Locking Up Our Own* (2017).** Forman documented Black political support for tougher sentencing in cities affected by violent crime in the 1980s and 1990s, complicating accounts that frame mass incarceration purely as imposed-from-above racial control.

These accounts disagree on emphasis. They share recognition that the U.S. carceral system is historically unusual, racially disparate, and deeply consequential.

### The trade-off — competing readings

Several distinct empirical and normative claims are sometimes conflated in popular treatment:

- That the U.S. incarcerates too many people (a claim about scale)
- That the system has racially disparate effects (a claim about distribution)
- That much current incarceration produces no meaningful crime-reduction benefit (a claim about effectiveness)
- That incarceration causes ongoing harm to communities (a claim about externalities)
- That alternatives would be more effective (a claim about counterfactuals)

Each is contested in detail; the strongest empirical case is for the racial-disparity claim and for the distinct exceptionalism of U.S. scale. The questions about effectiveness and counterfactuals are more contested. Recent declines in incarceration since 2008, combined with continued declines in violent crime through most of that period, have weakened the strong "incapacitation works" arguments that supported the rise. But causality runs in multiple directions and the empirical analysis is far from settled.

### Common misconceptions

**"The U.S. has high incarceration because it has high crime."** Crime rates in the U.S. are higher than in some European countries but not enough to explain the incarceration gap. The U.S. has roughly twice the violent crime rate of most Western European countries but five to seven times the incarceration rate. The difference is in punishment, not just in offense.

**"Most American prisoners are there for drug offenses."** Federal prisoners — yes; state prisoners — no. Most U.S. incarceration is at the state level, where violent and property offenses account for most incarceration. The drug-offense focus reflects the federal system, which is much smaller.

**"Mass incarceration is over."** Rates have declined since 2008 but remain very high by international standards. The peak was unusually severe; the current level is still historically and internationally extreme.

## Concept 4 — Moral panics and the social construction of crime

The Cohen framework introduced at the start of the chapter deserves more attention. Moral panics are recurring features of how societies deal with deviance, and recognizing the pattern is essential for reading contemporary debates.

### How moral panics work

A moral panic, in Cohen's account, has several stages:

1. **Identification of a threat.** A behavior, group, or trend gets identified as dangerous or as symbolizing dangerous social changes.
2. **Stylization in media coverage.** Specific incidents get amplified, framed in particular ways, presented as part of a larger pattern.
3. **Folk devils.** Individuals or groups become symbols of the threat — "moral entrepreneurs" of various kinds (Becker's term) take up the cause.
4. **Mobilization.** Police, politicians, media figures, advocacy groups mobilize around the threat.
5. **Policy response.** New laws, new enforcement, new programs — often disproportionate to the original problem.
6. **Continuation or deflation.** The panic either becomes institutionalized in lasting policy changes or fades as attention shifts elsewhere.

Historical examples include the 1980s "Satanic panic" about supposed organized Satanic ritual abuse (largely invented by recovered-memory therapy, prosecuted destructively in cases like the McMartin preschool trial); the 1990s "superpredator" panic about supposedly emerging cohorts of extremely violent young offenders (the predicted wave never materialized); various drug panics and immigration panics across U.S. history.

Not every public concern is a moral panic. Real problems exist; appropriate policy responses are not panics. The framework helps distinguish proportionate response from disproportionate response and recognizes that disproportionate response itself does sociological work.

### Contemporary applications

Several contemporary debates have moral-panic structures, even where the underlying concerns are real. Some scholars have argued that panic dynamics are operating around: youth social-media use; drug overdose deaths (where the response often does not match the concentration of the problem); immigration; certain crime trends; "groomer" rhetoric in some current discourse. Each of these concerns has substantial empirical content; whether the social response is proportionate or disproportionate is the question moral-panic analysis raises.

The framework is particularly useful when examining how an issue plays out in media coverage. The question to ask: is the public response calibrated to the empirical scale and severity of the problem? When yes, the policy response may be proportionate; when no, panic dynamics may be doing additional work.

### Common misconceptions

**"Moral panic just means people are overreacting."** No. The framework specifies the structural features of how disproportionate public response gets generated — through specific media patterns, mobilization processes, and political dynamics. Recognizing a moral panic is more specific than recognizing overreaction.

**"All public concerns about deviance are moral panics."** No. Some are proportionate responses to real problems. The framework helps distinguish; it does not license dismissing all moral concern.

## Synthesis — reading deviance and crime sociologically

A serious sociological reading of any deviance or crime question holds three things together.

First: the empirical patterns. What is the actual incidence of the behavior? What demographic and contextual patterns appear? What is the trajectory across time? These questions have empirical answers, even if the answers are sometimes contested in detail.

Second: the theoretical frameworks for explaining the patterns. Strain theory; labeling theory; conflict theory; control theory; combinations. Each illuminates different mechanisms; the strongest analysis usually integrates multiple frameworks.

Third: the social processes by which acts get classified and responded to. Who labels behavior as deviant or criminal? What political and institutional dynamics shape the response? Where are moral panics operating, and where is response proportionate?

The contemporary American case — particularly mass incarceration — illustrates all three. The empirical patterns are well-documented (high incarceration rates, racial disparities, distinct historical trajectory). Multiple theoretical frameworks compete to explain it. The social process — how political dynamics, media coverage, and policy choices produced the rise — is itself part of the phenomenon to be analyzed.

The student who has worked through this chapter sees crime and deviance with new tools. Not as natural categories given by the acts themselves but as social products requiring analysis at multiple levels. Not as problems with single causes but as outcomes of multiple interacting mechanisms. Not as questions with consensus answers but as contested terrain where careful empirical and theoretical work can narrow disagreement without eliminating it.

## Chapter summary

Deviance is behavior that violates social norms; crime is the subset of deviance that violates law. Both are sociologically interesting because they are not properties of acts but products of social judgment — what counts as deviant or criminal varies enormously across societies, eras, and contexts. The major theoretical frameworks (Durkheim on the function of crime; Merton's strain theory; labeling theory; conflict theory of deviance; Hirschi's control theory) each illuminate different mechanisms. The contemporary American case of mass incarceration is sociologically distinctive — historically unusual, internationally exceptional, racially disparate — with multiple competing readings of its causes and consequences. Moral panics (Cohen) describe a specific structural pattern by which public response can become disproportionate to the underlying problem. A serious sociological analysis integrates empirical patterns, theoretical frameworks, and the social processes by which deviance gets classified and responded to.

## What would change my mind

If careful research showed that punishment severity reliably and substantially reduces serious crime — beyond modest deterrent effects on the margin — I would update my reading of the limits of incarceration. Most rigorous empirical work shows modest or context-dependent effects of severity, not the strong relationship that "tough on crime" rhetoric assumes. A clear demonstration of strong causal effects would shift my view.

## Still puzzling

I do not have a confident view on the long-run trajectory of U.S. incarceration. Rates have declined since 2008 but remain very high. Whether the post-2008 decline continues toward something resembling Western European levels, stalls at current levels, or reverses with new crime trends and political dynamics is genuinely uncertain. The next decade will tell.

```
   - Structural (deserves system change, not individual response).
   Most problems get framed multiple ways simultaneously. Note
   how YOUR problem gets framed — and how the framing has shifted
   over time.

2. **Apply labeling theory.** Becker's insight: deviance isn't
   inherent in an act; it emerges from the LABELING by others.
   How does labeling of people experiencing your problem affect:
   - Their access to services?
   - Their self-perception (back to Mead/Cooley from Ch 5)?
   - Their opportunities (housing, employment, relationships)?
   The "felon" label after release is the classic case; similar
   labels operate for addicts, the homeless, the mentally ill.

3. **Apply strain theory (Merton).** Merton argued deviance
   emerges when culturally-valued goals are unattainable through
   legitimate means. Apply to your problem: what culturally-valued
   goals are unattainable for the affected population through
   legitimate means? What responses (innovation, retreatism,
   rebellion) might be expected?

4. **Social control mechanisms.** What formal and informal
   controls operate?
   - Formal: police, courts, prisons, regulators, licensing.
   - Informal: shame, exclusion, gossip, social pressure.
   For your problem: how have formal controls expanded (war on
   drugs, hostile architecture, anti-camping ordinances) vs.
   informal controls operated (NIMBY responses).

5. **The criminalization audit.** Has your problem been
   criminalized in ways that produce worse outcomes? (The war on
   drugs is the canonical case — criminalization increased
   incarceration without reducing addiction.) Has any
   decriminalization or medicalization shifted the situation?

End with: what would change about your problem if the dominant
framing shifted from criminal/moral to medical/structural — or
vice versa?
```
