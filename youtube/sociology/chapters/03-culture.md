**TL;DR.** Culture is the set of symbols, norms, values, and practices that organize collective life. It is what people in a group hold in common — language, customs, beliefs, material objects, ways of doing things — and it is the medium through which most social action moves. Culture is not the opposite of structure. Culture *is* structure, in a different idiom: the structure of meaning.

---

In 1973, an American anthropologist named Clifford Geertz published an essay called "Thick Description: Toward an Interpretive Theory of Culture." The essay opens with a thought experiment about a wink. Two boys in a schoolyard. One closes his right eye briefly. Is it a twitch, a wink to a friend, a parody of someone else's wink, a wink rehearsing a wink for later? The physical event is identical in each case. The meaning is entirely different.

Sociology and cultural anthropology cannot study the wink without studying what makes it a wink — which is to say, without studying culture. The same physical action can mean cooperation in one context, mockery in another, intimacy in a third. The act is the same; the act is also entirely different. What distinguishes them is not in the muscle movement but in the surrounding fabric of shared meaning that lets a wink count as a wink.

That fabric is what sociology means by culture. It is not the high-cultural artifacts (museum-grade paintings, opera, classical literature) that the word sometimes connotes. It is the everyday operating system that lets human social life work — the language people speak, the gestures that mean specific things, the foods that count as food, the bodily movements that count as polite or rude, the assumptions everyone shares without noticing. Without culture, no two humans could coordinate on anything more complex than a momentary task.

This chapter is about culture as sociology studies it. The discipline does not have one settled definition (Geertz's interpretive approach is one of several). But the central idea is consistent: human beings live in webs of meaning that are not their individual creations and that they nonetheless cannot live without.

## Concept 1 — What culture actually is

The word "culture" gets used in several different ways in everyday conversation. Sorting them out is the first move.

**Culture as art and refinement.** "She's a very cultured person; she goes to the opera." This usage equates culture with high-status art forms and the disposition to appreciate them. It is the older meaning that derives from the Latin *cultura* (cultivation) and the German *Bildung*.

**Culture as nationality or ethnicity.** "French culture," "Mexican culture," "Yoruba culture." This usage groups practices, beliefs, and lifestyles by national or ethnic origin and treats them as bounded wholes.

**Culture as the sociological sense.** Culture is the set of symbols, norms, values, and practices that organize collective life. Every group has a culture; every individual lives inside multiple cultures (national, regional, religious, occupational, generational); and the boundaries are fuzzy and contested.

The third sense is what sociology and anthropology mean. The term covers everything from the obvious (language, clothing, religious rituals) to the easy-to-miss (how close people stand when conversing, how loudly they speak in public, what counts as polite behavior, what topics are appropriate for what settings).

### The components of culture

Sociologists conventionally divide culture into several components. The taxonomy is rough but useful.

**Symbols.** Objects, gestures, words, or images that stand for something else and have meaning within a particular cultural context. A flag, a wedding ring, a swastika, a heart emoji. Symbols are the basic vocabulary of cultural life; they are how meaning gets compressed and transmitted.

**Language.** The most elaborate symbol system humans use. Every human group has a language; the language structures what categories are easy to think in and what concepts are easy to express. The Sapir-Whorf hypothesis (in its strong form: language determines thought; in its weaker, more credible form: language shapes habitual thought) has been debated in linguistics and cognitive science for over a century. Recent careful work (Lera Boroditsky and colleagues) has produced moderate empirical support for weak versions of the hypothesis on specific cognitive tasks (color perception, spatial orientation, number).

**Values.** Abstract standards of what is desirable, important, good. American cultural values often cited include individualism, achievement, freedom, equality, progress; Japanese cultural values often cited include harmony (*wa*), group loyalty, deference to seniority. Values are abstractions; they get expressed through more concrete norms and practices.

**Norms.** Specific rules of conduct. Norms range from formal laws to informal expectations. *Mores* are norms with strong moral weight (do not murder; do not steal); *folkways* are weaker conventions about everyday behavior (which fork to use; whether to chew with your mouth closed); *taboos* are prohibitions so strong that violation produces revulsion (incest taboos; cannibalism in most cultures). Sanctions enforce norms — formal sanctions (laws, punishments) for major norms, informal sanctions (disapproval, exclusion) for minor ones.

**Material culture.** Physical objects produced and used by a culture. Clothing, architecture, tools, technology, food. Material culture is not just the byproduct of culture; it shapes what cultural practice is possible. The shift from oral to literate cultures, from manuscript to print, from print to digital — each material-cultural transition has restructured what social life looks like.

**Non-material culture.** Beliefs, ideas, knowledge, language. The intangible part of culture. Some sociologists distinguish material from non-material culture; others argue the distinction is unhelpful (every cultural object is also an idea; every idea has material expression).

### Real culture vs. ideal culture

A useful distinction: what people say their culture believes vs. what they actually do.

Americans, in survey after survey, profess strong commitments to equality of opportunity, hard work, and the importance of family. Americans also live in a society with substantial inequality of opportunity (Chapter 9 documents this), substantial variation in willingness to work hard, and substantial divergence in family practice from the ideal. The gap between *ideal culture* (what people say they value) and *real culture* (what people actually do) is itself a sociological phenomenon.

This is not hypocrisy in any simple sense. Cultures often hold ideals as aspirational standards, even when behavior systematically falls short. The interesting sociological question is not "are people hypocrites" but "what work does the gap between ideal and real culture do for the society?"

> ### Dig Deeper — Real and ideal culture
> Pick a culture you know well — your national, regional, religious, or other identity group. Ask Claude: "What are the most-cited ideal cultural values of this group? What do empirical studies show about actual practice in this group? Where is the gap largest?" Then push: "What does the gap reveal about the society's structure?"

### The trade-off — abstraction vs. concreteness

The textbook list of cultural components is useful but loses something important. Real culture is messy, contested, and embedded in everyday life. The list cleanly separates symbols, values, norms, and material culture; in lived experience, they are not so cleanly separable. The wedding ring is a symbol, a piece of material culture, an expression of the value of monogamous commitment, and a norm-marked object (you don't take it off in certain situations). The taxonomy is a teaching device, not a description of how culture is actually organized.

The serious sociological move is to use the components as analytical tools while not mistaking the analytical tools for the phenomenon. Culture is whole; we slice it up to study it.

### Common misconceptions

**"Culture is what other people have; we're just normal."** Everyone has culture. The feeling that one's own culture is just "the way things are" rather than a specific cultural arrangement is itself a feature of being inside a culture. Encountering other cultures is one of the main routes to recognizing one's own.

**"Culture is the surface; structure is the substance."** Culture and structure are not separate. Culture is structure — the structure of meaning. The economic and political structures of a society operate through cultural categories (what counts as work, money, value, legitimate authority). Trying to study structure without culture, or vice versa, produces incomplete analysis.

**"Culture changes slowly."** Some cultural elements (language, religious practice) are slow to change. Others (fashion, slang, technology-mediated practice) change rapidly. The pace varies enormously by domain.

## Concept 2 — Subcultures, counter-cultures, and the relativism question

Within any society, multiple cultures coexist. Some are aligned with the dominant culture; some sit alongside it; some explicitly challenge it.

**Dominant culture** — the cultural patterns most associated with majority status, institutional power, and broadest visibility in a society. In the United States, this has historically meant the cultural patterns of (primarily) white, Protestant, English-speaking, middle-class Americans. The dominant culture is not the only culture, but it sets defaults — what gets taught in schools, what plays on national television, what gets normalized as "American."

**Subcultures** — distinct cultural patterns within a society, organized around specific identities, practices, or interests, that coexist with the dominant culture without explicitly challenging it. Skateboarders, religious denominations, ethnic communities, occupational subcultures (firefighters, software engineers, jazz musicians), regional cultures. Members participate in the dominant culture for many purposes and in their subculture for others.

**Counter-cultures** — distinct cultural patterns that explicitly oppose key elements of the dominant culture. The 1960s American counter-culture (anti-war, sexual liberation, communal living, drug use) is the canonical case; punk culture, certain religious-fundamentalist communities, and various radical political subcultures are others. The boundary between subculture and counter-culture is fuzzy; what counts as "explicit opposition" is contested.

These categories are not stable. Counter-cultures can become subcultures, then become elements of dominant culture; the 1960s counter-culture's emphases on environmentalism, gender equality, and skepticism of authority became, over decades, parts of mainstream cultural framework. Subcultures can become counter-cultures when they grow into explicit opposition. The categories describe positions in a fluid landscape, not fixed types.

### Cultural relativism vs. ethnocentrism

A foundational tension in cultural analysis: how should an outsider evaluate practices that differ from their own?

**Ethnocentrism** is the habit of evaluating other cultures by the standards of one's own — finding them deficient, strange, or inferior insofar as they differ from one's own assumptions. Ethnocentrism is not just intolerance; it is a pre-reflective disposition that everyone has and that requires conscious work to recognize. Without specific training, an American visiting Japan, a Japanese person visiting India, and an Indian visiting France will each find the others' patterns "strange" — meaning, deviating from what their own culture has trained them to expect.

**Cultural relativism** is the discipline of suspending one's own cultural standards when trying to understand another culture. Margaret Mead's work, Boasian anthropology more broadly, and Geertz's interpretive sociology all rest on cultural relativism as a methodological principle. The point is not that all practices are equally good; the point is that you cannot understand a practice if you immediately judge it against your own cultural standards.

The trade-off here is real. Pure ethnocentrism makes cross-cultural understanding impossible. Pure relativism — the position that *no* cross-cultural moral judgment is ever appropriate — runs into hard cases (slavery, female genital cutting, child marriage). Most thoughtful cultural anthropologists and sociologists adopt a position something like: methodological relativism (suspend judgment to understand), combined with moral commitment to certain near-universal goods (human flourishing, freedom from severe suffering), with the boundary contested case-by-case.

This is messy. It is also unavoidable. The student should resist the temptation to resolve the tension cleanly. Living within the tension — empathetic enough to understand, judgmental enough to take a position when serious harm is involved — is the work.

### Globalization and cultural change

A major contemporary phenomenon: the increasing intermixing of cultures through migration, trade, communication, and digital platforms.

Some patterns are well-documented: convergence on certain consumer practices (global brands, global pop culture), convergence on certain political-cultural categories (human rights discourse, environmental discourse), preservation and revival of local cultures alongside the convergence (the same period that saw Coca-Cola become globally available also saw the global revival of indigenous languages, traditional crafts, and regional cuisines).

The "homogenization" thesis — that globalization produces a single global culture — is empirically weaker than its strong proponents claim. Local cultures often appropriate, transform, and re-export global cultural elements rather than simply being replaced by them. Korean pop music, Indian cinema, Japanese animation, African textile design — each is a global cultural force that did not exist in its current form before the era of intensive globalization.

The "Americanization" thesis is also contested. Some American cultural products dominate globally; American cultural practices are also reshaped by global forces in turn. Contemporary American food culture is more globally hybrid than it was in 1960; contemporary American religious practice includes substantial influence from East Asian and Middle Eastern traditions; American slang draws heavily from immigrant communities and global media.

The honest reading: cultural change under globalization is multidirectional, contested, and not well captured by simple convergence or homogenization narratives.

> ### Dig Deeper — A specific cultural transmission
> Pick a specific cultural product or practice that has crossed borders (sushi globalizing; American hip-hop influencing Korean pop; Indian yoga becoming global wellness culture). Ask Claude: "Trace how this cultural element moved from its origin to its current global form. Who carried it? How did it change in transmission? What does it mean in the receiving cultures vs. the original?" Then verify the historical claims against scholarly sources.

### Common misconceptions

**"Globalization is destroying local cultures."** Some local cultures are weakened by global pressure (especially indigenous languages and small-scale local traditions losing speakers/practitioners). Others are strengthened or reshaped through engagement with global culture. The simple destruction narrative is partial.

**"Subculture members reject the dominant culture."** Most subculture members participate substantially in the dominant culture (work, school, civic life) and in their subculture for specific purposes. Pure separation is rare; selective participation is the norm.

**"Multiculturalism means tolerating any practice."** Multicultural commitment usually has limits — practices that produce serious harm to vulnerable members of subcultural groups are typically subject to mainstream-culture intervention. The boundary is contested but rarely absent.

## Concept 3 — Theoretical frameworks on culture

The three classical paradigms (Chapter 1) each offer distinctive analytical tools for studying culture.

### Functionalist analyses

What does culture *do* for the society it is part of? Functionalists argue that cultural patterns persist because they perform functions — providing meaning, coordinating action, maintaining social cohesion, transmitting accumulated knowledge across generations.

A functionalist analysis of a religious ritual asks what work the ritual does: integrating the community, marking life-transitions, providing structures of meaning, transmitting values to children. The function explains the ritual's persistence even if the ritual's nominal purpose (worship, communion with the divine) varies in subjective intensity across participants.

The strongest functionalist argument is that some level of shared culture is *necessary* for any society to function. Without shared symbols, language, basic norms, coordinated meaning would be impossible. Émile Durkheim's analysis of religion in *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* (1912) is the classical statement: religion produces the collective effervescence and shared moral framework that makes the society possible.

Limits: Functionalism has trouble explaining cultural elements that seem dysfunctional (gambling cultures that destroy individuals; status competitions that produce widespread misery without producing any social good). Functionalist accounts can drift toward "whatever exists must serve a function" tautology.

### Conflict-theoretic analyses

Who *benefits* from a given cultural arrangement, and at whose expense? Conflict theorists analyze culture as a site of contestation — the dominant culture privileges certain groups' meanings, values, and practices, and the contest over what counts as culturally legitimate is itself a political contest.

Pierre Bourdieu's work on cultural capital (which we will revisit in Ch 9 and Ch 16) is the most influential conflict-theoretic framework on culture. Bourdieu argued that the cultural skills, dispositions, and credentials valued by the dominant institutions of a society (especially schools) are not neutral skills but are systematically more accessible to children of the already-dominant class. Schools claim to be meritocratic but in fact reward the cultural capital of upper-middle-class families, thereby reproducing class advantage while presenting the result as merit.

Conflict-theoretic analyses of media, religion, education, and politics all share the move: ask whose meanings dominate, whose are marginalized, and how the cultural arrangement reflects and reproduces the broader structure of power.

Limits: Pure conflict-theoretic analyses can miss the genuinely shared and integrative dimensions of culture; not every cultural arrangement is straightforwardly an arena of class or status competition.

### Interactionist analyses

How do cultural meanings get *made*, *negotiated*, and *contested* in everyday interaction? Symbolic interactionists argue that culture is not a fixed structure handed down to actors but a continuous accomplishment in interaction.

Erving Goffman's analyses of frame, performance, and stigma are exemplary. Goffman showed that the cultural meaning of an interaction depends on how participants frame it, what roles they perform, what props and settings they use, and how their performances are received. The wedding becomes a wedding because the participants treat it as one and produce the cultural performances that make it recognizable as a wedding.

Cultural sociologists in the interactionist tradition (Ann Swidler, Wendy Griswold, Jeffrey Alexander) have studied how cultural repertoires get invoked, recombined, and reshaped in different settings. Swidler's *Talk of Love* (2001) argued that Americans hold contradictory cultural repertoires for thinking about love and use them strategically in different situations — a finding that pure functionalism or pure conflict theory could not have produced.

Limits: Interactionist analyses can underplay the structural constraints that shape what cultural performances are available and which ones are received credibly. Not all cultural meanings are equally available to all actors; structural positions condition cultural participation.

### The trade-off — multiple frameworks on the same phenomenon

Functionalism captures what culture does *for the society*; conflict theory captures who benefits and who loses *from cultural arrangements*; interactionism captures how culture gets *enacted in everyday life*. None is a substitute for the others; the most useful sociological work usually draws on multiple frameworks.

A study of, say, evangelical Christianity in the contemporary United States could ask: what work does this religious tradition do for its adherents and their communities (functionalist); what does the political-cultural alignment of evangelicalism with conservative politics reveal about whose meanings are being privileged (conflict-theoretic); how do evangelical practices and identities get performed and negotiated in church services, political rallies, and everyday interactions (interactionist). The full picture requires all three.

> ### Dig Deeper — Three frameworks, one cultural phenomenon
> Pick a contemporary cultural phenomenon — political polarization, the rise of self-help culture, the spread of hip-hop globally, the secularization of mainline Protestantism. Ask Claude: "Apply functionalist, conflict-theoretic, and interactionist analyses to this phenomenon. What does each illuminate? What does each leave out?"

### Common misconceptions

**"You have to pick a framework."** You don't. Most contemporary cultural sociologists draw on multiple frameworks pragmatically.

**"Cultural sociology is just descriptive."** Some is. The strongest cultural sociology connects description with explanation: not just "this is what people do" but "this is what people do, and here is why it persists, who benefits, and how it gets enacted."

**"Bourdieu won the argument."** Bourdieu's framework is enormously influential and empirically productive. It is not the last word. Other frameworks (network-analytic accounts of culture; cognitive cultural sociology; cultural-evolutionary approaches drawing on cognitive science) are active research traditions with their own contributions.

## Synthesis — culture as the medium of social life

Culture is not the surface of social life. It is the medium. Almost everything sociology studies — inequality, politics, family, work, religion, social movements — operates through cultural categories and is shaped by cultural patterns.

The chapter has tried to do three things. Specify what culture means in the sociological sense (rather than the popular high-art sense). Describe its components — symbols, language, values, norms, material culture — while noting that lived culture is messier than the analytical taxonomy suggests. Apply multiple theoretical frameworks productively rather than picking one as orthodoxy.

What this chapter has not done: settled which cultural elements are functional vs. dysfunctional; produced a single framework for cultural analysis; resolved the cultural-relativism vs. moral-universalism tension. These remain contested in the discipline and in adjacent fields.

What follows: subsequent chapters apply cultural analysis to specific domains. Ch 4 examines society and social interaction (with culture as the meaningful medium of interaction). Ch 5 examines socialization (how culture is transmitted to new members). Ch 11 (which you have seen as the pilot) examines race and ethnicity (cultural categories with material consequences). Ch 15 examines religion as the most concentrated case of cultural production and reproduction. Each chapter builds on the cultural analysis introduced here.

## Chapter summary

Culture is the set of symbols, norms, values, and practices that organize collective life. It includes language, material objects, beliefs, and the everyday operating system that makes social coordination possible. Sociologists distinguish dominant culture, subcultures, and counter-cultures within any society, and analyze the relationship among them. The cultural-relativism vs. ethnocentrism debate is a foundational tension in cultural analysis: full ethnocentrism makes understanding impossible; full relativism makes moral commitment impossible; most working scholars adopt a methodological relativism combined with limits on moral universalism. Functionalist, conflict-theoretic, and interactionist frameworks each offer distinctive tools for studying culture; the most productive work draws on multiple frameworks. Culture is not the surface of social life; it is the medium through which most of social life moves.

## What would change my mind

If careful comparative work showed that cultural categories are largely epiphenomenal — that material conditions, demographic structure, or economic position predict social outcomes regardless of cultural variation — I would update my reading of culture's causal weight. The current evidence supports culture as causally consequential in many domains; settling for a fully reductive account would require evidence that has not been produced.

## Still puzzling

I do not have a confident view on the long-run effects of digital media on cultural change. Rate of change is faster; geographic boundaries less constraining; the production of culture more distributed. Whether this produces unprecedented cultural diversity, accelerated convergence, or both at once is genuinely uncertain.
