---

In 1929, Robert and Helen Lynd published *Middletown* — a comprehensive sociological study of Muncie, Indiana. They had moved to Muncie, lived there for a year and a half, attended church services, factory meetings, school board sessions, and Rotary Club lunches. They interviewed business owners and assembly-line workers, ministers and teachers and bartenders. They counted things: how many radios per household, how many automobiles, how many divorces, how often people went to the movies. They produced a six-hundred-page book describing how a small American city actually worked — its class structure, its religious life, its schools, its leisure, its families.

A decade later, in 1939–40, *The American Soldier* studies began. By the war's end, sociologists working with the U.S. Army had administered surveys to roughly 600,000 soldiers, asking questions about morale, training, leadership, racial attitudes, fears, hopes, and post-war plans. The four-volume series that followed (Stouffer et al., 1949–50) did something the Lynds could not: it produced statistically generalizable findings about millions of soldiers, with comparable data across units, theaters, and ranks.

Both projects were sociology. Both produced findings still cited today. They used almost no methods in common.

This is the situation. Sociology has not one method but several, each suited to different questions. The Lynds could capture textures of life that no survey can — the meaning of going to church on Sunday morning in a 1920s Indiana town. The *American Soldier* researchers could establish patterns no ethnography could — the precise relationship between unit cohesion and combat effectiveness across hundreds of thousands of men. Neither could substitute for the other.

This chapter is about how sociology actually generates empirical knowledge. Most introductory texts treat methods as a chore to get through before the interesting substance. The chore framing is wrong. Method is the substance. A sociological claim is only as good as the evidence it rests on; understanding the evidence requires understanding how it was generated. The student who learns the methods reads the rest of this book differently — sharper, more critical, more able to locate where a claim's strongest support lies.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

1. Distinguish quantitative and qualitative research and identify which approach a given question most needs.
2. Describe the major specific methods — surveys, experiments, ethnography, content analysis, historical-comparative work, computational social science — and what each does well.
3. Diagnose threats to validity (selection bias, measurement error, confounding, the difference between correlation and causation) and recognize them in published claims.
4. Evaluate how a sociological claim is supported: what is the evidence, how was it generated, and what are its limits?
5. Read a study with appropriate skepticism — recognizing that careful methodology and careful presentation can still produce findings that fail to replicate or that mean something different on closer examination.
6. Apply the discipline's reflexive turn: name your own role as researcher, including how your position shapes what you can see.

## Concept 1 — Quantitative and qualitative, and the trade-off

The biggest methodological dividing line in sociology — and one that runs through every chapter of this book — is between quantitative and qualitative research.

**Quantitative research** measures things. It produces numbers, applies statistical analysis, and seeks generalizable findings. Surveys of representative samples, administrative-data analyses, experiments, computational analyses of large datasets — all are quantitative. The strength is generalizability and replicability: a well-designed national survey can tell you what is true of the U.S. population, not just the specific people surveyed.

**Qualitative research** describes things. It produces text — field notes, transcripts, archival documents — and interprets meaning. Ethnography (extended observation in a setting), in-depth interviews, life histories, archival research — all are qualitative. The strength is depth and texture: a six-month ethnography can reveal patterns of meaning, practice, and interaction that no survey question can capture.

These are not opposed methods. They are tools for different questions. If you want to know what fraction of Americans believe X, you need a survey. If you want to know what believing X actually means in the lives of believers, you need ethnography. Both questions are sociological. Neither method can answer the other's question.

### What quantitative research is good at

Generalization from sample to population. If you draw a random sample of U.S. adults and ask them whether they trust the federal government, you can estimate (with quantifiable uncertainty) what fraction of all U.S. adults would say they trust the federal government. The General Social Survey (GSS), conducted since 1972, has produced this kind of evidence about American social attitudes for half a century.

Causal inference under specific conditions. Randomized experiments — a research design developed in agriculture and refined in psychology — let researchers establish cause and effect by random assignment to treatment and control groups. Field experiments in sociology (Bertrand and Mullainathan's audit study of resume callbacks; Moving to Opportunity housing studies) have produced rigorous evidence about discrimination and neighborhood effects. Quasi-experimental designs (instrumental variables, regression discontinuity, difference-in-differences) extract causal estimates from observational data when randomization is impossible.

Pattern detection across large datasets. Administrative records — tax data, voting records, school records, hospital records — let sociologists answer questions at population scale that no survey could. Chetty's mobility studies (Ch 1, Ch 11) use IRS records linking parents and children across decades; Bruce Western's incarceration research uses corrections data; demographic studies use full censuses.

### What qualitative research is good at

Capturing meaning, context, and process. Survey questions impose categories on respondents. Ethnography lets the researcher discover what categories the people studied actually use. Sudhir Venkatesh's *Gang Leader for a Day* (2008), based on years of fieldwork in a Chicago housing project, captures patterns of life that no survey questionnaire could elicit. Mitchell Duneier's *Sidewalk* (1999), an ethnography of unhoused vendors on Sixth Avenue in New York, similarly records phenomena that survey methods would render unrecognizable.

Generating hypotheses. Quantitative research tests hypotheses; qualitative research often produces them. A researcher who immerses in a setting often notices patterns no one had named — patterns that subsequent quantitative work can then test in larger samples.

Studying small populations or unusual cases. If you want to study CEOs, foreign-policy decision-makers, or members of a small cult, no random sample is feasible. In-depth qualitative work with small numbers of cases is often the only available method.

Studying meaning, identity, and self-understanding. How do people understand themselves and their situations? What do they think they are doing and why? These questions are answered by listening at length, not by ticking survey boxes. Arlie Hochschild's *Strangers in Their Own Land* (2016), a study of conservative voters in Louisiana, draws its insights from extended interviews and immersion that survey research could not produce.

### The trade-off

Each method gives you something at the cost of something else.

Surveys give you generalizability at the cost of depth — you learn what large numbers of people say in response to standardized questions, not what they mean by their answers or how those answers fit into their lives.

Ethnography gives you depth at the cost of generalizability — you learn one setting in great detail, not whether what you saw is typical of similar settings elsewhere.

Experiments give you causal identification at the cost of artificiality — the manipulated conditions in a lab or field experiment may not transfer to natural settings, and ethical or practical limits prevent experimental study of many important phenomena.

Administrative data gives you population-scale findings at the cost of measurement crudeness — what is recorded in tax records or hospital records is whatever the recording institution chose to record, often a poor proxy for what you actually want to measure.

The serious sociologist matches the method to the question. The dogmatic methodologist — quantitative or qualitative — picks one method and forces it onto every question, which produces predictable failures.

> ### Dig Deeper — Mixed-methods studies
> Some sociological projects combine methods deliberately. Ask Claude: "Walk me through the methodological design of Matthew Desmond's *Evicted* (2016), which combines quantitative analysis of eviction records with extended ethnographic observation in Milwaukee. What did each method contribute? How does the combination produce findings neither method alone could produce?" The exercise reveals what mixed-methods design is for.

### Common misconceptions

**"Quantitative research is real science; qualitative research is just stories."** Both are systematic empirical inquiry with peer-reviewed methodological standards. Both can be done well or badly. The dismissal of qualitative work as "just stories" usually reflects unfamiliarity with what serious ethnographic and interpretive work involves. The dismissal of quantitative work as "scientism" usually reflects unfamiliarity with the difficulty and craft of producing reliable measurement.

**"Big data has made qualitative research obsolete."** It has not. Big data answers some questions better than older quantitative methods could; it does not answer the questions qualitative research was always for. A petabyte of social-media posts cannot tell you what people experience as they post them.

**"Mixing methods is just hedging."** Mixed-methods design, when done well, addresses questions that single-method designs cannot. Done badly, it can be hedging — generating both shallow numbers and shallow descriptions. The quality is in the design, not in the labels.

## Concept 2 — Specific methods and what they do

This concept walks through six specific methods more concretely. The list is not exhaustive — there are more — but covers the major approaches you will encounter throughout this book.

### Surveys

A survey is a structured questionnaire administered to a sample of respondents. The strengths are scale and standardization; the threats to validity are sample bias (who responded, who didn't) and measurement error (what the question actually measured vs. what you intended).

Major U.S. social surveys: the General Social Survey (GSS, since 1972, NORC at the University of Chicago); the American National Election Studies (ANES, since 1948); the Panel Study of Income Dynamics (PSID, since 1968, the longest-running household panel survey in the world); the National Longitudinal Surveys (NLS); the Health and Retirement Study (HRS). These produce most of the large-scale empirical findings cited in U.S. sociology.

International equivalents: the European Social Survey (ESS), the World Values Survey (WVS), Eurobarometer, country-specific panel surveys.

Major threats to survey validity: nonresponse bias (response rates have declined dramatically since the 1990s, complicating inference); social-desirability bias (people misreport on sensitive topics); question wording effects (small changes in question phrasing produce large changes in responses); mode effects (telephone vs. online vs. in-person produce different responses).

### Experiments

An experiment manipulates an independent variable and measures effects on a dependent variable, with random assignment to treatment and control groups. The randomization is what allows causal inference.

Lab experiments are common in social psychology and increasingly in some areas of sociology (small-group dynamics, prejudice studies). Field experiments — randomized interventions in real settings — have become central to sociological methods since the 2000s. Audit studies, where researchers send paired applications differing on one characteristic (race, gender, criminal record) and observe differential treatment, are a particularly powerful design.

Major U.S. field experiments in sociology: Bertrand and Mullainathan's resume audit (2004); the Moving to Opportunity housing experiment (1990s onward); the Oregon Health Insurance Experiment (2008); the Oklahoma City Adolescent Sexual Health Project. Each produces causal estimates that observational data cannot.

Limits: Experiments can manipulate only some variables (you cannot randomize race, age, or country of birth); they often require conditions that may not generalize; they may be ethically constrained on important questions.

### Ethnography

Extended observation in a social setting, usually combined with informal interviews and participant observation. The researcher embeds in the setting for months or years and documents through field notes what they observe.

Famous U.S. examples: William Foote Whyte's *Street Corner Society* (1943, on an Italian-American Boston neighborhood); Elliot Liebow's *Tally's Corner* (1967, on Black men in Washington, D.C.); Loïc Wacquant's *Body and Soul* (2004, on a Chicago boxing gym); Matthew Desmond's *Evicted* (2016, on Milwaukee tenants and landlords).

Strengths: depth, capture of meaning and process, generation of new questions. Weaknesses: not generalizable beyond the setting, vulnerable to selection of what the researcher notices and reports, expensive in time, relies on the researcher's individual capacity for observation.

Reflexive concerns: The researcher is part of the setting. Their presence shapes what they observe; their identity (race, gender, class) shapes what people say to them and what people will and won't show them. Modern ethnography has become more reflexive about these effects, naming the researcher's position rather than pretending to invisibility.

### In-depth interviews

Long, semi-structured conversations with research participants, often recorded and transcribed. The strength compared to surveys is depth (you can follow up, probe, let participants tell their own story); the weakness compared to ethnography is reliance on what people say rather than what they do.

Examples: Annette Lareau's *Unequal Childhoods* (2003), based on extended interviews with families of different class backgrounds; Kathryn Edin and Maria Kefalas's *Promises I Can Keep* (2005), based on interviews with low-income mothers about their views on marriage and childbearing.

### Content analysis and computational social science

Content analysis examines text, images, or other cultural products for patterns. Quantitative content analysis uses coding schemes; qualitative content analysis uses interpretive readings.

Examples: studies of how news media frame political issues; studies of how children's books represent gender across decades; studies of how advertising language has changed.

Computational social science extends this to digital data. Researchers analyze social-media posts, search queries, online networks, Wikipedia edits, court filings, and other large digital corpora using natural language processing, network analysis, and machine learning. The field has grown rapidly since 2010.

Limits: Digital trace data captures what people do online, which is a particular slice of social life. Sample composition is shaped by who uses which platforms. Computational methods can find patterns that don't generalize beyond the digital setting.

### Historical-comparative analysis

Studies of how social patterns have evolved over time and how they vary across societies. The data sources are diverse — historical archives, government records, demographic data, secondary literature.

Major examples: Theda Skocpol's *States and Social Revolutions* (1979); Charles Tilly's work on European state formation and contention; Barrington Moore's *Social Origins of Dictatorship and Democracy* (1966); Stein Rokkan's work on European political cleavages.

Strengths: long time horizons, attention to causal sequences, comparative leverage that single-society work lacks. Weaknesses: data quality varies dramatically across periods and societies, comparative cases are never perfectly comparable, the small-N nature of comparative analysis limits inferential leverage.

> ### Dig Deeper — Methods on the same topic
> Pick a topic — say, social mobility, residential segregation, or political polarization. Ask Claude: "What do quantitative survey research, ethnographic observation, computational analysis of digital traces, and historical-comparative work each contribute to understanding this topic? Where do they converge in findings, and where do they diverge?" The exercise reveals how different methods triangulate the same phenomena.

### Common misconceptions

**"All sociological knowledge eventually becomes statistical."** It does not. Many of the discipline's most consequential findings come from ethnographic and historical work, and the questions those methods answer are not reducible to statistical analysis.

**"Older sociology was less rigorous."** Methodologically, sociology has gotten more sophisticated in some respects (causal inference; computational methods; replicability standards) and lost some craft in others (extended ethnography is rarer; classical historical-comparative work has fewer practitioners). Different eras have different methodological strengths.

**"You can find a 'true' answer if you just use enough methods."** Sometimes you cannot. Some questions are partly empirical and partly normative; better methods narrow disagreement but do not eliminate it.

## Concept 3 — Threats to validity and reading studies critically

Sociology's empirical claims are only as good as the methods producing them. Several recurring threats to validity are worth knowing — both to evaluate sociological claims and to evaluate any other empirical claim about social life.

### Selection bias

The people studied are not a random sample of the population the claim is about. A study of college students cannot tell you about non-college-attending Americans. A study of social-media users cannot tell you about non-users. A study based on volunteers cannot tell you about people who would not volunteer.

The Literary Digest poll of 1936 is the canonical example: a 2-million-person poll predicted a Landon landslide victory over Franklin Roosevelt; Roosevelt won 46 of 48 states. The poll's sample was drawn from telephone books and automobile registrations — both more upscale than the 1936 electorate. Selection bias overwhelmed the large sample size.

### Measurement error

What you measured is not exactly what you wanted to measure. Survey questions about income are notoriously inaccurate at the high end (top earners under-report); survey questions about church attendance produce dramatically inflated estimates compared to behavioral measures (people over-report attendance). Administrative records can have systematic errors (police-reported crime statistics reflect what police chose to record, not crime actually occurring). Culturally specific concepts (happiness, well-being) may not translate cleanly across societies.

### Confounding and the correlation-causation problem

Two variables can be correlated without one causing the other if a third variable (a confounder) causes both. Correlation between ice-cream consumption and drowning rates is not evidence that ice cream causes drowning; both are caused by hot weather.

In sociological work, confounding is everywhere. Children of college-educated parents earn more in adulthood. Does parental education cause higher earnings? Or do shared genetic, cultural, neighborhood, or network factors cause both? The correlation is real; the causal interpretation requires careful design.

The "gold standard" for causal inference is the randomized experiment. When randomization is impossible, sociologists use various quasi-experimental designs (instrumental variables, regression discontinuity, propensity score matching, difference-in-differences) that approximate experimental conditions. These methods have transformed sociology since the 2000s but are sensitive to assumptions; the methodological literature is technical and worth working through carefully.

### Replicability

The "replication crisis" in psychology since 2011 has implicated some of sociology's neighboring fields and prompted serious reflection within sociology itself. Several findings widely cited in earlier decades have failed to replicate. The replicability conversation has produced reforms — pre-registration of analysis plans, larger sample sizes, registered replications — that strengthen the discipline but require methodological literacy from readers.

A finding that replicates across multiple studies, populations, and methods is more credible than a finding from a single study. A widely cited finding that has not been replicated, or has been replicated unsuccessfully, deserves more skepticism than a textbook treatment usually provides.

### Researcher position and reflexivity

The researcher is not invisible. Their position — as a member of certain groups, as an outsider to others, with their own preconceptions and political commitments — shapes what they ask, what they see, what they emphasize, what they overlook. Modern sociology has become more reflexive about this, naming the researcher's position rather than pretending to neutral objectivity.

Reflexivity is not a license for "any interpretation is as good as any other." It is a discipline of acknowledging the researcher's role so that other readers can evaluate claims accordingly. A study of the U.S. military's racial dynamics by a Black sociologist and a study by a white sociologist may notice different things; both can be valuable; the position is part of the data the reader uses to evaluate.

### Reading studies critically

A practical checklist when evaluating a sociological claim:

1. **What is the actual claim?** Specify it precisely. Sociological writing often slides between strong and weak versions of a claim.
2. **What kind of evidence supports it?** Survey, experiment, ethnography, administrative data?
3. **What is the population the claim is about?** Is the sample representative of that population?
4. **What are the threats to validity?** Selection bias, measurement error, confounding, social desirability?
5. **What alternative explanations could produce the same observation?** Could a third variable explain the observed pattern?
6. **Is the finding replicated?** Does other research find the same thing, with different samples and methods?
7. **What is the researcher's position?** Does the position raise specific concerns about interpretation or selection of what to study?

This is not paranoia; it is normal scientific reading. A sociological claim that survives this kind of scrutiny is a claim worth treating seriously. A claim that doesn't is a claim worth holding loosely until it does.

> ### Dig Deeper — Reading a specific study
> Pick a sociological finding referenced in any chapter of this book — Bertrand and Mullainathan on resume callbacks, Putnam on declining social capital, Chetty on mobility. Find the original study (Google Scholar). Read the methodology section. Apply the checklist above. Then look up subsequent work that has tested or refined the finding. What does the journey from one paper to its scholarly afterlife teach you about how knowledge in the discipline accumulates?

### Common misconceptions

**"A study with a big sample is reliable."** Sample size matters; sample composition matters more. The 2-million-person Literary Digest poll was wrong; small but well-sampled polls have been right.

**"If a study was published in a peer-reviewed journal, the finding is settled."** Peer review is a quality filter, not a guarantee. Many published findings have failed to replicate; many published findings have been refined or overturned. Peer review is necessary but not sufficient.

**"You should only trust your own intuition."** Your intuition is a useful starting point and a terrible final standard. The whole point of empirical method is to discipline intuition with evidence — including evidence that contradicts your priors.

## Synthesis — what evidence-based sociology looks like

A serious student of sociology, equipped with this chapter's tools, reads any empirical claim with three questions in mind.

**What's the claim?** Specifying precisely. Strong vs. weak versions; population claim vs. mechanism claim; descriptive vs. causal.

**What's the evidence?** What method produced the finding, and what does that method do well or poorly?

**What's the alternative?** What other explanations could produce the same observation, and how does the study rule them out (or fail to)?

These are not the questions of a hostile reader. They are the questions of a careful reader. They are the questions that distinguish reading sociology from absorbing sociology.

Throughout the rest of this book you will encounter many empirical claims. Some are well-supported. Some are well-supported in their broad outline but contested in their details. Some are widely repeated but rest on weaker foundations than the popular treatment suggests. The methods chapter is the reader's toolkit for telling these apart.

The discipline's best work is what happens when method, theory, and substantive question are matched. The Lynds in Muncie, the *American Soldier* researchers, Goffman in his ethnographic and theoretical work, Bourdieu on cultural reproduction, Chetty on mobility, Desmond on eviction, Hochschild on emotional labor — each is doing distinctive work because each has chosen the right method for the right question. The student who can read each on its own terms can use each productively. The student who applies one method's standards to all of them produces predictable misreadings.

## Chapter summary

Sociology generates empirical knowledge through multiple methods — surveys, experiments, ethnography, in-depth interviews, content analysis, computational social science, historical-comparative work. Each method answers specific questions well and others poorly. Reading sociological claims critically requires identifying what method produced the finding, what its threats to validity are, what alternative explanations could account for the observation, and whether the finding replicates. The reflexive turn in modern sociology — naming the researcher's position rather than pretending to invisibility — is part of how the discipline maintains its integrity. The student equipped with these tools reads the rest of the book differently: more carefully, more critically, more usefully.

## What would change my mind

If a methodological revolution produced a single dominant method that out-performed sociological pluralism on a wide range of questions, I would update my view that the discipline's methodological diversity is a strength rather than a transitional state. The current evidence does not support such convergence; computational methods have augmented but not replaced ethnographic, survey, and historical-comparative work.

## Still puzzling

I do not have a confident view on how the replicability conversation will reshape sociology over the next decade. Some areas will likely tighten methodologically and produce more reliable findings; other areas may become more cautious in their claims, with consequences for the kinds of questions the discipline can address. Whether the net effect is to make sociology stronger or to narrow what it can credibly say is genuinely uncertain.
