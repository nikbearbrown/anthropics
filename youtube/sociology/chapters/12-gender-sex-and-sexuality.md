---

In 1970, women in the United States held approximately 4% of seats in the U.S. House of Representatives, earned approximately 60% of what men earned for full-time work, and were excluded from many specific occupations either by law, by custom, or by both. Women had had the vote for fifty years; the major civil rights legislation of the 1960s had begun to address some forms of sex-based discrimination; the second-wave feminist movement was visible but had not yet achieved most of its substantive policy goals.

By 2024, women held approximately 28% of U.S. House seats, earned approximately 82% of what men earned for full-time work (the gender pay gap had narrowed but not closed), and worked in nearly every occupation, including many that had been entirely male in 1970 (medicine, law, engineering at higher rates than fifty years prior; astronaut, military pilot, governor and senator and vice president and presidential nominee). The transformation in women's participation in public life over half a century is one of the most dramatic social changes in the country's history.

It is also incomplete and contested. The pay gap has narrowed but not closed; representation has grown but not equalized; some kinds of work and family arrangements remain gender-stratified in ways that produce specific disadvantages. The political and cultural debates around gender — over caregiving expectations, over the meaning of equality, over the relationships between biological sex and social gender, over how children should be raised, over how institutions should accommodate gender variation — are among the most heated in contemporary American public life and many other societies'.

This chapter is about gender, sex, and sexuality as sociology studies them. The territory is heavily contested. The chapter aims for charitable presentation of multiple frameworks — including frameworks that disagree with each other on substantial questions — so that the student can leave with the strongest version of positions across the spectrum, equipped to evaluate the evidence themselves.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

1. Distinguish sex (the biological category) from gender (the social and cultural category), and recognize the points of contestation between them.
2. Describe the empirical patterns of gender-based stratification: pay gaps, occupational segregation, division of household labor, political representation, time use, gendered violence.
3. Apply the major theoretical frameworks — functionalist accounts of gender role; conflict-theoretic / feminist analyses; the sex/gender distinction (Rubin, Butler); biosocial integration approaches — to specific cases.
4. Identify intersectional dimensions: how gender interacts with race, class, sexuality, and other axes.
5. Read contemporary debates about gender — pay gap interpretations, the role of biology, transgender experience and policy, parenting and caregiving — through sociological frameworks.
6. Hold both views simultaneously: that gender categories are social constructions with substantial cultural variation, *and* that gender patterns and gender-based inequality are empirically real with substantial consequences.

## Concept 1 — Sex, gender, and the contested boundary

The first move in any sociological analysis of these categories is to specify what we are talking about.

### The standard sex/gender distinction

The conceptual distinction between sex and gender, as it has been developed in sociology and anthropology, holds that:

**Sex** refers to biological characteristics — chromosomes, hormones, gonads, primary and secondary sex characteristics. The standard human pattern is sexual dimorphism: most people are biologically male (XY chromosomes, male hormones, male reproductive anatomy) or biologically female (XX, female hormones, female reproductive anatomy). Some people are *intersex* — born with biological characteristics that don't fit neatly into the standard categories. Intersex variations occur in roughly 1-2% of births depending on what specific conditions are counted; the most common conditions involve hormonal or chromosomal patterns that produce variation rather than fitting cleanly into the binary.

**Gender** refers to the social and cultural categories that organize identity, behavior, and social roles in relation to sex. Gender includes the roles a society assigns to people of each sex, the expectations and norms applied to them, the identities they claim, and the institutional patterns that organize life along gendered lines.

The sociological insight: while sex is a biological pattern with substantial cross-cultural similarity (most societies recognize male and female as distinct biological categories), gender — what those categories mean, what roles they imply, what specific behaviors and identities are tied to each — varies enormously across societies and historical periods. Margaret Mead's *Sex and Temperament in Three Primitive Societies* (1935), based on fieldwork in Papua New Guinea, documented three societies with very different gender configurations: one in which both sexes were socialized into what Western observers would call "feminine" patterns; one in which both were socialized into "masculine" patterns; one with reversed expectations from the Western pattern. The fieldwork has been criticized in detail; the larger point — that gender is a specific cultural arrangement, not a direct expression of biology — has held up across subsequent comparative research.

### The contested boundary

The relationship between biological sex and social gender is contested in several specific ways.

**The biology-of-difference question.** Are observed gender differences in behavior, cognition, and outcomes primarily produced by socialization, or do biological differences (hormones; brain organization; evolutionary heritage) contribute substantially? The empirical literature is large and contested. Some specific findings — that newborn boys and girls differ in some behavioral measures from very early ages; that trans men taking testosterone show specific cognitive shifts; that certain spatial-cognition differences appear cross-culturally — suggest some biological contribution to gender-typed behavior. Other findings — the enormous cross-cultural variation in gender arrangements; the responsiveness of many gender differences to social conditions; the within-sex variation that often dwarfs between-sex differences — suggest that socialization is doing most of the explanatory work. The strong "biology determines gender" claim is not supported by the evidence; the strong "biology is irrelevant to gender" claim is also not supported. Where the balance lies depends on which specific question is being asked and which specific evidence is being weighted.

**The transgender-experience question.** Some people experience their gender identity as not aligning with their assigned sex at birth. The phenomenon is documented across many cultures and historical periods, with varying social responses. Contemporary debates concern: the appropriate medical and social response to gender dysphoria; how institutions should handle gender categories (in sports, in housing, in restrooms, in record-keeping); how children and adolescents experiencing gender questions should be supported; what the implications are for the categories of "men" and "women" as social and political groups. These debates are heated; the empirical evidence is partial; reasonable people reach different conclusions on specific contested questions.

**The political-philosophical question.** What is the appropriate goal of gender policy and culture? Some traditions emphasize gender equality (equal treatment regardless of sex/gender); some emphasize gender equity (equal outcomes, which may require different treatment); some emphasize respect for traditional gender roles and complementarity between the sexes; some emphasize transcending gender categories altogether. These positions are normative as well as empirical; the empirical evidence narrows what is reasonable but does not settle the normative questions.

A serious sociological reading recognizes the empirical questions as separate from the normative ones, presents multiple positions charitably, and resists collapsing the distinct disagreements into a single dimension.

### The trade-off — definitional disputes

The sex/gender distinction has been challenged from multiple directions.

Some scholars (Judith Butler in *Gender Trouble*, 1990, and subsequent work) have argued that even the category of "sex" is partly culturally constructed — that what counts as biologically male or female is itself produced by cultural categorization. The argument is sophisticated but has been resisted by many other scholars who maintain that biological sex has substantial reality independent of cultural framing.

Some scholars argue the sex/gender distinction is being eroded in problematic ways in contemporary discourse, with social-gender claims being treated as overriding biological-sex realities in ways that produce confusions in specific institutional contexts. Other scholars argue the rigid sex/gender distinction has always been simplistic and that contemporary thinking should embrace more complex understandings.

These debates are live in academic, policy, and public spheres. The serious sociological reading neither dismisses any of the positions as obvious nonsense nor takes any as settled truth. Each captures something; each has limits.

> ### Dig Deeper — Cross-cultural gender arrangements
> Pick a society with gender arrangements substantially different from contemporary American patterns. Examples: societies that recognize three or more gender categories (Hijra in South Asia; muxes in southern Mexico; two-spirit traditions in some Indigenous North American cultures; fa'afafine in Samoa); societies with strict gender segregation (some Gulf states); societies with high gender egalitarianism (Nordic countries). Ask Claude: "Walk me through how this society organizes gender. What does the comparison reveal about how gender categories vary cross-culturally?" Verify against scholarly anthropological sources.

### Common misconceptions

**"Gender is just a social construct."** This claim has multiple meanings. The minimal sociological claim — that the social meanings, roles, and expectations attached to sex categories vary substantially across cultures and historical periods — is well-supported. The maximal claim — that biological sex differences contribute nothing to observed patterns — is harder to defend against the empirical evidence on hormones, evolutionary biology, and within-individual change with sex-hormone treatment.

**"Biology determines gender."** This claim is also too strong. Biology contributes to some patterns; the enormous cross-cultural and cross-historical variation in gender arrangements rules out simple biological determinism.

**"You can't say anything about gender without being political."** Sociology can describe empirical patterns and theoretical frameworks without taking specific political positions. The political conclusions don't follow automatically from the empirical evidence; reasonable people draw different normative conclusions from the same empirical record.

## Concept 2 — The empirical patterns of gender-based stratification

What does the empirical record show about gender-based inequality in advanced economies, particularly the United States?

### The pay gap

Women in the U.S. earn approximately 82% of what men earn for full-time work in 2024. The gap has narrowed substantially since 1970 (60% then) but has narrowed only slightly since around 2000.

The interpretation of the pay gap is contested. Several factors contribute:

- **Occupational sorting.** Women and men work in different occupations on average; the occupations in which women are concentrated tend to pay less than those in which men are concentrated, even controlling for education and experience.
- **Hours and time allocation.** Women work fewer paid hours on average than men; they spend more time on unpaid household and caregiving work. Some of the headline pay gap reflects different paid hours, not different hourly rates.
- **Career interruptions.** Women experience career interruptions for caregiving more often than men. These interruptions reduce lifetime earnings substantially.
- **Discrimination.** Audit studies and other rigorous methods have documented some discriminatory treatment in hiring, promotion, and pay. The size of the discrimination effect, controlling for other factors, is contested but is non-zero.
- **Negotiation differences.** Some research (Linda Babcock's *Women Don't Ask* and subsequent work) suggests women negotiate less aggressively for compensation; other research shows that when women do negotiate aggressively, they face penalties men don't face.

The "unexplained" portion of the pay gap — what remains after controlling for occupation, education, experience, and hours — is roughly 5-10% in most rigorous estimates. This is the portion most plausibly attributable to discrimination and similar mechanisms. The much larger headline gap reflects the combination of factors above. Whether the occupational-sorting and career-interruption components reflect "choice" or "structural constraint" is a substantive sociological question; the dichotomy is not as clean as either pole would suggest.

### Occupational segregation

Men and women cluster in different occupations. Women dominate (60%+ of workers) in nursing, teaching (especially K-12), social work, administrative support, retail. Men dominate in engineering, computer science, construction, transportation, finance (at upper levels), much of management at senior levels.

Some occupations have shifted dramatically. Medicine has gone from very heavily male in 1970 to roughly equal at younger career stages. Law has followed a similar trajectory. Other occupations have shifted less. STEM fields (especially engineering and computer science) remain heavily male in most advanced economies. The reasons are contested.

### Household labor

Across advanced economies, women perform more household labor and caregiving than men, even when both partners work for pay. The gap has narrowed since 1970 but persists. The 2020 OECD data shows U.S. women performing about 4 hours per day of unpaid work to men's 2.5 hours; comparable European countries show similar but somewhat smaller gaps.

Arlie Hochschild's *The Second Shift* (1989) coined the now-standard term for this pattern. Hochschild's argument: women had increasingly entered paid work without a corresponding shift in household labor, producing a "second shift" of unpaid work that disadvantaged them in time and energy. The pattern has shifted since 1989; women's time on housework has decreased, men's has increased, and outsourced services (childcare, cleaning, food preparation) have absorbed some of the load. But the underlying pattern of asymmetric household labor persists.

### Political representation

Women's political representation has risen substantially in most democracies since 1970. The U.S. has been somewhat slow by international standards: women hold 28% of U.S. House seats and 25% of Senate seats in 2024. Other advanced democracies have gone further: Sweden, Norway, Mexico, Spain, and France have 40-50% women in their legislatures. The reasons for the cross-national variation include electoral system features (proportional representation tends to produce higher women's representation than first-past-the-post), party recruitment patterns, and broader cultural variation.

### Gendered violence

Women are dramatically more likely than men to be victims of intimate-partner violence and sexual assault; men are more likely to be victims of stranger violence. The CDC and the Bureau of Justice Statistics produce annual data; UN agencies produce comparable cross-national data.

These patterns have substantial implications for how gender shapes the experience of public space, intimate relationships, and political life. The empirical record is robust; the appropriate policy responses are contested.

> ### Dig Deeper — The pay gap, examined carefully
> Different organizations report the pay gap differently — Bureau of Labor Statistics; Census Bureau; OECD; private organizations with various methodologies. Ask Claude: "Walk me through the methodological choices involved in calculating the gender pay gap. Why do different organizations report different numbers? What do the different methodologies actually measure? Where is there genuine disagreement among researchers about the correct interpretation?"

### Common misconceptions

**"The pay gap is entirely explained by women's choices."** Some of the headline gap reflects different occupational and hours choices; some reflects discrimination and constraint. The "entirely" claim is too strong.

**"The pay gap is entirely explained by discrimination."** The unexplained residual after controlling for observable factors is substantial but is not the entire headline gap. The "entirely" claim in this direction is also too strong.

**"Gender progress has stalled."** Some measures have stalled (the unexplained pay gap; some occupational distributions; senior management representation). Other measures continue to progress (educational attainment, where women now exceed men in many advanced economies; women's representation at younger career stages in many fields). The picture is mixed.

## Concept 3 — Theoretical frameworks on gender

Several major frameworks compete to explain gender-based patterns. Each illuminates something real and has limits.

### Functionalist accounts of gender role

The mid-20th-century functionalist account (Talcott Parsons; many contemporaries) held that gender role differentiation served social functions — family stability, efficient division of labor, child-rearing. Men specialized in instrumental roles (paid work, public sphere); women specialized in expressive roles (caregiving, household, emotional labor). The arrangement was efficient for the family unit and the broader society.

The framework has been substantially challenged. The empirical claim that gender-differentiated arrangements are necessary for family functioning has been weakened by the experience of more egalitarian arrangements, dual-earner families, and same-sex parents — all of which can produce well-functioning families. The normative claim that traditional arrangements are best for women, men, and children has been contested both empirically and normatively.

A weaker version of the functionalist insight may still be defensible: some forms of role differentiation in households (not necessarily along sex lines) may produce efficiencies, and complete equality of role across all dimensions may have costs as well as benefits. This weaker version is more commonly held than the strong Parsonian version.

### Feminist and conflict-theoretic accounts

A diverse set of frameworks broadly arguing that gender inequality is structurally produced and reproduces over time through specific mechanisms. The major traditions include:

**Liberal feminism.** Emphasizes equal rights, equal opportunity, removal of formal and informal discrimination, integration of women into existing public spheres. The 19th and 20th-century women's-rights movements largely operated within this framework.

**Radical feminism.** Argues that gender inequality is a fundamental structural feature of patriarchal society, with sexual violence, domestic violence, and the institutional control of women's bodies as central mechanisms. The radical-feminist tradition has produced influential work on rape, domestic violence, sexual harassment.

**Marxist and socialist feminism.** Locates gender inequality within capitalism's organization of production and reproduction. The unpaid household labor of women subsidizes wage labor; the gender division of labor serves capital's interests; the overcoming of gender inequality requires the overcoming of capitalist structures.

**Black feminism / intersectional feminism.** Argues that mainstream feminism has often centered the experiences of white women and underplayed the distinctive experiences of women of color. Patricia Hill Collins, bell hooks, Kimberlé Crenshaw, and others developed frameworks that examine how race, class, and gender interact in ways not captured by single-axis analysis.

**Postmodern / poststructuralist feminism.** Drawing on Foucault, Butler, and others, examines how gender categories are produced through discourse and power, and how they could be reorganized.

Each tradition contains internal disputes; each has produced substantial empirical and theoretical work. The contemporary discipline draws on multiple traditions pragmatically.

### Biosocial and integrative frameworks

A growing tradition (Alice Eagly's social-role theory; Rebecca Bigler and Lynn Liben's developmental work; many others) attempts to integrate insights from biology, social-cognitive psychology, and sociology. The framework recognizes that:

- Some statistical sex differences appear cross-culturally and have biological correlates
- The same differences are responsive to social conditions (within-sex variation often exceeds between-sex variation; gender differences narrow under more egalitarian conditions; specific differences appear in some cultures and not others)
- Both biology and socialization contribute to observed patterns; the relative weights vary by domain
- Strong "all biology" or "all socialization" claims do not survive the evidence

The integrative framework lacks the polemical clarity of single-cause explanations. It has the advantage of better tracking the empirical record.

### Butler and the performativity tradition

Judith Butler's *Gender Trouble* (1990) and subsequent work has been enormously influential, particularly in the humanities and in newer sociological work. The core argument: gender is not an underlying essence expressed through behavior but is *performed* through repeated stylized acts. The repetition of gendered behavior produces the appearance of underlying gender; the underlying gender is in fact the product of the performance, not its source.

The argument is subtle and has been frequently misread. Butler is not claiming that people choose their gender each morning like an outfit; the performance is largely involuntary, conditioned by cumulative social pressures and incentives. The argument is that gender categories are produced through performance rather than expressing pre-existing essence.

The framework has illuminated specific phenomena (drag performance; the work of maintaining gender categories; the moments where gender performance breaks down or is contested) and has been productive in studies of gender variance and trans experience. It has also been criticized by scholars who hold that it underplays material and biological dimensions of gender; the criticism is itself part of the ongoing debate.

### Combining frameworks

As with race and stratification (Chs 9, 11), most contemporary work draws on multiple frameworks. The wage gap, the household labor gap, the violence gap, the political representation gap — each likely reflects multiple mechanisms. Functional efficiency arguments may capture some persistent patterns; structural-mechanism arguments capture others; cultural-construction arguments capture how gender categories shape what is even visible as gender; biosocial arguments capture how some specific patterns may have biological as well as social contributions.

> ### Dig Deeper — Multiple frameworks on one phenomenon
> Pick a specific gender pattern (occupational segregation; household labor asymmetry; political representation gap; gender-typed academic interest patterns). Ask Claude: "Apply functionalist, liberal-feminist, radical-feminist, intersectional, and biosocial analyses to this pattern. What does each illuminate? Where do they converge?"

### Common misconceptions

**"Feminism is one thing."** It is not. The traditions described above disagree with each other on substantial questions. Treating "feminism" as a monolith misreads the field.

**"Biology is just a cover for sexism."** Sometimes claims about biology have been used in sexist ways; the careful empirical work on biological contributions to gender patterns is not such a cover. Distinguishing the careful empirical work from polemical misuse is part of the work of sociological literacy.

**"The categories are entirely fluid."** Gender categories vary across cultures and individuals; they are not entirely fluid in any specific society. The categories that are operating in your society are real, even if other societies have organized things differently.

## Concept 4 — Intersectionality and the experience of multiple stratifications

Kimberlé Crenshaw introduced the term "intersectionality" in 1989 as a legal-theoretical framework for analyzing how gender interacts with other axes of inequality — particularly race. Crenshaw's specific argument: Black women's experiences are not the sum of "Black experiences" plus "women's experiences"; specific patterns affect Black women that single-axis analysis misses.

The concept has been extended substantially. Contemporary intersectional analysis examines how gender interacts with race, class, sexuality, religion, disability, immigration status, and other identity dimensions. The argument is empirically grounded:

- Black women in the U.S. experience employment, healthcare, and criminal-justice patterns that are not captured by either pure-race or pure-gender analysis.
- Working-class women face career-family tensions differently than middle-class women.
- LGBTQ+ women face specific patterns of discrimination and harassment that intersect with their other identities.
- Immigrant women often experience labor-market dynamics shaped by both gender and immigration status.

The framework has been productive in empirical research and policy analysis. It has also been criticized for sometimes proliferating categories without specifying mechanisms, for being applied in ways that resist falsification, and for becoming a kind of identity-checklist that obscures more than it illuminates. The strongest intersectional work, in my reading, is empirically grounded and mechanism-specific; the weakest work uses intersectional language as an alternative to analysis.

The lesson for the student: gender does not operate in isolation. Most actual gender patterns vary substantially across other identity dimensions. Single-axis analysis often misses these interactions; intersectional analysis, done carefully, captures them.

### Common misconceptions

**"Intersectionality means everything is identity politics."** It is a framework for analyzing how identity categories interact in producing specific outcomes. It is not, in its rigorous form, a license for any analysis claimed under the name.

**"Intersectionality is just an academic fad."** It has substantial empirical content and has produced rigorous research across many fields. It is also subject to misuse and overuse; both can be true.

## Synthesis — reading gender, sex, and sexuality with care

A serious sociological reading of these categories holds several things together.

The empirical patterns are robust. Gender-based stratification is real, large, and persistent. The trajectory has been substantial change combined with substantial remaining inequality. Specific patterns vary by class, race, and historical period; the broad pattern is consistent across advanced economies, with substantial cross-national variation in specifics.

The theoretical frameworks each illuminate parts of the picture. Functionalism captures some patterns of role differentiation; feminist and conflict-theoretic frameworks capture structural mechanisms; biosocial frameworks capture the modest but real contributions of biology; performativity frameworks illuminate how categories are produced and maintained. The strongest analyses integrate multiple frameworks pragmatically.

The political and normative debates do not follow automatically from the empirical and theoretical analysis. Reasonable people, working from the same evidence, reach different conclusions about what should be done. Sociology can clarify what is and what would change under different policies; sociology cannot, by itself, tell people what they should value.

The contested questions are genuinely contested. The biology-of-difference question, the trans-policy questions, the question of how to handle specific institutional contexts where sex and gender matters differently — none of these has a settled empirical answer that uniquely supports any specific normative position. The student should leave this chapter able to articulate the strongest version of multiple positions on these questions, equipped to evaluate the evidence themselves rather than absorbing any single position as orthodoxy.

What I, as the author, conclude: gender categories are real social arrangements with substantial cultural variation across cultures and historical periods, with material consequences that fall heavily on specific groups (especially women in patterns of household labor, caregiving, and gendered violence). The empirical patterns of gender-based stratification have moved substantially in the direction of greater equality over the past 60 years, with progress remaining uneven across dimensions and with significant remaining gaps. Multiple theoretical frameworks each illuminate parts of the patterns; integrative analyses are usually more productive than dogmatic adherence to a single framework. The contemporary debates about specific contested questions — on biology's role, on transgender experience and policy, on how institutions should accommodate gender variation — should be conducted with charitable presentation of multiple positions, attention to the empirical evidence as it actually stands rather than as advocates of any position would prefer it, and recognition that reasonable people disagree.

This is my reading. Multiple frameworks have been presented charitably; you should leave able to evaluate them yourself. The work of sociological literacy on this subject — perhaps more than on any other in this book — requires holding empirical, theoretical, and normative threads together while keeping them distinct.

## Chapter summary

Gender, sex, and sexuality are categories through which much of social life is organized. The sex/gender distinction holds that biological sex is a real (if imperfectly bimodal) biological pattern, while gender — the social meanings, roles, and expectations attached to sex — varies substantially across cultures and historical periods. The empirical patterns of gender-based stratification (pay gap, occupational segregation, household labor asymmetry, political representation gap, gendered violence) are robust and substantial; the explanations for the patterns are contested. Multiple theoretical frameworks (functionalist, feminist in various traditions, biosocial, performativity) compete; the strongest analyses integrate multiple frameworks pragmatically. Intersectional analysis examines how gender interacts with other axes of inequality. Contemporary debates on specific contested questions (the biology-of-difference question; transgender experience and policy; institutional accommodation of gender variation) should be approached with charitable presentation of multiple positions. The serious student of gender holds the empirical patterns and the theoretical contestation in mind simultaneously and resists collapsing either into the other.

## What would change my mind

If careful longitudinal work showed that gender-based stratification was rapidly dissolving without sustained policy attention — that current gaps were primarily transitional artifacts of the rapid changes since 1960 rather than products of structural mechanisms — I would update my reading of how durably structured gender inequality is. The current evidence shows substantial progress combined with substantial persistence; rapid full convergence would be a significant departure from the recent trajectory.

## Still puzzling

I do not have a confident view on how the contemporary debates over transgender experience and policy will resolve in coming decades. The empirical evidence on the long-run effects of various policy approaches is partial; reasonable people drawing on the same evidence reach different conclusions about specific questions. The next decade will likely be informative about effects that current evidence does not yet settle.
