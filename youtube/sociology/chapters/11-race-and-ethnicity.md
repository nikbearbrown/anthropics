# Chapter 11 — Race and Ethnicity


## TL;DR

- The chapter moves through Learning objectives, Prerequisites, Concept 1 — Specifying the categories, Race as biology, and related ideas.
- Read it for the main argument, the vocabulary it introduces, and the practical judgment it asks you to develop.

**Suggested titles:**
1. Race and Ethnicity
2. The Categories That Were Built and the Inequalities They Carry
3. How a Social Construction Becomes a Material Fact

**TL;DR.** Race and ethnicity are categories that human societies build, maintain, and revise. The categories are not biological in any sociologically useful sense — the genetic variation within "races" is far larger than the variation between them — but they are real in the sociological sense: they shape who lives where, who earns what, who marries whom, who is policed how, who lives how long. The chapter walks through what we can say with confidence about racial and ethnic stratification, what is genuinely contested, and what frameworks compete to explain it.

---

In 2014, Raj Chetty and his colleagues at the Equality of Opportunity Project published one of the most ambitious studies of social mobility in American history. They linked tax records for tens of millions of Americans across generations and asked: if you were born into a family at a given income rank, what is the probability that you reach a higher rank as an adult, and how does that probability vary by where you grew up?

The answers are dense — there is so much data that almost any specific question has an answer. The pattern that has stuck most sharply in the public conversation: a Black boy born into a family at the same income rank as a white boy, in the same neighborhood, with the same parental education, ends up on average several income ranks lower as an adult. The gap is large, persistent, and not explained by family income, family structure, neighborhood characteristics, or maternal education. The same gap does not appear, or appears much smaller, for Black girls compared to white girls.

Why does this happen? The Chetty study cannot fully say. It documents the pattern with unprecedented precision and rules out several common explanations (it is not just a matter of growing up poor; not just a matter of single-parent households; not just a matter of bad neighborhoods, because the gap persists in the best neighborhoods). The mechanism — what specifically is happening to Black boys as they grow up that produces this divergent trajectory — is a research question that the data narrows but does not answer. Hypotheses include differential treatment in schools, differential interaction with the criminal-justice system, differential expectations from teachers and employers, differential exposure to violence, and combinations of these.

The Chetty data is one entry point into the chapter. It is not the whole story of race in America or anywhere else. But it has a useful property: it is empirically settled in the sense that the pattern is real and not subject to serious dispute among researchers; and it is genuinely open in the sense that the explanation is contested. Holding both of those together — the empirical fact and the contested explanation — is the discipline this chapter asks of you.

This chapter is about race and ethnicity as sociological phenomena. It will be longer than some chapters because the territory is bigger and more contested. The contestation is part of what makes the chapter important. The student who emerges with sharp views on contested questions but cannot articulate the strongest version of the views they reject has not yet done the sociology.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

1. Distinguish race from ethnicity and apply both terms with care to specific cases.
2. Explain what "social construction of race" means and what it does not mean — including why social construction does not mean "not real."
3. Diagnose the empirical patterns of racial stratification in the United States — wage gaps, wealth gaps, residential segregation, criminal-justice contact, health outcomes, educational outcomes — and identify which patterns are robust and which are contested in measurement.
4. Compare frameworks for explaining these patterns — colorblind individualism, structural racism / institutional racism, cultural-conservative explanations, intersectionality — and identify the strongest version of each before evaluating.
5. Compare U.S. patterns of racial classification and stratification with those in Brazil, South Africa, France, Japan, and other societies; recognize what is universal about racial classification and what is specifically American.
6. Read a contemporary policy debate about race (affirmative action, reparations, color-conscious vs. color-blind policy, mass incarceration) as the joint product of empirical claims, theoretical frameworks, and normative commitments — and identify which is which in any given argument.

## Concept 1 — Specifying the categories

The term *race* hides several different things. Forcing the terms specific is the first move.

### Race as biology

The popular intuition — race as a biological category corresponding to ancestral populations — has been the subject of intensive scientific scrutiny for nearly a century, and the consensus among geneticists and biological anthropologists is clear: race in this strong biological sense is not a scientifically useful category for most purposes.

The reasons are technical but worth knowing. Human genetic variation is *clinal* — it varies gradually across geography, with no sharp boundaries. Almost all human genetic variation (something like 85–90%, depending on the study) exists *within* any given population, with the remaining 10–15% distributed across populations. The "races" recognized by U.S. census categories or by 19th-century anthropology do not correspond to genetic clusters that can be cleanly distinguished. A randomly chosen pair of Black Americans is, on average, about as genetically different from each other as a randomly chosen pair of one Black American and one white American.

This does not mean genetics is irrelevant to traits sometimes correlated with race — sickle-cell trait is more common in populations whose ancestors lived in malarial regions; lactose intolerance varies by geographic ancestry; certain disease risks cluster by population. These are real biological patterns. They do not map onto the sociological races used in U.S. census categories or American everyday life.

So when sociologists say "race is socially constructed," they are not denying that biology exists. They are saying the categories Americans (and Brazilians, and South Africans, and French people) use are sociological categories, not biological ones — built and maintained by societies, not read off from nature.

### Race as social construction

The phrase "social construction of race" is sometimes misunderstood as meaning "race is fake" or "race doesn't matter." Neither follows. The phrase means: the racial categories are made by societies, and different societies make different categories — and the categories, once made, have powerful real consequences.

The historical record is the clearest evidence. Who counts as "white" in the United States has changed across time. Irish immigrants in the mid-19th century were not consistently treated as white; Italian immigrants in the early 20th century were not consistently treated as white; Jewish immigrants were sometimes white and sometimes not. By the mid-20th century, all three groups had become unambiguously white in the U.S. racial classification — without any change in their genetics.

In Brazil, the categories are more elaborate (dozens of folk-racial terms describing skin color and features, with most Brazilians not fitting cleanly into any of the U.S. binary categories) and more fluid (a single individual may identify or be identified differently across contexts). In South Africa, the apartheid state created and maintained categories ("white," "Coloured," "Indian," "Black African") with sharply different rights, with elaborate bureaucratic procedures for determining who fell into which category. In Japan, the *Burakumin* — historical outcaste descendants — are visually indistinguishable from the rest of the Japanese population but face systematic discrimination in marriage and employment that operates much like racial discrimination elsewhere. In France, official ideology refuses to recognize race as a category at all (no racial questions on the census), but discrimination against people of North African and sub-Saharan African descent is well-documented.

The lesson: the categories are social. They are also real. They shape life chances. They do this even though, in some abstract sense, they could be drawn differently.

### Ethnicity

*Ethnicity* refers to shared cultural heritage — language, religion, customs, history, sense of common origin. The boundary between race and ethnicity is fuzzy and varies by context. In the U.S. census's current categories, "Hispanic/Latino" is treated as an ethnicity (which can be of any race), while "Black or African American" and "White" are races. The distinction is partly historical (Hispanic identity was added to the census separately from race in 1980) and partly contestable (many Latinos describe their experience in racial rather than ethnic terms).

In other contexts, the same group might be a race in one country, an ethnicity in another, or both. Jewish people in 19th-century Europe were treated as a race; in contemporary U.S. classification, Jewish identity is religious-ethnic, not racial. Irish identity was sometimes racialized in the 19th century U.S. and is now ethnic. The categories shift.

For sociological purposes, the most useful distinction: race generally refers to phenotypically marked categories used to classify people whether or not the classified individuals identify with the category, while ethnicity generally refers to shared culture and self-identification. The distinction is rough; the discipline uses both terms imperfectly.

### The trade-off — categories that are both real and constructed

The sociological move on race is to hold two ideas at once: the categories are constructed, *and* the consequences are material. Pretending the categories are biological misreads the science. Pretending they are unreal misreads the sociology. Both errors are common; neither is sustainable.

The student who arrives at this chapter believing race is biology gets one specific lesson: race in the U.S. sense is not biology, and the genetic evidence is unambiguous. The student who arrives believing race is fake or peripheral gets a different specific lesson: the categories matter enormously for life chances, even though they are socially built. The discipline's posture is precision about both — what is real, in what sense, with what evidence.

### Common misconceptions

**"Race is biological because we can see it."** Visible features — skin tone, hair texture, facial structure — are real and have biological causes (selection for skin pigmentation by latitude, etc.). But the *categories* — "Black," "white," "Asian," "Hispanic" — group these visible features in ways that are sociological choices, not biological clusters. A society could classify people by height or eye color or earlobe shape; ours classifies by certain features and ignores others.

**"Social construction means it's not real."** Money is socially constructed; that does not make it imaginary or inconsequential. Constitutions are socially constructed; that does not make them irrelevant. Race is in this category — built by societies, maintained by social practice, with real and serious consequences.

**"Race and ethnicity are the same thing."** They overlap in some contexts and diverge in others. The distinction is most useful when comparing how phenotypically-marked groups (race) are treated differently from culturally-marked groups (ethnicity), and how the categories interact (e.g., Black Latinos face race-based and ethnicity-based dynamics simultaneously).

## Concept 2 — The empirical patterns of racial stratification

What does the sociological evidence show about race-based inequality in the United States? More than I can fit in one chapter, but several robust patterns are worth naming.

### Income and wealth

The Black-white wealth gap is one of the most thoroughly documented patterns in U.S. social stratification. The Survey of Consumer Finances (Federal Reserve), conducted every three years, has shown a roughly 10:1 white-to-Black wealth ratio for decades. In the 2022 SCF, median white household wealth was about $285,000; median Black household wealth was about $44,900. The gap has widened since the 2008 financial crisis, partly because Black households were disproportionately affected by foreclosures, partly because the recovery delivered uneven gains across racial lines.

The income gap is real but smaller. Median Black household income in 2022 was about 60–65% of median white household income, depending on the specific measure. The wealth gap is larger than the income gap because wealth accumulates across generations, and the racial wealth gap reflects the cumulative effects of policies — slavery, Jim Crow, redlining, exclusion from postwar GI Bill benefits, exclusion from postwar suburban housing markets — that have prevented Black families from accumulating wealth at the rates white families have.

Hispanic/Latino, Asian American, and Native American households show distinct patterns: Asian American median income often exceeds white median income (with substantial within-group variation across national-origin subgroups); Hispanic median income is closer to Black levels; Native American median income is lower still, with very high variance across reservations and urban areas.

### Wages and the labor market

Black-white wage gaps persist after controlling for education, experience, and occupation, with most rigorous estimates putting the unexplained gap at about 10–15% — meaning that even comparing a Black and a white worker with identical observable characteristics, the Black worker earns about 10–15% less. The "unexplained" gap is contested in interpretation: some scholars attribute it primarily to discrimination; others argue some unobserved differences (specific skills, network effects) account for part of it. The gap exists and is large; what it means is debated.

Audit studies provide a cleaner test. Researchers send identical resumes to employers, varying only the names (suggesting different races). Marianne Bertrand and Sendhil Mullainathan's 2004 study (*Are Emily and Greg More Employable Than Lakisha and Jamal?*) found callback rates roughly 50% lower for resumes with stereotypically Black names. The result has been replicated. This is direct evidence of differential treatment, holding observables constant.

### Residential segregation

Residential segregation by race in the U.S. has declined slowly since 1970 but remains very high. The dissimilarity index — a standard measure of segregation, ranging from 0 (full integration) to 100 (complete segregation) — for Black-white segregation in U.S. metropolitan areas was around 60 in 2020, down from about 80 in 1970. Hispanic-white segregation is around 50 and stable. Asian-white segregation is somewhat lower, around 40.

The historical mechanisms producing segregation are well-documented: explicit racial covenants (legal until 1948), redlining by federal mortgage agencies (1930s-1960s), exclusion from suburban housing markets (G.I. Bill benefits administered locally; Federal Housing Administration policy), urban renewal that displaced Black neighborhoods, restrictive zoning, real-estate practices including blockbusting and steering. Richard Rothstein's *The Color of Law* (2017) documents the federal-government role specifically.

Contemporary mechanisms are more diffuse — exclusionary zoning that produces de-facto segregation without explicit racial reference; income-based segregation that tracks racial inequality; preferences (white preferences for predominantly white neighborhoods, Black preferences for some integration but resistance to being a tiny minority). Whether these add up to a durable pattern of new segregation, a slowly fading legacy of old segregation, or both at once is contested empirically.

### Criminal justice

Black Americans are roughly 12.5% of the U.S. population and roughly 33% of the prison population. The disparity has narrowed somewhat since 2000 but remains large. Drug enforcement disparities are particularly stark: Black Americans are arrested for drug offenses at rates several times higher than white Americans despite roughly equal rates of drug use across the populations.

The literature on what drives this disparity is large. Some factors are well-documented:

- Differential policing concentration in Black neighborhoods (more police presence produces more arrests)
- Differential prosecutorial decisions (sentencing disparities for similar offenses)
- Differential exposure to specific offenses with more severe penalties (the crack-vs.-powder cocaine sentencing disparity, partially corrected by the 2010 Fair Sentencing Act)
- Differential pretrial detention rates (which produce worse case outcomes regardless of guilt)

Other factors are debated: the extent to which higher Black arrest rates for some offenses reflect differential offending versus differential enforcement; the extent to which judicial bias contributes after controlling for prior offenses and crime severity. The 2014 National Academies report *The Growth of Incarceration in the United States* synthesizes the evidence.

### Health outcomes

Black Americans have a life expectancy roughly 4 years shorter than white Americans (around 73 vs. 77 in 2022, after a COVID-era widening of the gap). The gap has fluctuated but has been persistent for decades. Major contributors: cardiovascular disease, cancer, diabetes, infant mortality (Black infant mortality is approximately 2.3 times white infant mortality, a gap that exists across all maternal income and education levels — meaning college-educated Black mothers have higher infant-mortality rates than white mothers without college).

Native American life expectancy is lower still — around 65 years in some recent estimates, though the data is less complete. Hispanic life expectancy is, surprisingly to many, slightly higher than white life expectancy (the "Hispanic paradox" in epidemiology, with several proposed explanations).

The mechanisms producing these gaps include access to healthcare, neighborhood-level exposure to environmental contaminants, occupational hazards, chronic stress (the "weathering" hypothesis advanced by Arline Geronimus), differential treatment within healthcare settings, and others. The empirical work is large and well-established; the relative weights of mechanisms remain debated.

### Education

Black-white achievement gaps on standardized tests have narrowed significantly since the 1970s but remain large. The gap has been remarkably stable since about 2000, after several decades of decline. Why the narrowing stalled is contested.

Within-school differences in resources, teacher quality, advanced-course offerings, and disciplinary practices contribute to gaps. Across-school differences (which track residential segregation closely) contribute more. Sean Reardon's work documents the increase in income-based segregation in U.S. schools since 1990 and its consequences.

### The trade-off — what these patterns do and don't show

The patterns I have summarized are robust. The disparities are real, large, and persistent. This is settled.

What is not settled, and what remains genuinely contested, is *what produces and sustains* these patterns — and therefore what would change them. This is where the next concept becomes essential.

### Common misconceptions

**"The gaps are explained by class, not race."** Class-based factors explain part of every racial gap and most of some specific gaps. They do not explain all of any major gap. The racial wealth gap persists at every income level; the audit-study callback gap is unaffected by class; the infant-mortality gap is largest among college-educated mothers. Reducing race to class is a politically common but empirically unsupported move.

**"The gaps are entirely explained by discrimination."** Discrimination — past and present — explains a large fraction of the gaps. It does not explain everything. Family wealth, neighborhood effects, network differences, cultural patterns, and individual variation all contribute. The gaps would not disappear instantly even if all current discrimination ended; they would also not disappear from policies that ignore non-discriminatory mechanisms. The honest answer is "many causes, most of them traceable to historical discrimination, with contemporary discrimination still active."

**"The gaps are getting better / are getting worse."** Different gaps are doing different things. The Black-white income gap has narrowed slowly; the wealth gap has widened since 2008; segregation has declined slowly; incarceration disparities have narrowed somewhat; achievement gaps have stalled after decades of progress; life-expectancy gaps widened during COVID. Pick a specific gap and a specific time period before generalizing.

## Concept 3 — Frameworks for explaining the patterns

Several frameworks compete to explain the empirical patterns documented in Concept 2. None has a monopoly on the evidence. Each illuminates something important. The student who can articulate the strongest version of each before evaluating is doing the work; the student who only knows the weakest version of frameworks they reject is not.

### Colorblind individualism

The view that race-based inequality, once explicit legal discrimination is removed, will be progressively dissolved by individual achievement, market competition, and equal-opportunity legal frameworks. Government policy should be race-neutral; helping individuals based on individual circumstances (poverty, disability, regional disadvantage) is appropriate, but classifying citizens by race is itself a form of racism that perpetuates the categories.

The strongest case for colorblind individualism: the deep moral commitment that individuals should be treated as individuals, not as members of categorical groups; the empirical claim that explicit race-based policies have generated backlash and may have entrenched racial divisions; the concern that race-conscious policies require ongoing classification of citizens by race, with all the historical baggage that entails. Major intellectual proponents have included Glenn Loury (in some periods), John McWhorter, Thomas Sowell, Shelby Steele, and earlier figures including Frederick Douglass at points in his career.

The case against colorblind individualism: the empirical record since 1965 has not produced the expected dissolution of racial gaps. Many gaps have narrowed; many have not; some have widened. The claim that markets and equal-opportunity law would do the work has not been borne out by the data. Color-blind policy can also be implemented in ways that reproduce existing inequalities (e.g., school district lines that follow former segregation lines and produce unequal funding without explicit racial reference).

### Structural / institutional racism

The view that contemporary racial inequality is sustained by institutional patterns — laws, organizational practices, residential geographies, networks of advantage and disadvantage — that operate independently of any individual actor's racism. Policies and institutions designed under explicit racial discrimination produce racial inequalities long after the explicit discrimination has been removed; new policies that are race-neutral on their face often produce racially disparate outcomes because they operate within already-racialized contexts.

The strongest case for structural racism: the empirical patterns documented in Concept 2 are too persistent and too systematic to be explained by individual prejudice alone. The mechanisms producing them — residential segregation rooted in 20th-century policy; wealth gaps rooted in centuries of exclusion from wealth accumulation; differential treatment by police, courts, lenders, employers — operate through institutional routines, not just through individual hostility. Eliminating individual racial hostility (which has measurably declined since 1960) would not eliminate the structural patterns. Major intellectual proponents include Eduardo Bonilla-Silva, Patricia Hill Collins, William Julius Wilson (in some respects), Ta-Nehisi Coates, and many contemporary scholars.

The case against structural racism: the framework can become unfalsifiable when every disparate outcome is taken as evidence of structural racism without specifying the mechanism. If "structural racism" means "contemporary institutions produce racially disparate outcomes through specifiable mechanisms rooted in historical racism," the claim is strong and largely empirically supported. If "structural racism" means "any racially disparate outcome reflects racism," the claim becomes resistant to ordinary disconfirmation. Critics argue that the strong version of the framework is sometimes substituted for the weaker, more defensible version in popular discourse.

### Cultural-conservative explanations

A cluster of arguments — sometimes associated with figures like Thomas Sowell, the late Walter Williams, parts of the work of William Julius Wilson on the inner-city poor, parts of John McWhorter's work — that cultural patterns within minority communities (family structure, educational priorities, attitudes toward work and authority) contribute to racial gaps and that these cultural patterns require examination in their own right rather than being treated entirely as products of structural forces.

The strongest case for cultural-conservative arguments: cultural variation across groups is empirically real and consequential; treating all group-level outcome differences as exclusively the product of structural forces externalizes responsibility in ways that may impede progress; there are documented cases where specific cultural patterns within communities have changed and been associated with improved outcomes (Thomas Sowell's historical-comparative work on group economic mobility, for instance). The framework doesn't require "blaming the victim" — it requires asking how cultures form, what they reward, and how they change.

The case against cultural-conservative explanations: most "cultural" explanations of racial gaps have been shown, on careful examination, to be downstream of structural conditions. Family-structure changes among Black Americans since the 1960s, for instance, track economic deindustrialization in heavily-Black urban areas (Wilson's *The Truly Disadvantaged*); the supposed "cultural" patterns are in significant part adaptive responses to changing labor markets. Critics argue that cultural-conservative arguments often understate the structural forces and overstate independent cultural variation.

### Intersectionality

The view that race interacts with gender, class, sexuality, and other axes of inequality in ways that cannot be analyzed separately and then added together. Black women's experiences are not the sum of "Black experiences" plus "women's experiences" — there are specific patterns affecting Black women that neither analysis captures. The framework was developed by Kimberlé Crenshaw (1989, 1991) as a legal-theoretical and sociological tool.

The strongest case for intersectionality: the empirical patterns at intersections often differ qualitatively from patterns along single axes. The Chetty mobility study found very different mobility patterns for Black men and Black women, with Black women's mobility comparing favorably to white women's while Black men's diverged sharply from white men's. The wage gap, the incarceration gap, the educational outcome gap, the health gap — most show substantial gender-by-race interactions that are invisible in single-axis analysis. Intersectionality names these patterns and provides analytical tools for studying them.

The case against simple-minded uses of intersectionality: the framework can multiply categories without specifying mechanisms, producing arguments that are sociologically rich but causally vague. The framework's strongest empirical applications (Crenshaw's original work; subsequent demographic and labor-market research) are tightly connected to specific data; the weakest applications use intersectional language as a substitute for analysis. The student should know the strong version, which is widely accepted; some popular versions are weaker.

### The trade-off — choosing among frameworks

These frameworks are not mutually exclusive. A serious analysis of any specific outcome — racial wealth gaps, criminal-justice disparities, educational outcomes — usually draws on multiple frameworks. Wealth gaps reflect structural mechanisms (cumulative exclusion from wealth accumulation), individual mechanisms (current discrimination), cultural mechanisms (savings rates, financial-literacy variation that are partly downstream of structural conditions and partly independently variable), and intersectional mechanisms (very different patterns by gender within racial groups).

The serious sociological move is to use multiple frameworks pragmatically rather than picking one as orthodox. The serious political move — which is different from the sociological move — is to take the empirical evidence about mechanisms and use it to evaluate policy proposals. Different policy proposals follow from different weights on different mechanisms. The empirical evidence narrows what positions are reasonable; it does not, by itself, settle the political questions.

### Common misconceptions

**"There's a scientific consensus that one of these frameworks is right."** There is sociological consensus on many empirical patterns; there is not consensus on the relative weight of mechanisms. Treating one framework as the consensus position misrepresents the field.

**"The frameworks are just political positions dressed up as analysis."** They have political associations and political consequences. They also have empirical content. Each framework makes specific testable claims; some of those claims are well-supported, some weakly supported, some mixed. Treating the frameworks as pure political signals obscures the empirical work each is trying to do.

**"You have to pick one framework to be intellectually serious."** You don't. Most contemporary scholars draw on multiple frameworks pragmatically. Picking one framework dogmatically usually produces worse analysis than acknowledging the multiple mechanisms in play.

## Synthesis — what this chapter has and hasn't done

This chapter has tried to do three things at once. First: present the empirical record clearly enough that the patterns are visible and verifiable. Second: present the major theoretical frameworks for explaining the patterns charitably enough that you encounter the strongest version of each, not the strawman version. Third: identify what is settled, what is contested, and what is normative — three different kinds of claim that are routinely conflated.

What this chapter has done: documented robust empirical patterns of racial stratification; introduced the social-construction-of-race argument with its specific meaning (constructed and real, not constructed therefore unreal); presented multiple competing frameworks for explaining the empirical patterns; flagged where consensus exists and where it doesn't.

What this chapter has not done: settled the contested questions; pronounced on which framework is correct; recommended specific policies; evaluated current political debates by name. These are partly normative questions and partly questions where the evidence does not yet distinguish positions. The chapter's claim is that you should leave it knowing more about what is and isn't settled, equipped with the analytical tools to evaluate further claims you encounter.

What I, as the author, conclude: the empirical record is unambiguous that racial stratification in the United States is real, large, and not currently dissolving on its own. The mechanisms are multiple and contested; the strongest empirical case is for structural mechanisms producing most of the persistent gaps, with current discrimination still actively contributing in ways that audit studies and other careful work have documented. Cultural mechanisms are real but mostly downstream of structural conditions, with some independent variation that is worth studying. The colorblind-individualist prediction that race-based inequality would dissolve under formally race-neutral policy has not been borne out by 60 years of evidence. The intersectional framework, used carefully, captures real patterns that single-axis analyses miss.

This is my reading. I have tried to present the evidence so you can evaluate it. Reasonable people disagree on the relative weights of mechanisms and on what follows for policy. The work of disagreeing well — knowing what the other side's strongest argument is, knowing where the evidence is strong and weak, knowing where the disagreement is empirical and where it is normative — is what this chapter has tried to teach.

## Chapter summary

Race and ethnicity are categories built by societies, maintained by social practice, and consequential for life chances. The biological evidence is clear that the racial categories used in the U.S. and elsewhere do not correspond to genetic clusters; the sociological evidence is equally clear that the categories shape who lives where, earns what, marries whom, dies when. Racial stratification in the United States is empirically robust — wealth gaps, residential segregation, criminal-justice disparities, health and educational gaps — and theoretically contested. Multiple frameworks (colorblind individualism, structural racism, cultural-conservative explanations, intersectionality) compete to explain the patterns; each illuminates something real and has limits. The serious student of race holds the empirical patterns and the theoretical contestation in mind simultaneously and resists collapsing either into the other.

## What would change my mind

If a sustained period of formally race-neutral policy produced rapid dissolution of the major racial gaps in the United States, I would update my reading of how much current racial stratification is structurally rooted vs. dissolvable through individual mobility under non-discriminatory conditions. The post-1965 evidence has not shown this; the most rigorous mobility studies (Chetty et al.) document persistent disparities that survive controls for individual circumstance. A clear reversal would change my reading; the current evidence does not support it.

## Still puzzling

I do not have a confident view on the long-run trajectory of U.S. racial stratification. Several patterns suggest slow improvement (declining segregation, narrowing income gaps among the college-educated, declining explicit prejudice on most survey measures); other patterns suggest stagnation or worsening (wealth gap widening since 2008, achievement-gap progress stalled since 2000, life-expectancy gap widened during COVID). Whether the next several decades produce continued slow improvement, stagnation, or some new dynamic is genuinely uncertain.

The intersectional framework introduced briefly in this chapter will be developed further there. The empirical methods of Chapter 2 are essential background for any of the disputed claims in either chapter.
