# Chapter 10 — Global Inequality

**Suggested titles:**
1. Global Inequality
2. Why Some Nations Got Rich
3. The Patterns Across Borders That Domestic Stratification Misses

**TL;DR.** Global inequality — the differences in income, wealth, opportunity, and well-being across countries — is dramatically larger than within-country inequality and structures the life chances of most of the world's population. The major frameworks for explaining global inequality (modernization theory, dependency theory, world-systems theory, institutionalism) each illuminate different mechanisms; recent developments (the rise of China, the persistence of African low-income economies, the shifting global middle) have complicated all the major theoretical traditions and produced new analytical questions.

---

In 2008, an economist named Branko Milanovic published a paper with a striking finding. If you were comparing two random people drawn from the world's population, the largest predictor of the difference in their incomes was not their education, not their occupation, not their family background, not their effort or talent — it was the country they were born in. Country of birth accounted for about two-thirds of the variation in lifetime income. Within-country factors accounted for the rest.

The implication was uncomfortable. Most sociology focuses on within-country stratification: who succeeds and who fails within a given society. The global frame revealed that these within-country variations operate within enormous between-country differences that dwarf them. A median-income worker in the U.S. has a higher income than approximately 90% of the world's population. The poorest American has a higher income than roughly the bottom 30-40% of the world's population. These are not abstract differences. They translate into life expectancies, child mortality rates, health outcomes, and access to opportunities of every kind.

Milanovic called this the *citizenship premium*. The country one is born into is, statistically, the most consequential single fact about one's economic life chances. Within that country, individual variation operates. Across countries, the variation is much larger.

This is the territory of global inequality. The questions are different from the within-country questions of Chapter 9, even though some frameworks overlap. Why are some countries rich and others poor? How did the current configuration come about? Is it changing, and if so, how? What mechanisms produce and reproduce the patterns?

This chapter walks through what we can say with empirical confidence, the major theoretical frameworks, and the specific contemporary developments (especially the rise of China and the persistence of African poverty) that have reshaped the field.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

1. Describe the empirical patterns of global income inequality and how they have changed over the past 50 years.
2. Distinguish modernization theory, dependency theory, world-systems theory, and contemporary institutionalist approaches.
3. Apply each framework to specific country trajectories, recognizing what each illuminates and what each misses.
4. Diagnose the rise of China as a development case that has complicated all major frameworks.
5. Compare the trajectories of countries that have escaped poverty (the East Asian Tigers, China, Vietnam) with those that have not (much of sub-Saharan Africa, parts of Latin America).
6. Read contemporary debates about development aid, trade, and global governance through the lens of these frameworks.

## Concept 1 — The empirical patterns

Several patterns of global inequality are worth knowing.

### The current distribution

In 2024, World Bank data shows global GDP per capita varying enormously across countries. The wealthiest small economies (Luxembourg, Switzerland, Norway, Ireland, Singapore) have GDP per capita above $80,000-100,000. The wealthiest large economies (the United States, Germany, Japan, the UK) cluster around $50,000-80,000. Middle-income countries (China, Mexico, Brazil, Turkey, South Africa) range from $10,000-25,000. Lower-middle-income countries (India, Indonesia, the Philippines, Vietnam, Egypt) are around $4,000-10,000. The lowest-income countries (concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa: Ethiopia, Madagascar, Burundi, Sierra Leone, Niger) are below $2,000 per capita.

The differences are not just numerical. They translate into life-affecting realities:

- Life expectancy at birth ranges from approximately 85 (Japan, Switzerland, Singapore) to approximately 55-65 in some sub-Saharan African and conflict-affected countries.
- Under-five mortality ranges from below 5 per 1,000 live births in advanced economies to above 100 per 1,000 in some lowest-income countries.
- Access to electricity, clean water, basic education, and primary healthcare varies dramatically across countries.
- Internet access, smartphone penetration, formal employment, and basic banking access track GDP per capita closely.

### The trajectory

Several major patterns stand out in the recent trajectory.

**Convergence in some places.** A substantial group of countries has narrowed the gap with high-income economies over the past several decades. East Asia (South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, Hong Kong) accomplished this convergence in the late 20th century. China has accomplished extraordinary convergence since 1980 — moving from one of the world's poorest countries to upper-middle-income status, with hundreds of millions of people lifted out of poverty. Vietnam, Bangladesh, and several other countries have followed similar trajectories at different scales.

**Persistence of low-income status.** Other countries have made limited progress despite decades of development aid and policy attention. Much of sub-Saharan Africa remains in the lowest-income category. Some countries (Haiti, Yemen, parts of Central America) have stagnated or declined. The reasons are contested.

**The "middle-income trap."** Some countries have grown to middle-income status but struggle to advance beyond it. Latin American economies (Brazil, Mexico, Argentina) have spent decades at middle-income levels without breaking through to high-income status. Whether the "trap" is a real economic phenomenon or just a statistical artifact is debated.

**Within-country inequality.** Several major economies — China most notably — have experienced rising within-country inequality alongside rapid average-income growth. The two trends are not contradictory; they describe different dimensions of economic change.

### Global poverty

Global extreme poverty — the share of the world's population living below extreme poverty lines (the World Bank's $2.15 per day in 2017 PPP terms is the current benchmark) — has declined dramatically. From approximately 40% of the world's population in 1980 to approximately 9% in 2019, before COVID-related setbacks. This is one of the most dramatic positive trends in human history.

The decline is heavily concentrated in China and to a lesser extent India. Sub-Saharan Africa has not seen comparable declines; the region's share of global extreme poverty has risen even as global poverty has fallen, because the population has grown while poverty has not declined as quickly.

> ### Dig Deeper — Compare two country trajectories
> Pick two countries that started at similar levels in 1960 and ended up very differently — South Korea and the Philippines; Botswana and Zimbabwe; Singapore and Sri Lanka; Vietnam and the DRC. Ask Claude: "Walk me through each country's trajectory since 1960. What were the major economic and political turning points? What policies and institutions made the difference? What does each country's trajectory tell us about development?"

### Common misconceptions

**"Globalization has made the world more unequal."** Between-country inequality has narrowed substantially in some periods (especially since 1980, driven by China's rise). Within-country inequality has often risen. Whether overall global inequality has risen or fallen depends on which measure and which time period; the patterns are complex.

**"Aid causes development."** The empirical record on aid effectiveness is mixed. Some aid programs have succeeded; many have not. Strong claims in either direction tend not to survive careful examination.

**"Some countries are just stuck."** None has been permanently stuck. The countries currently in low-income positions have been at various positions across different historical periods; future trajectories are not determined by current positions.

## Concept 2 — Modernization theory

The dominant theoretical framework of mid-20th-century development economics. Walt Rostow's *The Stages of Economic Growth* (1960) is the classical statement.

Modernization theory holds that countries move through identifiable stages of development:

1. **Traditional society** — agricultural, slow change, limited technology
2. **Preconditions for takeoff** — increasing trade, infrastructure, basic education, beginning industrialization
3. **Takeoff** — rapid industrialization, sustained growth, new institutions
4. **Drive to maturity** — continued growth, technological sophistication, services begin to grow
5. **Age of high mass consumption** — the configuration of post-1945 advanced economies — durable goods, services, leisure

Each country, on this account, is somewhere on this progression. Development is the process of moving through the stages. Policies that produce the right conditions (capital investment, education, modern institutions, integration into global markets) accelerate the process. Failure to develop reflects failure to put the right conditions in place.

The framework was politically influential — much development aid in the 1960s and 1970s was organized around modernization-theory assumptions. It was also academically influential as a unifying framework for the new field of development studies.

### Strengths and weaknesses

The framework's strengths: it identified a real pattern (the cluster of changes that often accompany economic development — urbanization, education expansion, sectoral shifts from agriculture to industry to services); it predicted, broadly correctly, that several Asian economies would develop rapidly; it articulated a coherent theory connecting economic and social change.

The framework's weaknesses: the implicit assumption that all countries will follow the same path is empirically problematic. Many countries have not moved through the stages in the predicted sequence. Modernization theory's emphasis on individual countries' internal characteristics underplays the role of international economic relationships. The strong predictive claims have not been borne out for many countries that should, on the theory's premises, have developed by now.

The framework also carried implicit values that critics have challenged. The "high mass consumption" society is presented as the endpoint of development, with implicit acceptance of the U.S. and Western European model. Alternative paths and alternative endpoints are not well accommodated.

### Common misconceptions

**"Modernization theory is dead."** It is no longer the dominant framework, but key insights from it (the empirical patterns of co-occurring changes) remain influential. Most development economics today is post-modernization but still draws on some of the framework's empirical findings.

**"Modernization theory was just ideology."** It had ideological elements; it also had genuine empirical content. Treating it as pure ideology obscures what it correctly identified.

## Concept 3 — Dependency theory and world-systems theory

The Latin American structuralist response to modernization theory, developing from the 1950s onward. Raúl Prebisch and Hans Singer at the UN Economic Commission for Latin America (ECLA, later ECLAC) made the early arguments. Andre Gunder Frank, Fernando Henrique Cardoso (later President of Brazil), and others extended them.

The core dependency-theory claim: the global economy is structured into a *core* (the developed, industrialized economies) and a *periphery* (the developing, primary-commodity-exporting economies). The relationship is not one of equal partners but of structural inequality. The core extracts value from the periphery through:

- Unequal terms of trade (manufactured goods exchange for primary commodities at terms favoring the core)
- Financial flows (interest payments on debt, profit repatriation by multinational corporations, capital flight)
- Technological dependence (peripheral countries import technology from the core, paying licensing fees and ongoing dependence)
- Political mechanisms (foreign-policy interventions, IMF/World Bank conditionality, control of international institutions)

On this account, peripheral countries cannot simply follow the modernization-theory path of "developing through stages." Their position in the global economy structurally limits their opportunities; the same factors that produced the core's prosperity reproduce the periphery's poverty.

Immanuel Wallerstein's **world-systems theory** (developed from the 1970s onward) extended this into a more comprehensive framework. Wallerstein argued that the modern world has been a single capitalist world-system since the 16th century, organized hierarchically into core, semi-periphery (intermediate countries), and periphery. The world-system has its own dynamics — long economic cycles, hegemonic transitions among core countries, peripheralization and incorporation of new regions — that operate at the system level rather than within individual countries.

### Strengths and weaknesses

The frameworks' strengths: they highlighted structural mechanisms operating at the international level that modernization theory underplayed. They predicted (correctly) that import-substitution industrialization in mid-20th-century Latin America would not produce sustained growth. They drew attention to the political economy of international relations — how trade and financial relationships shape developmental possibilities.

The frameworks' weaknesses: their central empirical prediction — that peripheral countries cannot break out of dependency through engagement with the global economy — has been substantially challenged. The East Asian Tigers (South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, Hong Kong) developed rapidly through export-oriented engagement with the world economy, not through delinking. China has accomplished even more dramatic growth through deep integration into global markets. The strong dependency claim that integration produces dependency has not held.

A weaker version — that integration is necessary but not sufficient for development, that the specific terms of integration matter, that some configurations of global economic relationships are more developmentally productive than others — is more defensible. Most contemporary development scholarship draws on this weaker version pragmatically.

### Common misconceptions

**"Dependency theory was disproved by China's rise."** The strong version was challenged. Specific insights about how international economic relationships shape developmental possibilities remain influential.

**"World-systems theory is just leftist ideology."** The empirical work in this tradition (Wallerstein's historical-comparative analysis; Giovanni Arrighi's work on hegemonic cycles; subsequent scholars) is substantial and rigorous. Treating it as pure ideology obscures the analytical work.

## Concept 4 — Institutionalism and the contemporary mainstream

The contemporary dominant framework in development economics: institutional analysis. Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson's *Why Nations Fail* (2012) is the most-read recent statement; the underlying framework draws on Douglass North, Acemoglu's earlier work, and many others.

The core argument: countries' long-run economic trajectories are shaped primarily by their institutions — the rules, norms, and organizations that structure economic and political activity. *Inclusive institutions* (broad political participation, secure property rights, rule of law, accountable government) support sustained development; *extractive institutions* (concentrated political power, insecure property rights, weak rule of law, government oriented around extracting from the population) do not.

The framework provides a coherent answer to the modernization-vs.-dependency debate: countries that develop have built (or had imposed on them, in some cases) inclusive institutions; countries that don't have not. The mechanisms run through specific institutional features — independent courts protecting property; broad political franchise constraining elite extraction; competitive markets with rule-based governance.

The framework has produced substantial empirical work. Studies of natural experiments (German reunification; the Korean partition; colonial-era institutional differences across regions) have provided evidence that institutions do shape long-run trajectories.

### Strengths and weaknesses

Strengths: the framework integrates political and economic analysis productively. It explains some of the variation that modernization theory predicted incorrectly and that dependency theory underexplained. It provides actionable analysis (institutions can in principle be reformed; specific institutional features have specific effects).

Weaknesses: the framework can become circular (institutions are good if development follows; how do we know they're good in advance?). The "institutional" category is broad enough to include many specific mechanisms; specifying which institutional features matter most is harder than the general framework suggests. Some critics argue the framework underplays the historical specificity of how institutions are actually built and changed.

### The Beijing-vs.-Washington consensus debate

A specific contemporary debate worth knowing.

The **Washington Consensus** — a set of policy prescriptions associated with the IMF, World Bank, and U.S. Treasury in the 1980s and 1990s — emphasized fiscal discipline, privatization, trade liberalization, financial liberalization, labor-market deregulation, and openness to foreign investment. This package was widely promoted as the recipe for development.

China's actual development path diverged from the Washington Consensus in important respects. The **"Beijing Consensus"** (the term was coined by Joshua Cooper Ramo in 2004 and has been used in different ways by different scholars) describes the distinctive Chinese path: gradualist reform (rather than shock therapy), capital controls (rather than full financial liberalization), substantial state-owned enterprises in strategic sectors, infrastructure-led investment, authoritarian political governance combined with substantial market liberalization in the economy.

The empirical observation: China achieved the most dramatic poverty reduction in human history through a development path that did not follow the Washington Consensus prescriptions. This has weakened the strong claim that Washington-Consensus reforms are necessary for development.

Whether the Chinese path is replicable is contested. Several factors specific to China (size, demographics, savings rates, state capacity, the specific historical moment of reform) may not transfer to other countries. But the existence of an alternative high-growth path has weakened the strong claim of Washington-Consensus universalism.

> ### Dig Deeper — The China case
> Ask Claude: "Walk me through China's development since 1978 — the major reforms, the key policy choices, the institutional changes. What does the case challenge in modernization, dependency, and institutionalist accounts? What features of the Chinese trajectory may be transferable to other contexts, and which are likely China-specific?"

### Common misconceptions

**"Institutions are everything."** Institutions matter substantially. They are not the only thing that matters; geography, climate, demographic factors, cultural patterns, and historical contingency all play roles in specific country trajectories.

**"China's success means authoritarianism is better for development."** This is too strong a claim. The conditions of China's success include features (massive population, very high savings rates, state capacity built over centuries) that don't transfer easily. Other authoritarian regimes have not produced comparable growth.

**"The Washington Consensus failed."** Some specific WC prescriptions did fail in some specific contexts; others succeeded. The blanket claim that WC failed is too strong; the specific claim that WC was insufficient as a universal recipe is well-supported.

## Concept 5 — What we can and can't say

A serious sociological reading of global inequality holds several things together.

The empirical patterns are clear. Enormous gaps exist between countries; the gaps have evolved in complex ways; some countries have made dramatic progress (most prominently China) while others have not.

The major theoretical frameworks each illuminate aspects:

- Modernization theory captures real patterns of co-occurring changes during economic development.
- Dependency theory and world-systems theory capture real structural mechanisms of international economic relationships.
- Institutionalist approaches capture real differences in how economic and political organization shapes long-run trajectories.

None of the frameworks has succeeded at predicting specific country trajectories with high accuracy. The historical record has surprised proponents of each framework.

What we can say:
- Some country trajectories have been clearly successful (East Asian Tigers, China, Vietnam) and these have features that other countries' policymakers can learn from.
- Some institutional features (rule of law, secure property rights, broad political participation, sound macroeconomic management, investment in human capital) are correlated with sustained development across many cases.
- Specific contexts and historical circumstances matter; what worked in one place may not work in another.

What we cannot say:
- Whether any specific currently-low-income country will develop and how quickly.
- Whether specific institutional reforms will produce specific developmental outcomes.
- Whether the post-1945 international economic order will continue to provide a context for low-income country development or will fragment in ways that make development harder.

The discipline's posture should be empirical humility combined with continued analytical work. The student who has worked through this chapter holds the empirical patterns clearly, recognizes the theoretical disagreements, and resists strong simple narratives that the evidence does not support.

## Chapter summary

Global inequality is dramatic — between-country differences in income, wealth, and life-chance dwarf within-country differences for most of the world's population. The major theoretical frameworks (modernization theory, dependency theory, world-systems theory, institutionalism) each illuminate parts of the explanation. Empirical work has produced findings that complicate every framework: development success in some unexpected places (China most prominently), persistent poverty in others, the rise of middle-income economies that don't fit neatly into either pole. The strongest contemporary work integrates frameworks pragmatically, attends to historical specificity, and exercises empirical humility about predicting specific trajectories. Sociology offers a structured way to think about questions whose answers are still being determined and that will affect the lives of billions of people.

## What would change my mind

If a sustained period showed that the development success cases (China, East Asian Tigers) were systematically reversed and that the long-stagnant low-income countries (much of sub-Saharan Africa) made dramatic gains, I would update my reading of which factors are most causally important. The current evidence suggests the success cases have produced durable transformation; substantial reversal would require explanation.

## Still puzzling

I do not have a confident view on whether the next 30 years will see continued global convergence (with most low-income countries gradually catching up) or persistent divergence (with some countries remaining stuck while others advance). The factors that distinguish the trajectories are imperfectly understood; specific predictions about specific countries are unreliable.
