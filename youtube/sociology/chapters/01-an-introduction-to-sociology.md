**TL;DR.** Sociology is the systematic study of how human social life is organized — how we form groups, build institutions, transmit culture, generate inequality, and change over time. Its founding insight is the *sociological imagination*: the recognition that what looks like a private trouble is often a public issue, that the individual life is shaped by forces the individual did not choose, and that those forces can be studied and named. The discipline has three classical theoretical traditions, each illuminating different parts of the same social reality.

---

In 1959, an American sociologist named C. Wright Mills published a book called *The Sociological Imagination*. The book opens with a description of unemployed men. When one man is unemployed, Mills wrote, the problem is personal — his skills, his network, his decisions, his luck. Solutions are individual: retrain, network harder, move to where the jobs are.

When fifteen million men are unemployed at the same time, the problem is no longer personal. It cannot be. There are not fifteen million simultaneous failures of skill and effort and luck. Something has happened to the structure of work, of capital, of geography, of policy — something that produces unemployment as a feature of how the system is currently arranged. The individual man cannot fix that. The individual man may not even be able to see it from where he stands.

Mills called the move from the first description to the second the **sociological imagination**: the capacity to see how *personal troubles*, lived in the small details of an individual life, connect to *public issues*, the structural patterns of an entire society. The sociological imagination is not consoling. It does not tell the unemployed man that his unemployment is not his fault — sometimes it is partly his fault. It tells him that fault is the wrong frame for understanding what he is in. He is in a system. The system has features. Those features can be named, measured, and — sometimes — changed.

This is what sociology offers. It is not a self-help discipline. It does not promise to solve your personal problems. It promises something stranger and, in some ways, more useful: the ability to see your life as one instance of a pattern that is bigger than you, and to see that pattern clearly enough that you can make informed decisions about how to live within it, push back against it, or work to change it.

This chapter introduces the discipline. By the end you should know what sociology actually is (more specific than "the study of society"), where it came from, what its three main theoretical traditions argue, and what the discipline can and cannot tell you. You will also see, in the small details of the chapter, the voice the rest of the book will use — explanatory, empirically grounded, charitable about positions you may not yet share, willing to admit what is contested.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

1. State Mills's sociological imagination in your own words and apply it to a personal situation.
2. Distinguish sociology from psychology, anthropology, economics, and political science — what each studies, where they overlap.
3. Identify the founding figures (Durkheim, Weber, Marx, DuBois) and what each contributed to the discipline.
4. Compare the three classical theoretical paradigms — functionalism, conflict theory, symbolic interactionism — and predict what each would say about a contemporary social phenomenon.
5. Name the key features of sociological method: empirical, comparative, structural, reflexive.
6. Diagnose what sociology can and cannot tell you about a specific question — when it has settled empirical answers, when it has competing theoretical frameworks, when it can offer description but not prediction.

## Concept 1 — What sociology actually studies

The textbook one-liner — "sociology is the study of society" — is true and useless. It is true because sociologists do study society. It is useless because every social science studies society, and the differences are where the work is.

The clearer way: sociology studies the *patterns* that emerge when human beings live together in groups. The patterns are real — they can be measured, compared, predicted (within limits) — and they are not the same as the sum of the individuals composing them. A group of one hundred people behaves in ways that no single one of those people would. A society of three hundred million produces phenomena (urban segregation, generational shifts, economic cycles, social movements) that no one in the society chose to produce. The patterns are emergent, structural, and durable.

This is what makes sociology distinct from the disciplines closest to it.

**Psychology** studies the individual mind — perception, cognition, emotion, motivation, mental disorder. The unit of analysis is the person. Where psychology studies social phenomena, it does so through the lens of how individual psychology produces social behavior (social psychology).

**Economics** studies how individuals and organizations make decisions about producing, distributing, and consuming scarce resources. The unit of analysis is typically the rational actor (or some refinement of that), and the standard tool is the model that derives aggregate patterns from individual choices.

**Political science** studies the institutions and processes through which power is acquired, exercised, and constrained. The unit of analysis varies — voters, parties, institutions, states — but the focus is on what political theorists since Aristotle have called the polis: the organization of political authority.

**Anthropology** studies human variation across cultures and (in the physical-anthropology branch) across the human evolutionary lineage. Cultural anthropology shares much with sociology — both study how groups organize life, both use ethnographic methods — but anthropology has historically focused more on non-Western, non-industrial societies and on the totality of culture rather than on specific institutional arenas.

Sociology sits in the middle of all of these and is bounded by none of them cleanly. A sociologist of the family is doing work that a cultural anthropologist might also do. A sociologist of work is in conversation with economists. A political sociologist overlaps with political scientists on questions of power, authority, and legitimacy. The discipline is defined less by an exclusive subject matter than by a distinctive *posture*: the recognition that social patterns are real, structural, and irreducible to individual psychology, and that those patterns are the object of study.

### What sociology studies, more concretely

Some of the questions sociology asks (with examples from the recent literature):

- Why do people in different countries trust their neighbors at very different rates, and what does that trust correlate with? (Robert Putnam's *Bowling Alone*; Eric Uslaner's work on generalized trust)
- Why has marriage rates declined sharply in advanced economies since 1970, and what differs across class lines? (Andrew Cherlin's *The Marriage-Go-Round*; Kathryn Edin and Maria Kefalas, *Promises I Can Keep*)
- How does the place a child grows up affect their adult earnings, and how much of that effect is the place vs. the family? (Raj Chetty and colleagues' work on the Equality of Opportunity Project)
- Why do social movements succeed or fail, and what factors predict the difference? (Doug McAdam, Sidney Tarrow, Charles Tilly's *Dynamics of Contention*; Erica Chenoweth and Maria Stephan's *Why Civil Resistance Works*)
- How does the way we organize work shape mental health, family life, and political behavior? (Arlie Hochschild's *The Second Shift*; Jennifer Silva's *Coming Up Short*)

These questions have empirical answers. Not always settled answers — sociology has its disagreements like any science — but the questions are the kind that careful research can illuminate.

### The trade-off

Sociology's position between disciplines is its strength and its weakness.

**The strength.** Social phenomena are messy. Most real questions about the world cross disciplinary lines. Why is American life expectancy declining for people without college degrees? The answer touches economics (job markets, healthcare access), public health (drug overdoses, suicide, obesity), political science (policy failures), and culture (what it feels like to live in a community without a future). Sociology's willingness to follow the question across these lines is a feature, not a bug. Anne Case and Angus Deaton's *Deaths of Despair*, the major work on this question, is methodologically eclectic in exactly that way.

**The weakness.** Disciplines that specialize harder produce more refined methods, more precise findings, and more accumulated knowledge in their narrow domain. Sociology's breadth comes at the cost of methodological consensus. There is no single thing that "sociologists do" the way "economists run regressions" or "anthropologists do ethnography." This makes the discipline harder to summarize and easier to caricature. The flip side: sociologists have to be polyglot. A good sociologist can read a regression, a Foucauldian analysis, an ethnographic monograph, and a historical-comparative argument, and judge each on its own terms.

> ### Dig Deeper — Three disciplines, one question
> Pick a contemporary social phenomenon — declining birth rates in advanced economies, rising mental-health rates among adolescents, the gig economy, the loneliness epidemic. Ask Claude: "How would a sociologist, an economist, and a psychologist each frame and study this question? What does each emphasize? What does each tend to miss?" The exercise reveals what sociology distinctively brings.

### Common misconceptions

**"Sociology is just common sense."** It is not. Sociology routinely produces findings that contradict common-sense expectations: that money has diminishing returns on happiness above a certain income (but only up to a point — recent work by Killingsworth and Kahneman complicates the older Easterlin findings); that interracial contact under specific conditions reduces prejudice but in other conditions increases it (Allport's contact hypothesis and its modern refinements); that strong family ties can constrain economic mobility as well as enable it; that increased educational attainment can increase political polarization rather than dissolve it. Common sense is the sociologist's first hypothesis, frequently overturned by evidence.

**"Sociology is just politics."** It has political implications. So does every social science. The discipline aspires to empirical rigor; its findings are often used in political argument. The best sociology presents what the evidence shows and lets the reader make political judgments. The worst sociology — and there is some — substitutes political conclusion for evidence. The student should learn to tell the difference.

**"Sociology is just opinion."** Some popular treatments of sociology are. Academic sociology is not. The discipline has methods, peer review, replication standards (currently strained by the same crisis affecting psychology and other social sciences), and accumulated empirical findings. A sociologist who claims something has, in good practice, evidence backing the claim. The student's job is to learn what the evidence is, what it does and doesn't support, and where genuine disagreement remains.

## Concept 2 — The founding figures and what they argued

Sociology has four founding figures whose names you will encounter throughout this book and any other sociology you read. Each gave the discipline something specific. None had everything right.

### Émile Durkheim (1858–1917, French)

Durkheim is the figure most associated with sociology's claim that *social facts are real*. Social facts — durable patterns of behavior that constrain individuals — are not reducible to the individuals making them up. They have to be studied as themselves, with their own causes and consequences.

Durkheim's most famous demonstration is *Suicide* (1897). Suicide looks like the ultimate individual act — the most personal possible decision. Durkheim showed that suicide rates vary systematically across groups (Protestants vs. Catholics, married vs. unmarried, soldiers vs. civilians, urban vs. rural), and that the variation tracks features of the *social environment*, not features of individual psychology. He proposed four types — egoistic (too little integration), altruistic (too much integration), anomic (too little regulation), fatalistic (too much regulation) — and argued that the rate of suicide in a society is a window into the society's structure.

The methodology was a breakthrough. The substantive findings have been refined; some specific claims have been challenged. The deeper move — that social facts have explanatory power independent of individual decisions — is the foundation Durkheim laid for the discipline.

Other major Durkheim contributions: the analysis of the *division of labor* in society and how it shifts as societies modernize; the analysis of *religion* in *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* as a social phenomenon producing solidarity rather than primarily as a system of beliefs about gods; the concept of *anomie* — the breakdown of social regulation — as a recurring feature of modern life.

### Max Weber (1864–1920, German)

Weber added something Durkheim's framework lacked: the meanings actors give to their actions. A Durkheimian sociology can describe patterns from outside; a Weberian sociology asks what the actors think they are doing and why those meanings matter.

Weber's *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* (1905) is the most cited example. Weber traced the rise of modern capitalism partly to the cultural-religious framework of Calvinist Protestantism — the doctrine of predestination producing a worldly asceticism in which work and accumulation became signs of grace. The argument is contested in its specifics (some historians have substantially revised Weber's empirical claims about the timing and geography). The deeper move — that culture, ideas, and meaning are not just decorative but causally significant — is one of sociology's foundational commitments.

Other Weber contributions: the analysis of *bureaucracy* as the characteristic organizational form of modernity (covered in Ch 6); the typology of legitimate authority (traditional, charismatic, rational-legal); the methodology of *Verstehen* — interpretive understanding of action — alongside or against quantitative methods; the analysis of stratification as multidimensional (class, status, party — distinct and not reducible to each other).

### Karl Marx (1818–1883, German)

Marx is the founding figure most associated with conflict theory. The core Marxian claim: every society organized around private ownership of the means of production produces class division, with the owners (bourgeoisie in capitalism) extracting value from the workers (proletariat). The conflict between classes is the engine of social change.

Marx's analyses of 19th-century capitalism — the formation of an industrial working class, the trends toward concentration of capital, the cyclical instability of capitalist economies, the alienation of workers from their labor — produced an enormous body of work whose influence has shaped social thought for a century and a half.

What is right and what is wrong about Marx is genuinely contested. The empirical predictions of immiseration of the working class and revolutionary collapse have not played out as Marx predicted in advanced capitalist societies. The analytical tools — the attention to material conditions, to class formation, to the politics of production — have proven enormously productive even for scholars who reject Marx's revolutionary politics.

Most contemporary sociologists are not Marxists. Many use Marxian tools selectively. Almost all take seriously the analytical move Marx made: that economic structure shapes social possibilities in ways that cannot be reduced to individual choice or cultural preference.

### W.E.B. Du Bois (1868–1963, American)

Du Bois was historically excluded from the canonical lists of "founders" — the older textbooks named only Durkheim, Weber, and Marx — and was reincorporated into the discipline's self-understanding only in the past few decades. The reincorporation is correct.

Du Bois's *The Philadelphia Negro* (1899) is one of the earliest large-scale empirical sociological studies in any language — a comprehensive survey of African American life in Philadelphia using methods (door-to-door interviews, statistical analysis, ethnographic observation) that would not become standard in the discipline for another generation. *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903) introduced the concept of *double consciousness* — the experience of seeing oneself through the eyes of a society that has racialized you — which has shaped sociological work on identity, race, and self for over a century.

Du Bois's broader contribution: insisting that race and racism are central, not peripheral, features of modern social life, and that sociology cannot understand the United States or the modern world without taking them seriously. This claim was contested in the discipline's first century; it is now a substantial subfield of contemporary sociology (covered in Ch 11 of this book).

### Why these four

The list could be longer. Other figures who would appear in many recent retellings: Harriet Martineau (1802-1876, English; one of the first to translate Comte and produce empirical sociology of British society); Georg Simmel (1858-1918, German; on the forms of social interaction, urban life, the philosophy of money); George Herbert Mead (1863-1931, American; on the social self and symbolic interaction). The four-name canon is a working starting point, not a complete account.

> ### Dig Deeper — Apply a founding figure to a current question
> Pick a contemporary social phenomenon (rising loneliness, declining marriage rates, the gig economy, the polarization of media). Ask Claude: "How would Durkheim, Weber, Marx, and DuBois each have analyzed this phenomenon? What would each emphasize? Where would they disagree?" The exercise tests whether you can use the founding frameworks rather than just recite their names.

### Common misconceptions

**"The founders had it all figured out."** They did not. Each saw a piece of social life clearly and missed others. Durkheim's analysis of religion captures something real and misses others. Weber's account of capitalism's origins is partial. Marx's predictions about capitalist collapse have not played out. The discipline has developed by working past, around, and through the founders, not by treating them as authorities.

**"You just have to pick one founder to follow."** You do not. Most contemporary sociologists draw on multiple traditions. The interesting question for any specific phenomenon is: which framework illuminates the most? Often the answer is several frameworks together.

**"DuBois was a token addition to make the canon diverse."** He was not. His empirical work in the 1890s and his theoretical contributions across the 20th century earned canonical status independently of contemporary diversity politics. The reason he was excluded from the early canon was professional racism, not lack of contribution. The reincorporation is a correction.

## Concept 3 — The three theoretical paradigms

Most introductions to sociology organize the discipline's theoretical traditions into three big paradigms. The taxonomy is rough — many contemporary scholars do not fit neatly into one — but it captures the range of frameworks the discipline has developed for explaining social life.

### Functionalism (or structural functionalism)

The view that society is a system of interconnected parts, each contributing to the whole's stability and reproduction. Talcott Parsons (1902–1979) is the most associated American figure; the deeper roots are in Durkheim and the British anthropologists (Radcliffe-Brown, Malinowski).

The functionalist asks: what does this institution, practice, or pattern *do* for the society it is part of? Why does it persist? What would happen if it disappeared? The framework treats social arrangements as having functions — sometimes obvious, sometimes hidden — that explain their durability.

A functionalist analysis of the family, for example, asks what work the family does that other institutions could not do as well: raising children, transmitting culture, providing emotional support, regulating sexuality. The argument explains why the family persists across societies even as its specific form varies.

Functionalism's strength: it captures the genuine fact that institutions persist for reasons, and that gross failures of social function (collapse of legal order, hyperinflation, civil war) produce dramatic social consequences. Its weakness: the framework can drift into justifying whatever exists by claiming it must serve a function, which becomes circular. Why does inequality exist? Because it serves a function. What function? The function it serves. The Davis-Moore thesis on stratification (Ch 9) is the canonical example of this critique.

### Conflict theory

The view that society is structured by competition over scarce resources — material, status, power — and that the patterns we see reflect the outcomes of this competition. Marx is the classical source; contemporary conflict theory is broader, encompassing class, race, gender, and other lines of conflict.

The conflict theorist asks: who benefits from this arrangement? Who is harmed? Where does the power lie that produces and reproduces this pattern? The framework treats social arrangements as outcomes of struggle, with winners and losers, rather than as functional adaptations.

A conflict-theoretic analysis of education, for example, asks not "what does schooling do for society" but "who does schooling serve, and how does it reproduce existing inequalities?" Pierre Bourdieu's work on cultural capital (Ch 16) is a sophisticated example: schools claim to be meritocratic but reward forms of cultural capital that middle-class students arrive with, thereby reproducing class advantage while presenting the result as the product of individual achievement.

Conflict theory's strength: it captures power, inequality, and the political dimensions of social arrangements that functionalism can underplay. Its weakness: it can treat all social arrangements as zero-sum competition, missing the genuinely cooperative and integrative dimensions of social life.

### Symbolic interactionism

The view that society is built up from the meanings actors give to their interactions, and that those meanings are negotiated in the small details of everyday life. The framework's roots are in pragmatist philosophy (William James, Charles Peirce, John Dewey) and the Chicago School of sociology (George Herbert Mead, Herbert Blumer); Erving Goffman's later work extended it powerfully.

The symbolic interactionist asks: what does this situation mean to the actors in it? How are they reading each other? What roles are they performing? How do the meanings get negotiated, contested, and revised? The framework treats social life as a continuous accomplishment of meaning-making rather than as a fixed structure.

Goffman's *Presentation of Self in Everyday Life* (1959) is the classic example. Goffman analyzed everyday interaction as performance — actors stage themselves for audiences, manage impressions, and use the resources of their setting to construct credible identities. The analysis applied to job interviews, hospital rooms, classrooms, dinner parties, and public sidewalks. The framework illuminated something the macro-paradigms missed: the fine-grained work of social life happening in interactions every day.

Symbolic interactionism's strength: it captures the lived texture of social life and the agency of individuals in interpreting and reshaping their situations. Its weakness: it can underplay the structural constraints that shape what meanings are available and what performances will be received credibly.

### Why three paradigms

The three-paradigm taxonomy is a teaching device, not a complete map. Many important traditions fit awkwardly: feminist theory (multiple strands), critical race theory, world-systems theory, network analysis, rational choice theory, ethnomethodology, the Bourdieusian framework. A genuinely complete map would have a dozen branches and many cross-cutting connections.

But the three-paradigm taxonomy captures something real. Most sociological analysis can be located somewhere on the triangle of "what does this do for society" (functionalism), "who benefits and who pays" (conflict theory), and "how do the people in it understand it" (interactionism). The interesting work is often at the intersections, drawing on more than one.

### The trade-off

The three paradigms are not in fact in competition for being the right one. They are tools for different questions.

If you want to understand why an institution persists across centuries despite changing personnel, functionalism's "what work does it do" question is productive. If you want to understand the politics of who benefits and who is harmed, conflict theory's "who has power" question is productive. If you want to understand how the institution is lived from inside, interactionism's "what does it mean to the participants" question is productive.

A serious sociologist asks all three questions about most phenomena. The pure paradigm-warriors of older sociology textbooks are mostly retired or dead; the contemporary discipline draws on multiple frameworks pragmatically.

> ### Dig Deeper — Three frameworks, one phenomenon
> Pick a contemporary social arrangement — say, the modern American workplace, contemporary social media, the structure of higher education, the rise of the gig economy. Ask Claude: "Apply functionalist, conflict-theoretic, and symbolic-interactionist analyses to this phenomenon. What does each illuminate? Which one captures the most about it, and what does each leave out?" The exercise tests whether you can move among frameworks productively rather than picking one as orthodoxy.

### Common misconceptions

**"Functionalism is conservative; conflict theory is radical."** The political associations are weaker than the textbooks sometimes suggest. Functionalism can support critique: an institution that has lost its function or developed dysfunctional consequences can be analyzed and reformed within functionalist terms. Conflict theory can be conservative: realist analyses of power competition can be used to defend existing power arrangements against romantic alternatives.

**"You have to pick a paradigm."** You do not. Picking a paradigm and sticking with it dogmatically produces worse analysis than drawing on multiple frameworks. The paradigm question is "which frame illuminates this question?" not "which frame is correct in general?"

**"Symbolic interactionism is just psychology."** It is not. Symbolic interactionism studies how meanings are *negotiated socially* — between actors, in real settings, with real stakes. Individual psychology contributes to interaction; interaction is not reducible to individual psychology. Goffman's analyses of total institutions (asylums, prisons), of stigma, of public order are doing distinctively sociological work that no individual psychology could do.

## Synthesis — what sociology can and cannot tell you

A serious student of sociology holds three things at once.

First: sociology has empirical findings. Not on every question, and not always settled, but on a great many social phenomena there is accumulated evidence — about the distribution of income and wealth, about social mobility, about residential segregation, about marriage and fertility patterns, about the social determinants of health, about how social movements form and succeed and fail. These findings are not "just opinions." They are contested in detail; they are well-supported in their broad outlines.

Second: sociology has theoretical frameworks that organize what counts as relevant evidence. Functionalism, conflict theory, symbolic interactionism, and the dozens of more refined contemporary frameworks each direct attention differently. Two scholars examining the same phenomenon through different frameworks can produce substantially different analyses without either being wrong. Which framework is most useful depends on the question being asked.

Third: sociology has political consequences and is sometimes politically inflected. The discipline aspires to empirical rigor; its findings are used in political argument. Some sociology is more honest about this than other sociology. The student should learn to tell when a sociological claim is supported by evidence, when it is supported by theoretical framework, and when it is making a normative claim with sociological vocabulary. All three can be valuable; conflating them produces bad work.

What sociology can offer you: the capacity to see your own life as one instance of a larger pattern, the analytical tools to study those patterns systematically, the historical and comparative perspective to recognize what is variable across societies and what seems to be common, and the methodological habits to evaluate claims about social phenomena critically.

What sociology cannot offer you: a complete predictive science of social life, settled answers on every contested question, neutral verdicts on disputes that are partly normative, or a substitute for your own ethical and political judgment. Sociology gives you better tools for those judgments. It does not make them for you.

The remainder of this book is a guided tour of what those tools look like in practice. Each chapter takes a domain of social life — culture, socialization, inequality, family, religion, work, health, politics, environment, change — and asks the discipline's questions of it. By the end, you should have not only knowledge of specific findings but a habit of mind: the sociological imagination Mills described, made operational across two dozen specific contexts.

## Chapter summary

Sociology is the systematic study of how human social life is organized — the patterns that emerge when people live in groups, the institutions they build, the inequalities those institutions reproduce, and the changes those patterns undergo over time. The discipline's founding insight, articulated by C. Wright Mills, is the *sociological imagination*: the capacity to see how individual lives connect to larger structural patterns. Four founding figures — Durkheim, Weber, Marx, and DuBois — gave the discipline its core analytical commitments: that social facts are real, that meaning matters, that economic structure shapes social possibility, and that race and racism are central rather than peripheral to modern social life. Three classical theoretical paradigms — functionalism, conflict theory, symbolic interactionism — illuminate different aspects of the same phenomena and are most productive when used in combination. Sociology offers empirical findings, analytical tools, comparative perspective, and methodological habits; it does not offer complete prediction, neutral verdicts on contested questions, or a substitute for ethical judgment.

## What would change my mind

If a research program in another discipline (perhaps a more rigorous evolutionary psychology, or an extended computational social science) succeeded in producing a fully predictive theory of human social behavior — one that out-predicted sociological accounts on a broad range of phenomena — I would substantially revise my view that sociology's irreducible interest in meaning, structure, and history reflects something genuine about social life rather than just the discipline's current methodological limitations. The current state of the evidence does not support such a research program; computational social science has produced impressive findings but has not displaced the kind of analysis sociology distinctively offers.

## Still puzzling

I do not have a confident view on whether sociology will, over the next several decades, become more or less methodologically unified. The discipline currently encompasses approaches ranging from quantitative-experimental work that closely resembles economics or social psychology to qualitative-interpretive work that closely resembles cultural anthropology or literary scholarship. Whether these methodological poles converge, diverge further, or persist in productive tension is a question about the discipline's future I cannot confidently predict.

```
imagination" (the ability to see the connection between "personal
troubles" — biographical, individual — and "public issues" —
historical, structural); the three major theoretical perspectives
(structural functionalism, conflict theory, symbolic interactionism)
plus contemporary additions (feminist theory, intersectionality);
the difference between sociology and psychology, journalism, or
common-sense observation.

Part A — Pick the problem.

Pick ONE specific social problem to track across all 22 chapters.
Specific is critical — "inequality" is too broad; "housing
affordability in [your city]" is workable. Suggested anchors:
- Homelessness in your city or region.
- The opioid epidemic in a specific community.
- Mental-health crisis among adolescents.
- Gentrification of a specific neighborhood.
- Food insecurity in a region.
- Police-community relations in a specific place.
- The loneliness epidemic among young adults.
- Long-COVID consequences for a specific population.
- A specific labor-market crisis (gig-economy precarity, etc.).
- A specific education crisis (achievement gap, college-affordability).
- An environmental-justice case (a specific polluted community).

The problem must be (a) specific enough to model with real data,
(b) currently contested, (c) something you care enough about for
22 chapters of engagement.

Part B — Apply Mills.

Write the brief's "Personal Troubles vs. Public Issues" section in
500-800 words.

1. **The personal-trouble framing.** Describe the problem as it
   appears in one specific person's life. (Use a real person if
   you can — a news profile, a documentary subject, a person you
   know — or a clearly-labeled hypothetical.) Document the daily
   reality, the immediate causes, the constraints.

2. **The public-issue framing.** Translate the same problem to
   the structural level. What numbers describe its scale?
   (Population affected; trend over time; geographic concentration.)
   What historical and economic forces produced it? What policies
   either created or address it?

3. **The Mills move.** Show the connection. The same set of facts
   describes one person's life AND a structural condition.
   Sociology insists on seeing both. State the connection in one
   precise sentence.

4. **The three perspectives — preview.** Briefly (one paragraph
   each) apply structural functionalism, conflict theory, and
   symbolic interactionism to your problem. What does each
   tradition see? What does it miss? This sets up the
   theoretical contestation that the next 21 chapters will
   explore.

End with one specific question the project will need to answer
about this problem by Chapter 21. (E.g., "Is housing-first or
sobriety-first the more effective intervention for street
homelessness in this city?")
```

---

**What this produces:** A 500-800 word foundational section. The Mills move and the three-perspectives preview frame everything that follows.

**How to adapt this prompt:**

- *For your own project:* Pick a problem you have ongoing exposure to (through your job, your community, your news consumption). The 22-chapter engagement is much easier on a problem you care about.
- *For ChatGPT / Gemini:* Works as written.
- *For Claude Code:* Not the primary tool here.
- *For a Claude Project:* Append. The Mills foundational section is one of the most-referenced sections across all later chapters.

**Connection to previous chapters:** Ch 00's Charter set the discipline; Ch 1 picks the substantive target.

**Preview of next chapter:** Chapter 2 is methods — how is your problem measured, by whom, with what biases? You'll do a methodological audit of the major data sources on your problem.
