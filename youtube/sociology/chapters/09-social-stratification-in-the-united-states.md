**TL;DR.** Social stratification — the systematic, durable division of a society into ranked groups — is universal in large-scale societies and intensely consequential for individual lives. The United States has substantial economic inequality, modest social mobility relative to peer economies, and contested empirical and theoretical accounts of how its stratification system actually works. The chapter walks through what we can say with empirical confidence and where the major theoretical frameworks produce different readings.

---

In 2014, the French economist Thomas Piketty published *Capital in the Twenty-First Century*. The book documented, with painstaking detail and centuries of historical data, a striking pattern: wealth and income inequality in advanced economies had declined from roughly 1910 through the 1970s, then risen sharply since. The 21st-century United States, in particular, was returning to levels of inequality not seen since the early 20th century — what Piketty called the *belle époque*, the period before the world wars when capital concentration had reached extreme levels.

Piketty's book was 700 pages of charts. It became a global bestseller. The reasons illuminate something important about social stratification: it is one of the most consequential subjects sociology studies, and it is subject to intense political debate. The same data set can produce different readings; the same readings produce different policy prescriptions. People care about inequality not as an abstraction but because it shapes their lives, their children's lives, and the political-economic order they live within.

The empirical patterns are well-documented. Income inequality in the United States is high by international standards and has grown since around 1970. Wealth inequality is higher than income inequality and has grown more. Social mobility — the rate at which people move up or down relative to their parents — is moderate to low in the U.S. compared to most other advanced economies. Different racial and ethnic groups have very different mobility experiences (Ch 11 examined some of this).

The theoretical disagreements are substantial. Some frameworks (functionalist, in some readings) treat inequality as the inevitable consequence of differential rewards for differential contribution; others (conflict-theoretic, in some readings) treat inequality as the durable outcome of struggle over resources, with power as the central variable; others (Bourdieusian) emphasize the cultural mechanisms through which class advantage gets reproduced across generations.

This chapter is about all of that — the empirical patterns of inequality, the major frameworks for explaining them, and the contested questions about what could or should be done. The territory is heavily political and heavily contested. The chapter aims for charitable presentation of multiple readings; the student should leave able to articulate the strongest version of positions they may not yet share.

## Concept 1 — Specifying stratification

The first move on any inequality question is to specify what dimension of inequality we are discussing. The discipline distinguishes several.

### Income

Money received in a given period — wages, salaries, business income, investment income, government transfers. The Census Bureau's Current Population Survey produces the standard U.S. measures. Median household income in the United States in 2023 was approximately $80,000 (roughly $20,000 in real terms above 1970 levels). Income inequality is conventionally summarized by Gini coefficients (a measure ranging from 0 — full equality — to 1 — full inequality) or by ratios (the income of the top 10% to the bottom 10%, etc.). U.S. Gini for income has risen from about 0.35 in 1970 to about 0.48-0.49 today, depending on the measure.

### Wealth

Net worth at a moment in time — assets (housing, investments, savings, business ownership) minus liabilities (mortgages, student loans, credit-card debt). Wealth is more concentrated than income everywhere; in the U.S., dramatically so. The Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances (SCF) is the standard data source. Median household net worth in 2022 was approximately $193,000; the top 10% of households held approximately 70% of total wealth; the top 1% held approximately one-third.

The income-wealth distinction matters. A family with high income and no savings is differently situated than a family with moderate income and inherited assets. Wealth provides resilience to shocks (job loss, medical emergencies), the capacity to take risks (starting a business, changing careers), and the resources to provide intergenerational advantages (housing in better school districts, college education without debt, gifts toward home purchase, eventually inheritance). Income inequality understates the durability of class differences; wealth inequality captures more of it.

### Occupation

The kind of work people do — and the prestige, security, and autonomy that comes with that work. Sociology has multiple traditions of occupational classification: the U.S. NORC scale of occupational prestige; the Erikson-Goldthorpe class scheme used in much European research; the contemporary classifications based on labor-market position. Different classifications cut the data differently and produce different findings.

### Education

The credentials people hold (bachelor's, master's, professional degrees) — strongly correlated with income, with occupation, with mobility, and with broader life outcomes. Educational stratification has its own dynamics (Ch 16 covers this in detail).

### How these align and don't

These dimensions are correlated but not identical. A high-income earner without wealth and a wealth-holder without much current income are differently situated despite being at similar overall positions in some sense. A high-prestige but moderate-income occupation (clergy, university professor, public-interest lawyer) and a high-income but moderate-prestige occupation (some sales, some skilled trades) are differently situated despite similar economic standing.

Weber's multidimensional account of stratification (in his 1922 essay "Class, Status, and Party," posthumously published) distinguished three dimensions — *class* (economic position in markets), *status* (social honor, prestige), *party* (organized political power). Weber argued that these can but need not align; a society can have wealthy but socially marginal groups, prestigious but economically modest groups, politically powerful but unrespected groups. The three-dimensional framework captures variation that purely class-based accounts miss.

### The trade-off — single-dimension vs. multidimensional analysis

Single-dimension analyses (income only; or wealth only; or occupational class only) are simpler and produce cleaner findings but miss important variation. Multidimensional analyses are messier but capture more of the actual structure of stratification.

Most contemporary sociology uses multiple dimensions, with the specific choices depending on the question. A study of political polarization might focus on educational stratification (which has become a major political cleavage); a study of life-chances mobility might emphasize wealth (which transmits across generations more strongly than income); a study of social honor might engage Weber's status concept directly.

> ### Dig Deeper — Locate yourself
> Pick four dimensions of stratification (income, wealth, occupation, education). Where does your family fall on each, relative to U.S. medians? Where do these dimensions align, and where do they diverge? Ask Claude: "What does the empirical data show about how these dimensions correlate in contemporary America? Where are the alignments tightest, and where most loose?"

### Common misconceptions

**"Class is just income."** It is not. Class — in the strong sociological sense — captures the durable position of a household in the structure of economic life. Income is part; wealth is part; occupational position is part; the relationship to the means of production (in Marxian terms) is part.

**"There is no class system in America; we have classes only of opportunity."** The empirical evidence on durable class positions, intergenerational transmission of class advantage, and limited social mobility makes this claim hard to defend in any strong form. Mobility exists; it is not so high that prior class disappears.

**"Inequality is just a numbers question."** It is partly empirical (the Gini coefficient, the wealth share of the top 1%) and partly normative (what level of inequality is just? what are acceptable trade-offs against other goods?). Conflating the empirical and normative produces bad analysis.

## Concept 2 — The empirical record

What does the data actually show about U.S. stratification?

### Income inequality

Income inequality in the U.S. has risen sharply since around 1980. The share of national income going to the top 10% rose from approximately 33% in 1980 to approximately 47% in 2020 (Piketty/Saez data, refined by the Distributional National Accounts project). The share going to the top 1% rose from approximately 11% to approximately 21% over the same period. The top 0.1% saw the most dramatic gains, with their share more than doubling.

The bottom 50% of earners have seen real-income gains of roughly 20-30% over the same four decades — substantial, but much smaller than the gains at the top. Different measures (taxable income vs. comprehensive measures including transfers; pre-tax vs. post-tax) produce somewhat different numbers, but the broad pattern is robust.

Comparison with other advanced economies: U.S. inequality is high. Canada, Australia, and the UK have inequality somewhat below the U.S. but well above continental European democracies. Sweden, Germany, and France have substantially lower Gini coefficients. The Nordic countries cluster at the lower end of OECD inequality.

### Wealth inequality

Wealth inequality is dramatically higher than income inequality. The top 1% of U.S. households hold approximately 30-35% of total wealth; the top 10% hold approximately 70%. The bottom half of households holds approximately 1-2% of total wealth (a remarkable statistic that reflects how many households have negative or near-zero net worth).

The wealth gap by race is even more pronounced (Ch 11). Median white household wealth is approximately 6-10 times median Black household wealth, depending on the year and the specific measure.

The wealth gap has widened since the 2008 financial crisis. Recovery has been uneven; asset prices have grown faster than wages for most of the post-crisis period; the share of households holding substantial wealth has narrowed.

### Social mobility

Social mobility — the rate at which children's socioeconomic position differs from their parents' — is the question most directly about whether the inequality is "fair" in the sense of reflecting individual merit and effort.

The findings are sobering. Raj Chetty and colleagues at the Equality of Opportunity Project (now Opportunity Insights) have produced the most rigorous recent measurements. Their findings:

- Absolute mobility (the chance of earning more in real terms than one's parents) has declined dramatically. About 90% of Americans born in 1940 earned more than their parents; only about 50% of Americans born in 1980 do.
- Relative mobility (the chance of changing position in the income distribution) is moderate. The U.S. is near the OECD median for relative mobility — substantially below Nordic countries and Canada, similar to the UK, above Italy and a few others.
- Mobility varies dramatically by where people grow up. Children growing up in some places (the Salt Lake City area, large parts of the upper Midwest) have substantially higher mobility than children growing up in other places (the Deep South, parts of the Rust Belt). The variation is large enough that the same family's outcomes likely depend significantly on where they live.
- Mobility varies dramatically by race. Within the same neighborhoods, with the same family backgrounds, Black boys have substantially worse mobility outcomes than white boys (the pattern documented in Ch 11). The same gap does not appear for women.

These findings have substantial empirical foundation (linked tax-record analysis across millions of cases) and have been widely replicated. The interpretation — what the patterns *mean* and what should be done — is more contested.

### The trade-off — what the data does and doesn't show

The empirical record establishes:

- U.S. inequality is high by international standards
- It has risen substantially since around 1980
- Wealth inequality is dramatically higher than income inequality
- Mobility is moderate, varies by place and race, and absolute mobility has declined

The empirical record does not by itself establish:

- What the optimal level of inequality is
- What mechanisms have driven the rise
- What policies would most effectively address it
- Whether and to what extent inequality is "fair"

These are partly normative questions and partly empirical questions where careful research has produced different findings. The next concept turns to the major theoretical frameworks for interpreting the patterns.

> ### Dig Deeper — Compare U.S. with another country
> Pick another advanced economy (Germany, Sweden, France, the UK, Canada, Japan). Ask Claude: "Compare the inequality and mobility patterns of this country with the U.S. What are the major differences? What policies and institutional differences are typically credited with the divergence?" Then verify against OECD data and academic comparative work.

### Common misconceptions

**"Inequality has always been this high."** It has not. The post-1945 decades of relative equality in advanced economies were historically unusual and policy-driven. The current inequality is a return toward historical norms in some senses, but the specific configuration of contemporary inequality is new.

**"American mobility is exceptional."** It is not, by international comparison. The U.S. has substantial mobility but is not unusually mobile compared to peer economies; some have higher mobility, some lower.

**"Mobility has stayed roughly stable."** Absolute mobility has declined substantially. Relative mobility has been more stable, but starting from a baseline that is mediocre by international standards.

## Concept 3 — Theoretical frameworks for understanding stratification

Multiple major frameworks compete to explain the empirical patterns. Each illuminates something real and has its limits.

### Davis-Moore: the functionalist account

Kingsley Davis and Wilbert Moore's "Some Principles of Stratification" (1945) is the canonical functionalist account. Some social positions, they argued, are more important than others (CEO of a major firm vs. mailroom clerk). Some require longer training, more talent, or scarce skills. Society needs to motivate qualified people to fill these positions and undertake the long preparation. Therefore, society offers differential rewards — money, prestige, autonomy — to motivate people into difficult and important positions. Stratification, on this account, is functionally necessary.

The argument has empirical and logical limits.

**Empirically.** It is hard to defend the proposition that current rewards track functional importance. CEOs of large firms earned roughly 20-30 times the average worker's wage in 1965; they earn roughly 250-300 times the average worker's wage now (depending on the specific calculation). The functional importance of CEOs has not increased ten-fold over six decades; the rewards have. Similarly, financial professionals are extraordinarily well-paid by historical standards; whether they are functionally more important than their predecessors is hard to argue. The Davis-Moore prediction that rewards track function is not robustly supported by the historical evidence.

**Logically.** The argument can be circular: if rewards track function, then high rewards must signal important function, and important function must justify high rewards. This becomes hard to falsify.

**Charitable extension.** Some proponents of broadly functionalist accounts argue not that the current distribution of rewards is exactly right but that some differential rewards are necessary to motivate effort and skill acquisition, and that the alternative (full equality of reward regardless of contribution) would produce worse outcomes. This weaker claim is more defensible. The empirical question is what level of differential reward produces the best outcomes — a question for which the answer is not "as much as we currently have," based on cross-national evidence.

### Marxian and conflict-theoretic accounts

The Marxian framework holds that stratification reflects the structural relationships of capitalist production. Capitalists own the means of production; workers sell their labor; the surplus value produced by labor goes to capital. This division produces inequality structurally, not as the natural consequence of differential ability or contribution.

Contemporary conflict-theoretic accounts have refined the framework. Erik Olin Wright's neo-Marxist work distinguishes multiple class positions (capitalists; managers; petty bourgeoisie; workers with varying degrees of skill and authority). Recent work in this tradition (Branko Milanovic's *Capitalism, Alone*; Thomas Piketty's later work; the work of Emmanuel Saez and Gabriel Zucman on wealth concentration) has been enormously productive in documenting and analyzing the political economy of contemporary inequality.

The framework's strength: it captures structural mechanisms (the political economy of capital accumulation, the share of national income going to capital vs. labor, the mechanisms by which inequality reproduces itself across generations) that purely individualist accounts miss.

The framework's limits: classical Marxism's specific predictions (immiseration of the working class, revolutionary collapse of capitalism) have not played out. The contemporary working class in advanced economies is materially better off than its 19th-century counterparts, even as relative inequality has grown. Strong revolutionary predictions are weakly supported.

### Weber's multidimensional account

Max Weber's framework (mentioned in Concept 1) is more capacious than Marx's. Class is one dimension of stratification; status (social honor) is another; party (organized political power) is a third. Weber's framework illuminates phenomena that purely class-based accounts miss — why some economically-modest groups (clergy, intellectuals, certain ethnic communities) command substantial status and political weight; why some economically-prosperous groups have limited political organization; why status hierarchies often resist alignment with economic position.

Contemporary work in this tradition includes Pierre Bourdieu (next), Goldthorpe and other scholars in comparative class analysis, and the broader sociology of professions, status, and prestige.

### Bourdieu on cultural capital

Pierre Bourdieu's framework (developed across his career, with major statements in *Distinction*, 1979, and *The State Nobility*, 1989) extends Weber's multidimensional account in influential ways. Bourdieu argued that class advantage is reproduced through multiple forms of capital, not just economic capital:

**Economic capital** — money, investments, property.

**Cultural capital** — taste, knowledge, cultural skills, credentials. This includes the ability to navigate certain kinds of cultural settings (museums, classical music, fine dining), the cultural skills rewarded by elite educational institutions, and the dispositions ("habitus") absorbed in childhood that make certain forms of cultural participation feel natural.

**Social capital** — networks of relationships, especially relationships that provide access to resources or opportunities.

**Symbolic capital** — prestige, recognition, honor.

Bourdieu's most influential claim: schools and other institutions claim to be meritocratic, rewarding talent and effort, but in practice reward forms of cultural capital that are systematically more accessible to children of the already-dominant class. The result: schools reproduce class advantage while presenting the result as the product of individual achievement, which makes the reproduction more durable because it has been legitimated.

Empirical work has substantially supported parts of Bourdieu's framework. Annette Lareau's *Unequal Childhoods* (Ch 5), for example, documents how middle-class American families systematically transmit cultural capital that pays off in school and labor markets. Lauren Rivera's *Pedigree* (2015), on hiring practices at elite professional firms, documents how cultural fit (often a marker of class background) shapes hiring decisions.

### The trade-off — combining frameworks

Most contemporary stratification research integrates multiple frameworks. The Davis-Moore intuition — that some differential rewards are necessary to motivate skill acquisition — captures something real, even as the strong functionalist account does not survive the empirical record. The Marxian insight — that structural relationships of production shape outcomes — captures something real, even as classical Marxism's specific predictions have not held. The Weberian multidimensional framework — that class, status, and political power can diverge — captures real variation. The Bourdieusian framework — that cultural capital reproduces class through ostensibly neutral institutions — captures real mechanisms.

The strongest analyses of specific phenomena draw on multiple frameworks. The U.S. wealth gap is partly about the political economy of capital concentration (Marxian/Piketty); partly about the multidimensional position of different households (Weber); partly about the cultural and institutional mechanisms reproducing class across generations (Bourdieu); and partly about specific historical and policy circumstances that don't fit cleanly into any framework (the post-2008 recovery; specific tax provisions; the long shadow of racial discrimination).

> ### Dig Deeper — Multiple frameworks on one phenomenon
> Pick a specific stratification pattern (the wealth gap; intergenerational mobility; the educational advantage of upper-middle-class children; the rise of the top 0.1%). Ask Claude: "Apply Davis-Moore functionalist, Marxian/conflict-theoretic, Weberian, and Bourdieusian analyses to this pattern. What does each illuminate? Where do they converge?"

### Common misconceptions

**"You have to pick a framework."** You don't. Most contemporary work integrates multiple frameworks pragmatically.

**"Critiquing inequality is the same as advocating revolution."** It isn't. Many serious analysts of inequality (including most contemporary stratification researchers) advocate for various forms of policy reform, not for revolutionary reorganization.

**"Acknowledging structural forces denies individual agency."** Both can be true at once. Individuals make consequential choices within structural constraints; the constraints are real and consequential; individuals also exercise meaningful agency. Treating one as the only relevant variable produces bad analysis.

## Synthesis — reading stratification honestly

A serious sociological reading of inequality holds several things together.

The empirical record is robust. U.S. inequality is high; it has grown; mobility is moderate; race interacts substantially with stratification. These claims are not in serious dispute among researchers, even as they remain contested in popular political discourse.

The theoretical frameworks each illuminate parts of the picture. Pure functionalism, pure Marxism, pure Weberianism, pure Bourdieusianism — each captures something. None captures everything.

The political conclusions don't follow automatically from the empirical and theoretical analysis. Different value commitments produce different policy responses to the same empirical patterns. Sociology can clarify what the patterns are and how they came to be. Sociology cannot, by itself, settle which policies should be adopted.

What sociology can offer the citizen confronting questions of inequality: a clearer view of how the U.S. system actually works, comparative perspective on alternatives, attention to mechanisms that the popular debate often misses (the role of wealth as distinct from income; the place-specific dimensions of mobility; the importance of cultural capital; the structural roles of capital concentration). The student equipped with these tools can evaluate political arguments more carefully and can hold more nuanced views than uninformed intuition would produce.

What I, as the author, conclude: the empirical record makes a strong case that contemporary U.S. inequality is high by historical and international standards, that it is sustained by multiple mechanisms (capital concentration; cultural reproduction; place-based geographies; race-specific dynamics), and that the strong claim "current inequality reflects merit" is hard to defend against the cross-national evidence showing that countries with comparable productivity have substantially lower inequality. What follows from this for policy is a separate question with multiple defensible answers; the empirical analysis can narrow the range of reasonable positions without settling it.

This is my reading. I have tried to present multiple frameworks charitably so you can evaluate the evidence yourself. Reasonable people, working from the same data, reach different policy conclusions. The work of disagreeing well is to know what the strongest version of the other side's position is — what evidence and arguments support it — before evaluating one's own.

## Chapter summary

Social stratification is the systematic, durable division of society into ranked groups. Multiple dimensions (income, wealth, occupation, education) intersect to produce class positions that have substantial consequences for individual lives. The U.S. has high inequality and moderate mobility by international standards; both have grown more pronounced since around 1980. Multiple theoretical frameworks (Davis-Moore functionalism; Marxian/conflict-theoretic accounts; Weber's multidimensional framework; Bourdieu's cultural capital) compete to explain the patterns; the strongest analyses integrate multiple frameworks rather than picking one as orthodoxy. Policy debates about inequality are partly empirical (what mechanisms operate, what works) and partly normative (what level of inequality is just). Sociology can clarify the empirical picture without settling the normative question; both kinds of work are valuable.

## What would change my mind

If a sustained period of cross-national experience showed that high inequality reliably produces better outcomes (faster growth; higher mobility; better well-being) than lower inequality, I would update my reading of the U.S. configuration. The cross-national evidence does not currently support this; if anything, the most-egalitarian advanced economies (Nordic countries) have outcomes generally comparable to or better than the U.S. on most well-being measures, with substantially lower inequality. Strong evidence of an inequality-prosperity tradeoff would shift my view.

## Still puzzling

I do not have a confident view on the long-run effects of digital and AI-driven economic change on inequality. Some scenarios produce dramatically increased inequality (capital substituting for labor across many domains; productivity gains captured narrowly); other scenarios produce broader gains. The empirical evidence so far points in different directions on different dimensions; the next decade will be particularly informative.
