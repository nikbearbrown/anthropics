---

In 1950, approximately 30% of the world's population lived in urban areas. The United Nations Population Division estimates that by 2050, approximately 68% will. The transition from a predominantly rural to a predominantly urban species is one of the most dramatic transformations in human history — comparable in scale to the agricultural revolution or the Industrial Revolution. It has happened in less than a century.

The transformation is not just about where people live. Cities organize life differently than rural settings. They produce different family forms, different employment patterns, different political dynamics, different cultural production, different environmental impacts. The shift to urban majorities means that human social life is increasingly being lived in environments — dense, anonymous, infrastructurally complex — that humans inhabited only marginally for most of our history.

The shift also has environmental implications. Urban populations consume resources differently than rural populations. They produce different waste streams, use different transportation, eat different food. The combined effect of urbanization plus economic growth has been, over the past two centuries, an unprecedented impact on the natural systems that sustain human life. The relationship between human social organization and environmental sustainability has become one of the central questions of the 21st century.

This chapter is about these macro-scale processes — population, urbanization, and the environment — and what sociology contributes to understanding them. The territory is large; the chapter focuses on the social dimensions of demographic change, the patterns and consequences of urbanization, and environmental sociology including climate change.

## Concept 1 — Population dynamics

Several major patterns in global population are worth knowing.

### Global population growth

The world population was approximately 1 billion in 1804, 2 billion in 1927, 4 billion in 1974, and approximately 8 billion in 2022. The rate of growth peaked around 1970 (approximately 2% per year) and has been slowing; current growth is approximately 0.9% per year and projected to slow further.

The growth has been geographically uneven. Most has occurred in developing countries, with population growth in advanced economies slowing dramatically since 1970. Sub-Saharan Africa has the highest current growth rates; East Asia and Europe have the lowest (some are declining).

Total fertility rates have declined in nearly every region. Globally, the rate has fallen from approximately 5 children per woman in 1950 to approximately 2.3 today (close to the global replacement rate of 2.1). The decline has been one of the most consequential demographic shifts of the past century — changing the trajectory of population growth, family forms, and economic development.

### Components of population change

Population in any region grows or shrinks based on three components:

**Fertility** (birth rates). The total fertility rate (average lifetime births per woman) is the standard measure. Above 2.1 produces population growth absent migration and mortality changes; below 2.1 produces population decline absent migration.

**Mortality** (death rates). The crude death rate, age-specific death rates, life expectancy at birth, and other measures track mortality. Mortality has declined globally over the past century, especially child mortality.

**Migration.** Movement of people from one region to another. Migration substantially shapes population change in many countries — boosting populations in receiving countries (the U.S. is one example) and reducing populations in sending countries (parts of Latin America; Eastern Europe; the Philippines).

### Migration

Migration has been one of the largest human movements in recent decades. Hundreds of millions of people live in countries other than where they were born. Major migration patterns include:

- Latin American and Caribbean migration to the U.S. and to Europe
- South Asian migration to the Gulf states for labor; also to Western countries
- Sub-Saharan African migration northward (intra-African and to Europe)
- East Asian migration globally (Chinese diaspora; Filipino workers worldwide)
- Refugee flows from conflict zones (Syria; Afghanistan; Ukraine; many others)
- Climate-related migration (still emerging as a major category)

Migration has substantial consequences for both sending and receiving countries:

**For sending countries.** Loss of working-age population; remittances flowing back from migrants (often economically substantial — for some countries, remittances exceed direct foreign investment); brain drain in some sectors; sustained connection to diaspora communities.

**For receiving countries.** Population growth (substantial in some advanced economies); labor-market effects (varies by skill level and industry); cultural change; political contestation around immigration; substantial economic contributions documented in many studies.

The empirical literature on migration is large; the policy debates are heated. Strong claims in either direction often run ahead of the careful empirical work.

### Population aging

The combination of declining fertility and longer lifespans produces population aging (Ch 13 examined this in more detail). Most advanced economies are aging substantially; many middle-income economies are beginning to age (China is aging rapidly while still at middle-income status); some lower-income economies remain young.

The aging trend has substantial implications for labor markets, pension systems, healthcare, and intergenerational politics. Different countries are at different stages and are responding differently. The pattern will substantially shape global economics and politics in coming decades.

> Pick a country. Ask Claude: "Walk me through this country's demographic trajectory over the past 50 years and the projected trajectory over the next 50. What are the implications for the labor market, family structures, and political life?" Verify against UN Population Division data.

### Common misconceptions

**"Population is exploding."** Growth is slowing globally; some regions are shrinking. Strong "population explosion" framings track 1970s patterns, not contemporary patterns.

**"Migration is at unprecedented levels."** It is at high levels in absolute terms; as a share of global population, it is similar to or below historical levels in some periods. Specific patterns are new (the climate-migration connection; specific corridors); the overall scale is not historically unprecedented.

**"Population growth causes environmental problems."** Population matters; consumption per capita matters more in many cases. The environmental impact of population growth in lower-consumption regions is less than the per-capita impact of population growth or stability in high-consumption regions.

## Concept 2 — Urbanization

The shift from predominantly rural to predominantly urban populations is one of the major social transformations of the modern era.

### The pattern

Globally, urbanization has been continuous over the past 200 years, with substantial acceleration since 1950:

- 1800: approximately 5% urban
- 1900: approximately 13% urban
- 1950: approximately 30% urban
- 2000: approximately 47% urban
- 2024: approximately 58% urban
- 2050 (projected): approximately 68% urban

The most rapid urbanization is currently happening in Africa and parts of Asia. China has urbanized more rapidly than any country in history — approximately 18% urban in 1978 to approximately 65% urban in 2024. The shift has involved hundreds of millions of people moving from rural areas to cities.

### Megacities and urban hierarchy

Modern urbanization has produced megacities (urban areas with populations exceeding 10 million). In 1950, only New York and Tokyo qualified. By 2024, there are approximately 35 megacities globally, most in Asia. Tokyo, Delhi, Shanghai, São Paulo, Mexico City, Cairo, Mumbai, Beijing, Dhaka, Osaka, and many others have populations of tens of millions.

Below the megacities, the global urban hierarchy has many cities of various sizes. Different cities serve different functions (financial centers; political capitals; manufacturing centers; educational hubs; cultural centers); the relationships among them shape global economic and cultural flows.

### U.S. urbanization

The U.S. has gone through several phases of urbanization:

- 19th-century urbanization driven by industrial cities (New York, Chicago, Detroit, Pittsburgh, Cleveland, Buffalo)
- Mid-20th-century suburbanization (after World War II, with substantial federal support through highway construction, mortgage subsidies, and other policies)
- Late-20th-century Sun Belt growth (population shifting south and west)
- 21st-century urban revival in some cities (gentrification of urban cores) alongside continued suburban and exurban growth

The U.S. has approximately 80% of its population in metropolitan areas. The largest metros (New York, Los Angeles, Chicago, Dallas, Houston, Atlanta, etc.) have populations in the millions. Smaller metros and non-metropolitan areas show very different population trajectories — with some growing, others stagnant, others declining.

### Theoretical frameworks on urban life

Several major frameworks have shaped urban sociology.

**The Chicago School.** Sociologists at the University of Chicago in the early 20th century (Robert Park; Ernest Burgess; Louis Wirth; Roderick McKenzie) produced foundational work on urban sociology. The framework treated cities as ecological systems, with different zones (commercial; residential; industrial), specific patterns of succession (one population replacing another in an area), and characteristic urban forms.

Wirth's "Urbanism as a Way of Life" (1938) argued that cities produced specific cultural patterns — anonymity, impersonality, weak primary-group ties, strong instrumental relationships, secular outlook, individualism. The pattern, Wirth argued, was a function of urban size, density, and heterogeneity. Subsequent work has refined the argument; the pattern Wirth identified was real but partial.

**Political economy of urban development.** A different tradition (Henri Lefebvre; David Harvey; Manuel Castells; many others) examines cities as products of capitalist development. Cities are sites of capital accumulation, real-estate speculation, infrastructure investment, and conflicts between different economic interests. Major decisions about urban development (which neighborhoods get investment; what kinds of housing get built; where transportation infrastructure goes) reflect political-economic dynamics rather than purely market-driven outcomes.

The framework illuminates phenomena like gentrification (where rising incomes in specific neighborhoods displace existing residents); the politics of zoning; the relationship between urban development and racial segregation; the post-industrial transformation of older industrial cities.

**Globalization and global cities.** Saskia Sassen's *The Global City* (1991) argued that contemporary globalization has produced a set of "global cities" — New York, London, Tokyo, with later additions including Hong Kong, Singapore, Shanghai, others — that serve as command centers for the global economy. These cities concentrate the highest-paying knowledge work, attract substantial international migration, and produce distinctive forms of urban inequality (high-paid global elite alongside low-paid service workers).

Ask Claude: "Walk me through how this city has changed. What forces drove the transformation? What were the consequences?"

### Common misconceptions

**"Suburbanization in the U.S. was just consumer preference."** It was substantially shaped by federal policies (highway construction; FHA mortgage subsidies that excluded many minorities and urban areas; tax policies favoring homeownership). The "consumer preference" framing obscures the policy context.

**"Cities are inevitably alienating."** Wirth's framework captures real patterns; many city dwellers report dense and meaningful social networks, strong communities, and substantial well-being. The Wirth framework illuminates one dimension of urban life among others.

**"Gentrification is just a question of changing neighborhoods."** It involves displacement of existing residents, changes in commercial composition, sometimes substantial wealth transfers from incumbent residents to newcomers and developers. The dynamics are politically and economically significant.

## Concept 3 — The environment and environmental sociology

The relationship between human society and natural systems is complex and increasingly central to sociological analysis.

### The basic empirical situation

Several environmental patterns have substantial sociological implications:

**Climate change.** Greenhouse-gas emissions from human activities — primarily fossil-fuel combustion, plus deforestation, agriculture, and industrial processes — have raised global average temperatures by approximately 1.1-1.2°C above pre-industrial levels. The trend continues. Projections vary by emissions scenario; current emissions trajectory points toward substantial further warming. The empirical scientific consensus on the human cause of warming is robust.

**Biodiversity loss.** Species are going extinct at rates dramatically higher than background rates. The "sixth extinction" is the term sometimes used — implying that contemporary biodiversity loss is comparable in scale to the major extinction events in geological history.

**Pollution and toxic exposures.** Air pollution, water pollution, and chemical contamination affect billions of people. Air pollution alone is estimated to cause approximately 7 million premature deaths globally per year.

**Resource depletion.** Some resources (fossil fuels; specific minerals; water in many regions; arable land) are being consumed faster than they can be replenished or substituted. The implications vary by resource and region.

### Climate change as a sociological phenomenon

Climate change is centrally a physical phenomenon — atmospheric chemistry, ocean dynamics, ice-sheet behavior. It is also centrally a social phenomenon — produced by human social systems, with consequences mediated through human social systems, addressable through human social systems.

Sociology contributes specifically:

**Causes.** Greenhouse-gas emissions are produced by specific economic systems, specific consumer cultures, specific patterns of land use, specific political economies of fossil-fuel production. Reducing emissions requires changes in social systems, not just technical changes.

**Differential responsibility.** The world's wealthier countries have produced the great majority of historical emissions. Wealthier individuals within any country produce dramatically more emissions than poorer individuals. The distribution of responsibility is highly unequal.

**Differential consequences.** Climate change affects different populations very differently. Lower-income countries and lower-income populations within countries are more vulnerable to climate impacts (less able to adapt, more dependent on climate-sensitive sectors like agriculture, more exposed to specific threats). The gap between who produces climate change and who suffers from it is one of the central justice questions.

**Political dynamics.** Climate change is heavily contested politically. Specific industries (fossil fuels) have strong incentives to delay action; specific economic interests benefit from continued emissions; specific cultural patterns make climate concern more or less salient politically. The political-economic and political-cultural dynamics shape what is feasible.

**Behavioral and cultural change.** Reducing emissions at the scale needed requires substantial changes in how people consume energy, transport themselves, eat, and use land. The cultural and behavioral changes are real and contested.

### Environmental justice

A specific framework that emerged from the U.S. environmental-justice movement of the 1980s. The movement documented systematic patterns: hazardous-waste sites, polluting industries, and environmental contaminants concentrated in low-income and minority communities; access to environmental amenities (parks, clean air, healthy food, safe water) concentrated in wealthier and whiter communities. The framework argued that environmental policy could not be analyzed without attention to the unequal distribution of environmental benefits and burdens.

Specific findings include: Black and Hispanic Americans are exposed to higher levels of air pollution than white Americans, even controlling for income; Native American reservations have been disproportionately targeted for waste disposal; lead-poisoning rates have been substantially higher in poorer neighborhoods (the Flint water crisis being the most prominent recent case); urban heat islands disproportionately affect specific racial-economic neighborhoods.

The framework has informed both research and policy. Federal agencies have adopted environmental-justice mandates with varying enforcement; state and local efforts continue. The empirical patterns are robust; the policy responses have varied.

### Theoretical frameworks in environmental sociology

Several frameworks have shaped the field:

**Treadmill of production** (Allan Schnaiberg). Modern capitalism is structured to require continuous expansion of production, which requires continuous consumption of natural resources, regardless of environmental consequences. The framework analyzes environmental problems as products of specific economic structures.

**Ecological modernization.** A more optimistic framework arguing that economic development, properly directed, can reduce environmental impacts through efficiency improvements, cleaner technologies, and shifting industries. The framework has substantial empirical support for some specific dimensions (efficiency improvements; reduced air pollution in many advanced economies); critics argue it underplays the scale of changes needed.

**Risk society** (Ulrich Beck). Modern societies have produced a new kind of risk — global, technological, often invisible until consequences appear. Climate change, nuclear waste, chemical contamination — these are risks that traditional political and social structures struggle to address.

**Political ecology.** A framework analyzing environmental conflicts as political conflicts — over land, water, resources, environmental quality — with attention to the unequal distribution of power and consequences. Particularly important for understanding environmental conflicts in lower-income countries and for understanding how environmental policy intersects with other social conflicts.

### Common misconceptions

**"Environmental problems are just technical."** They have technical dimensions; they also have political, economic, and cultural dimensions. Reducing them to technical problems often produces policy responses that fail.

**"Climate change is debated scientifically."** The scientific consensus on human-caused climate change is strong. Political contestation around climate is real; the scientific consensus is not seriously in doubt among researchers.

**"Environmental concerns are luxuries for rich countries."** Some environmental concerns are particularly salient in advanced economies; many environmental problems (air pollution; water access; climate adaptation) affect lower-income countries and people more severely than higher-income ones.

## Concept 4 — Connections among population, urbanization, and environment

The three macro-processes of this chapter are interrelated.

**Population and environment.** More people, all else equal, produce more environmental impact. But "all else equal" rarely holds; consumption per capita varies enormously across regions and class positions. Reducing global emissions through population reduction would be slow (demographic momentum) and politically difficult; reducing per-capita emissions in high-consumption populations is feasible but politically contested.

**Urbanization and environment.** Urban populations have specific environmental footprints. Per-capita energy and transportation use is sometimes lower in dense urban areas than in suburban or exurban areas (efficiency from density; less driving). But urban consumption patterns can be more material-intensive than rural patterns. The net effect of urbanization on environmental impact varies by specific urban form.

**Population, urbanization, and inequality.** Demographic change interacts with urbanization in producing specific patterns of inequality. The young populations of African cities will face different opportunities and challenges than the aging populations of European or East Asian cities. Migration patterns shape both sending-region demographics and receiving-region urban dynamics.

**Climate change and urbanization.** Many cities face direct climate-related challenges — sea-level rise (coastal cities); heat waves (heat-island effects); water stress; specific extreme-weather risks. Urban planning and climate adaptation are increasingly intertwined.

> ### Dig Deeper — Connect the three
> Pick a region or country. Ask Claude: "How do population dynamics, urbanization patterns, and environmental conditions interact in this place? What does the analysis suggest about the trajectory and challenges?" Verify against academic and policy sources.

### Common misconceptions

**"These are separate issues."** They interact substantially. Treating them in isolation produces incomplete analysis.

**"There are simple solutions."** There aren't. Each process has substantial momentum; the interactions are complex; trade-offs among different goods are real.

## Synthesis — reading macro-forces sociologically

A serious sociological reading of these macro-processes holds several things together.

The empirical patterns are well-documented. Demographic transition has unfolded across most of the world with substantial variation in pace; urbanization is continuing; environmental impacts (especially climate change) are real and substantial.

The theoretical frameworks each illuminate parts of the picture. Demographic frameworks illuminate population dynamics; urban-sociology frameworks illuminate cities; environmental-sociology frameworks illuminate human-nature relationships. The strongest analyses integrate these.

The political-economic and policy dynamics around these processes are heavily contested. Population policy, immigration policy, urban development policy, environmental policy — all involve substantial political conflict. Sociology can clarify the patterns and consequences without settling the political debates.

The U.S. case has specific distinctive features — among advanced economies, the U.S. has higher fertility (modestly), more substantial immigration, distinct urban patterns (substantial suburbanization; distinct race-and-place dynamics), and exceptional patterns of environmental policy (lower-than-peer commitments to climate action; specific environmental-justice patterns).

The student equipped with this chapter's tools reads contemporary debates about population, immigration, urban policy, and environmental change through structured frameworks that connect these processes to broader social structures. The connections are essential; treating any of these in isolation misses much.

## Chapter summary

Population dynamics, urbanization, and environmental change are macro-scale processes shaping the contexts within which other social phenomena unfold. Global population is approaching 10 billion (with substantial regional variation in trajectories); urbanization has shifted majority of humans to urban settings (with substantial variation by region); environmental impacts (especially climate change) have produced substantial consequences with more to come. Multiple theoretical frameworks (Chicago School urban sociology; political economy of urban development; environmental-justice analysis; treadmill of production; ecological modernization) compete to explain these phenomena; the strongest analyses integrate multiple frameworks. The processes interact substantially; treating them in isolation produces incomplete analysis. Contemporary policy debates around population, immigration, urban development, and environmental change are heavily contested; sociology clarifies the patterns and consequences without settling the political questions.
