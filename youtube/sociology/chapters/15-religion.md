---

In 1965, the year after Vatican II concluded, an American sociologist named Peter Berger published *The Sacred Canopy* — one of the most influential treatments of religion in modern sociology. The book made an argument many sociologists at the time found obvious: as societies modernize, they secularize. Religious institutions lose authority. Religious belief becomes private and individual rather than communal and binding. Religion's ability to provide an overarching framework of meaning — Berger's "sacred canopy" — fragments under the pressure of pluralism, science, and individualism. The trajectory of modern Western societies, Berger argued, was toward a world in which religion would persist as private commitment for some individuals but would no longer be a major institutional force.

Three decades later, Berger publicly revised his view. In a 1999 essay, he wrote: "The world today, with some exceptions ... is as furiously religious as it ever was, and in some places more so than ever." The strong secularization thesis had not held empirically. Modernization had not uniformly produced secularization. Religious vitality had grown in many parts of the world. Pentecostalism had exploded across the global south. Islam had grown and intensified in many societies. Even in the United States — the major exception that classical secularization theory had struggled to explain — religion remained much stronger than the theory predicted.

The empirical complication did not entirely overturn the secularization thesis; specific aspects of secularization were observable, particularly in northern and western Europe, in some segments of American society, and increasingly in East Asia. But the simple "modernization causes religious decline" claim was harder to defend against the global evidence than it had seemed in 1965.

This chapter is about religion as sociology studies it. The territory is large; the empirical patterns are complicated; the theoretical frameworks have been contested. The chapter walks through what we can say with confidence and what remains debated.

## Concept 1 — What religion is and how it varies

Defining religion is harder than it sounds. Different sociologists have drawn the boundary differently, with consequences for what gets analyzed.

### Durkheim's definition

Émile Durkheim's *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* (1912) is the classical sociological definition. Religion, for Durkheim, was "a unified system of beliefs and practices relative to sacred things, that is to say, things set apart and forbidden — beliefs and practices which unite into one single moral community called a Church, all those who adhere to them."

The key features:

- **The sacred-profane distinction.** Religion involves things, places, times, and practices set apart as sacred — not for ordinary use, treated with reverence, surrounded by rituals.
- **Beliefs and practices.** Religion includes both — what people believe and what they do (rituals, worship, practices).
- **Moral community.** Religion is collective. Individual private spirituality, in Durkheim's framework, is not yet religion; religion involves a community of practitioners.

Durkheim's definition has been productive. It applies to traditional religious institutions; it can also be extended to other phenomena that share some features (nationalism with sacred symbols; certain political movements; civic religion).

The definition has limits. Some traditions (some forms of Buddhism; some forms of Confucianism) are more philosophical and less focused on sacred-profane distinctions in Durkheim's strong sense. Some contemporary "spiritual but not religious" practices may have religious features without the strong communal element. The boundary remains contested.

### Substantive vs. functional definitions

Two different approaches to defining religion:

**Substantive definitions** specify what religion is *about* — beliefs in supernatural beings; the sacred; ultimate reality; transcendent forces. Religion is identified by its content.

**Functional definitions** specify what religion *does* — provides meaning; organizes communities; addresses ultimate questions; provides moral framework. Religion is identified by its function.

Substantive definitions usually exclude phenomena that don't involve specifically religious content (nationalism; sports fandom; political ideology). Functional definitions may include phenomena that share religion's functions (Robert Bellah's "civil religion" identifying the U.S.'s national-religious dimension; some analyses of secular ideologies as functioning like religions).

Most working sociologists use both kinds of definition pragmatically, depending on the question. The substantive definition is more useful for studying institutions explicitly recognized as religious; the functional definition is more useful for studying religion's broader social role.

### Major world religions

Brief overview of the major traditions and their sociological characteristics:

**Christianity.** The largest world religion (approximately 2.4 billion adherents). Centered on belief in Jesus Christ as savior. Includes Catholic (the largest single Christian tradition), Protestant (with hundreds of denominations), Orthodox, and many other branches. Geographically dispersed; major growth currently in sub-Saharan Africa and parts of Asia.

**Islam.** Approximately 1.9 billion adherents. Centered on belief in the prophet Muhammad and the revelation of the Qur'an. Includes Sunni (majority globally) and Shia (concentrated in Iran, Iraq, parts of Lebanon and elsewhere). Major demographic expansion through high birth rates in Muslim-majority countries.

**Hinduism.** Approximately 1.2 billion adherents, concentrated in India and surrounding regions and in diaspora communities. A diverse set of traditions sharing some common features (concept of dharma; reincarnation in many traditions; multiple paths to spiritual realization).

**Buddhism.** Approximately 500 million adherents, concentrated in East and Southeast Asia. Includes Theravada (Southeast Asian), Mahayana (East Asian), and Vajrayana (Tibetan) traditions. Diverse forms range from monastic-philosophical to popular-devotional.

**Judaism.** Approximately 15 million adherents globally. The smallest of the major world religions but central to the Abrahamic tradition. Distributed largely in Israel, the U.S., and a smaller European and other diaspora.

**Other.** Sikhism (approximately 30 million); Bahá'í faith; Jainism; Shinto; many indigenous and traditional religious systems.

**The unaffiliated.** A growing global category — approximately 1.2 billion people identify as not belonging to any organized religion. The category includes secular humanists, agnostics, atheists, and people with religious feelings outside organized traditions.

### Forms of religious organization

Sociology has developed typologies for how religious groups organize.

**Church.** A large, established religious organization integrated with broader society. The Catholic Church in many historically Catholic countries is a paradigm case.

**Sect.** A smaller, more intense religious group, often in tension with the surrounding society. Sects may be branches of larger churches or independent groups. Many evangelical Protestant churches in their early forms were sects.

**Denomination.** An organized religious tradition that is one of several recognized options — accepted by society but not exclusive. Most U.S. Protestant churches operate denominationally.

**Cult / new religious movement.** A new religious group, often built around a charismatic founder, with novel beliefs or practices. The term "cult" carries negative connotations in popular discourse; sociologists more often use "new religious movement" as a more neutral term.

The categories are not rigid. Sects sometimes evolve into denominations; new religious movements that survive and grow may become accepted as denominations or churches.

### Common misconceptions

**"All religions believe in God."** Many religions have substantially different conceptions of the divine — multiple gods (some Hindu traditions); a creator God plus many spiritual beings (some indigenous traditions); no creator God in the Western sense (some Buddhist traditions). The blanket "belief in God" framing misreads many religions.

**"Religion is mainly about beliefs."** It is also about practices, communities, identities. In many traditions, what people *do* (ritual, worship, community participation) is more central than what they specifically believe.

**"Religion is one thing."** It is enormously diverse across traditions, within traditions, and across historical periods within traditions.

## Concept 2 — Theoretical frameworks on religion

Several major frameworks have shaped the sociology of religion.

### Durkheim on religion's social function

Durkheim's *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* (1912) is the classical functionalist analysis. Religion, Durkheim argued, performs essential social functions:

- **Social cohesion.** Religion produces shared meaning and shared moral framework that bind communities together. Religious rituals reinforce social solidarity.
- **Collective effervescence.** Religious gatherings produce specific emotional energy that participants experience as transcendent. The energy is real and binds the community.
- **Moral framework.** Religion provides the moral concepts and standards by which a community judges behavior. Even in secularizing societies, the moral framework often retains religious roots.
- **Identity.** Religious membership provides identity and belonging.

Durkheim's analysis was based partly on study of Australian Aboriginal religious life, partly on broader theorizing. The framework has been productive across many subsequent studies. Critics have argued that Durkheim underplayed conflict and power within religious traditions; his framework illuminates cohesion better than it illuminates struggle.

### Marx on religion as ideology

Marx's analysis of religion (across multiple writings) was different. Religion, in Marx's view, was an ideology that legitimized existing social arrangements — including unjust ones. The famous phrase: "Religion is the sigh of the oppressed creature, the heart of a heartless world, and the soul of soulless conditions. It is the opium of the people."

The phrase is more nuanced than its popular use suggests. Marx was acknowledging religion's role in providing comfort to people whose lives were materially hard; he was also noting that this comfort discouraged the revolutionary action that would address the underlying conditions.

The Marxian framework has been productive for studying how religious institutions have legitimized political and economic arrangements — the alliance of throne and altar in pre-modern Europe; the role of religious teachings in supporting slavery in some periods; the contemporary alliance of certain Christian traditions with specific political coalitions.

### Weber on religion and rationalization

Weber's analysis was both substantive and methodological. Methodologically, Weber argued for *Verstehen* — interpretive understanding of what actors mean by their actions — as the appropriate sociological approach to religion. You cannot understand religion if you cannot enter into how it makes sense to its practitioners.

Substantively, Weber's most influential work was *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* (1905). Weber argued that the Calvinist Protestant doctrine of predestination, combined with the worldly asceticism it produced, contributed to the rise of modern capitalism. Specific religious-cultural patterns shaped specific economic outcomes. The argument has been extensively debated; the empirical specifics have been challenged; the deeper move — that culture and ideas have causal weight in economic and social development — remains influential.

Weber's broader framework on religion examined how religious traditions develop and how they relate to the broader pattern of *rationalization* — the historical trend toward calculated, rule-bound, instrumentally-oriented action that Weber saw as the master pattern of Western modernity. Religion was both a contributor to rationalization and a victim of it; its sphere shrank as the rationalized institutions of business, science, and bureaucracy expanded.

### Secularization theory

The dominant 20th-century framework on religious change. Secularization theory in its strong form held that modernization produces religious decline through several mechanisms:

- **Differentiation.** Modern societies separate spheres (economic, political, scientific, religious); religion's institutional reach contracts as other spheres develop their own logics.
- **Privatization.** Religion becomes a private matter; its public role declines.
- **Pluralism.** Multiple religions coexist in modern societies, undermining any single religion's claims to absolute truth.
- **Rationalization.** The growth of scientific, instrumental rationality undermines religious worldviews.

The strong secularization thesis predicted continued religious decline globally as modernization spread. The empirical record has complicated this. Religion has declined sharply in some places (most of Europe; some segments of advanced economies) but has persisted or grown in others (much of the global south; the U.S. especially compared to Europe; many Muslim-majority societies).

Modified versions of secularization theory remain useful. Some specific aspects of secularization (declining religious institutional authority in the public sphere of advanced economies; weakening of religious affiliation among younger cohorts in many places) are real and well-documented. The strong global prediction has been weakened.

### Religious economy theory

Rodney Stark, William Sims Bainbridge, Roger Finke, and others have developed an alternative framework. The argument: religious vitality varies across societies based on the structure of the religious "market." Where religious diversity and competition exist, religion thrives; where religious monopolies or heavy state involvement exist, religion declines. The U.S. (with its religious pluralism and competition) has higher religious participation than European countries (with their established churches or heavy state regulation).

The framework has been productive in explaining cross-national variation. It has also been criticized for treating religion through too-strict an analogy with economic markets, missing dimensions of religious life that don't fit the market model.

### Combining frameworks

Most contemporary sociology of religion draws on multiple frameworks. Religious phenomena have functional dimensions (Durkheim); ideological dimensions (Marx); meaning-making and rationalization dimensions (Weber); aspects of religious-economic competition (Stark and others); aspects of secularization in some places and contexts. The strongest analyses integrate multiple frameworks.

### Common misconceptions

**"Secularization theory is dead."** The strong global version has been weakened. Specific elements (institutional differentiation; the decline of religious authority in many advanced-economy public spheres; the decline of affiliation in many Western countries) remain useful as empirical descriptions.

**"Religion is incompatible with modernity."** The contemporary U.S. is highly modern and highly religious; many other modern societies sustain substantial religious participation. The strong incompatibility claim has not held empirically.

**"Religion is just culture."** Religion is partly cultural; it also has institutional, political, economic, and identity dimensions. Reducing religion to culture misses much.

## Concept 3 — The empirical record of religious change

What do we actually know about religious change in advanced economies and globally?

### Advanced economies

**Western Europe.** Substantial decline in religious participation, affiliation, and belief over the past 60 years. The Netherlands, France, the UK, Sweden, and others have seen church attendance fall sharply; affiliation with no religion has risen substantially. The institutional power of established churches has declined.

**United States.** A more complex picture. Religious affiliation has declined in recent decades — the "nones" (people who don't identify with any religion) have grown from approximately 7% of U.S. adults in 1990 to approximately 28% in 2024. But the U.S. remains substantially more religious than European peers — higher rates of belief in God, higher rates of regular church attendance, more public religiosity. The decline has been concentrated in specific age cohorts (Millennials and Gen Z) and specific religious traditions (mainline Protestantism declined sharply; evangelical Protestantism declined more recently and less; Catholicism has declined; the unaffiliated have grown).

**East Asia.** Variable. Japan and South Korea have substantial unaffiliated populations and modest religious participation; China is officially secular but has substantial folk-religious practice; Taiwan has religious diversity.

**Other.** Australia and Canada have followed European-like patterns of decline. Israel has substantial religious revival in some communities alongside secular majority. Latin American countries have seen substantial Pentecostal growth at the expense of Catholicism.

### Globally

The global picture is one of religious vitality, with substantial regional variation:

**Sub-Saharan Africa.** Major Christian growth (especially Pentecostal); growing Muslim populations; high religious participation across multiple traditions.

**Middle East and North Africa.** Persistent Islamic majority; some growth of Pentecostal Christianity in specific countries; varying patterns of religious revival and political-religious activism.

**South Asia.** Large Hindu population in India; substantial Muslim populations across the region; significant Christian and Sikh minorities; complex relationships among religious traditions and politics.

**East Asia.** Christianity growing in China (with restrictions) and South Korea; religious diversity in Japan; the question of Buddhism's classification (philosophical tradition vs. religion in Western sense) shapes how participation is measured.

**Latin America.** Pentecostal growth has substantially reshaped what was a uniformly Catholic region. Contemporary Latin American religion is much more diverse than fifty years ago.

The Pew Research Center produces detailed comparative data on global religious patterns; the Religious Landscape Study and the World Religion Database are major resources.

### Specific phenomena worth knowing

**Pentecostalism.** Charismatic Christian movement emphasizing direct experience of the Holy Spirit, healing, prosperity. Largest religious growth movement of the 20th and 21st centuries — from a few thousand adherents in 1900 to over 600 million today, concentrated in the global south.

**Evangelical Christianity in the U.S.** Has been politically and culturally influential since the 1970s; aligned closely with Republican politics; experiencing complicated dynamics in the 21st century with shifting demographics and contested directions.

**Islamic revival movements.** Various movements across Muslim-majority countries since the 1970s have intensified religious practice and political activism. The diversity within "Islamic revival" is enormous; treating it as a single phenomenon misses important variation.

**The "nones" and their varieties.** The growing religiously-unaffiliated population includes atheists, agnostics, and people who say they are "spiritual but not religious." The category is internally diverse; different sociological dynamics may produce different parts of it.

**New religious movements.** Specific movements have produced both substantial growth (Mormonism, Scientology, certain Buddhist movements) and dramatic conflicts with broader society (Branch Davidians; Heaven's Gate; Aum Shinrikyo).

### Common misconceptions

**"The world is becoming less religious."** Globally, religious populations are growing in absolute terms. The unaffiliated category is growing as a share in some places and as an absolute number, but most of the world remains religious.

**"All decline in advanced economies looks similar."** Different countries have followed different trajectories. The U.S. is exceptional; Europe is more uniformly secularizing; East Asia has its own patterns.

**"Religious revival means fundamentalism."** Some religious revival has fundamentalist dimensions; much does not. The two are distinct phenomena.

## Synthesis — reading religion sociologically

A serious sociological reading of religion holds several things together.

Religion is empirically diverse and persistent. The strong "secularization is the future" prediction has been weakened by global evidence. Religion remains a major institutional force in most of the world, with substantial variation in form and trajectory.

The major theoretical frameworks each illuminate parts of the picture. Durkheim's functionalism captures real social functions of religion; Marx's analysis captures real ideological dimensions; Weber's framework captures the relationship between religious and economic-rationalization patterns; secularization theory captures real changes in advanced economies; religious-economy theory captures the dynamics of religious markets. The strongest analyses integrate multiple frameworks.

The empirical patterns are complicated and not adequately captured by simple narratives in any direction. Some regions are secularizing; others are religiously vital; others are seeing religious change without unidirectional secularization or revival.

The political and cultural debates about religion are often heated and often run ahead of careful empirical analysis. Religious people are sometimes caricatured by secular observers; secular people are sometimes caricatured by religious observers. Charitable analysis on all sides is rare and valuable.

The student equipped with this chapter's tools reads contemporary religious phenomena — the rise of evangelical political activism, the growth of Pentecostalism, the secularization of Europe, the persistence of Islam, the dynamics within American religious traditions — through structured frameworks. They can articulate what is genuinely contested, what is empirically settled, and where their own normative commitments are contributing to their reading.

## Chapter summary

Religion is a near-universal feature of human societies, with enormous variation in form across cultures and dramatic change in advanced economies over the past 60 years. The strong secularization thesis has been weakened by global evidence; modified versions remain useful for specific contexts. Multiple theoretical frameworks (Durkheim's functionalism; Marx's ideology critique; Weber's rationalization analysis; secularization theory; religious-economy theory) compete; the strongest analyses integrate multiple frameworks. The empirical record is complicated — religion declining in some places, growing in others, changing in form everywhere. Sociology offers structured ways to think about questions where careful work narrows reasonable positions without settling normative debates about religion's role in public life.

## What would change my mind

If a sustained period of global secularization produced clear and measurable religious decline across most regions — including those currently religious — I would update my reading of religion's persistence. The current evidence supports religious vitality globally with regional variation; substantial uniform decline would be a significant departure from current trends.

## Still puzzling

I do not have a confident view on the long-run trajectory of religion in the United States. The past 30 years have seen substantial decline in some specific dimensions (mainline Protestant decline; the rise of nones); other dimensions remain robust by international comparison. Whether the U.S. will move toward European-pattern secularization, maintain its current configuration, or see new forms of religious life emerge is genuinely uncertain.
