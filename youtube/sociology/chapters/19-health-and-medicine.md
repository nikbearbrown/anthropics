How long people live, what diseases they get, what care they receive, and what counts as illness in the first place are shaped by social structures (class, race, gender, geography) and by the institutional organization of medicine. ---

In 2014, an economist named Anne Case and her husband Angus Deaton, both at Princeton, noticed something striking in U.S. mortality data. Among middle-aged white Americans without college degrees, mortality rates had been *rising* since around 2000 — driven by suicide, alcohol-related liver disease, and drug overdoses. Case and Deaton called these "deaths of despair." Across nearly every other demographic group in the U.S. and across nearly every comparable advanced economy, mortality rates had continued their long-run decline. Among this specific demographic, they had reversed.

The finding was striking because mortality reversals at the population level are rare. Modern advanced economies had not seen anything like this since wartime or major disease outbreaks. Yet here it was, hidden in the data, affecting millions of people in specific communities. By the time Case and Deaton's work appeared in 2015 and after, it had transformed how American observers thought about health, work, community, and inequality.

Their explanation: the long-term economic and community decline experienced by non-college whites — the loss of manufacturing employment from Chapter 18; the weakening of religious congregations and other community institutions; family disruption; the broader sense of decline in specific places — was producing a social health crisis. The "despair" of the term was not metaphorical; the deaths reflected social conditions, not just biological ones.

The Case and Deaton finding illustrates a broader theme: health is not just a matter of individual biology and individual choice. It is shaped substantially by the social conditions in which people live. The richer the social-determinants-of-health literature has become, the clearer this has been. Where you live, what you do for work, what your relationships look like, what your stress levels are, what your access to care is — all shape how long you live and how healthy you are.

This chapter is about health and medicine as sociology studies them. The territory is large; the chapter focuses on the social determinants of health, the institutional organization of medicine, and the comparative analysis of healthcare systems.

## Concept 1 — The social determinants of health

A foundational sociological insight: health and longevity are not just functions of individual biology and individual choice. They are systematically shaped by social conditions.

### The social gradient

A robust finding across many studies and many countries: health outcomes track social position closely. Wealthier people live longer, in better health, with fewer chronic diseases, than poorer people. The gradient is not just at the bottom (the very poor) — it operates across the entire social distribution. Middle-class people live longer than working-class people; upper-middle-class people live longer than middle-class; the highest-income groups have the best outcomes.

The Whitehall studies of British civil servants (Michael Marmot and colleagues, beginning in the 1960s) documented this powerfully. Higher-ranking civil servants — even controlling for many specific behaviors and exposures — lived longer than lower-ranking civil servants. The gradient operated across the full hierarchy, not just at the extremes. Subsequent work has confirmed similar gradients across many populations.

In the U.S., the social gradient is pronounced. The richest 1% of American men live approximately 15 years longer than the poorest 1% of American men; for women, the gap is approximately 10 years. The gap has widened since 2000 — life expectancy has fallen for some lower-income groups while continuing to rise for higher-income groups.

### Mechanisms

What produces the social gradient? Multiple mechanisms operate:

**Material conditions.** Lower-income people have less access to healthy food, safe housing, clean environments, regular preventive care. The material conditions of life shape health.

**Behavioral patterns.** Smoking, alcohol use, exercise, diet — all vary by social class in ways that affect health outcomes. The behaviors are partly individual choices and partly shaped by social conditions (stress; available options; cultural patterns).

**Stress and "weathering."** Chronic stress, especially the stress of social subordination, has measurable physiological effects (elevated cortisol; cardiovascular strain; immune-system effects). Arline Geronimus's "weathering hypothesis" argues that the chronic stress of being marginalized — particularly for Black Americans facing racism — produces accelerated biological aging that contributes to health disparities.

**Healthcare access.** Lower-income people have less access to high-quality healthcare, both for prevention and for treatment of acute conditions. The gap shapes outcomes.

**Place-based exposures.** Lower-income people are more likely to live in areas with environmental contaminants, polluted air, contaminated water, less green space. Geographic factors shape health.

**Networks and social support.** Stronger social networks correlate with better health outcomes; lower-income people often have weaker network resources to draw on, especially in mobility-disrupted communities.

The mechanisms interact. Material conditions affect stress, which affects behavior; healthcare access affects outcomes; place-based exposures interact with all of these. The cumulative pattern is the social gradient.

### Race-specific patterns

Racial gradients in health, in the U.S. and many other societies, are pronounced and not fully explained by class differences alone (Ch 11 covered some of this).

Black Americans have approximately 4 years lower life expectancy than white Americans, with substantial variation by class within each group. The Black-white gap in infant mortality (approximately 2.3 times higher for Black infants) persists across all class levels. Chronic disease patterns (cardiovascular disease; diabetes; specific cancers) show substantial racial gradients.

The mechanisms include those discussed for class (with race-specific patterns of exposure) plus specific patterns of differential treatment in healthcare (documented in many studies — Black patients receive less aggressive treatment for some conditions; physicians underestimate their pain; specialty consultations come less frequently). The combination of structural and interpersonal factors produces the racial gradient.

### Gendered patterns

Women have higher life expectancy than men in nearly all societies. The gap has narrowed in some societies in recent decades; it remains substantial. The mechanisms include behavioral differences (men engage in more risky behaviors; suffer more from suicide, accidents, violence); biological factors (women have some biological advantages on certain diseases); social factors (men's social networks tend to be smaller; their health-seeking behavior differs).

Women, however, often have higher morbidity (more illness, even with longer lifespans) — a pattern sometimes summarized as "women get sicker but men die quicker." The pattern reflects various factors: women have higher rates of some chronic diseases; women's symptoms have historically been less attended to in medical practice; gendered patterns of stress and illness differ.

### Common misconceptions

**"Health is just about individual behavior."** Behavior matters; behavior is also shaped by social conditions. The strong individual-responsibility framing misses substantial mechanisms.

**"More healthcare access would close all gaps."** Healthcare access is one factor; the social gradient persists in many societies with universal healthcare access (though typically smaller than in the U.S.). Healthcare alone doesn't fully address social-determinants-of-health patterns.

**"The U.S. health disadvantage is just about insurance."** The U.S. has lower life expectancy than many advanced economies despite spending more on healthcare per capita. The pattern reflects multiple factors — specific healthcare-system features; broader social conditions; specific health behaviors; specific patterns of injury and chronic disease.

## Concept 2 — Theoretical frameworks on health and medicine

Several major frameworks shape sociological analysis of health and medicine.

### The social construction of illness

A foundational sociological insight: what counts as "illness" is partly socially constructed. The biological substrate may be real (specific physiological conditions exist), but the categories used to identify, name, treat, and respond to those conditions are shaped by social processes.

Examples of the construction:

**Homosexuality.** Classified as a mental illness in the DSM (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders) until 1973. The reclassification reflected substantial social and political change, not just new scientific evidence about the underlying phenomenon.

**ADHD.** Diagnosis rates have grown dramatically since 1990. Whether the underlying condition has actually become more common, or whether diagnostic practices have changed, or whether broader cultural patterns have produced more recognition of existing patterns, is contested.

**Hysteria.** A common diagnosis in 19th-century medicine, applied almost exclusively to women, encompassing many specific symptoms. The diagnosis no longer exists in modern medicine; what was called hysteria has been reclassified into specific other conditions.

**Anorexia nervosa.** Recognized as a specific condition only in the late 19th century; with rates rising substantially in advanced economies in the late 20th century. The interaction of cultural patterns, individual psychology, and social pressures produces the condition.

**Pre-disease conditions.** Pre-diabetes, pre-hypertension, mild cognitive impairment — categories created in the past 30 years that medicalize states that previously were considered normal. The categories serve some legitimate purposes (early intervention) and some less legitimate (expanding the market for pharmaceutical treatment).

The framework illuminates how illness categories are produced through complex processes involving medical-scientific work, cultural patterns, professional interests, and pharmaceutical economics. Recognizing this is not the same as denying that real biological conditions exist; it is recognizing that the categories used to describe and treat them are social products.

### Medicalization

A specific aspect of the social construction of illness: *medicalization* is the process by which conditions previously considered non-medical come to be defined and treated as medical conditions.

Peter Conrad's *The Medicalization of Society* (2007) is a major synthesis. Conrad documented how many specific conditions had been medicalized over the previous decades — childbirth (increasingly managed as a medical event); ADHD (becoming a medical diagnosis with pharmaceutical treatment); aging-related changes (now subject to medical intervention); sexual dysfunction (treated with pharmaceuticals); even normal experiences (sadness; shyness; ordinary worry) increasingly framed in medical-pharmaceutical terms.

Medicalization has costs and benefits. Costs include over-treatment, side-effects of pharmaceuticals, the economic costs of treating conditions, the loss of meaning when human experiences are recategorized as medical problems. Benefits include real reduction of suffering for some conditions, professional accountability for treatment, medical insurance coverage for previously unrecognized problems.

The process is shaped by multiple actors — physicians and medical organizations seeking to expand their professional jurisdiction; pharmaceutical companies seeking markets; patients seeking recognition for their conditions; insurers seeking to define what they will and won't cover. Different specific conditions have followed different medicalization trajectories.

### Foucault on biopower

Michel Foucault's *The Birth of the Clinic* (1963) and subsequent work developed an influential analytical framework. Foucault argued that modern medicine is a specific historical formation — emerging in the late 18th and 19th centuries — that constitutes a new kind of power over bodies and populations.

*Biopower*, in Foucault's framework, is power that operates through the management of populations — through public health surveillance, statistical analysis of health and disease, regulatory frameworks for living conditions, increasingly detailed knowledge of individual bodies. Modern medicine doesn't just treat sick individuals; it produces detailed knowledge of normal populations, identifies deviations, and intervenes to manage outcomes at the population scale.

The framework illuminates phenomena like public health surveillance; the COVID-19 pandemic response; the relationship between individual medicine and population statistics; the role of medical knowledge in regulating various aspects of life (nutrition recommendations; exercise guidelines; reproductive health). The framework has been productive across many subsequent studies of medicine, public health, and the regulation of bodies.

### Political economy of healthcare

A different framework: medicine and healthcare as institutions shaped by economic and political forces. Different societies organize healthcare differently, with different consequences for outcomes, costs, and equity. The U.S. system — with its mix of employer-provided insurance, public programs (Medicare; Medicaid), and private payment — is exceptional internationally in its complexity, costs, and outcomes.

The political-economy framework analyzes:

- How healthcare-system features produce specific patterns of outcomes
- The role of specific actors (insurance companies; pharmaceutical companies; physician groups; hospital systems; the state) in shaping healthcare
- The political dynamics of healthcare reform
- The international variation in approaches and their consequences

Major scholars in this tradition include Theda Skocpol, Jacob Hacker, Paul Starr, and many others.

> ### Dig Deeper — A specific medicalization
> Pick a condition that has been medicalized in recent decades (ADHD; gender dysphoria; chronic pain; obesity; mild depression; aging-related changes). Ask Claude: "Walk me through the trajectory of this condition's medicalization. What were the key events? Which actors drove it? What are the costs and benefits of the medicalization?"

### Common misconceptions

**"Sociologists deny that illness is real."** They do not. The framework recognizes that biological conditions are real; the categories used to identify and treat them are social products. Both parts matter.

**"Medicalization is just bad."** It has produced real benefits as well as costs. Distinguishing the productive from the problematic medicalization is the analytical work.

**"Foucault was just postmodernist obscurantism."** Foucault's empirical historical work is rigorous; his theoretical framework has been productive across many subsequent studies. Dismissive characterizations miss what the framework actually does.

## Concept 3 — The U.S. healthcare system in comparative perspective

The U.S. healthcare system is internationally exceptional in specific ways worth understanding.

### Spending and outcomes

The U.S. spends approximately 17-18% of GDP on healthcare in 2024, compared with approximately 11-12% in most other advanced economies. The U.S. spends approximately 50-100% more per capita than peer economies.

Despite this spending, U.S. health outcomes lag many peer economies on common measures:

- Life expectancy at birth is approximately 77 years in the U.S., compared with approximately 82-84 in Japan, Switzerland, Australia, and several European economies.
- Infant mortality is higher in the U.S. than in many peer economies.
- Maternal mortality has risen in the U.S. while declining in most peer economies.
- The U.S. has higher rates of chronic disease burden among adults.

Why the spending-outcomes gap? Multiple factors:

- Higher prices in the U.S. for the same services and pharmaceuticals (a major component of the spending excess)
- Less universal coverage producing worse access for some
- Different patterns of disease and injury (higher U.S. rates of obesity, gun deaths, drug overdoses)
- Less spending on social-determinants-of-health interventions in the U.S. relative to medical interventions

### The structure of U.S. healthcare

The U.S. has a multi-payer mixed system:

**Employer-provided private insurance** covers approximately 50-55% of Americans. Employers pay premiums (often deducted partly from employee compensation); employees may have copays and deductibles; coverage varies enormously by employer.

**Medicare** covers most Americans 65+ and some younger Americans with disabilities or specific conditions. Publicly funded; multi-part program with various coverage components.

**Medicaid** covers low-income Americans through joint federal-state programs. Coverage varies by state because states have substantial discretion in eligibility and benefits.

**Individual insurance markets** (including those established by the Affordable Care Act in 2010) cover those without employer or government-program coverage.

**Uninsured** Americans — the share has varied by year and by political administration; approximately 8-10% in recent years.

The system produces specific patterns: complex administrative costs (insurance billing, pre-authorizations, claims processing); inequality in access (depending on employer, geography, ACA-eligibility, etc.); high prices (sustained partly by limited competition and limited price regulation); disconnection of healthcare from employment when employment changes ("job lock").

### Major alternative models

**Single-payer.** A single insurance pool, usually government-administered, covers all citizens. Examples: the UK's National Health Service (NHS); Canada's provincial single-payer systems. Generally produces broader coverage at lower cost than the U.S. system; some access concerns (waiting times for non-urgent procedures); broad political support in countries that have implemented it.

**Multi-payer with universal coverage.** Multiple insurers (often non-profit or regulated) cover all citizens, often through employer or self-purchased plans, with strong regulation ensuring universal coverage and limited price variation. Examples: Germany's social health insurance system; Switzerland's regulated multi-payer system. Produces universal coverage with somewhat higher administrative costs than single-payer but with more individual choice.

**National health service.** State-owned and operated healthcare delivery with state-employed physicians and state-owned hospitals. The UK NHS is the canonical case. Generally produces universal coverage at lower cost; some quality and access concerns in different periods.

These models trade off different things: single-payer reduces administrative costs but reduces individual choice; multi-payer with universal coverage allows more choice but is more expensive; national health service may produce more efficient pricing but may be slow to innovate.

### Contemporary U.S. debates

Several major debates:

**Insurance coverage.** Should the U.S. move toward universal coverage? If so, through what mechanism (Medicare for All; public option; ACA expansion; state-level reforms)? The empirical evidence on each option is contested; the political coalitions vary.

**Pharmaceutical pricing.** U.S. pharmaceutical prices are the highest in the world by substantial margins. Should the federal government negotiate drug prices? (Recent legislation has begun limited negotiation for Medicare; the broader question remains contested.)

**Mental health access.** Mental healthcare access is more limited than physical healthcare access in many U.S. settings; the gap has worsened during the COVID-19 pandemic and after; reform efforts continue.

**The opioid crisis.** Beginning in the late 1990s and intensifying since, the opioid overdose crisis has killed hundreds of thousands of Americans. The crisis involves pharmaceutical-industry behavior, regulatory failures, social conditions producing demand, and ongoing policy failures.

**Long-term and elderly care.** As the population ages (Ch 13), the demand for long-term care has grown substantially. The U.S. has limited public coverage of long-term care; most is paid privately or through Medicaid (after spending down assets). Reform proposals exist; political support has been limited.

> ### Dig Deeper — Compare the U.S. and another country
> Pick another advanced economy (the UK; Germany; France; Canada; Sweden; Japan; Australia; Switzerland). Ask Claude: "How does this country's healthcare system differ from the U.S.? What outcomes does it produce? What are the strengths and weaknesses?" Verify against OECD health data and academic comparative work.

### Common misconceptions

**"U.S. healthcare is the best in the world."** It produces some excellent outcomes for some patients with some conditions; on broad population health measures, it lags many peer economies despite higher spending. Strong claims of overall superiority are not supported by the data.

**"All countries with universal coverage have rationing."** Some have specific waiting times for specific procedures; the U.S. has different forms of rationing (by ability to pay, by employer-provided insurance access). All systems ration in some way; the question is how.

**"Single-payer is impossible in the U.S."** It is politically difficult; not impossible. Several states (Vermont; California; others) have considered single-payer reforms; the political and economic challenges are real but not insurmountable.

## Concept 4 — Specific contemporary health phenomena

Several specific contemporary phenomena deserve sociological analysis.

### The opioid crisis

A specific U.S. health crisis worth understanding. Beginning in the late 1990s, prescription opioid use expanded dramatically — driven partly by aggressive pharmaceutical-company marketing (especially Purdue Pharma's promotion of OxyContin), partly by changing pain-management practices, partly by social conditions producing demand. By the mid-2010s, transition to heroin and synthetic opioids (especially fentanyl) had produced a wave of overdose deaths.

The crisis killed approximately 500,000 Americans through overdose between 1999 and 2020, with the toll continuing to rise. The deaths were concentrated in specific demographic and geographic groups — middle-aged white Americans without college degrees in the Rust Belt and Appalachian regions especially affected initially, with subsequent waves affecting other groups.

The Case-Deaton "deaths of despair" framework partially captures the crisis. So does the political-economy framework analyzing pharmaceutical-industry behavior; so does analysis of regulatory failures (the FDA's role; the DEA's tracking of distribution); so does analysis of broader social conditions producing demand.

### The COVID-19 pandemic

The 2020-2023 pandemic killed approximately 1.2 million Americans and many millions globally. The pandemic illustrated multiple sociological patterns:

- Class and racial disparities in mortality (lower-income workers and minorities had higher exposure to infection and worse outcomes)
- The role of place and occupation (essential workers; high-density living; specific industries had different exposures)
- Healthcare-system stress (different countries' healthcare systems performed differently)
- Political polarization (vaccination rates and behavioral compliance varied substantially by political affiliation)
- Long-term effects (long COVID; mental-health effects; educational disruption)

The pandemic was a natural experiment in many dimensions; sociological work analyzing it continues.

### Mental health

Mental health has become a more central concern in contemporary U.S. discourse. Adolescent mental health problems have risen substantially since around 2012 (Ch 8 examined the contested debate about smartphones and platforms as drivers). Adult depression, anxiety, and suicide rates have shifted in concerning ways. The COVID-19 pandemic produced substantial mental-health effects.

The empirical patterns are real; the explanations are contested; the policy responses have been limited relative to the scale of the problem.

### Reproductive health

A heavily contested area. Maternal mortality rates have risen in the U.S. while declining in most peer economies. The 2022 Dobbs decision overturning Roe v. Wade has produced substantial state-level variation in access to abortion. Contraception, fertility treatment, and reproductive autonomy are subjects of ongoing legal and political debate. The health implications of policy choices in this area are substantial.

### Common misconceptions

**"Health crises are just public-health problems."** They are public-health problems; they are also social problems shaped by economic, political, and cultural factors. Reducing them to public-health problems misses important dimensions.

**"More medical intervention always improves health."** Sometimes; sometimes not. Some forms of medicalization produce iatrogenic effects (harms caused by medical intervention); some interventions have been shown to produce more harm than benefit on average. Calibrating intervention is harder than the popular framing suggests.

## Synthesis — reading health and medicine sociologically

A serious sociological reading of contemporary health and medicine holds several things together.

The biological reality is real. Bodies get sick; treatments work; medical knowledge has produced enormous gains in human health and longevity over the past century.

The social dimensions are real. Health outcomes track social position systematically; medical categories are partly socially constructed; healthcare systems are political-economic institutions shaped by specific historical choices.

The U.S. case is internationally exceptional in specific ways — higher spending, lower outcomes than peer economies, persistent inequalities by class and race, specific gaps in coverage and care.

Multiple theoretical frameworks each illuminate parts of the picture. Social-determinants frameworks; social-construction-of-illness analyses; medicalization theory; Foucauldian biopower; political-economy frameworks — each captures real patterns. The strongest analyses integrate multiple frameworks.

The policy debates are partly empirical and partly normative. Different value commitments produce different policy preferences. Sociology can clarify the patterns and consequences of different choices without settling the value questions.

The student equipped with this chapter's tools reads contemporary health debates — about coverage, about pharmaceutical pricing, about mental health, about pandemic response, about end-of-life care — through structured frameworks that recognize health as both a biological and social phenomenon. The recognition is the foundation of thoughtful engagement with one of the most contested areas of contemporary policy.

## Chapter summary

Health is shaped by biological, behavioral, and social factors. The social gradient in health — the systematic relationship between social position and health outcomes — is one of the most robust findings in the literature. Multiple theoretical frameworks (social-determinants; social construction of illness; medicalization; Foucault's biopower; political economy) compete to explain health phenomena; the strongest analyses integrate multiple frameworks. The U.S. healthcare system is internationally exceptional in cost, coverage, and outcomes; comparative analysis with other advanced economies reveals trade-offs of different institutional designs. Specific contemporary health crises (opioids; pandemic; mental health; reproductive health) require sociological analysis that integrates structural, institutional, and individual dimensions. The serious student of health holds biological, social, and political-economic perspectives together.

## What would change my mind

If sustained reform of the U.S. healthcare system produced rapid convergence with peer-economy outcomes — substantial improvement in life expectancy, infant mortality, chronic disease outcomes — I would update my reading of how durable the current configuration is. The current evidence suggests deep structural and political-economic forces sustain the U.S. pattern; substantial change would require significant reform.

## Still puzzling

I do not have a confident view on whether the U.S. life-expectancy decline since around 2014 (worsened by the COVID-19 pandemic) reflects a temporary disruption or a more durable change in long-run U.S. health trajectories. Sustained reversal of the long-run progress would be historically distinctive; recovery to pre-2014 patterns is also possible. The next decade will be informative.
