**TL;DR.** Society — the totality of social relationships within a defined community — gets produced and reproduced one interaction at a time. Macro-sociology studies the pattern across millions of encounters; micro-sociology studies what happens inside individual encounters. Both approaches are necessary; neither can substitute for the other. Goffman's dramaturgical analysis showed how much sociological work is hidden inside the small details of how people present themselves to each other.

---

In 1955, a 33-year-old sociologist named Erving Goffman finished his PhD dissertation. He had spent eighteen months on the Shetland Islands, observing a small community of crofters and their interactions with mainland visitors. The dissertation was eventually published as *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life* (1959).

The book opened with a question that no one had quite asked before: what does it mean to *put on a show* of being a particular kind of person?

Goffman's answer was that this is what social interaction *is*. When you walk into a job interview, you are performing being a competent professional. When you sit down to dinner with your in-laws, you are performing being a good son-in-law. When you stand in line at the grocery store, you are performing being a normal customer. The performances are not insincere — most of the time, you really are the things you are performing — but they are performances, with stages, props, costumes, audiences, scripts, and skills. Society happens when actors successfully perform recognizable roles to audiences who recognize and respond.

The argument was disorienting because it took something invisible — the work of being a person in front of other people — and made it visible. Once you have read Goffman, you cannot un-see the performances. You notice when someone is doing them well or badly, what props they are using, what audience they are playing to, what happens when the performance falls apart. The world becomes a complex stage with everyone simultaneously performing and watching.

This chapter is about that level of social life — what sociologists call the *micro* level, the level of face-to-face (and increasingly, screen-to-screen) interaction. It is also about how the micro level connects to the *macro* — the large-scale patterns of social life that emerge from millions of micro-interactions. Both levels are real; both are sociology; both require their own methods and frameworks. The chapter introduces the major concepts and shows how the levels connect.

## Concept 1 — Levels of analysis

Sociology operates at multiple levels of analysis, with different methods, different theoretical frameworks, and different kinds of findings at each.

**Micro-sociology** studies face-to-face (and screen-mediated) interaction — what happens when people are actually with each other. The unit of analysis is the encounter, the interaction, the conversation. Methods: ethnography, conversation analysis, ethnomethodological breaching experiments, in-depth interviews about specific interactions.

**Macro-sociology** studies large-scale social patterns — institutions, populations, structural inequalities, historical change. The unit of analysis is the society, the institution, the demographic group. Methods: surveys, administrative-data analysis, historical-comparative work, computational social science.

**Meso-sociology** studies the middle range — organizations, networks, communities — that mediate between micro-interactions and macro-structures. The unit of analysis is the firm, the neighborhood, the social network, the social movement organization. Methods: organizational ethnography, network analysis, comparative case studies.

These levels are not separate sociologies. They are different angles on the same social reality. A complete account of any phenomenon usually requires at least two levels. The Black-white wage gap (Chapter 11) is a macro-level pattern; it is produced through meso-level mechanisms (organizational hiring practices, neighborhood-level network effects); and it is enacted in micro-level interactions (specific job interviews, specific conversations between supervisors and workers). The macro pattern cannot be explained without the meso and micro mechanisms; the micro and meso processes cannot be understood without the macro context that frames them.

### The trade-off — what each level captures and misses

Macro-level work captures patterns that no individual could see — the trajectory of an entire population's marriage rates, the long-run shift in demographic structure, the cross-society variation in inequality. The cost: it can render lived experience invisible. The 30-year decline in marriage rates among Americans without college degrees (Cherlin, Edin and Kefalas, others) is real and important; the macro number does not tell you what marriage means to specific people who are or aren't entering it.

Micro-level work captures meaning, texture, and process that no statistic can. The cost: limited generalizability. A study of one classroom, one prison, one workplace tells you about that setting in detail; whether the patterns extend to other settings is a separate question.

Meso-level work tries to bridge the gap. Network analysis of how information flows through communities; organizational ethnography of how a specific firm produces specific outcomes; case-study research that compares similar organizations under different conditions. The bridge is genuinely useful when done well; it can also fall between the strengths of the levels it bridges.

The serious sociologist matches level to question and recognizes when a question requires multiple levels.

> ### Dig Deeper — Three levels on one question
> Pick a sociological question — say, why some neighborhoods have higher crime rates than others, or why some workplaces have more cooperative cultures, or why marriage rates have declined. Ask Claude: "What does macro-level (national/aggregate), meso-level (neighborhood/organizational), and micro-level (interactional) analysis each contribute? Where do they converge, and where do they need to be combined?"

### Common misconceptions

**"Macro is real; micro is just stories."** Both levels capture real social phenomena. Macro patterns are real; the interactional processes producing them are also real. Reducing micro to "anecdote" misreads what micro-sociology actually does.

**"Micro is real; macro is just abstraction."** Macro patterns are not less real for being aggregations. The fact that median wealth differs across racial groups is a real fact about the society, even though it lives only at the population level.

**"You should pick a level and stick with it."** The interesting work often crosses levels. Picking a level dogmatically produces narrower research.

## Concept 2 — Goffman's dramaturgical framework

Erving Goffman's *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life* (1959) gave sociology a vocabulary for analyzing face-to-face interaction that has been productive for over six decades. The framework treats social interaction as theatrical performance.

**Front stage.** The setting where the performance is given to an audience. The waiter at the table; the doctor in the consultation room; the professor at the lectern; the politician at the podium; the social-media user posting publicly. The performer monitors what they are doing and shapes it for the audience's reception.

**Back stage.** The setting where the performance is prepared, where actors can step out of role, rehearse, complain about the audience, repair their costumes. The kitchen behind the restaurant dining room; the doctor's lounge between patients; the teachers' lounge during class; the green room before a public appearance. The strict separation of front stage and back stage is one of the most consequential features of organized social life.

**Performance team.** A group of actors collaborating on a single performance. The waitstaff at a restaurant performing "competent service" together. The medical team performing "professional care." The family performing "respectable hosts" when guests are over. Team performances require coordination — what Goffman called *dramaturgical loyalty* (don't break character) and *dramaturgical discipline* (recover quickly when something goes wrong).

**Impression management.** The work of monitoring and shaping the impression you give to others. Choosing what to wear, what to say, how to stand, what facts to disclose, what to keep hidden. This is not a skill some people have and others don't; it is a continuous feature of social life that everyone does, with varying levels of skill and effort.

**Props and setting.** The physical objects and environments that support the performance. The doctor's stethoscope, the professor's books, the politician's flag backdrop. Goffman observed that the right props are not just optional decorations; they are constitutive of the performance. A doctor without a stethoscope, a professor without books, a politician without a flag backdrop is performing a different role.

### Key extensions

Goffman's framework expanded across his career.

*Asylums* (1961) studied total institutions — settings (prisons, mental hospitals, military boot camps, monasteries) where the normal separation of front and back stage collapses. In a total institution, the inmate is on display continuously; there is no place to step out of role. Goffman's analysis of how inmates carve out hidden spaces for selfhood within total institutions remains influential.

*Stigma* (1963) studied how people handle attributes that disqualify them from full social acceptance — physical disability, mental illness, criminal records, marginalized racial or sexual identities. Goffman distinguished *discredited* stigma (visible) from *discreditable* stigma (hidden but exposable) and analyzed the strategies people use to manage each.

*Frame Analysis* (1974) generalized the framework. Goffman argued that all social interaction operates within "frames" — interpretive structures that tell participants what kind of activity they are in. The same physical event can be a real fight, a play fight, a fight in a movie, a fight in a dream — different frames, with very different consequences.

### The trade-off — what dramaturgy illuminates and misses

The framework's strength is its capacity to make the invisible work of social life visible. Once you have it, you can analyze interactions in unprecedented detail.

The framework's limit is that it can underplay structural constraints. Not everyone has equal access to the props, settings, and skills that produce credible performances. A homeless person performing "competent member of society" has different resources to draw on than a wealthy person performing the same. The dramaturgical framework names the work but is sometimes taken as if everyone were equal in their access to performance resources, which they are not. Bourdieu's concept of habitus (Ch 9) and Goffman's later work on stigma both extend the framework to handle these structural constraints.

> ### Dig Deeper — Goffman in the digital age
> Goffman wrote in the era of face-to-face interaction. Ask Claude: "How would Goffman analyze contemporary social-media interaction? What is the front stage, back stage, performance team, and impression management on Instagram, TikTok, LinkedIn? Where does his framework extend cleanly, and where does it need adjustment?" The exercise tests whether you can apply a 1959 framework to 2026 phenomena.

### Common misconceptions

**"Goffman says everyone is fake."** No. Goffman observed that all social action involves performance, and that performance is not the opposite of authenticity. People can be sincere performers, performing roles they genuinely identify with. The performance frame describes the structure of social action, not its sincerity.

**"Impression management is manipulation."** It is not, in Goffman's analysis. Everyone does it, all the time. The question is not whether you do it but how skillfully and toward what ends.

**"Goffman is just a theorist."** Goffman's books are dense with empirical observation — his Shetland fieldwork, his detailed observation of psychiatric patients in *Asylums*, his case studies throughout. He is one of the most empirically grounded theorists in 20th-century sociology.

## Concept 3 — Ethnomethodology and the constructed-ness of social order

A second tradition that emerged alongside Goffman's: ethnomethodology, founded by Harold Garfinkel and developed by his students at UCLA. The framework asks an unusual question: how is ordinary social order *produced* by participants? What invisible work makes everyday situations recognizable as the situations they are?

Garfinkel's most famous teaching device was the **breaching experiment**. Send students into ordinary situations and ask them to do something slightly off — to violate a small assumption that everyone usually takes for granted. The reactions reveal what the assumption was doing.

Examples of breaching experiments:

- A student is asked to act in their own home as if they were a polite stranger. Greet their parents formally; ask permission to go to the bathroom; thank them for dinner with extended formality. The family's reactions — confusion, anger, demand for explanation — reveal that ordinary family interaction depends on assumptions of intimacy and informality that are usually invisible.
- A student is asked, when greeting an acquaintance, to ask in detail what specifically the person means by "How are you?" Do you mean physically, mentally, emotionally? Are you asking about today or this week? Do you want a complete answer? The conversational discomfort reveals that "How are you?" is not a literal question; it is a ritualized greeting whose function is the greeting itself, not information transfer. Asking for the literal answer breaks the ritual.

The lesson: ordinary social order is not natural or automatic. It is *produced* through skilled interactional work that participants do continuously, mostly without awareness. The breaches reveal the work by suspending it.

### The reflexivity of social order

Ethnomethodology's deeper claim: social facts are produced through *practical reasoning* by participants, and the participants' reasoning is constitutive of the facts.

A "normal" classroom is normal because everyone in it acts as if it were a normal classroom — students sit in approximately their assigned spots, look approximately at the teacher, take approximately notes, etc. The classroom is not normal first and then participants adjust to its normalcy; the normalcy is *produced* by the participants' actions, every time, in every class. Suspend the actions and the normalcy disappears.

This is harder to grasp than it sounds. It is not the claim that "if you imagine a classroom isn't normal, it ceases to be normal" — that would be magical thinking. It is the claim that the *features* we recognize as making a classroom normal are produced by the participants' coordinated actions. Without those actions, the room is just a room with chairs and a person in front. The "classroom-ness" lives in the practice.

Garfinkel called this the *reflexive* character of social facts: the facts are produced by the practical actions of participants, who are at the same time orienting their actions to those very facts.

### The trade-off — what ethnomethodology illuminates and what it misses

Ethnomethodology's strength is its capacity to denaturalize social phenomena that seem inevitable. The classroom seems just to *be* a classroom; ethnomethodology shows the work that makes it one. This denaturalization is sociologically and politically valuable: things that seem just to be the way the world is are revealed as the products of human practice, and therefore changeable.

The framework's limits: ethnomethodology has often been studied in small interactional settings and can struggle to scale up to larger structural patterns. The work of making a classroom into a classroom is real; explaining why some classrooms have well-funded teachers and others don't requires structural analysis that ethnomethodology by itself cannot provide.

> ### Dig Deeper — Run a breaching experiment thought-experiment
> Pick an everyday situation (greeting an acquaintance, riding an elevator with strangers, ordering at a restaurant, attending a class, scrolling a social-media feed). Ask Claude: "What would a Garfinkelian breaching experiment in this setting look like? What small assumption could be violated? What would the violation reveal about the work participants are doing to maintain ordinary social order?" Then think through whether you would actually want to run the experiment or just analyze it conceptually.

### Common misconceptions

**"Ethnomethodology is just about being weird in social situations."** The breaching experiments are pedagogical devices. The actual research program — including conversation analysis, which has produced detailed empirical findings about how speakers coordinate in real conversations — is rigorous empirical work.

**"Ethnomethodology rejects structure."** It does not. It studies how structure is produced and reproduced in interaction. The relationship between micro-interaction and macro-structure is contested in the literature, but the framework does not deny structure's reality.

## Concept 4 — Types of societies and the trade-off in any typology

Sociology has a long tradition of classifying societies by mode of subsistence, economic organization, or developmental stage. The most common typology distinguishes:

**Hunter-gatherer (foraging) societies.** Subsist by hunting wild game and gathering wild plant foods. Small-scale (typically 25-100 members), relatively egalitarian (with some gender-based and age-based hierarchy), low-tech, mobile. The mode that humans lived in for the vast majority of our species' history.

**Pastoral societies.** Subsist primarily by herding domesticated animals. Often nomadic or semi-nomadic, with social organization built around livestock and grazing-territory access. Pre-agricultural in some regions; coexistent with agriculture in others.

**Horticultural societies.** Practice small-scale plant cultivation, typically without plows or draft animals. Often sedentary or semi-sedentary, with somewhat larger settlements than hunter-gatherer groups. Common in tropical and subtropical zones.

**Agricultural societies.** Practice large-scale plant cultivation using plows, draft animals, and irrigation. Permanent settlements, increased population density, more complex division of labor, the emergence of social classes, large-scale state organization. The mode in which most of recorded human history has unfolded.

**Industrial societies.** Subsist primarily through manufacturing, with production organized in factories and offices using machinery and increasingly fossil-fuel energy. Began in 18th-century Britain, spread across Europe and North America in the 19th century, globally in the 20th century. Associated with urbanization, the rise of bureaucratic organization, the emergence of mass democracy and mass-consumer culture.

**Post-industrial societies.** Subsist primarily through services and information work rather than manufacturing. The term was coined by Daniel Bell in the 1970s. Most advanced economies have moved substantially toward this configuration since around 1970. Manufacturing employment has fallen dramatically as a share of advanced-economy jobs; service, knowledge, and care work have grown.

### The trade-off — what the typology captures and what it loses

The typology captures genuine differences. Hunter-gatherer societies are not just smaller industrial societies; they have qualitatively different organizing principles. Industrial societies are not just larger agricultural ones; the shift in technology and organization has restructured everything from family form to political authority.

The typology also misleads in specific ways. It can imply a developmental sequence (each type "progresses" toward the next) that has not in fact been universal — many societies skipped stages, or moved through them in different orders, or maintained mixed economies for long periods. It can imply that any given society is one type cleanly, when most societies have always been mixtures (hunter-gatherers traded with neighbors; agricultural societies had pastoral and craft sectors; industrial societies have always had agricultural sectors). It can be deployed in ways that imply moral hierarchy (more "advanced" = better), which the historical record does not support — life expectancy was lower in industrial cities than in many pre-industrial settings until the early 20th century.

The serious use of the typology is heuristic: it organizes patterns of variation without committing to a developmental ladder. The dogmatic use treats it as a sequence that all societies move through, which empirical work does not support.

### Common misconceptions

**"Industrial societies are more advanced than hunter-gatherer ones."** "Advanced" is a moral judgment that the typology does not, by itself, license. Industrial societies have specific advantages (much larger populations supportable; longer life expectancy in mature industrial economies; more elaborate division of labor) and specific disadvantages (greater inequality on most measures; environmental impact; alienation phenomena Marx described). Hunter-gatherer societies have different advantages and disadvantages. The comparison is apples to oranges in many dimensions.

**"Post-industrial societies don't have manufacturing."** They do; manufacturing is just a smaller share of total employment. Most things consumed in advanced economies are still manufactured, often in lower-income countries.

**"Every society moves through these stages."** The historical record shows much messier trajectories than this view suggests.

## Synthesis — how levels and frameworks combine

A serious analysis of any social phenomenon usually combines levels and frameworks.

Take a single example: contemporary American adolescents and their relationship to mental health. Survey research documents a striking rise in adolescent depression, anxiety, and suicidal ideation since around 2012, particularly among girls (Twenge; Haidt; CDC data). That is a macro pattern. Meso-level studies of specific schools and digital platforms show variation in the patterns and identify mechanisms (school environment; platform features; peer dynamics). Micro-level interviews and ethnography reveal how specific adolescents experience and manage their own mental states. Each level contributes; no level alone provides a complete picture.

The frameworks contribute differently. Functionalist analysis asks what work the platforms and the school environments do for the broader social system, and how the rise in depression relates to functional or dysfunctional features of those systems. Conflict analysis asks who benefits from the platform economy and who pays — pointing to the political-economy of attention markets and the externalization of mental-health costs onto users and their families. Interactionist analysis examines what social-media interaction *means* to adolescents, how identity and self-worth get performed and damaged in these settings, and what the experience is like from the inside.

Goffman's dramaturgical framework illuminates the performance dimension of social-media life — front stage (the public profile) vs. back stage (the messages and group chats), continuous impression management, the difficulty of having any genuine "back stage" in an environment where everything can be screenshotted. Ethnomethodology illuminates the work participants do to maintain ordinary social order on platforms whose architecture often disrupts that work.

The student equipped with these tools reads the rise in adolescent mental-health issues as a complex phenomenon with macro, meso, and micro dimensions, and with multiple analytical frameworks contributing. The student who has only one tool produces predictable misreadings.

## Chapter summary

Social interaction is the medium through which society gets produced and reproduced. Sociology operates at micro-, meso-, and macro-levels, with different methods and frameworks at each. Goffman's dramaturgical framework treats interaction as theatrical performance, illuminating the invisible work of impression management, performance teams, front stage and back stage. Ethnomethodology, developed by Garfinkel, examines how participants produce ordinary social order through skilled interactional work — work that becomes visible when breached. Different types of societies (hunter-gatherer, agricultural, industrial, post-industrial) organize interaction differently, with different available roles, props, and performance contexts. A serious sociological analysis of any phenomenon combines levels and frameworks pragmatically, recognizing that no single level or framework can capture the full picture.

## What would change my mind

If empirical work showed that micro-interactional patterns were largely irrelevant to macro outcomes — that structural variables predicted social outcomes regardless of the texture of face-to-face interaction — I would update my reading of micro-sociology's importance. The current evidence supports interaction as causally consequential in many domains, especially for stratification mechanisms (audit-study findings; ethnographic observation of differential treatment). Mass deflation of these findings would change my view.

## Still puzzling

I do not have a confident view on how digital and AI-mediated interaction will reshape Goffman-style analysis over the next decade. The framework was developed for face-to-face encounters; it has been productively extended to mediated interaction; whether the extensions are sufficient or whether new theoretical apparatus is needed is genuinely uncertain.
