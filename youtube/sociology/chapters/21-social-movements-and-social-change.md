In December 1955, a Black woman named Rosa Parks refused to give up her seat to a white passenger on a bus in Montgomery, Alabama. She was arrested. The local NAACP, which had been waiting for an appropriate test case, organized a boycott of the Montgomery bus system. The boycott lasted 381 days. It succeeded — the buses were desegregated. It also produced a young pastor named Martin Luther King Jr. as a national figure and helped launch the civil rights movement that, over the next decade, transformed American law and substantially shifted American culture.

The Montgomery bus boycott illustrates several features of social movements that sociology has worked to understand. The movement did not come out of nowhere — it built on decades of organizing, NAACP litigation, Black churches, and earlier protest. Rosa Parks was not a tired seamstress; she was a longtime NAACP activist who had been preparing for years for the moment. The movement required organization, leadership, communication, financial resources, and tactical decisions. It faced substantial opposition — legal, economic, and physical violence. Its eventual success required broader political conditions, not just the movement's own action.

This is what social movements are. They are not spontaneous eruptions of grievance; they are organized collective action that requires resources, organization, framing, opportunity, and tactical skill. The major social changes of the past century — civil rights; women's rights; LGBTQ+ rights; environmental protection; labor protections; many others — have come substantially through movements. Understanding how movements work is understanding one of the major mechanisms of social change.

This chapter is about social movements and social change. It is the closing chapter of the book; it draws on the frameworks introduced earlier and applies them to the specific question of how societies actually change.

## Concept 1 — What social movements are

The first move on social movements is to specify the category.

### Defining social movements

A *social movement* is sustained, organized collective action by a group of people seeking to produce or resist social change. Several features distinguish movements from other collective phenomena:

- **Sustained.** Movements operate over extended time, not just for a single event.
- **Organized.** Movements have some level of formal or informal organization — leadership, communication, division of labor, decision-making.
- **Collective.** Movements involve groups of people, not just individual actions.
- **Aimed at change.** Movements have specific goals, whether to produce change (most commonly) or to resist it.

The boundary between social movements and other collective phenomena is fuzzy. A single protest is collective behavior but may not constitute a movement. A long-standing organization (a church; a union; a political party) is organized collective action but may not constitute a movement (movements typically have more programmatic-change orientation than mature institutions). A "moment" of widespread cultural change without specific organization (the spread of fashion; the diffusion of a cultural innovation) is social change but may not involve a movement.

### Types of movements

Sociology distinguishes types of movements by what they aim for:

**Reform movements** seek specific, limited changes within existing institutional frameworks. The American civil rights movement (which sought equal rights within American constitutional democracy); the LGBTQ+-rights movement; environmental movements pushing for specific regulatory changes; labor movements seeking specific workplace protections.

**Revolutionary movements** seek dramatic transformation of social-political systems, often through replacement of existing power structures. The American Revolution; the French Revolution; the Russian Revolution; many anti-colonial movements; some left and right radical movements in various countries.

**Resistance movements** seek to defend existing arrangements against change. Conservative movements opposing specific reforms; some religious-traditional movements; movements against specific policy changes.

**Reactive movements** seek to reverse changes that have already happened. The post-Civil-War U.S. white-supremacist movement; movements seeking to repeal specific reforms; movements resisting specific demographic or cultural changes.

**Religious and identity-based movements** organize around specific religious or identity goals — the Pentecostal movement (Ch 15); various ethnic and indigenous movements; identity-based political movements.

The categories are not exclusive; movements often combine features.

### The trade-off — categories that overlap

The categories help organize analysis but should not be applied mechanically. The civil rights movement was a reform movement in its goals (equal rights within the constitutional framework) but used revolutionary rhetoric and tactics in some moments; the women's-rights movement combined reform and revolutionary elements depending on which strand and which period. Movements often contain factions with different orientations.

> ### Dig Deeper — Classify a contemporary movement
> Pick a contemporary social movement (Black Lives Matter; the Tea Party; #MeToo; the Sunrise Movement on climate; specific anti-immigration movements; specific religious movements). Ask Claude: "What kind of movement is this? Reform, revolutionary, resistance, or some combination? What does the classification reveal about its goals and tactics?"

### Common misconceptions

**"Movements are always progressive."** Movements can be progressive, conservative, or radical in any direction. The Tea Party, the Brexit campaign, various anti-immigration movements are conservative or right-leaning movements; they are still movements with substantial organizational sophistication.

**"Movements always succeed when they are right."** They often fail. The history of social movements includes many that pursued goals widely seen as just but failed to achieve them, and others that achieved goals that produced mixed or negative consequences.

**"Movements are spontaneous."** Almost never. Behind every "spontaneous" movement is usually substantial prior organization, resource accumulation, and tactical preparation.

## Concept 2 — Theoretical frameworks on social movements

Sociology has developed substantial frameworks for understanding why movements emerge and what makes them successful.

### Classical collective behavior

Earlier sociology (Gustave Le Bon's *The Crowd* (1895); Herbert Blumer's collective behavior theory; Neil Smelser's *Theory of Collective Behavior* (1962)) treated movements as relatively spontaneous responses to social strain — generalized beliefs, structural conduciveness, precipitating events combining to produce collective action.

The framework had limits. It treated movements as responses to underlying frustration rather than as organized strategic actors; it underplayed the role of resources, organization, and tactical skill; it tended to treat movements as somewhat irrational compared to ordinary politics. Most contemporary work has moved beyond this framework.

### Resource mobilization

A major reorientation in the 1970s. John McCarthy and Mayer Zald, Charles Tilly, and others argued that movements should be analyzed as organized political actors. The question is not "why do people get angry enough to act" — anger and grievance are widespread — but "how do organized groups mobilize resources to act effectively?"

The framework emphasizes:

- **Resources.** Money, time, expertise, organizational infrastructure, communication channels. Movements need substantial resources; resource accumulation and management is part of what distinguishes successful movements from unsuccessful ones.
- **Organizations.** Formal organizations (NAACP, NOW, Sierra Club, ACLU, many others) provide the infrastructure within which movement activity happens. Movements with strong organizational infrastructure tend to outperform movements without.
- **Strategic action.** Movement activity is intentional, strategic, often planned. Specific tactics (boycotts, sit-ins, marches, lawsuits, electoral organizing) are chosen and adapted based on assessment of what will work.
- **Networks.** Existing networks (churches, unions, professional associations, social clubs) often provide the seedbeds for movement organization. Movements rarely emerge from nothing; they emerge from existing networks of relationship.

The resource mobilization framework has been productive in many empirical studies. It treats movements seriously as political actors, not as expressions of inchoate frustration.

### Political opportunity structures

A complementary framework. Sidney Tarrow, Doug McAdam, Charles Tilly, and others have examined how the broader political environment — what they call "political opportunity structures" — affects movement emergence and success.

Key political-opportunity factors include:

- **Openness or closedness of the political system.** Movements emerge more easily where political institutions allow them space; they are repressed more easily in closed systems.
- **Stability of political alignments.** Periods of political realignment (when coalitions are shifting) often produce movement opportunities; periods of stable coalitions are less hospitable.
- **Influential allies.** Movements that find sympathetic actors within established political institutions often succeed; movements without such allies typically fail.
- **State capacity for repression.** States that can effectively repress movements often do; movements operating in such contexts face higher costs.
- **External shocks.** Wars, economic crises, natural disasters, technological changes — all can open opportunities for movements that were previously unable to advance their goals.

The framework illuminates why the same grievances can produce successful movements at one time and failed movements at another. The civil rights movement of the 1950s-60s succeeded partly because of the political opportunity structures — the post-WWII context, the Cold War's external pressures on U.S. racial policy, the receptiveness of specific federal officials, the splits within the Democratic Party. Earlier civil rights efforts, lacking these conditions, had been less successful.

### Framing

Robert Benford and David Snow developed framing analysis. Movements engage in active *framing work* — constructing the meanings that motivate participation, attract supporters, and challenge opponents. A movement's frames address:

- **Diagnostic framing.** What is the problem? Who is responsible?
- **Prognostic framing.** What is the solution? What should be done?
- **Motivational framing.** Why should people participate? What is the call to action?

Successful movements develop frames that resonate with potential supporters, fit the broader cultural environment, and effectively challenge opposing frames. The framing dimension explains why movements with similar grievances can attract very different levels of support depending on how they frame their cause.

### New social movements

A framework developed by European scholars (Alain Touraine; Alberto Melucci; Manuel Castells in some work) emphasizing how movements changed in advanced societies after the 1960s. The "new" social movements — civil rights, women's rights, LGBTQ+ rights, environmental movements, anti-war movements, student movements — differed from older labor movements in:

- Centering identity-based and quality-of-life concerns rather than economic-distributive concerns
- Operating largely outside traditional party-political channels
- Using cultural and symbolic action alongside political action
- Drawing support from middle-class and educated populations, not just working class
- Emphasizing autonomy and authentic self-expression as values

The framework illuminates how movement repertoires shifted in advanced societies. It has been complicated by subsequent developments — economic-distributive concerns have returned to prominence in some contexts; movements have hybridized; the strict "new vs. old" distinction has weakened.

### Combining frameworks

Most contemporary work integrates multiple frameworks. A specific movement requires resource analysis (does it have what it needs?); political-opportunity analysis (what is the broader environment?); framing analysis (how is it constructing meaning?); identity and cultural analysis (what does it mean to participants?); historical analysis (what came before, what is shifting?). The strongest analyses integrate these.

> ### Dig Deeper — Multiple frameworks on a movement
> Pick a specific historical movement (the civil rights movement; the women's-suffrage movement; the labor movement of the 1930s; the environmental movement of the 1970s). Ask Claude: "Apply resource mobilization, political opportunity, and framing frameworks to this movement. What does each illuminate?"

### Common misconceptions

**"Movements are mostly about emotion."** Emotion is part; rational strategic action is also part. Reducing movements to emotional responses misses much.

**"You can't predict movement success."** It's hard to predict, but the factors are reasonably well-understood. Movements with stronger resources, more favorable political opportunity, more resonant frames, and better tactical choices tend to succeed more often than those without.

**"Movements work mostly through legislative change."** They work through many channels — legislative; legal (court cases); cultural; economic (boycotts; corporate pressure); informal social pressure. Reducing movements to legislative work misses much.

## Concept 3 — Major movements of the modern era

Several movements deserve specific recognition for their substantial impact on contemporary societies.

### The labor movement

The 19th-and-early-20th-century labor movement transformed industrial capitalism. Through union organizing, strikes, political mobilization, and eventual partnerships with progressive parties, the labor movement won the 8-hour workday, weekend rest, child labor restrictions, workplace safety regulations, recognition of unions, collective bargaining rights, social security, unemployment insurance, and many other features of contemporary advanced economies.

The labor movement weakened in the U.S. dramatically after 1955 (Ch 18). It remains stronger in many European economies and in specific U.S. sectors (public employees, healthcare, parts of the entertainment industry).

### The civil rights movement

The U.S. civil rights movement of the 1940s-60s ended legal segregation in the South, secured voting rights, and produced major federal legislation (Civil Rights Act of 1964; Voting Rights Act of 1965; Fair Housing Act of 1968) that transformed American law. The movement built on decades of prior organizing, used a variety of tactics (legal cases; mass protest; nonviolent civil disobedience; voter registration; political organizing), and operated within specific political-opportunity conditions.

The movement's effects continue to ramify. Subsequent movements have drawn from its tactical and organizational lessons. Its limits — particularly the substantial inequality that persists despite legal changes (Ch 11) — remain subjects of ongoing analysis and contestation.

### Women's movements

Women's movements have been central to social change across the past 200 years. The first wave (focused on suffrage; achieving the 19th Amendment in 1920); the second wave (1960s-70s; focused on reproductive rights, employment equality, family law reform); the third wave (1990s-2000s; focused on intersectionality, sexual harassment, broader feminist concerns); and the contemporary movements (#MeToo from 2017 onward; contests over reproductive rights post-Dobbs; ongoing debates about specific feminist questions).

The movements have produced substantial change — in legal frameworks; in workplace and educational equality; in cultural patterns. Substantial gaps remain (Ch 12 documented many of these).

### LGBTQ+ rights movement

The post-Stonewall (1969) LGBTQ+ rights movement transformed laws and culture in many countries over the past 50 years. Same-sex marriage was illegal everywhere in the world before 2001 (Netherlands); it is now legal in over 30 countries including the U.S. (since 2015). Anti-sodomy laws have been struck down in most advanced economies. Anti-discrimination protections have been extended in many jurisdictions.

The movement has produced very rapid change in some dimensions, with substantial contestation continuing in others (especially around transgender rights as of 2024).

### Environmental movement

The U.S. environmental movement that emerged in the 1960s-70s produced major federal legislation (the Clean Air Act, Clean Water Act, Endangered Species Act, Environmental Protection Agency creation). It has continued in various forms, with the climate movement as the most prominent contemporary strand.

Climate movements (Sunrise Movement; Extinction Rebellion; many others) have grown substantially since 2015, with mixed success in producing climate policy outcomes. The movement has shifted some specific corporate and political behavior; the scale of change needed for climate stabilization remains far above what has been achieved.

### Movements of the 2010s-2020s

Several recent movements have shaped contemporary politics:

**Black Lives Matter.** Emerging from 2013 onward, with substantial growth after 2020 in response to specific high-profile cases. The movement has shaped policy debates around policing, criminal justice, racial justice; its specific impacts and trajectories continue to develop.

**#MeToo.** From 2017, focused on sexual harassment and assault, particularly in workplace contexts. Has produced substantial change in some industries and ongoing contestation in others.

**Right-populist movements.** The Tea Party (2009 onward); MAGA movement; various European right-populist movements. Have substantially reshaped political coalitions in their countries.

**Climate movements.** As above; with continuing scale and political contest.

### The trade-off — what movements achieve and what they don't

Movements have produced enormous changes in laws, institutions, and cultures over the past century. They have not produced unmitigated success; many have failed; some have produced change with mixed consequences.

The accomplishments are real. Civil rights, women's rights, LGBTQ+ rights, labor rights — all reflect substantial movement achievement. The failures are also real. Many movement goals remain unaccomplished; some movement strategies have produced backlash; some movements that succeeded in immediate goals failed to produce the broader transformations they sought.

The student should hold both — substantial movement achievement and substantial movement limitation — in view simultaneously. Neither pure heroic narratives nor pure dismissal capture what movements have actually accomplished.

> ### Dig Deeper — A specific movement's trajectory
> Pick a specific movement and trace it carefully. Ask Claude: "Walk me through this movement's trajectory — origins, peak organizing, major successes, major failures, current status. What does the case teach about what movements can and can't do?" Verify against academic sources.

### Common misconceptions

**"Movements just need to organize and demand."** They need to organize, but also build resources, frame effectively, identify opportunities, and adapt tactically. Demand alone rarely succeeds.

**"Successful movements are always remembered as legitimate."** Movements often succeed despite being treated as illegitimate at the time. The labor movement, civil rights movement, women's-rights movement were all treated as illegitimate by substantial portions of the establishment during their peak organizing.

**"Movements either win completely or fail completely."** Most produce partial outcomes — some goals achieved, others not, with substantial unintended consequences. The simple win/lose framing oversimplifies.

## Concept 4 — Social change beyond movements

Social movements are one mechanism of social change, not the only one. Other mechanisms include:

### Demographic change

Population dynamics produce social change without organized movements. Aging populations transform politics and institutions (Chs 13, 20); declining fertility reshapes family form and labor markets; migration changes the cultural and political composition of countries.

### Technological change

New technologies produce social change. The Industrial Revolution transformed nearly everything about advanced societies (Ch 18). Computer networking, smartphones, and AI have transformed contemporary life (Ch 8). The transformations are not always intentional; they often produce social changes that no specific actors planned or sought.

### Economic structural change

Shifts in economic structure produce social change. The decline of manufacturing, the rise of services, the growth of platform-based work — all have produced substantial social change in advanced economies (Ch 18).

### Cultural change

Cultural patterns shift through accumulated changes in attitudes, beliefs, practices. The decline of religious affiliation in many advanced economies (Ch 15); the expansion of women's labor force participation; the transformation of family forms — all involve cultural change that is partly driven by movements but partly broader cultural shifts not reducible to movement activity.

### Crisis-driven change

Wars, economic crises, pandemics produce substantial social change in concentrated periods. World War II transformed the U.S. economy and society in profound ways; the 2008 financial crisis reshaped politics and economics; the COVID-19 pandemic produced rapid changes in work patterns, technology adoption, public-health institutions.

### Combinations

Most actual social change is produced by combinations of these mechanisms. The civil rights movement built on demographic change (Black migration to cities), economic change (industrialization providing economic foundations for organizing), technological change (television bringing protests into living rooms), and crisis-driven change (the Cold War creating pressure for U.S. racial reform). Movements operate within and contribute to broader patterns.

> ### Dig Deeper — Multiple mechanisms in a specific change
> Pick a specific social change of the past 50 years (the rise of dual-earner households; the decline of religious affiliation; the shift in attitudes about same-sex relationships; the spread of computing). Ask Claude: "Walk me through how this change happened. What role did movements play? Demographic change? Technological change? Economic structural change? Cultural shift?" The exercise teaches you to see change as multi-causal.

### Common misconceptions

**"Movements drive most social change."** They drive some; many other mechanisms also drive change. The "movements drive everything" framing oversimplifies.

**"Most social change is unintentional."** Some is; some is intentionally produced by movements and other organized actors. Both are true; reducing change to either is incomplete.

This is the closing chapter. The student has worked through 21 chapters covering the major institutions, structures, and processes of social life as sociology studies them.

The frameworks introduced — the sociological imagination (Ch 1); the methods of sociology (Ch 2); the concepts of culture, society, socialization (Chs 3-5); the meso-level structures of groups and organizations (Ch 6); the dynamics of deviance, media, and stratification (Chs 7-12); the patterns of aging, family, religion, education, government, work, health, and macro-forces (Chs 13-20); and now the mechanisms of social change (Ch 21) — provide a structured way of seeing social life.

The chapters have not produced consensus answers on contested questions. They have provided tools for thinking. The serious student leaves the book equipped to:

- Recognize how individual lives are shaped by social structures
- Identify multiple frameworks for analyzing any specific phenomenon
- Read empirical claims critically, distinguishing what evidence supports from what doesn't
- Hold the empirical and normative dimensions of social-political debates separately while taking both seriously
- Navigate contemporary issues with structured frameworks rather than ideological intuition
- Use LLMs as tools for thinking sociologically while recognizing their limits and biases

This is, in miniature, what a working sociologist does. The toolkit is not the destination; it is the equipment for further work, whether in academic sociology, in policy analysis, in journalism, in civic engagement, or in the ongoing project of understanding the world one lives in.

The closing thought: sociology offers something distinctive — the recognition that what looks like individual experience is one instance of broader patterns; that what looks like inevitable arrangements are specific historical configurations; that what looks like nature is often social product. Holding this view does not require abandoning individual responsibility, moral judgment, or the recognition of biological reality. It requires holding the social dimension alongside these other dimensions and refusing to collapse it into them.

What I, as the author, conclude after working through these 22 chapters: contemporary societies face substantial challenges (inequality; political polarization; climate change; demographic transitions; institutional stress) that require careful sociological analysis. Sociology by itself does not solve these challenges; politics, ethics, and individual judgment all play roles. But informed sociological analysis sharpens the political-ethical work and reduces the kinds of analytical errors that have produced bad policies and worse politics across many domains. The student who has worked through this book has, I hope, gained tools that will be useful — and humility about the limits of those tools, given the genuinely contested terrain we have walked through.

## Chapter summary

Social movements — sustained, organized collective action seeking change — are one of the major mechanisms through which societies actually change. Multiple theoretical frameworks (collective behavior; resource mobilization; political opportunity; framing; new social movements) compete to explain movement emergence and success; the strongest analyses integrate multiple frameworks. Major movements of the past century (labor; civil rights; women's; LGBTQ+; environmental) have transformed contemporary societies; substantial gaps between movement goals and current conditions remain. Movements are one mechanism of social change among others (demographic; technological; economic structural; cultural; crisis-driven); most actual change is produced by combinations of mechanisms. The serious student of social change holds multiple frameworks together and recognizes that contemporary challenges will be addressed (or not) through complex interactions of movements and other change mechanisms.

## What would change my mind

If sustained scholarly work showed that movement-driven change has been substantially smaller than the dominant narratives suggest — that demographic, technological, and economic changes have produced most of what we attribute to movements — I would update my reading of movement importance. The current evidence supports movements as substantial contributors to specific changes; if rigorous comparative work showed otherwise, my reading would shift.

## Still puzzling

I do not have a confident view on whether contemporary movements (climate; racial justice; movements of various political orientations) will produce changes comparable in scale to the great movements of the 20th century, or whether they will be more limited in impact. The conditions are different; the political opportunity structures are different; the tactical possibilities are different. The answer depends on dynamics that have not yet played out.

## Connections forward

This is the closing chapter. The student should leave the book equipped to read social phenomena through multiple frameworks, to evaluate empirical claims carefully, to recognize where reasonable people disagree, and to engage civically with informed sociological analysis. The work continues — in further reading, in continued engagement with social phenomena, in the ongoing project of understanding the world.
