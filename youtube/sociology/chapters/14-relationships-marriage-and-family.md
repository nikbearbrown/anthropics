**TL;DR.** The family is among the most basic and most studied institutions in human societies — universal in some form, enormously varied in specific structure, and rapidly changing in advanced economies over the past 60 years. The chapter walks through what we mean by "family," the dramatic recent changes in family form across advanced economies, the major theoretical frameworks for understanding family, and the contested empirical and normative debates about what these changes mean.

---

In 1960, the typical American household was a married couple with children at home. The husband worked for pay; the wife worked at home (about a third of married women were also employed for pay, but households with both spouses working full-time were less common). Children were expected; divorce was relatively rare; cohabitation outside marriage was uncommon; same-sex relationships were largely hidden from public visibility.

By 2024, the typical American household was none of these. Married couples with children had become a minority of households. Single-person households had grown to represent roughly 28% of all U.S. households. Cohabitation outside marriage was widespread, with most marriages now preceded by cohabitation. Same-sex marriage had been legal nationwide for a decade. Median age at first marriage had risen by approximately five years for both sexes since 1970. About 40% of births occurred outside marriage. Average family size had declined; childlessness had risen.

The transformation in family life over six decades is one of the most thorough social changes in modern history, and it has not finished playing out. The implications run through every other chapter of this book — through education and labor markets, through religion and politics, through aging and inequality, through gender and sexuality. The family is not just one institution among many; it is the institutional context within which most people are socialized, supported, and (eventually) cared for. Changes in family form ramify outward.

This chapter is about that. The territory is contested — in some communities, the changes are seen as progress toward greater equality and freedom; in others, as decline of an institution central to human flourishing. The chapter aims for charitable presentation of multiple readings and clear documentation of what we know empirically, separating the empirical claims from the normative ones.

## Concept 1 — What family is and what it does

The first move on family questions is to specify what we mean by the term.

### Defining family

The traditional sociology textbook definition: family is a group of people related by blood, marriage, or adoption who share economic resources and provide for child-rearing.

The definition is not quite right. It excludes some arrangements that function as families (long-term cohabiting partners; close kin networks operating across multiple households; chosen families in LGBTQ+ communities). It includes some arrangements that don't function as families (legal spouses living separately; adopted-but-estranged relationships).

A more functional definition: family is the set of people who provide ongoing material support, emotional support, child-rearing, and care for older members. By this definition, family includes what people actually do for each other, not just what their formal status says.

Both definitions have value. Sociology of family has shifted somewhat from the first toward the second over the past several decades, as family forms have diversified and the gap between formal status and actual practice has widened.

### Universal features and cross-cultural variation

Some features of family appear nearly universal across human societies:

- Some institutional arrangement for organizing reproduction and child-rearing
- Some recognized relationship statuses creating obligations of mutual support
- Some pattern of intergenerational transfer of resources and responsibilities
- Recognition of kinship — relationships through descent and marriage

The specific forms these take vary enormously:

**Nuclear vs. extended.** The nuclear family (parents and children, separated from broader kin) is dominant in contemporary Western societies. Extended family arrangements (multiple generations, multiple related nuclear units, sometimes including various forms of plural marriage) are the historical and contemporary norm in much of the world.

**Patrilineal, matrilineal, bilateral.** Different societies trace descent through fathers' lines (patrilineal — historically common); through mothers' lines (matrilineal — significant in some societies); or through both (bilateral — Western pattern).

**Monogamy, polygyny, polyandry.** Most societies practice monogamy; some practice polygyny (one man, multiple wives); fewer practice polyandry (one woman, multiple husbands). Among societies that permit polygyny, only a minority of marriages are typically polygynous; resource constraints limit the practice.

**Patrilocal, matrilocal, neolocal.** Where do newly-married couples live? With the husband's family (patrilocal — common historically); with the wife's family (matrilocal); in a new household (neolocal — Western pattern).

The cross-cultural variation establishes that no single family form is "natural" in some strong biological sense. The contemporary American nuclear-family pattern is one specific cultural arrangement among many.

### What families do

Functionalist analyses identify several functions families perform:

**Reproduction and primary care of children.** The biological process of reproduction occurs within or in connection to family arrangements; the years-long process of raising children to social maturity occurs primarily within families.

**Economic cooperation.** Families pool resources, share housing, and produce goods and services for joint consumption. The specific economic functions have shifted — family farms and family businesses are less common; specialized purchase of goods and services more common — but families remain economic units in important respects.

**Emotional support.** Families provide ongoing emotional support, intimacy, and identity. The role of the family in emotional life has arguably *increased* as other supporting institutions (extended kinship networks; tight-knit local communities; religious congregations for many) have weakened.

**Care for the elderly and the infirm.** Most care for older adults and for adults with disabilities is provided by family members, not by formal institutions. The aging-related discussions in Chapter 13 connect directly here.

**Socialization.** As Chapter 5 explored, most primary socialization occurs in families. The family is the first society the child encounters and remains the model against which later social experiences are interpreted.

**Identity and meaning.** Families provide identity, lineage, narratives of belonging. They are sites of meaning-making across generations.

### Common misconceptions

**"There is one natural form of the family."** No. Cross-cultural evidence rules out simple naturalist claims. The contemporary American nuclear family is one specific cultural arrangement among many.

**"The family is dying."** Family persists in some form across all societies. Specific forms change; the underlying institution adapts.

**"Family change is just decline."** Some changes have produced losses (declining caregiving in some forms; some family stress); others have produced gains (more egalitarian relationships; greater individual freedom; better outcomes for many groups). The "decline" framing captures one dimension of recent change but misses others.

## Concept 2 — The transformation of American family

The U.S. case (and parallel developments across most advanced economies) is among the most dramatic social transformations of recent history. The empirical patterns:

### Marriage trends

**Median age at first marriage** has risen dramatically. In 1960, median age at first marriage was approximately 23 for men and 20 for women. In 2024, it is approximately 30 for men and 28 for women. The rise has been nearly continuous over six decades.

**Marriage rates** (the share of adults currently married) have declined substantially. In 1960, approximately 72% of U.S. adults were married. In 2024, approximately 50%. The decline is steeper among adults without college degrees.

**Cohabitation** has risen dramatically. In 1960, cohabitation outside marriage was uncommon and stigmatized. Today, the majority of marriages are preceded by cohabitation, and a substantial share of couples cohabit without marrying.

**Divorce** rose substantially from the 1960s through the early 1980s, peaked, and has slightly declined since. Roughly 40-50% of first marriages end in divorce, varying somewhat by cohort and methodology.

**Same-sex marriage** became legal nationwide in the U.S. in 2015 (Obergefell v. Hodges) and is now available in roughly two dozen countries. The dramatic expansion of legally-recognized same-sex relationships is one of the rapid social changes of recent decades.

### Childbearing trends

**Total fertility rate** has declined substantially. The U.S. TFR was approximately 3.7 in 1960; it is approximately 1.6 in 2024 (well below the 2.1 replacement rate). Most other advanced economies have lower TFRs; some (South Korea, Italy, Spain) are well below 1.5.

**Childlessness** has risen. The share of women reaching age 40-44 without having had a child rose from approximately 10% in the 1970s to approximately 17-18% in recent years.

**Births to unmarried parents** have risen substantially. About 40% of U.S. births now occur outside marriage, with substantial variation by demographic group.

**Single-parent households** have risen. About 23% of U.S. children live with a single parent in 2024, up from about 9% in 1960.

### Class divergence

A specific feature of recent U.S. family change: family forms have diverged substantially by class. College-educated Americans largely continue to marry, have children within marriage (often after extended cohabitation), and maintain marriages at higher rates than less-educated Americans. Non-college Americans have seen dramatic increases in non-marital births, single-parent households, and family instability.

The divergence has been widely documented. Andrew Cherlin's *Labor's Love Lost* (2014); Edin and Kefalas's *Promises I Can Keep* (2005); the Brookings work on the marriage gap. The patterns are real; the explanations are contested.

Cherlin's argument: changing economic conditions for non-college workers (declining manufacturing wages; growing labor-market instability) have undermined the foundations on which working-class marriage rested in mid-20th-century America. Edin and Kefalas's argument: low-income women still value marriage but find acceptable male partners scarce; non-marital childbearing reflects valuing motherhood combined with not finding marriageable partners. Other arguments emphasize cultural shifts in views of marriage; policy effects of welfare programs; specific labor-market dynamics.

### Cross-national comparison

Different advanced economies have experienced these changes at different paces and to different degrees.

European countries generally have lower marriage rates and higher cohabitation than the U.S.; some (Sweden, Denmark, France) have very high non-marital birth rates that don't track with the same patterns of family instability that often accompany non-marital births in the U.S. The Scandinavian model — high cohabitation, high non-marital births, low family instability, strong public support for parents — represents a distinct pattern from the U.S.

East Asian countries (Japan, South Korea, Taiwan) have low marriage rates, very low fertility, and largely low cohabitation — a different pattern again. Strong cultural and policy disincentives to non-marital childbearing combine with economic and gender-cultural pressures that produce extremely low fertility.

Middle Eastern and South Asian societies have generally maintained higher marriage rates and family form continuity, though with substantial change in some countries.

What policies and cultural patterns explain the differences? What can each country learn from the other?" Verify against OECD family data and country-specific scholarly work.

### Common misconceptions

**"Family change is just American."** It is largely shared across advanced economies, with different specific configurations.

**"Marriage rates have declined uniformly."** The decline is much steeper among non-college Americans; college-educated Americans continue to marry, just later.

**"Lower fertility is always bad."** It has consequences (population aging; pension challenges) but is not, by itself, a problem. It allows greater per-child investment; it tracks women's expanded opportunities; it is a feature of advanced economies that have generally produced high quality of life. Whether the current low fertility levels are sustainable is a separate question.

## Concept 3 — Theoretical frameworks on family

Several major frameworks compete to explain family patterns and changes.

### Functionalist accounts

Talcott Parsons's mid-20th-century analyses argued that the nuclear family had evolved to fit industrial society's needs. The traditional gender division of labor (husband as instrumental breadwinner; wife as expressive home-maker) was functional for economic specialization, child-rearing, and emotional support of the husband-wage-earner.

The framework has been substantially challenged by family changes since 1960 — many family forms now function effectively without the gender division Parsons described. The strong functionalist claim that this specific arrangement was necessary has not held.

A weaker version may still be defensible: families do perform specific functions (reproduction, child-rearing, support, identity); the specific family forms that work for these functions may be more varied than Parsons recognized. The empirical question is which family forms perform which functions well.

### Conflict-theoretic and feminist analyses

A diverse set of frameworks broadly arguing that family arrangements reflect and reproduce broader power relations. Marxist family theory examined how families serve as units of reproduction for capitalism — producing future workers, providing unpaid domestic labor, transferring wealth across generations.

Feminist family theory has been particularly productive. The traditional family, in feminist analyses, has been a site of male power over women — economic dependence; the second shift; the domestic-violence pattern; the gender-stratified expectations around caregiving. Feminist analyses have examined how family forms have been reorganized as women have entered paid work and gained economic independence.

### Cherlin's deinstitutionalization argument

Andrew Cherlin's *The Marriage-Go-Round* (2009) and subsequent work has been influential. Cherlin argued that marriage in the U.S. has been *deinstitutionalized* — the rules and expectations governing marriage have weakened, leaving couples to negotiate the terms of their relationships individually. This deinstitutionalization, combined with high cultural valuation of marriage, has produced specific patterns: high marriage-and-divorce volatility; later marriage; class-divergent patterns; the contemporary U.S. configuration.

Cherlin's framework explains a great deal of recent change. Critics argue the framework underplays the institutional features that remain (legal status; tax treatment; cultural expectations) and overstates the deinstitutionalization. The debate is ongoing.

### Second demographic transition

Lesthaeghe and van de Kaa's framework (Ch 13) connects family change to broader cultural shifts. The second demographic transition (delayed childbearing, lower fertility, rising cohabitation, rising single living, rising childlessness) reflects, in this framework, a cultural shift toward individualism, autonomy, and self-realization. The framework places family change in cultural-demographic context.

### Combining frameworks

Most contemporary scholarship draws on multiple frameworks. Family change is partly economic (Cherlin's labor-market analysis); partly cultural (the second-demographic-transition framework); partly gender-related (feminist analyses of women's expanded options); partly policy-driven (welfare policies, marriage policies, family policy varies dramatically across countries). The strongest analyses integrate these.

> ### Dig Deeper — Multiple frameworks on a specific change
> Pick a specific family change (the rise of cohabitation; the decline of teen marriage; the rise of childlessness; the legalization of same-sex marriage). Apply multiple theoretical frameworks. Ask Claude: "What does each framework illuminate about this change? Where do they converge?"

### Common misconceptions

**"There is one explanation for family change."** There are multiple explanations operating in combination.

**"Family change is purely cultural."** Economic, demographic, and policy factors all contribute.

**"Family change is purely economic."** Cultural shifts have been substantial and not reducible to economic factors alone.

## Concept 4 — Contested questions: family structure and child outcomes

A specific area of contested empirical and normative debate worth examining.

### What the data shows

Children raised in different family structures show, on average, different outcomes on multiple measures. Children raised in stable two-parent households have, on average, better educational outcomes, better mental-health outcomes, lower rates of poverty, and better outcomes in adulthood than children raised in single-parent households. This pattern is robust across many studies and many countries.

The challenging question: what is *causing* the differences?

### Competing explanations

**Selection effects.** Adults who form and maintain stable two-parent families differ from adults who don't on many dimensions (income, education, mental health, conflict-resolution skills). Their children's better outcomes may reflect these parental differences rather than the family structure per se.

**Resource effects.** Two-parent households have more resources (income, time, social capital) to devote to children than single-parent households. The resource differences may produce the outcome differences without family structure being the operative variable.

**Direct effects of family structure.** Family stability itself, beyond parental characteristics and resources, may matter for child outcomes. Children may benefit from continuity; the absence of major family disruption may have value distinct from selection and resources.

**Conflict effects.** High-conflict two-parent households may produce worse outcomes than low-conflict single-parent households. The relevant variable may be parenting quality, not parent count.

The empirical literature is contested. Different studies, with different controls and identification strategies, produce different findings. The strongest empirical claim that survives careful examination: family structure correlates with child outcomes; some of the correlation reflects selection and resource effects; some likely reflects direct effects of family structure; the relative weights are debated.

### The trade-off — empirical and normative

The debate has substantial normative implications. Some scholars argue the empirical evidence supports policy efforts to encourage stable two-parent families. Other scholars argue the evidence does not support such efforts (because the underlying causes are selection and resources, not structure) and that policy should focus on resources and parenting quality rather than family form.

Both positions can find empirical support; the disagreement is about how to weigh the partial findings. The political debates often run ahead of the empirical debates, with strong claims being made in either direction that the evidence does not clearly support.

A serious sociological reading recognizes the empirical complexity, the contestation, and the gap between what the evidence shows and what political advocates claim.

### Common misconceptions

**"Family structure causes the outcomes."** Some of the effect is causal; much of it reflects selection and resources. Strong causal claims based on simple correlation oversimplify.

**"Family structure has nothing to do with the outcomes."** It probably does have some causal effect; rejecting all causal contribution overcorrects.

**"The science is settled."** It is not. Specific empirical questions are settled (the correlations are real); the causal interpretation remains contested.

## Synthesis — reading family sociologically

A serious sociological reading of contemporary family holds several things together.

The empirical patterns are clear. Family forms have changed dramatically in the U.S. and other advanced economies since 1960. The changes include later marriage, more cohabitation, more divorce, lower fertility, more single-parent households, more class divergence in family form, and (recently) recognition of same-sex marriage.

The theoretical frameworks each illuminate parts of the picture. Functionalism captures real functions families perform; conflict and feminist analyses capture power dynamics; Cherlin's deinstitutionalization argument captures the contemporary U.S. configuration; the second-demographic-transition framework places change in cultural-demographic context.

The cross-national comparison reveals enormous variation. Different societies have organized family change differently, with different policy frameworks producing different specific outcomes. The Scandinavian "high cohabitation, low instability" pattern; the East Asian "low marriage, very low fertility" pattern; the U.S. "class-divergent" pattern — each is a specific configuration.

The contested questions remain contested. The empirical evidence on family-structure effects on child outcomes does not uniquely support any specific policy position. Reasonable people, drawing on the same evidence, reach different normative conclusions about what should be done.

What I, as the author, conclude: the family is a real institution that performs real functions; its specific forms have changed substantially in advanced economies and continue to change; the changes are produced by the joint action of economic, cultural, and policy factors; some changes have produced gains (greater individual freedom; more egalitarian relationships) and some have produced losses (family instability; class-divergent patterns producing worse outcomes for some children). What follows for policy is genuinely contested; the evidence narrows what is reasonable without settling the normative questions.

## Chapter summary

The family is a basic and substantially universal institution that has undergone dramatic change in advanced economies over the past 60 years. The changes include later marriage, more cohabitation, more divorce, lower fertility, more single-parent households, class-divergent family forms, and recognition of same-sex marriage. Multiple theoretical frameworks (functionalist, conflict-theoretic, feminist, Cherlin's deinstitutionalization, second demographic transition) compete to explain the patterns; the strongest analyses integrate multiple frameworks. Cross-national comparison reveals enormous variation in how family change has unfolded. Contested empirical questions (the effects of family structure on child outcomes) remain contested; political claims often run ahead of the evidence. Sociology offers a structured way to think about questions where careful work narrows reasonable positions without settling the normative questions.

## What would change my mind

If sustained policy intervention or cultural change reversed the class divergence in family form — restoring family stability across the income distribution — I would update my reading of how durable the contemporary divergence is. The current evidence suggests the divergence reflects deep economic and cultural mechanisms; substantial reversal would require explanation.

## Still puzzling

I do not have a confident view on what the next 30 years of family change will look like. Continued evolution of the patterns since 1960 is one possibility; partial reversal of some trends is another; new configurations not yet visible are a third. The major drivers (economic conditions; cultural shifts; policy environments; technology) all have uncertain trajectories.
