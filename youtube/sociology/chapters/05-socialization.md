In 1799, in southern France, hunters in the forest of Aveyron found a human boy living alone. He appeared to be about twelve years old. He was naked, scarred from extensive exposure, mute, and indifferent to fire and cold. He ate only acorns, raw potatoes, and chestnuts. He could not speak, could not respond to his name (he had no name), and showed no interest in other humans.

The boy was eventually taken to Paris and put in the care of a young physician named Jean-Marc-Gaspard Itard. Over five years, Itard tried systematically to teach the boy — whom he named Victor — language, social manners, emotional attachment. Victor learned some specific tasks; he developed some affection for his caretakers; he never learned to speak. He died in 1828, in his early forties, having lived in human society for most of his life but never fully participated in it.

Victor's case fascinated 19th-century thinkers, and continues to fascinate, because it raised a question with no obvious answer: what is human about human beings, and how much of it is *given* by biology vs. *acquired* through living among other humans? Victor was indisputably human in genetic terms. He was also indisputably not the same kind of being as people raised in human society from infancy. The features we associate with being human — language, complex social cooperation, recognition of self in mirrors, the capacity for symbolic thought — appeared to develop in him only partially and only in particular conditions. The full development of those features depended on something that did not happen to him in childhood: the ongoing process of *socialization*.

This is what this chapter is about. Socialization is the lifelong process through which biological human beings become *members* of a society — learning its language, its norms, its categories of identity, its scripts for action. The process is so pervasive and so invisible that most people most of the time do not notice it happening. Cases like Victor, which are rare but not unique (other "feral children" cases include Genie, Kamala and Amala, others), reveal what is normally hidden: socialization is doing enormous work, and the work is specific, identifiable, and consequential.

## Concept 1 — What socialization is and what it does

Socialization is the process through which individuals learn to participate in their society — its language, customs, norms, values, identities, and expectations.

**The biological starting point.** Humans are biologically prepared for socialization in ways that other animals are not. The human infant is born with a capacity for language acquisition that, unlike most mammalian capacities, requires extended interaction with other humans to develop. The capacity for theory of mind (recognizing others as having beliefs and intentions distinct from one's own) develops in the first few years of life. Attachment to caregivers shapes later capacities for emotional regulation. None of this happens in the absence of social interaction.

**The cultural overlay.** What specific language gets acquired, what specific cultural categories shape thought, what specific norms feel natural — these are not given by biology. They are transmitted through socialization, and they vary enormously across societies and across time.

The combination is what produces a person who is recognizably human and recognizably *of* a specific society. A baby born in 2026 in Tokyo and a baby born in 2026 in São Paulo share essentially identical biology. The 25-year-olds they grow into will share much (language, cultural categories, norms, identities) with their respective societies and very little with the other society that they did not grow up in.

### Four types of socialization

The literature distinguishes several phases or types.

**Primary socialization.** The earliest and most foundational — the period from birth through early childhood, when basic capacities (language, attachment, emotional regulation, basic norms) are first acquired. Most of primary socialization happens in the family. The agents involved (parents, siblings, immediate caregivers) have the strongest influence in this period because they are nearly the only available teachers.

**Secondary socialization.** The continued process throughout life — at school, at work, in voluntary associations, in religious communities. Secondary socialization typically deals with more specific roles (student, worker, citizen, member of a club) within the broader social membership established by primary socialization.

**Anticipatory socialization.** Learning the norms and expectations of a role you do not yet have. A college student learning to be a lawyer through pre-law clubs and internships. An engaged person learning what marriage will require. A new parent learning what parenting will involve before the baby arrives. Anticipatory socialization eases later transitions by familiarizing the actor with the role in advance.

**Resocialization.** The replacement of one set of norms and identities with another, usually through extended exposure in a new setting. Boot camp resocializing civilians into soldiers. Religious conversion resocializing the convert into a new community. Therapy resocializing the patient into new patterns of thought and feeling. Resocialization in total institutions (Goffman's term, Ch 4) — prisons, asylums, monasteries, intense rehab programs — is the strongest form, designed to break with prior identity and rebuild from a different foundation.

### What socialization accomplishes

Several specific outcomes:

**Language.** Almost certainly the most consequential. A child socialized into a particular language acquires not just vocabulary but a specific way of carving up the world into categories. The Sapir-Whorf hypothesis (Ch 3) addresses what specific aspects of cognition the language affects. Whatever the resolution of that debate, language is not a thin overlay on a pre-existing human; it is a substantial part of how the person thinks.

**Cultural categories.** What counts as food, work, family, kinship, manhood, womanhood, success, failure, honorable, shameful — these categories vary across cultures and are transmitted through socialization. They feel natural to those who have been socialized into them and strange to outsiders.

**Norms and values.** What you should and shouldn't do; what you should and shouldn't want. The transmission of norms is partly explicit (parents and teachers tell you) and largely implicit (you absorb expectations from observed adult behavior, peer reactions, media depictions).

**Identity categories.** Who you are, in the categorical senses of national membership, ethnic membership, gender, religious identity, generational cohort, class position. Some of these are assigned at birth and reinforced throughout life; some are negotiated; some are contested.

**Skills.** From basic locomotion and toilet-training to specific occupational competencies, the skills you have are mostly products of socialization. The biological capacity for skill acquisition is given; the specific skills acquired are not.

### The trade-off — what socialization makes possible and what it constrains

Socialization makes possible everything we recognize as human social life. Without it, no language, no shared meaning, no coordinated action, no transmission of accumulated knowledge across generations.

Socialization also constrains. The categories one has been socialized into shape what one can imagine, value, recognize as legitimate. People socialized to expect rigid gender roles experience non-conforming behavior as confusing or wrong; people socialized into specific religious frameworks experience alternative frameworks as alien. Reframing one's own categories — recognizing that they are categories rather than reality — is one of the major analytical moves sociology asks of its students.

This is part of what the sociological imagination (Ch 1) does: it makes one's own socialization visible as one specific socialization rather than as just "the way things are." That visibility is uncomfortable; it is also the precondition for much of what serious sociological work tries to do.

> ### Dig Deeper — Your own socialization
> Pick three specific things about yourself — preferences, beliefs, mannerisms — that feel deeply yours. Ask Claude: "Walk me through how each of these might have been transmitted through socialization. What agents (family, school, peers, media) were likely involved? What cultural and historical context shaped their content?" The exercise denaturalizes the self in a useful way.

### Common misconceptions

**"Socialization ends in childhood."** It does not. Socialization continues throughout life, with major resocialization episodes (entering college, starting a career, becoming a parent, retiring, immigrating) producing significant new learning at every life stage.

**"Only children get socialized; adults are who they are."** Adult life involves continuous secondary socialization — learning new roles, adapting to new contexts. The capacity for socialization is highest in childhood but not absent thereafter.

**"Socialization is brainwashing."** No. Socialization has neutral and positive valences as well as negative ones. The acquisition of language, of moral values, of practical competencies — all are socialization. The pejorative term applies to specific extreme cases (cult indoctrination, totalitarian education) where socialization is conducted under unusually coercive conditions and toward unusually narrow ends.

## Concept 2 — The agents of socialization

Specific institutions, actors, and channels do the work of socialization. The major agents:

### Family

The most important agent in primary socialization. Almost everything a young child learns is learned through family — language acquired from caregivers; basic social norms; first experiences with authority, fairness, conflict, love. The family is the first society the child belongs to and remains the model against which later social experiences get interpreted.

Family-as-socializer is shaped by several features: family structure (single-parent, two-parent, extended family, institutional care); class position (the resources, time, and cultural capital the family has); cultural context (what "family" means and does in this society). Annette Lareau's *Unequal Childhoods* (2003), based on extended observation of middle-class and working-class American families, documented systematic differences in how children from different class positions are socialized — middle-class families practicing "concerted cultivation" (intensive schedule of activities, articulate child-adult dialogue, training in negotiation with institutions), working-class families practicing "natural growth" (more unstructured time, clearer hierarchical authority, less institutional negotiation training). Lareau's argument: these differences in socialization produce differences in skills and dispositions that shape later educational and occupational outcomes.

Family structure and dynamics are not stable across history. The intensive nuclear family is a relatively recent historical formation in most societies; extended kinship networks and varied household compositions have been more typical. The contemporary American emphasis on parenting as a deeply intentional, time-intensive project ("intensive parenting" in Sharon Hays's term) is also historically specific.

### School

Schools take over a substantial part of secondary socialization. They explicitly teach specific skills (reading, math, content knowledge) and implicitly teach much else — norms of authority, time discipline, individual achievement, classroom interaction protocols, bureaucratic compliance. The "hidden curriculum" of schools (Philip Jackson's term) refers to these implicit lessons.

Schools also sort. Tracking, ability grouping, advanced courses, vocational vs. college-prep tracks — these structures shape what specific skills different students develop, what credentials they acquire, what occupational paths they pursue. The sorting function is partly explicit (test-based placement) and largely implicit (families' differential capacity to navigate school institutions in the child's interest).

The class effects of school socialization are well-documented and contested. Bowles and Gintis's *Schooling in Capitalist America* (1976) argued that schools reproduce class structure by training working-class children for compliant subordinate work and middle-class children for autonomous professional roles. Subsequent work has refined the argument — the mechanisms are more varied than Bowles and Gintis claimed, and the school system has both reproductive and mobility-promoting features. The empirical literature is large; the patterns of class-based school socialization are real.

### Peers

Peer influence is enormous, especially in adolescence. The peer group provides socialization in domains the family cannot — what is currently cool, what specific subcultural norms apply, how to navigate romantic and sexual relationships, how to manage status competition. Judith Rich Harris's *The Nurture Assumption* (1998) controversially argued that peers, not parents, do most of the work in shaping personality and outcomes. Subsequent work has moderated the strong claim while confirming peers as enormously influential, especially in domains specific to peer interaction.

The peer group's influence has likely intensified in the era of social media. Adolescents now spend large fractions of their attention immersed in peer-driven environments, with parental visibility into those environments often limited. The political and mental-health implications of this shift are still being worked out (Ch 8 examines media and technology in more detail).

### Mass media and digital platforms

Television, film, news media, and digital platforms are powerful socializing forces. They depict what kinds of people exist, what they do, what they want; they normalize some patterns and stigmatize others; they provide models of speech, dress, and behavior.

The empirical literature on media effects is mixed. Strong "media effects" claims (the simple view that violent media produce violent children, that specific advertisements make specific consumer behaviors) have generally not survived rigorous testing. Weaker but more credible claims — that long-term exposure to media patterns shapes cultural assumptions, expectations, and self-images — are better supported.

Digital platforms add new dimensions: algorithmically personalized feeds; the dynamics of social-media social comparison; the affordances of asynchronous text communication that differ from face-to-face interaction. The socializing effects of these technologies are an active area of research; results are partial and contested.

### Other agents

Religious communities, workplaces, voluntary associations, the state (through laws, public communication, civic rituals), neighborhoods, branches of the military, fraternal organizations — all have socializing effects, with varying weight in different lives.

> ### Dig Deeper — Compare two socialization environments
> Pick two settings you have lived in or know well — perhaps two schools you attended, two workplaces, or two cultural environments. Ask Claude: "What were the dominant agents of socialization in each setting? What did each setting teach explicitly? What did each teach implicitly? Where do the two environments overlap, and where do they conflict?" The exercise reveals how socialization works through specific situated learning.

### The trade-off — coordination and conflict among agents

When the agents of socialization align — family, school, peer group, religious community, and media all delivering compatible messages — socialization proceeds smoothly. The child receives consistent expectations across settings.

When they misalign — when the family says one thing, school another, peers a third, media a fourth — socialization becomes more complex, sometimes conflictual. Children of immigrants often experience this acutely: the parents' culture transmitted at home, the host society's culture at school, the peer culture in adolescence, often pulling in different directions. The same child may be trilingual or quadricultural in a productive sense or feel torn between incompatible demands depending on how the various agents coordinate or fail to.

The contemporary American debate about parenting, schooling, and media often turns on perceived misalignments. Parents who feel that schools or media are teaching values inconsistent with their own; schools that feel parents are not reinforcing classroom norms; media critics who argue digital platforms socialize adolescents in directions parents and educators cannot match. The debates are at root debates about whose agents should have what weight in socialization.

### Common misconceptions

**"Parents are the most important socializing agent."** Parents are extremely important, especially in primary socialization. Other agents (peers, school, media) become increasingly important as children age, and may rival or exceed parental influence in specific domains.

**"Media effects are minor."** They are more complex and harder to identify than simple cause-and-effect models suggest, but they are not zero. Long-run exposure shapes cultural categories and expectations in ways that careful research can detect.

**"You can opt out of socialization."** You cannot fully. Even refusing to participate in mainstream socializing institutions (homeschooling, media abstinence, religious withdrawal) involves alternative socializing arrangements. The choice is which agents shape socialization, not whether socialization happens.

## Concept 3 — The social self: Mead, Cooley, and the development of identity

Where does the sense of self come from? Sociology's classical answer, developed by George Herbert Mead and Charles Horton Cooley in the early 20th century, is that the self is fundamentally social — produced through interaction, not given prior to it.

### Cooley's looking-glass self

Charles Horton Cooley (1864–1929) introduced the **looking-glass self** in *Human Nature and the Social Order* (1902). The self, Cooley argued, develops through three steps:

1. We imagine how we appear to others.
2. We imagine how others judge that appearance.
3. We feel an emotional response — pride, shame, embarrassment — to that imagined judgment.

The self is, on this account, a kind of mirror — the person seeing themselves through what they imagine others see. Cooley's framework captures a feature of self-consciousness that other accounts had missed: the self is not first private and then made public; it is constituted from the beginning by imagined others.

The framework illuminates phenomena like blushing (a physiological response to imagined social judgment), shyness (anxiety about imagined social evaluation), pride and shame (emotions that depend on imagined social standing). It also illuminates the developmental sequence: very young children do not yet have a fully developed looking-glass self; the capacity emerges through social experience.

### Mead's I and me

George Herbert Mead (1863–1931) developed Cooley's framework into a more elaborate theory in *Mind, Self, and Society* (1934, published posthumously from his lectures at the University of Chicago).

Mead distinguished two aspects of the self:

**The "I"** — the self as active, spontaneous subject. The "I" is the source of action, the part of the self that initiates rather than reacts.

**The "me"** — the self as social object, the self viewed through the perspectives of others. The "me" is the internalized image of self that the actor uses to monitor and adjust the "I"'s actions.

Self-aware action, in Mead's account, is a continuous interplay between the "I" and the "me." The "I" wants to do something; the "me" anticipates how it will be received; the actor adjusts. Without the "me," action is impulsive; without the "I," action is rigidly conformist; together, they produce the kind of socially-monitored action that characterizes most adult social life.

### The generalized other

Mead's most influential concept: the **generalized other**.

Children, Mead argued, develop selfhood through stages. In the first stage (play), they take on specific roles — playing "house," playing "doctor," playing the role of a particular other person. In the second stage (game), they learn to coordinate with multiple others simultaneously — like a baseball player who has to anticipate the actions of teammates and opponents. In the final stage, they internalize the "generalized other" — the standpoint of the community as a whole, or of any specific reference group.

The generalized other lets the actor monitor their behavior against an internalized image of "what people would think." It is the social standpoint built into the self, allowing self-regulation that does not depend on the immediate presence of any specific other.

The framework explains how socialization produces a person who can act "appropriately" even alone — who knows, without being told, what is expected and self-regulates accordingly. The generalized other is the social writ-large inside the individual.

### Erikson and lifelong development

Erik Erikson's *Childhood and Society* (1950) extended this developmental framing across the lifespan. Erikson proposed eight stages of psychosocial development, each defined by a central tension:

1. Trust vs. mistrust (infancy)
2. Autonomy vs. shame and doubt (early childhood)
3. Initiative vs. guilt (preschool)
4. Industry vs. inferiority (school age)
5. Identity vs. role confusion (adolescence)
6. Intimacy vs. isolation (young adulthood)
7. Generativity vs. stagnation (middle adulthood)
8. Integrity vs. despair (late adulthood)

Each stage's tension, in Erikson's account, gets resolved more or less successfully through interaction with the relevant social environment. Successful resolution at each stage produces specific psychological capacities (trust, autonomy, identity, intimacy, etc.). Unsuccessful resolution produces specific deficits.

The framework has been criticized — it is a stage theory in an era when stage theories have generally been replaced by more continuous models; the empirical evidence for the specific stages is mixed. The broader argument — that personal development continues throughout life and is shaped by ongoing social interaction — has held up.

### The trade-off — what Mead/Cooley/Erikson illuminate and what they miss

The frameworks make sense of the social character of selfhood. They explain phenomena (shame, identity development, the capacity for self-monitoring alone) that purely individualistic accounts of personality cannot.

The frameworks have limits. They focus heavily on internalized others and may underplay structural constraints on selfhood — the ways that material conditions, embodied experience, and macro-social forces shape who one can be, not just how one is internally constituted. Bourdieu's habitus (Ch 9) and feminist work on embodied subjectivity address some of these limits.

### Common misconceptions

**"The looking-glass self means we just are what others see."** No. We are what we imagine others see, which is filtered through our own interpretation. The self is not a passive mirror; it is an active interpreter.

**"Mead's framework is just psychology."** The "I" and "me" describe a structure of self-consciousness produced by social interaction. The framework is sociological precisely because it traces the formation of the inner life back to the social world that produced it.

**"Identity is just one thing."** It is not. Most adults have multiple identities — occupational, familial, religious, political, etc. — that are activated in different contexts. The integration of these multiple identities, and conflicts among them, is one of the recurring questions in sociological work on the self.

## Synthesis — socialization as the bridge between individual and society

Socialization is the bridge between the macro-sociological patterns this book studies and the individual lives those patterns are made of. The wage gap, the educational gap, the political polarization, the family-form differences — all are produced through millions of individual lives whose trajectories are shaped by the socialization those lives received.

This means socialization is consequential. The kind of family a person was born into, the schools they attended, the peer groups they joined, the media they consumed — all contribute to who they became and what they can do.

It also means socialization is contestable. What schools should teach, what families should emphasize, what media should be available, how peer groups should be constituted — all are subjects of ongoing political contest, because they shape the kind of people the next generation will be.

The student equipped with this chapter's tools holds three things at once. First, that their own self was substantially constituted through socialization processes they did not choose. Second, that the socialization processes were shaped by the specific society and historical moment they were born into. Third, that the socialization continues — every day's interaction is a continued socialization episode — and that they can, with some effort, begin to direct it more intentionally.

This is part of what sociological literacy buys you: the ability to see the social as it shapes the personal, and the personal as one outcome of many possible outcomes that the social could have produced.

## Chapter summary

Socialization is the lifelong process through which individuals learn to participate in their society — its language, norms, values, identities, and skills. The major agents (family, school, peers, media, others) operate with varying weight at different life stages. Mead's and Cooley's frameworks explain how the self is constituted through social interaction — through the looking-glass self, through the interplay of "I" and "me," through the internalization of the generalized other. Erikson extended developmental analysis across the lifespan. Socialization makes possible everything we recognize as human social life and constrains what individuals can imagine, value, and do; recognizing one's own socialization is part of what sociological literacy provides. The student who has worked through this chapter sees their own self as a social product — partly chosen, mostly transmitted — and gains the capacity to direct further socialization more intentionally.

## What would change my mind

If extensive twin and adoption studies showed that environmental variation in socialization explained very little of adult outcome variation — that genetic factors and individual choice did most of the work — I would update my reading of socialization's importance. The behavioral-genetics literature does in fact show substantial heritability for many outcomes, and the share of variance attributable to "shared environment" (family-level effects) is often smaller than naive expectations suggest. But the literature also shows substantial environmental effects, especially through "non-shared environment" (which includes peer and broader social context). My current view balances the empirical findings; substantial revision of the behavioral genetics consensus would prompt revision of my view.

## Still puzzling

I do not have a confident view on how digital socialization in childhood will affect adult outcomes for the cohort growing up now. The first generation to spend substantial portions of their early life in algorithmically curated environments is now reaching adulthood, and we are starting to see results, but the long-run effects are not yet clear.
