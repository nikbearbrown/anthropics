**TL;DR.** Media and technology are not just tools we use; they are environments we live in. Each major communication technology — print, broadcast, digital networks, algorithmic feeds — has restructured the social world in ways that took decades to be visible. The contemporary platform economy is the latest instance, with consequences for politics, mental health, culture, work, and intimacy that the discipline is still working to characterize.

---

In 1994, a journalist named Howard Rheingold published a book called *The Virtual Community*. The book described early online communities — the WELL, USENET groups, IRC channels — and made an argument that sounded extravagant at the time: that human social life was being restructured by computer-mediated communication, and that the consequences would extend far beyond the small community of early adopters who had access in 1994. The book described people forming friendships through text alone; supporting each other through life crises across continents; building shared culture, conflicts, and politics in environments that had no physical location.

Rheingold was right about the trajectory and roughly right about the importance, even if specific predictions did not all come true. Three decades later, the world Rheingold glimpsed has become the dominant social environment for billions of people. By 2025, the average American adult spent something like 7-8 hours per day on screens; the average teenager spent more. Most of the world's population had a smartphone within reach for most of the day. Most of the political news, most of the social comparison, most of the friendship maintenance, most of the cultural production and consumption — all flowed through screens connected to networks that did not exist when Rheingold was writing.

The transformation has been so total that it is hard to see clearly. We are inside the change. The fish, as the saying goes, do not notice the water. This chapter is an attempt to step back enough to see the water — to ask what these technologies are doing to social life, what the empirical evidence shows about their effects, and what frameworks help us think about questions whose answers are still being settled.

3. Diagnose the digital divide — its specific dimensions and how it intersects with other forms of stratification.
4. Read the empirical literature on social media's effects (especially on adolescent mental health, political polarization, and information environments) carefully — recognizing what is established, what is contested, and what is unsettled.

## Concept 1 — Communication technologies and their consequences

A useful way to think about media and technology: each major communication transition has restructured social life in ways that took decades to fully appear and that have not always been welcome.

### Oral, print, broadcast, digital

Until the development of writing (around 5,000 years ago in Mesopotamia, with parallel developments elsewhere), human communication was almost entirely face-to-face and oral. Memory was the medium for transmitting accumulated knowledge across generations. Authority was personal, bounded by who could reach whom physically. Communities of meaning were generally bounded by geography.

**Print.** The development of moveable type in 15th-century Europe (Gutenberg, c. 1440) — and the parallel earlier developments in East Asia — made text mass-producible. The consequences took two centuries to fully unfold: the Reformation depended on cheap printed Bibles; the scientific revolution depended on printed journals enabling collaboration across distances; the rise of nationalism depended partly on the standardization of vernacular languages through print. Benedict Anderson's *Imagined Communities* (1983) traced how print made possible the imagined fellowship of nation-states whose members would never meet.

**Broadcast.** Radio (in the 1920s) and television (in the 1950s) allowed simultaneous transmission to mass audiences from a small number of sources. The mid-20th century in advanced economies was the era of broadcast — a few major networks reaching most of the population; shared informational baselines; mass-audience cultural forms (network television, top-40 radio, mass-circulation newspapers). The consequences included unprecedented capacity for political mobilization (FDR's fireside chats; political rallies broadcast nationally), mass-consumer markets organized around branded advertising, and a degree of cultural homogenization within national boundaries.

**Digital networking.** The personal computer (1980s), the World Wide Web (early 1990s), broadband internet (2000s onward), smartphones (2007 onward) — each step in this sequence shifted what was possible. Digital networking enabled bidirectional communication at scale (anyone could publish; anyone could be read), custom production and distribution (no longer dependent on a small number of broadcasters), persistent storage and search, computer-mediated relationships across any distance.

**Algorithmic curation.** From around 2009-2012 onward (with the introduction of algorithmic feeds at major platforms — Facebook, YouTube, eventually Instagram, TikTok, and others), the experience of digital media has been increasingly shaped by algorithms that select and order content based on engagement metrics. This is a distinct phase from the earlier "Web 1.0 / Web 2.0" eras, with consequences worth examining specifically.

### What each transition made possible

Each transition increased certain capacities and constrained others.

Print made possible distributed authority over knowledge (no longer requiring personal presence with a teacher), but constrained knowledge to those literate in the languages of print. Broadcast made possible mass coordination of national-scale audiences but constrained who could speak to those audiences. Digital networking enabled democratized publication but constrained attention through information overload. Algorithmic curation enabled personalized media experiences but constrained the shared informational baseline that earlier media had provided.

The pattern: each transition has been both democratizing and centralizing, both enabling and constraining, in different dimensions. Strong "this technology is good" or "this technology is bad" framings miss the structure. The interesting questions are about specific dimensions of effect.

### The trade-off — technological determinism vs. social shaping

A foundational debate in the sociology of technology: how much do technologies determine social outcomes vs. social conditions shape what technologies become?

**Technological determinism** holds that technologies have specific properties that produce specific social outcomes, more or less independently of who uses them. Marshall McLuhan's *Understanding Media* (1964) is a classical statement: "the medium is the message" — the technology itself shapes social life, regardless of what specific content flows through it.

**Social shaping of technology** holds that technologies are products of social choices, designed and deployed in specific contexts, and that social arrangements substantially shape what technologies do. The same technology can produce very different outcomes depending on regulatory frameworks, business models, cultural patterns of use, and other social variables.

Most contemporary sociologists adopt some hybrid: technologies have specific affordances (what they make easy and hard) that shape the range of likely outcomes, but social context determines which outcomes within that range actually emerge. The same internet has produced very different patterns in China, the EU, the U.S., and other places — partly because of different regulatory environments, partly because of different cultural and political traditions. Technology is shaping; social shaping of technology is also real.

> ### Dig Deeper — A specific transition's consequences
> Pick a communication technology transition (print to broadcast; broadcast to digital; pre-algorithmic digital to algorithmic feeds). Where were the predictions accurate, and where did they miss?" The exercise provides perspective on contemporary changes still in progress.

### Common misconceptions

**"Technology is neutral; only its use matters."** Technologies are not neutral. They have specific affordances that shape what is easy and what is hard. The same person trying to publish a printed book in 1500 vs. a tweet in 2024 faces dramatically different constraints; the technology shapes what kinds of speech are easy. Neutrality framings miss this.

**"Technology determines social change."** Pure determinism misses how social arrangements shape technological deployment. The same internet has produced different societies in different countries.

**"New technologies are always disruptive."** Some are; some are not. Many technologies are absorbed into existing patterns without restructuring them; some produce limited niche change; a few produce dramatic restructuring. Predicting in advance which is which is difficult.

## Concept 2 — The digital divide

A specific empirical pattern that sociology has tracked: the **digital divide** — systematic differences across populations in access to and use of digital technology.

The first-order digital divide is access: who has internet, who has high-speed internet, who has smartphones, who has access to the devices and connectivity needed for digital life. In the United States in 2024, roughly 90% of adults used the internet, but with substantial gaps: rural areas had lower broadband access; lower-income households had less reliable connectivity; older adults had lower usage rates; some racial and ethnic groups had different access patterns.

Second-order divides — once everyone has access — include differences in *how* people use digital technology. Some uses (information-seeking, networking for opportunities, accessing services) translate into resources and capabilities; other uses (passive consumption, exposure to misinformation, problematic gambling, exploitation by predatory platforms) produce harms. Eszter Hargittai and other researchers have documented that simply having access doesn't equalize digital outcomes; the skills, the social context, and the platforms' design all shape what specific outcomes follow from access.

Third-order divides include the algorithmic stratification produced by personalized feeds. Different users see different versions of the digital world, with consequences for what information they have, what opportunities they encounter, what social comparisons they make. The variation is partly chosen (people select what they engage with) and partly imposed (algorithms select and rank what users see).

Globally, the divide is much larger. Roughly half the world's population has internet access; the other half does not. The digital economy operates at very different scales in advanced and developing economies, with consequences for opportunity, productivity, and inequality both within and between countries.

### Digital divide and inequality

A specific concern: the digital divide intersects with and amplifies other forms of inequality. Children in higher-income households arrive at school with more digital skills and home digital resources; the gap shapes educational outcomes. Workers with digital skills earn more and have more job-market mobility. Communities with better digital infrastructure attract more economic investment.

The COVID-19 pandemic made this concrete. When schools shifted to remote learning in 2020, students with reliable home internet and adequate devices continued to learn; students without these did not. The pandemic-era learning losses were dramatically larger for lower-income students, with consequences that follow them through subsequent education and likely into labor-market outcomes.

This is not a story about technology causing inequality. It is a story about technology operating within existing patterns of inequality and producing differential outcomes that track those patterns.

> ### Dig Deeper — Map the divide in a specific community
> Pick a community you know — your hometown, your campus, a city you have visited. Ask Claude: "What does the digital divide look like in this community? Who has and doesn't have reliable broadband? Who has and doesn't have digital skills? How does the divide intersect with other forms of inequality?" Verify against publicly available data (FCC broadband maps; Pew Research; community surveys).

### Common misconceptions

**"The digital divide is about technology alone."** It is about technology, skills, social context, and the broader inequalities that shape who can benefit from digital opportunity.

**"As technology spreads, the divide will close."** First-order access has narrowed substantially; second- and third-order divides have proven more persistent. Closing physical-access gaps does not automatically close usage and outcome gaps.

## Concept 3 — Platform capitalism and the attention economy

A specific contemporary phenomenon worth examining: the rise of digital platforms as dominant economic and social institutions.

### What platforms do

A platform, in the sense most relevant here, is a digital service that connects users and produces value through that connection. Google connects searchers and information; Amazon connects buyers and sellers; YouTube and TikTok connect creators and viewers; Uber connects riders and drivers; Facebook and Instagram connect users in social networks; Twitter/X connects users in public conversation.

Most major platforms make money through advertising. The platform attracts users by providing some valued service; it captures user attention and data; it sells access to that attention to advertisers. The revenue model has specific consequences for how platforms operate: maximizing user time on the platform, maximizing engagement metrics that correlate with ad value, gathering as much user data as legally permissible.

The economic scale is enormous. A small number of platforms dominate substantial fractions of digital advertising. Google and Meta together capture something like 60% of global digital advertising spending. The advertising-supported business model is particularly powerful for free services with very large user bases — and it creates incentives that shape user experience in ways users don't always notice.

### The attention economy

The phrase "attention economy" — used by various scholars including Herbert Simon (who pointed in the 1970s that an information-rich world produces an attention-poor experience), Tim Wu, and others — describes the economic dynamics of an environment where attention rather than content is scarce. In the print era, content was scarce and expensive to produce; people paid to access it. In the digital era, content is essentially free and infinitely replicable; what is scarce is the user's attention. Platforms compete to capture and hold attention.

This has reshaped many things. News media has been transformed (Ch 16 of the political-science book covers this in depth). Music industry has been restructured. Television has fragmented. Long-form content competes with short-form, with engagement metrics often favoring shorter and more emotionally engaging content. Algorithmic optimization for engagement, given the data on what produces engagement, often favors content that produces strong emotional reactions — outrage, surprise, fear, social comparison, parasocial intimacy.

The user-experience consequences of this optimization are still being studied. Some are clear: time on platforms has grown dramatically; some kinds of content reliably outperform others (videos with high emotional content, posts that produce comments and shares, content that triggers comparison); the average attention span for sustained engagement with a single text appears to have declined.

### Empirical evidence on social media's effects

A specific area of contested empirical literature: the effects of social media on adolescent mental health, political polarization, and democratic discourse.

**Adolescent mental health.** Several lines of evidence converge on a striking pattern: rates of depression, anxiety, self-harm, and suicide among adolescents (especially girls) in advanced economies have risen substantially since around 2012, coincident with the rise of smartphones and algorithmic social media. Jonathan Haidt's *The Anxious Generation* (2024) synthesizes much of this evidence and argues a strong causal claim. Critics including Andrew Przybylski, Candice Odgers, and others have argued that the evidence is more mixed; some specific findings replicate poorly, the effect sizes in many studies are small, and the causal claim from smartphones to mental-health declines has not been definitively established. The pattern is real; the magnitude and the specific causal mechanism remain debated.

**Political polarization.** Some research suggests social media intensifies political polarization through filter bubbles, algorithmic amplification of polarizing content, and the destructive dynamics of online political discourse. Other research has found weaker effects — most users are exposed to diverse political content, polarization predates social media in most measures, and the strongest effects are concentrated among small numbers of highly active users. The pattern is genuinely contested.

**Information environment.** Concerns about misinformation, polarized information environments, and erosion of shared baseline have been widely raised. Empirical work (Allcott and Gentzkow 2017; Guess et al. multiple papers; Grinberg et al. 2019) has produced findings that complicate simple narratives — most users are not heavily exposed to misinformation; the most-affected users are concentrated demographic and political groups; the policy implications are genuinely uncertain.

### What this means for sociology

A serious sociological reading of platform capitalism holds several things together. The platforms are real, they have real effects, and the effects are at least partly produced by deliberate design choices made for commercial reasons. The empirical evidence on specific harms is substantial but contested. The strong "platforms are destroying society" narratives oversimplify; the strong "platforms are just neutral tools" framings also oversimplify. The interesting analytical work is in the middle.

> ### Dig Deeper — A specific platform's design
> Pick a platform you use regularly — TikTok, Instagram, X/Twitter, YouTube, Facebook, LinkedIn. Ask Claude: "Walk me through the specific design features of this platform that shape user behavior. How does the algorithm select content? What metrics does the platform optimize for? What user behaviors does the design encourage and discourage? How do the design choices connect to the platform's revenue model?" Verify against the platform's public documentation, journalist reporting, and academic studies.

### Common misconceptions

**"Platforms are just providing what users want."** Platforms shape what users want by selecting and ranking content. The supply side is not neutral.

**"Engagement metrics measure user satisfaction."** They measure user response — clicks, comments, shares, time. Some user responses are pleasant; some are compulsive or distressing. Engagement is not the same as satisfaction.

**"All concerns about social media are moral panic."** Some are. Some are based on genuine empirical findings. The framework of moral panic (Ch 7) is helpful for distinguishing proportionate and disproportionate concern, but it does not license dismissing all concern.

## Concept 4 — Theoretical frameworks on media and technology

The major sociological frameworks each illuminate different aspects of media and technology.

**Functionalist framing.** Media and communication technologies serve specific functions: information transmission, social coordination, cultural reproduction, social cohesion. From a functionalist standpoint, the rise of mass media in the 20th century was a functional response to the coordination problems of large-scale industrial society; the digital era is a functional response to the coordination problems of an even more complex globalized society.

**Conflict-theoretic framing.** Media and technology are tools of power and capital. Whose interests are served by the platforms' design? Whose voices are amplified? Whose are marginalized? How does the political economy of platforms shape what gets produced and what gets distributed? Robert McChesney's work on the political economy of communication and many subsequent scholars work in this tradition.

**Interactionist framing.** What does media use mean to users? How do platforms get integrated into the texture of everyday life? How do users construct identity, relationships, and social meaning through digital interaction? The interactionist tradition has produced rich studies of specific platform cultures, online communities, and the meaning of digital interaction in users' lives.

These frameworks are not mutually exclusive. The fullest analysis usually integrates multiple framings. A study of how teenage girls use Instagram, for example, benefits from interactionist attention to what it means to them, conflict-theoretic attention to who profits from their use and who pays the costs, and functionalist attention to what work the platform does in their broader social lives.

### Common misconceptions

**"You should pick a framework."** The interesting work draws on multiple frameworks pragmatically.

**"Cultural critique of platforms is just nostalgia."** Some is. Some is not. Critiques that identify specific mechanisms and document specific harms are doing different work from generalized nostalgia for pre-digital life.

## Synthesis — reading media and technology sociologically

A serious reading of contemporary media and technology holds several things together.

The technologies are powerful. They have reshaped daily life, social interaction, political discourse, work, and intimacy in ways that took less than two decades to play out. The transformation has been dramatic.

The empirical evidence on specific effects is substantial and partial. Much of it is contested in detail. Strong causal claims are often weakened by careful examination; some effects are real but smaller than initial discussion suggests; some are real and serious; some are still being characterized.

The political economy of platforms shapes what they do. The advertising-revenue model produces specific incentives. Different ownership structures and regulatory environments would produce different platforms.

The frameworks (functionalist, conflict-theoretic, interactionist) each illuminate parts of the picture. The pure techno-determinist and pure technological-neutralist views both miss important features.

Reform is hard. The market dynamics that produced the current platform configuration are not easily disrupted; the regulatory tools available are limited; the cultural patterns of use are entrenched. Whether the next decade produces meaningful reform of the current platform configuration, the entrenchment of current patterns, or some unforeseen disruption is genuinely uncertain.

The student equipped with this chapter's tools reads contemporary media and technology critically — neither uncritically embracing nor dismissing — and recognizes both the genuine new capabilities digital technology has produced and the specific harms its current configuration produces.

## Chapter summary

Media and technology have reshaped social life across every major transition — print, broadcast, digital networking, algorithmic curation. Each transition has been simultaneously democratizing and centralizing, enabling and constraining, in different dimensions. The contemporary platform economy is the latest instance, with a specific revenue model (mostly advertising), specific design features (engagement-optimizing algorithms), and specific empirical consequences (still being characterized) for mental health, political discourse, work, and intimacy. The digital divide intersects with and amplifies other forms of inequality. The empirical literature on platform effects is substantial and partial; strong simple narratives in either direction tend not to survive careful examination. The serious sociological reading of contemporary media and technology integrates multiple theoretical frameworks, takes the political economy seriously, and resists both uncritical embrace and reflexive dismissal.

## What would change my mind

If a sustained period of strong regulatory intervention or platform redesign produced substantial reductions in the harms documented in current empirical literature, I would update my view that the current platform configuration is more problematic than reformers can easily address. The current evidence on policy effectiveness is mixed; meaningful reform has not yet been demonstrated at scale.

## Still puzzling

I do not have a confident view on what large language models — including the technology this textbook explicitly engages with — will do to social and informational life over the next decade. The shift may be on the scale of earlier major communication technology transitions, or it may be smaller and more easily absorbed. The next decade will tell.
