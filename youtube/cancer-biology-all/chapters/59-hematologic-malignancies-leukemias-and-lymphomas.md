# Chapter 59 — Hematologic Malignancies: Leukemias and Lymphomas


## TL;DR

- The cancers of blood are the cancers we've made the most progress on.
- The chapter moves through The hematologic malignancy landscape, Acute lymphoblastic leukemia, Acute myeloid leukemia, Chronic myeloid leukemia, and related ideas.
- Read it for the main argument, the vocabulary it introduces, and the practical judgment it asks you to develop.

The cancers of blood are the cancers we've made the most progress on.

Hold the framing. If you ask which cancers have been most dramatically transformed by modern therapy, the answer keeps coming back to hematologic malignancies. Childhood acute lymphoblastic leukemia went from uniformly fatal to >90% cure. Chronic myeloid leukemia went from a 5-year median survival to a chronic disease managed by daily pill. Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma is curable in the majority of patients. Hodgkin lymphoma is one of the most curable cancers. Multiple myeloma median survival has more than tripled. CAR-T cell therapy was first proven in B-cell leukemias. Bispecific T-cell engagers were first approved for B-cell malignancies. The bone marrow transplant infrastructure that enables many cancer therapies emerged from hematologic malignancy treatment.

Several features make blood cancers more treatable than solid tumors. The cells are accessible — easily sampled through blood and bone marrow, monitored through laboratory tests, treated with drugs that distribute in the bloodstream. The cancer cells often have well-defined surface markers (CD19, CD20, CD22, CD30, CD33, BCMA) that make them targetable. Many are driven by single dominant genetic events (BCR-ABL in CML, PML-RARA in APL) that are exquisitely druggable. The bone marrow is a privileged site for stem cell transplantation, allowing replacement of an entire affected hematopoietic system.

This chapter — the first of two on hematologic malignancies — covers the leukemias and lymphomas. The next — 33B — covers plasma cell neoplasms and broader treatment principles.

Hold the question: *given the special features of blood cancers, what makes them more tractable than solid tumors, and what does this mean for the future of oncology?*

## The hematologic malignancy landscape

Hematologic malignancies are cancers of blood-forming and immune cells. The major categories:

*Leukemias*. Cancers of hematopoietic cells circulating in blood and accumulating in bone marrow. Acute (rapidly progressive) versus chronic (more indolent). Lymphoid versus myeloid based on cell of origin.

*Lymphomas*. Cancers of lymphocytes forming solid masses, primarily in lymph nodes. Hodgkin versus non-Hodgkin. Various subtypes by cell of origin and molecular features.

*Plasma cell neoplasms*. Multiple myeloma and related disorders. Discussed in 33B.

*Myeloproliferative neoplasms*. Chronic myeloid leukemia, polycythemia vera, essential thrombocythemia, primary myelofibrosis.

*Myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS)*. Disorders of hematopoietic differentiation that can progress to AML.

The 5th edition WHO Classification of Hematolymphoid Tumours (2022, updated 2023) and the International Consensus Classification (ICC, 2022) provide the current frameworks. Both heavily integrate molecular features into diagnosis.

## Acute lymphoblastic leukemia

Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is the most common childhood cancer and also affects adults. Treatment has improved dramatically over decades.

*Epidemiology*. Annual incidence about 1.5 per 100,000. Peak incidence in children 2-5 years. Second peak in older adults. Slight male predominance.

*Subtypes*. Based on lymphoid lineage (B-cell vs T-cell) and molecular features:

*B-cell ALL*. The majority. Subtypes by molecular abnormalities:
- *Philadelphia chromosome-positive (Ph+ ALL)*. BCR-ABL fusion. More common in adults. Historically had poor prognosis; transformed by addition of TKIs (imatinib, dasatinib, ponatinib) to chemotherapy regimens.
- *Ph-like ALL*. Genetic similarity to Ph+ but without the BCR-ABL fusion. Various other kinase-activating alterations. Often poor prognosis without targeted therapy.
- *Hyperdiploid* (gain of chromosomes). Generally good prognosis.
- *Hypodiploid* (loss of chromosomes). Generally poor prognosis.
- *t(12;21)/ETV6-RUNX1*. Good prognosis. Common in children.
- *KMT2A (MLL)-rearranged*. Common in infants. Generally poor prognosis. Menin inhibitors (revumenib, approved 2024) are a recent advance.
- *iAMP21*. Intrachromosomal amplification of chromosome 21. Poor prognosis.

*T-cell ALL*. About 10-15% of ALL. Several molecular subtypes including early T-cell precursor (ETP) ALL.

*Presentation*. Symptoms reflect bone marrow failure (anemia, thrombocytopenia, neutropenia) and tissue infiltration:
- Fatigue, pallor (anemia).
- Bruising, bleeding (thrombocytopenia).
- Infection (neutropenia).
- Bone pain (marrow infiltration).
- Lymphadenopathy.
- Hepatosplenomegaly.
- CNS involvement.
- Mediastinal mass in T-ALL.

*Diagnosis*. Bone marrow aspiration with flow cytometry, cytogenetics, FISH, and molecular profiling. Peripheral blood often shows lymphoblasts. CSF analysis to assess CNS involvement.

*Treatment*. Multi-agent intensive chemotherapy in defined phases:
1. *Induction*. Several weeks of intensive chemotherapy to achieve remission. Typical agents include vincristine, anthracyclines (daunorubicin), corticosteroids, asparaginase, sometimes cyclophosphamide.
2. *Consolidation*. Months of continued intensive therapy to deepen response.
3. *CNS prophylaxis*. Intrathecal methotrexate and/or high-dose methotrexate to prevent CNS relapse. Cranial radiation rarely used now in standard cases.
4. *Maintenance*. Lower-intensity chemotherapy (6-mercaptopurine + methotrexate) for 2-3 years total treatment duration.

Multiple variations of regimens. CALGB, COG (pediatric), GMALL, hyper-CVAD (adult) are examples.

*Targeted therapy additions*:
- *TKIs for Ph+ ALL*. Imatinib, dasatinib, ponatinib added to chemotherapy. Has transformed Ph+ ALL outcomes.
- *Blinatumomab*. Anti-CD19/CD3 bispecific T-cell engager. Now used in consolidation and for MRD-positive disease.
- *Inotuzumab ozogamicin*. Anti-CD22 antibody-drug conjugate.
- *CAR-T cells*. Tisagenlecleucel for pediatric and young adult relapsed/refractory B-ALL; brexucabtagene autoleucel for adult B-ALL.

*Stem cell transplant*. For high-risk disease, MRD-positive after intensive therapy, or relapsed disease.

*Minimal residual disease (MRD) monitoring*. Flow cytometry or PCR-based detection of low-level residual leukemia after treatment. MRD status guides treatment intensification or de-escalation.

*Outcomes*. Pediatric ALL: 5-year overall survival around 90%. Adult ALL: 50-60% with modern protocols. Older adults have worse outcomes. The improvement in pediatric ALL outcomes from <10% in the 1960s to >90% today represents one of the great success stories in oncology.

## Acute myeloid leukemia

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is more common in adults and biologically diverse.

*Epidemiology*. Annual incidence about 4-5 per 100,000. Median age at diagnosis around 68. Increases with age. Risk factors include prior chemotherapy or radiation, certain occupational exposures (benzene), genetic predispositions, antecedent hematologic disorders (MDS, MPN).

*Molecular classification*. The 2022 ICC and 5th edition WHO Classification organize AML primarily by molecular features:

*AML with defining genetic abnormalities*:
- *RUNX1::RUNX1T1* (formerly t(8;21)). Generally favorable prognosis.
- *CBFB::MYH11* (formerly inv(16) or t(16;16)). Favorable prognosis.
- *PML::RARA* (acute promyelocytic leukemia, APL, formerly t(15;17)). Highly curable with ATRA + arsenic trioxide.
- *KMT2A (MLL)-rearranged*. Often poor prognosis.
- *DEK::NUP214*, *RBM15::MRTFA*, *MECOM*, *BCR::ABL1* (rare in AML), and others.
- *NPM1-mutated*. Generally favorable when isolated.
- *CEBPA-mutated* (biallelic). Favorable.

*AML with mutated TP53*. Always poor prognosis.

*AML with myelodysplasia-related changes*. Defined by specific cytogenetic abnormalities, mutations (SRSF2, SF3B1, U2AF1, ZRSR2, ASXL1, EZH2, BCOR, STAG2), or history of MDS.

*Therapy-related AML*. After prior chemotherapy or radiation.

*AML, NOS (not otherwise specified)*. Doesn't fit specific subtypes.

Additional important mutations affecting prognosis or treatment:
- *FLT3* mutations (internal tandem duplication or kinase domain mutations). FLT3 inhibitors (midostaurin, gilteritinib, quizartinib) for treatment.
- *IDH1, IDH2* mutations. IDH inhibitors (ivosidenib, enasidenib) approved.
- *NPM1*. Affects prognosis and MRD monitoring.
- *DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1*. Common in older AML; associated with clonal hematopoiesis.

*Presentation*. Similar to ALL — marrow failure symptoms (anemia, thrombocytopenia, neutropenia) and sometimes tissue infiltration. APL has specific presentation with bleeding/coagulopathy (DIC) due to leukemic cells releasing tissue factor.

*Diagnosis*. Bone marrow aspiration with comprehensive workup including flow cytometry, cytogenetics, FISH, molecular profiling. Rapid identification of APL is critical because of bleeding risk.

*Treatment by category*:

*APL (PML-RARA)*. ATRA (all-trans retinoic acid) + arsenic trioxide (ATO). For lower-risk patients, ATRA + ATO alone may be sufficient (no traditional chemotherapy). For higher-risk patients, ATRA + ATO + chemotherapy. Cure rates exceed 90% in modern era. The use of ATRA + ATO represents one of the most successful molecularly-targeted approaches in oncology.

*Younger fit adults (≤65-70, good performance status) with non-APL AML*:
- *Induction*. 7+3 regimen (cytarabine for 7 days + anthracycline for 3 days) is the classical approach. CPX-351 (liposomal cytarabine + daunorubicin) for therapy-related AML and AML with myelodysplasia-related changes.
- *FLT3 inhibitor* (midostaurin, quizartinib) for FLT3-mutated AML during induction and consolidation.
- *IDH inhibitor* for IDH-mutated AML in selected settings.
- *Consolidation*. High-dose cytarabine for several cycles.
- *Stem cell transplant* for high-risk disease.

*Older or unfit adults*:
- *Venetoclax + hypomethylating agent (azacitidine or decitabine)*. The major recent advance. Substantial improvement over hypomethylating agent alone. Now standard of care for AML patients not fit for intensive induction.
- *Lower-intensity chemotherapy* alternatives.
- *FLT3 inhibitor* (gilteritinib) for relapsed/refractory FLT3-mutated AML.
- *IDH inhibitor* for IDH-mutated AML (ivosidenib, enasidenib, olutasidenib).
- *Menin inhibitor* (revumenib) for KMT2A-rearranged or NPM1-mutated AML.
- *Stem cell transplant* if feasible.

*MRD monitoring*. Various approaches including flow cytometry, NPM1 PCR, and emerging molecular methods.

*Outcomes*. Overall AML 5-year survival around 30% but highly variable by subtype. APL: 90%+. Favorable genetics (core binding factor leukemias): 60-70%. Adverse genetics (TP53-mutated, complex karyotype): 5-15%. Older adults have substantially worse outcomes regardless of subtype.

## Chronic myeloid leukemia

The poster child for targeted therapy in oncology.

*Biology*. Defined by the *Philadelphia chromosome* — t(9;22) translocation creating the BCR-ABL1 fusion gene. The BCR-ABL protein is a constitutively active tyrosine kinase that drives CML proliferation.

*Epidemiology*. Annual incidence about 1-2 per 100,000. Median age at diagnosis 64. CML accounts for about 15% of adult leukemias.

*Phases*:
- *Chronic phase*. The presentation of most patients. Indolent disease.
- *Accelerated phase*. Increased disease activity.
- *Blast phase*. Acute leukemia transformation (myeloid or lymphoid).

*Presentation*. Often discovered incidentally on routine blood counts. Symptoms when present include fatigue, weight loss, splenomegaly (sometimes massive), bone pain, night sweats.

*Diagnosis*. CBC shows leukocytosis with full myeloid spectrum. Cytogenetics confirms Philadelphia chromosome; FISH or PCR for BCR-ABL fusion.

*Treatment*. Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) have revolutionized CML:

*Imatinib* (Gleevec). The original BCR-ABL TKI, approved 2001. Daily oral pill. Excellent first-line option, particularly for low-risk disease.

*Dasatinib* (Sprycel). More potent BCR-ABL inhibitor; also inhibits SRC family kinases. Effective against many imatinib-resistance mutations.

*Nilotinib* (Tasigna). More potent and selective BCR-ABL inhibitor. Specific tolerability profile.

*Bosutinib* (Bosulif). Dual SRC/ABL inhibitor.

*Ponatinib* (Iclusig). Active against T315I (the gatekeeper mutation that confers resistance to other TKIs). Cardiovascular toxicity concerns limit broader use.

*Asciminib* (Scemblix). Allosteric BCR-ABL inhibitor binding the myristoyl pocket. Active against T315I and other resistance mutations. Better tolerability than ponatinib.

*Treatment monitoring*. Molecular response assessed by quantitative BCR-ABL PCR. Major molecular response (MR3.0, BCR-ABL ≤0.1% on the international scale) at 12 months predicts long-term outcome. Deep molecular response (MR4.0-4.5, BCR-ABL ≤0.01-0.0032%) may enable treatment-free remission.

*Treatment-free remission*. Selected patients with sustained deep molecular response can stop TKI therapy and remain in remission. About 40-50% of attempts at TFR succeed; the rest relapse but typically respond to TKI re-initiation. This represents a remarkable outcome — patients functionally cured of a previously universally fatal cancer.

*Resistance management*. Mutational analysis at progression guides next-line TKI selection. Sequential use of different TKIs addresses different resistance patterns. For T315I, ponatinib or asciminib. Allogeneic stem cell transplant for TKI-refractory disease.

*Blast phase treatment*. Aggressive treatment with TKI + chemotherapy (similar to AML or ALL depending on lineage) and allogeneic transplant.

*Outcomes*. Modern CML on TKI therapy has near-normal life expectancy in most patients. The 10-year survival on imatinib in the IRIS trial cohort was about 85%. Substantially better outcomes than historical natural history. CML has gone from a leukemia with 5-year median survival to a chronic condition managed by daily pill, with some patients achieving treatment-free remission.

## Chronic lymphocytic leukemia

The most common adult leukemia in Western countries.

*Biology*. Mature B-cell leukemia. CLL cells coexpress CD5 and CD19/CD20. Various molecular subtypes:
- *Mutated IGHV*. Better prognosis. About 60% of cases.
- *Unmutated IGHV*. More aggressive disease.
- *Del(17p)/TP53 mutation*. Adverse prognosis. Responds poorly to chemotherapy; responds to BTK inhibitors and venetoclax.
- *Del(11q)*. Adverse prognosis. ATM gene affected.
- *Trisomy 12*. Intermediate prognosis.
- *Del(13q) as sole abnormality*. Favorable prognosis.

*Epidemiology*. Annual incidence about 5 per 100,000. Median age at diagnosis 70. More common in men. Family history is a risk factor.

*Presentation*. Often incidentally detected lymphocytosis on routine blood counts. Symptomatic disease causes lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, cytopenias, B symptoms (fevers, night sweats, weight loss), recurrent infections.

*Staging*. Rai and Binet systems based on lymphocytosis, lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, and cytopenias.

*Treatment*. Many patients can be observed for prolonged periods (years) before requiring treatment.

Treatment indications include progressive lymphocytosis, progressive lymphadenopathy, progressive cytopenias, autoimmune complications, B symptoms.

Modern treatment options:

*BTK inhibitors*. Ibrutinib (first-generation), acalabrutinib and zanubrutinib (second-generation, more selective with better tolerability), pirtobrutinib (non-covalent, third-generation, active against BTK C481S resistance).

*Venetoclax*. BCL-2 inhibitor. Often combined with anti-CD20 antibody (obinutuzumab or rituximab). MURANO and CLL14 trials established venetoclax-based regimens. Fixed-duration treatment is possible (typically 12-24 months) — a major advantage over indefinite BTK inhibitor therapy.

*Anti-CD20 antibodies*. Rituximab, obinutuzumab. Used with chemoimmunotherapy or with venetoclax.

*Chemoimmunotherapy*. FCR (fludarabine + cyclophosphamide + rituximab) for younger fit patients with mutated IGHV without TP53 abnormalities. Less used than previously as targeted therapies have improved.

*CAR-T cells*. Lisocabtagene maraleucel approved for relapsed/refractory CLL. Brexucabtagene autoleucel in development.

*Allogeneic stem cell transplant*. Reserved for high-risk relapsed/refractory disease.

*Outcomes*. Median survival has substantially improved with targeted therapies. Most CLL patients now die of causes other than CLL. 10-year survival exceeds 80% in many cohorts.

## Lymphomas: Hodgkin and non-Hodgkin

Lymphomas are cancers of lymphocytes forming solid masses. Two major categories: Hodgkin lymphoma and non-Hodgkin lymphoma.

### Hodgkin lymphoma

*Biology*. Characterized by Reed-Sternberg cells (and variants) in a background of inflammatory cells. The cancer cell is a minority of the tumor mass.

*Subtypes*. Classical Hodgkin lymphoma (most common, with several histological subtypes including nodular sclerosing, mixed cellularity, lymphocyte-rich, lymphocyte-depleted) and nodular lymphocyte-predominant Hodgkin lymphoma (a distinct entity now often considered a B-cell lymphoma).

*Epidemiology*. Bimodal age distribution — peak in young adults (20-30) and another peak in older adults (>55). EBV is associated with a fraction of cases. Family history is a risk factor.

*Presentation*. Painless lymphadenopathy, often cervical or mediastinal. B symptoms (fevers, night sweats, >10% weight loss). Pruritus.

*Staging*. Ann Arbor staging (with Cotswold modifications and Lugano updates). PET-CT is the standard staging modality.

*Treatment*. Hodgkin lymphoma is highly curable.

*Early-stage favorable*. ABVD (doxorubicin, bleomycin, vinblastine, dacarbazine) chemotherapy followed by involved-site radiation. Modern regimens use fewer cycles and reduced radiation to minimize long-term toxicity. PET-adapted approaches modify treatment based on interim PET response.

*Early-stage unfavorable*. More cycles of ABVD with radiation.

*Advanced stage*. ABVD (6-8 cycles), or escalated BEACOPP for fit patients with adverse features. PET-adapted approaches. *A+AVD* (brentuximab vedotin + AVD, omitting bleomycin) is increasingly used; ECHELON-1 trial showed improved PFS.

*Relapsed/refractory disease*. Salvage chemotherapy followed by autologous stem cell transplant. Brentuximab vedotin (anti-CD30 antibody-drug conjugate) effective. Anti-PD-1 antibodies (nivolumab, pembrolizumab) very effective — Hodgkin lymphoma has some of the highest checkpoint inhibitor response rates of any cancer.

*Outcomes*. Early-stage 5-year survival >95%. Advanced-stage 5-year survival 80-85%. One of the most curable cancers.

### Non-Hodgkin lymphomas

A diverse group of lymphoid malignancies with many subtypes. Major subtypes:

*Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL)*. The most common lymphoma. Aggressive but curable with modern therapy. Molecular subtypes (germinal center B-cell, activated B-cell, and genetic subtypes — MCD, BN2, EZB, ST2, N1, A53).

Treatment: R-CHOP (rituximab + cyclophosphamide + doxorubicin + vincristine + prednisone) is the standard, 6 cycles. Pola-R-CHP (polatuzumab vedotin replacing vincristine, no bleomycin or prednisone variations) is an increasingly used alternative. For relapsed/refractory disease: salvage chemo + autologous stem cell transplant, CAR-T cells (axicabtagene ciloleucel, tisagenlecleucel, lisocabtagene maraleucel), bispecific antibodies (epcoritamab, glofitamab), tafasitamab + lenalidomide, loncastuximab tesirine, polatuzumab vedotin combinations.

Outcomes: 60-70% cure with R-CHOP. Higher cures in some molecular subtypes; lower in adverse ones. CAR-T cells transformative for relapsed/refractory disease.

*Follicular lymphoma*. Indolent. Characterized by t(14;18) BCL2 translocation. May not require immediate treatment. Treatment options include rituximab maintenance, rituximab + chemotherapy (bendamustine + rituximab is common), EZH2 inhibitor (tazemetostat for EZH2-mutated disease), CAR-T cells for relapsed/refractory disease.

*Mantle cell lymphoma*. Distinctive subtype with cyclin D1 overexpression from t(11;14). Aggressive variants treated with intensive chemoimmunotherapy + transplant. BTK inhibitors (ibrutinib, acalabrutinib, zanubrutinib, pirtobrutinib) and CAR-T cells (brexucabtagene autoleucel) for relapsed disease.

*Marginal zone lymphoma*. MALT lymphoma (mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue) is associated with chronic antigen stimulation. *H. pylori* eradication treats gastric MALT. Rituximab-based regimens, BTK inhibitors for relapsed disease.

*Burkitt lymphoma*. Highly aggressive. Endemic (African) and sporadic forms. EBV association in endemic form. MYC translocations universal. Treatment: intensive chemotherapy regimens (CODOX-M/IVAC, R-CHOP intensive variants, dose-adjusted EPOCH-R). Highly curable with prompt intensive treatment.

*Peripheral T-cell lymphomas*. Diverse group with generally worse prognosis than B-cell lymphomas. CHOP or CHOEP regimens; brentuximab vedotin + CHP for CD30-positive variants. ALK-positive anaplastic large cell lymphoma has better prognosis with ALK-directed therapy.

*Cutaneous T-cell lymphomas* (mycosis fungoides, Sézary syndrome). Skin-directed therapy (topicals, phototherapy, radiation), systemic therapies (interferon, retinoids, methotrexate, brentuximab vedotin, mogamulizumab — anti-CCR4 antibody).

*HIV-associated lymphomas*. Specific entities including primary effusion lymphoma (KSHV-associated), plasmablastic lymphoma, others. Treated with antiretroviral therapy plus lymphoma-directed treatment.

The lymphoma field has been transformed by anti-CD20 antibodies (rituximab the prototype), anti-CD30 ADC (brentuximab vedotin), CAR-T cells, bispecific antibodies, BTK inhibitors, BCL-2 inhibitors, checkpoint inhibitors (especially in Hodgkin lymphoma), and increasingly precise molecular targeting.

## What this chapter gives you

Hematologic malignancies have been among the most transformed by modern oncology. Several special features (accessible cells, well-defined surface markers, single dominant driver mutations, bone marrow transplant infrastructure) make them more tractable than solid tumors.

*Acute lymphoblastic leukemia* has gone from <10% childhood cure rates in 1960 to >90% today through multi-agent intensive chemotherapy in defined phases (induction, consolidation, CNS prophylaxis, maintenance) plus targeted additions (TKIs for Ph+ ALL, blinatumomab, inotuzumab, CAR-T cells). Adult outcomes (50-60%) have improved more modestly. MRD monitoring guides treatment intensity.

*Acute myeloid leukemia* is biologically diverse. Molecular classification (PML-RARA for APL, FLT3 mutations, IDH mutations, KMT2A rearrangements, TP53 mutations, NPM1 mutations, CEBPA mutations, others) drives treatment. APL has been transformed by ATRA + arsenic trioxide. Older patients treated with venetoclax + hypomethylating agent. FLT3 inhibitors, IDH inhibitors, and menin inhibitors target specific subgroups. Outcomes vary widely by subtype.

*Chronic myeloid leukemia* exemplifies targeted therapy. The Philadelphia chromosome (BCR-ABL fusion) is the foundation; tyrosine kinase inhibitors (imatinib through asciminib) have made CML a chronic condition with near-normal life expectancy. Treatment-free remission is possible for selected patients.

*Chronic lymphocytic leukemia* is the most common adult leukemia in Western countries. Modern treatment uses BTK inhibitors (ibrutinib, acalabrutinib, zanubrutinib, pirtobrutinib) and venetoclax-based regimens (often fixed duration). Median survival has dramatically improved.

*Hodgkin lymphoma* is highly curable (>80-95% depending on stage). Modern treatment uses chemotherapy (ABVD or A+AVD) with reduced radiation, PET-adapted approaches, and checkpoint inhibitors and brentuximab vedotin for relapsed disease.

*Non-Hodgkin lymphomas* are a diverse group. Major subtypes:
- DLBCL: R-CHOP or similar regimens with 60-70% cure rates; CAR-T cells and bispecific antibodies transformative for relapsed disease.
- Follicular lymphoma: indolent, multiple treatment options.
- Mantle cell lymphoma: BTK inhibitors and CAR-T cells improved outcomes.
- Burkitt lymphoma: intensive chemotherapy with high cure rates.
- T-cell lymphomas: generally worse prognosis with various subtypes.
- Cutaneous T-cell lymphomas: skin-directed and systemic options.
