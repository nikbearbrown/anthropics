The treatment that cures the cancer is sometimes worse than the cancer itself.

Hold the framing. Modern cancer treatment is intense. Chemotherapy produces myelosuppression, mucositis, neuropathy, nausea, fatigue. Radiation causes acute and late toxicities. Targeted therapy has its own side effect profile. Immunotherapy can cause autoimmune-like reactions affecting any organ system. Surgery has its own complications. And the cancer itself produces symptoms — pain, fatigue, weight loss, cachexia, organ dysfunction, distress. The patient receiving cancer treatment is dealing with the disease and the treatment simultaneously, often with substantial cumulative impact on quality of life.

*Supportive care* is the medical specialty addressing this reality. The goal is to manage cancer symptoms and treatment toxicities so that patients can complete their treatment, function as well as possible, and maintain quality of life. Without good supportive care, even the most effective cancer treatments cannot be delivered — patients abandon therapy due to intolerable symptoms, develop complications that prevent continued treatment, or simply suffer unnecessarily.

The field of supportive care has matured substantially over recent decades. Modern antiemetics make chemotherapy-induced nausea largely controllable. Growth factors reduce the incidence and severity of treatment-related cytopenias. Pain management has evolved with better understanding of pharmacology and procedural options. Nutritional support, psychosocial care, and integrative approaches have all become more sophisticated.

This chapter — the first of two on supportive and palliative care — covers the management of cancer-related symptoms and nutritional support. The next — 36B — covers psychosocial support, palliative care principles, and end-of-life care.

Hold the question: *given that cancer treatment is harsh, how do we make it tolerable, and how do we maintain quality of life throughout the cancer experience?*

## The framework of supportive care

Supportive care includes:

*Symptom management*. Treating cancer-related and treatment-related symptoms.

*Treatment toxicity prevention and management*. Strategies to reduce treatment side effects and manage them when they occur.

*Nutritional support*. Maintaining adequate nutrition through cancer and treatment.

*Psychosocial support*. Addressing emotional, social, and spiritual needs.

*Survivorship care*. Long-term management for cancer survivors.

*Palliative care*. Specialized care for serious illness, focused on quality of life.

*End-of-life care*. Care during the dying process.

The components are integrated. Many patients need multiple aspects simultaneously. The expertise is increasingly recognized as essential to comprehensive cancer care, not optional. Multiple guidelines (ASCO, NCCN, ESMO, others) provide evidence-based recommendations.

A foundational concept is that *supportive care should be integrated with cancer treatment from the start*. Historical models often separated "active treatment" (curative intent, aggressive) from "supportive care" (palliative, less aggressive), with patients transitioning when active treatment was no longer effective. Modern models recognize that supportive care needs exist throughout the cancer experience, not only at end of life.

## Cancer-related pain management

Pain is one of the most common and feared cancer symptoms. About 50-70% of cancer patients experience pain at some point; in advanced disease, this rises to 70-90%. Adequate pain management is both a clinical priority and an ethical imperative.

*Types of cancer pain*:

*Nociceptive pain*. From tissue damage. Includes somatic pain (well-localized, throbbing — bone metastases are classic example) and visceral pain (poorly localized, often referred — from organ involvement).

*Neuropathic pain*. From nerve damage. Burning, shooting, or electric-shock quality. From tumor invasion of nerves, post-surgical neuropathy, chemotherapy-induced neuropathy, post-radiation neuropathy, paraneoplastic syndromes.

*Mixed pain*. Most cancer pain has both nociceptive and neuropathic components.

*Cancer-treatment-related pain*. Mucositis, peripheral neuropathy from chemotherapy, post-surgical pain, post-radiation pain.

*Breakthrough pain*. Pain that breaks through otherwise controlled pain — flares related to movement, dressing changes, procedures, or unpredictably.

*Pain assessment*. Should be regular and systematic:
- *Intensity*. Numeric rating scale (0-10) or visual analog scale.
- *Quality*. Aching, throbbing, burning, shooting, etc. (helps identify type).
- *Location*. Specific sites; may be multiple.
- *Temporal pattern*. Constant, intermittent, breakthrough.
- *Aggravating and relieving factors*.
- *Impact on function and quality of life*.
- *Response to current treatment*.

The Edmonton Symptom Assessment System (ESAS) and similar tools provide systematic symptom assessment.

*Pharmacologic management*. The WHO analgesic ladder provides a framework, though modern practice often skips steps or combines approaches:

*Non-opioid analgesics*. Acetaminophen and NSAIDs. Useful for mild pain or as adjuncts to opioids. NSAIDs may have specific value for bone pain.

*Opioids*. The mainstay of moderate to severe cancer pain management. Many options:
- *Morphine*. Standard of care. Available in immediate-release and sustained-release. Multiple routes (oral, IV, SC, intrathecal).
- *Oxycodone*. Similar to morphine; some patients prefer.
- *Hydromorphone*. More potent than morphine; useful for high opioid requirements.
- *Fentanyl*. Available as transdermal patch (good for stable pain), buccal/sublingual lozenges or films (for breakthrough), IV/SC for inpatient use.
- *Methadone*. Long half-life. Useful for difficult pain syndromes. NMDA receptor activity may help neuropathic pain. Complex pharmacokinetics require expertise.
- *Hydrocodone, codeine*. Generally less preferred for cancer pain due to pharmacology issues.
- *Tramadol, tapentadol*. Atypical opioids with additional mechanisms.

*Adjuvant analgesics*:
- *Antidepressants*. Tricyclics (amitriptyline, nortriptyline) and SNRIs (duloxetine, venlafaxine) for neuropathic pain.
- *Anticonvulsants*. Gabapentin, pregabalin for neuropathic pain.
- *Corticosteroids*. For bone pain, spinal cord compression, increased intracranial pressure, organ capsule pain.
- *Bisphosphonates and denosumab*. For bone metastasis pain.
- *Topical agents*. Lidocaine patches, capsaicin for localized neuropathic pain.
- *Cannabinoids*. Some evidence for pain, particularly when combined with other approaches.

*Procedural interventions*:
- *Nerve blocks*. Celiac plexus block for pancreatic cancer pain. Other regional blocks for specific pain syndromes.
- *Neurolytic procedures*. Chemical or radiofrequency ablation of pain pathways.
- *Spinal cord stimulation*. For selected chronic pain syndromes.
- *Intrathecal pumps*. For refractory pain requiring high-dose opioids.
- *Vertebroplasty/kyphoplasty*. For painful vertebral compression fractures.
- *Palliative radiation*. Particularly effective for bone metastases and other site-specific pain.

*Non-pharmacologic approaches*:
- *Physical therapy*.
- *Psychological approaches* (cognitive behavioral therapy, mindfulness, hypnosis).
- *Acupuncture*.
- *Massage*.
- *Heat/cold therapy*.

*Opioid management principles*:

*Around-the-clock dosing*. Sustained-release opioids for baseline pain; immediate-release for breakthrough.

*Titration*. Doses adjusted to achieve adequate pain control. Cancer pain often requires substantial doses.

*Rotation*. Switching opioids when toxicity or inadequate effect from one. Equianalgesic conversion with caution (clinical assessment essential).

*Side effect management*. Constipation requires bowel regimen (stool softener + stimulant laxative). Nausea typically transient or responds to antiemetics. Sedation typically resolves with continued use or dose adjustment.

*Tolerance and dependence*. Tolerance (need for increasing doses) is biological but often manageable. Physical dependence (withdrawal if stopped abruptly) is expected with chronic use. Addiction (compulsive use despite harm) is rare in cancer patients with appropriate use.

The opioid epidemic has complicated cancer pain management. Some clinicians have under-prescribed opioids out of concern. Patient access to opioids has been restricted in some contexts. Balanced approaches that maintain adequate cancer pain treatment while preventing diversion and inappropriate use are essential.

*Refractory pain*. Some cancer pain is difficult to control with conventional approaches. Specialized pain medicine consultation, palliative care involvement, and consideration of all available options (procedural, psychological, integrative) are appropriate.

## Cancer-related fatigue

The most common and underappreciated cancer symptom. About 60-90% of patients experience fatigue during treatment; 30-50% have persistent fatigue after treatment completion.

*Distinguishing features*. Cancer-related fatigue (CRF) differs from normal fatigue:
- Disproportionate to recent activity.
- Not relieved by rest.
- Impairs function substantially.
- Can persist for months to years.

*Multifactorial etiology*:
- *Cancer itself*. Inflammation, anemia, cachexia.
- *Treatment effects*. Chemotherapy, radiation, surgery all contribute.
- *Anemia*. Common; should be addressed.
- *Pain*. Untreated pain worsens fatigue.
- *Depression*. Overlapping with fatigue; both should be addressed.
- *Sleep disorders*. Often disrupted by treatment, anxiety, pain.
- *Endocrine dysfunction*. Hypothyroidism, hypogonadism.
- *Medications*. Many cancer medications contribute.
- *Deconditioning*. Reduced physical activity during treatment.
- *Nutritional issues*. Malnutrition.

*Assessment*. Should be systematic. ESAS and similar tools include fatigue. Visual analog scales. Identifying reversible contributors (anemia, depression, sleep, medications).

*Management*:

*Treat reversible causes*. Anemia (transfusion or ESAs in appropriate contexts), thyroid dysfunction, depression, sleep disorders, medication adjustment.

*Exercise*. Counterintuitively, exercise is one of the most effective interventions for cancer-related fatigue. Aerobic and resistance training. Improves multiple aspects of cancer-related symptoms. Should be tailored to patient capacity.

*Cognitive behavioral therapy*. Specific approaches for cancer-related fatigue.

*Mind-body interventions*. Yoga, mindfulness, tai chi. Some evidence of benefit.

*Pharmacologic*. Limited evidence for medications. Methylphenidate sometimes used. Modafinil has been studied. Generally adjunctive.

*Energy conservation strategies*. Pacing, prioritization, environmental modifications.

Cancer-related fatigue is often underrecognized and undertreated. Systematic assessment and multimodal management can substantially improve patient function and quality of life.

## Nausea and vomiting

Once a major problem in cancer treatment, now substantially controllable. Multiple types:

*Acute chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting (CINV)*. Within 24 hours of chemotherapy. Different mechanisms than later forms.

*Delayed CINV*. After 24 hours and up to 5 days post-chemotherapy. Often less responsive to acute treatment.

*Anticipatory CINV*. Before treatment, conditioned response from prior experience.

*Breakthrough CINV*. Despite prophylactic antiemetics.

*Refractory CINV*. Continued nausea/vomiting despite multiple treatments.

*Other causes of nausea in cancer patients*. Opioids, constipation, bowel obstruction, brain metastases, hepatic failure, hypercalcemia, anxiety, and many others. Cancer patients with nausea need systematic evaluation, not just antiemetics.

*Chemotherapy emetic risk*. Drugs classified by emetic potential:
- *Highly emetogenic*. Cisplatin >50 mg/m², AC regimen (doxorubicin + cyclophosphamide), carmustine, others.
- *Moderately emetogenic*. Many chemotherapy regimens.
- *Low or minimal emetogenic*. Various.

Recommendations differ by emetic potential.

*Antiemetic classes*:

*5-HT3 receptor antagonists*. Ondansetron, granisetron, palonosetron, others. Cornerstone of acute CINV prevention. Particularly effective for serotonin-mediated nausea (chemotherapy).

*NK1 receptor antagonists*. Aprepitant, fosaprepitant, netupitant, rolapitant. Add to 5-HT3 antagonists for highly emetogenic chemotherapy. Important for delayed CINV.

*Corticosteroids*. Dexamethasone. Synergistic with other antiemetics.

*Olanzapine*. Atypical antipsychotic with antiemetic activity. Increasingly used for highly emetogenic chemotherapy and for refractory nausea.

*Dopamine antagonists*. Metoclopramide, prochlorperazine, haloperidol. Various roles.

*Cannabinoids*. Dronabinol, nabilone. For refractory nausea in some patients.

*Benzodiazepines*. For anticipatory nausea (anxiety component).

*Antihistamines*. Diphenhydramine, hydroxyzine. For motion-sensitivity-related nausea.

*Standard prophylactic regimens*:

*Highly emetogenic chemotherapy*. 4-drug regimen: 5-HT3 antagonist + NK1 antagonist + corticosteroid + olanzapine (the modern approach showing best efficacy).

*Moderately emetogenic chemotherapy*. 5-HT3 antagonist + corticosteroid; consider adding NK1 antagonist or olanzapine.

*Low emetic*. Single agent (e.g., dexamethasone, 5-HT3 antagonist).

The modern antiemetic regimens make CINV largely controllable. Patients with persistent nausea despite optimal regimens need evaluation for other causes.

## Mucositis

Inflammation of mucosal surfaces — most commonly oral but can affect the entire GI tract. Particularly common with:
- *Chemotherapy*. 5-fluorouracil, methotrexate, anthracyclines, high-dose chemotherapy for transplant.
- *Head and neck radiation*. Almost universal in treated patients.
- *Pelvic/abdominal radiation*. Can cause severe proctitis and intestinal mucositis.

*Severity*. Graded by CTCAE criteria (0-5). Severe mucositis (grade 3-4) prevents eating, drinking, sometimes speaking. Major source of treatment-limiting toxicity.

*Management*:

*Prevention*:
- *Oral hygiene protocols*. Pre-treatment dental evaluation. Regular oral care during treatment.
- *Cryotherapy* (oral ice). During bolus 5-FU infusion. Reduces oral mucositis incidence.
- *Palifermin* (keratinocyte growth factor). Approved for high-dose chemotherapy preceding hematopoietic stem cell transplant. Reduces severity.
- *Smoking cessation and reduced alcohol* during treatment.

*Treatment*:
- *Pain management*. Topical agents (lidocaine, "magic mouthwash" with various components, doxepin rinse). Systemic analgesics including opioids for severe pain.
- *Bland rinses*. Salt water, baking soda solution.
- *Avoiding irritants*. Spicy, acidic, hot foods.
- *Nutritional support*. May require feeding tube for severe mucositis preventing oral intake.

*Infection prevention*. Mucositis breaches mucosal barriers, allowing infection. Antiseptic rinses, antifungal/antiviral prophylaxis in selected patients, prompt treatment of suspected infections.

The integration of supportive care with treatment delivery is essential for mucositis management. Treatment breaks may be needed for severe mucositis, but supportive care often allows continuation of treatment.

## Other common symptoms

*Constipation*. Universal with opioids; common with vinca alkaloids and various other drugs and treatments. Should be addressed proactively with bowel regimens. Stool softeners + stimulant laxatives. Lactulose, polyethylene glycol, others as needed. Peripherally-acting mu opioid receptor antagonists (methylnaltrexone, naloxegol) for opioid-induced constipation refractory to standard approaches.

*Diarrhea*. Common with 5-FU, irinotecan, capecitabine, EGFR inhibitors, immune checkpoint inhibitors (colitis), radiation enteritis. Specific management depending on cause:
- Hydration and electrolyte management.
- Loperamide (cancer-related, NOT for immune-mediated colitis).
- Octreotide for severe carcinoid-related or chemotherapy-related diarrhea.
- Investigation for *C. difficile* and other infectious causes.
- For immune-related colitis: corticosteroids and immunosuppression (covered in Chapter 25A).

*Anemia*. Common with cancer and treatment. Multiple etiologies (myelosuppression, iron deficiency, B12/folate deficiency, anemia of chronic disease, bleeding). Treatment:
- Transfusion for symptomatic anemia.
- Iron supplementation for iron deficiency.
- Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents in selected non-curative-treatment contexts (with awareness of cancer outcome concerns).

*Cytopenias and infection*. Neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, anemia. Management with growth factors (G-CSF, pegfilgrastim), transfusion support, prophylactic antimicrobials, prompt treatment of febrile neutropenia.

*Dyspnea*. Multiple causes — anemia, pulmonary disease, pleural effusion, pulmonary embolism, infection, others. Specific treatment of cause. Opioids for refractory dyspnea (effective at lower doses than for pain). Oxygen supplementation. Fan therapy. Anxiety treatment when contributory.

*Cough*. Various causes. Specific treatment when cause identified (bronchodilators for airway disease, treatment of infection, etc.). Symptomatic treatment with codeine, dextromethorphan, others.

*Lymphedema*. After axillary or inguinal lymphadenectomy or radiation. Manual lymphatic drainage, compression garments, exercise, surgical interventions (lymphaticovenular anastomosis, lymph node transfer) in some cases.

*Hot flashes*. From hormonal therapy in breast and prostate cancer. SSRIs/SNRIs (venlafaxine, paroxetine — though paroxetine has interactions with tamoxifen), gabapentin, clonidine, acupuncture. Hormonal alternatives generally not appropriate in hormone-receptor-positive cancers.

*Pruritus*. From cholestasis, opioids, paraneoplastic. Specific treatment of cause. Topical agents (steroids, cooling agents), antihistamines, opioid antagonists for opioid-related pruritus.

*Insomnia*. Common from anxiety, pain, treatment effects, steroids. Sleep hygiene, treatment of underlying issues, cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia, medications when needed.

Each symptom has specific management approaches. Comprehensive supportive care requires attention to multiple symptoms simultaneously.

## Cancer cachexia and nutritional support

Cancer cachexia is a specific wasting syndrome characterized by involuntary weight loss (especially muscle mass), anorexia, weakness, and metabolic abnormalities. Different from simple starvation — caloric supplementation alone doesn't reverse cachexia.

*Pathophysiology*. Driven by inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, others), proteolytic factors (proteolysis-inducing factor), tumor-derived factors, and complex metabolic alterations. The cachectic state has its own biological program that resists nutrition-only interventions.

*Stages*:
- *Pre-cachexia*. Weight loss <5%, anorexia, metabolic changes.
- *Cachexia*. Weight loss >5% (or 2% if BMI <20), reduced food intake, systemic inflammation.
- *Refractory cachexia*. Very low performance status, expected survival <3 months, treatment-refractory.

*Assessment*. Weight loss history, body composition (when available), nutritional intake assessment, functional assessment, inflammatory markers.

*Management*:

*Nutritional support*:
- *Oral supplementation*. High-protein, high-calorie supplements. Often well-tolerated.
- *Enteral nutrition*. Tube feeding (NG, NJ, PEG) when oral intake inadequate.
- *Parenteral nutrition*. IV nutrition when GI tract not functional or accessible.

The choice depends on:
- Disease prognosis. Aggressive nutritional support more appropriate for curable disease or expected long survival.
- GI tract function. Enteral preferred over parenteral when GI tract accessible.
- Patient preferences and goals of care.

*Pharmacologic management*:
- *Megestrol acetate*. Increases appetite and weight. Side effects include thromboembolic risk, hypogonadism, adrenal suppression.
- *Corticosteroids*. Short-term appetite improvement. Side effects accumulate with prolonged use.
- *Olanzapine*. Some evidence for appetite improvement.
- *Cannabinoids*. Modest effect on appetite in some patients.
- *Anamorelin* (ghrelin receptor agonist). Approved in Japan for cancer cachexia; not approved in US.
- *Newer agents in development* targeting specific cachexia pathways.

*Exercise*. Resistance training preserves muscle mass when feasible. Aerobic exercise improves multiple cachexia-related outcomes.

*Symptom management*. Nausea, mucositis, taste alterations, depression, pain — all affect nutritional intake and should be addressed.

*Multidisciplinary care*. Dietitians with oncology expertise are essential team members.

*Goals of care discussion*. As disease progresses, the goals of nutrition shift. In refractory cachexia near end of life, aggressive nutritional support may not improve outcomes and may add burden. Family education about declining appetite at end of life is important.

The cancer cachexia field has been challenging — many proposed interventions have failed to show meaningful benefit. The recognition that cachexia is not just inadequate intake but a specific syndrome with its own biology has improved understanding but has not yet translated to highly effective interventions. Multidisciplinary management addressing multiple factors is the current best practice.

## What this chapter gives you

Supportive care has matured into an essential component of cancer treatment, with modern approaches making most cancer treatments substantially more tolerable.

Cancer-related pain affects 50-90% of patients depending on disease stage. Management includes systematic assessment, the WHO analgesic ladder framework (often skipped or combined in modern practice), non-opioid analgesics, opioids (morphine, oxycodone, hydromorphone, fentanyl, methadone, others), adjuvant analgesics (antidepressants and anticonvulsants for neuropathic pain, corticosteroids, bone-targeting agents), procedural interventions (nerve blocks, vertebroplasty, intrathecal pumps), and non-pharmacologic approaches. Opioid management principles include around-the-clock dosing, titration, rotation, side effect management, and balanced approach in the era of opioid concerns.

Cancer-related fatigue affects 60-90% of patients during treatment with persistent fatigue in 30-50% of survivors. Management addresses reversible causes (anemia, depression, sleep, medications) and includes exercise (counterintuitively the most effective intervention), cognitive behavioral therapy, mind-body interventions, energy conservation, and selected pharmacologic approaches.

Nausea and vomiting are now substantially controllable with modern antiemetic regimens. Highly emetogenic chemotherapy is treated with 4-drug regimens including 5-HT3 antagonist + NK1 antagonist + corticosteroid + olanzapine. The systematic approach has made CINV manageable for most patients.

Mucositis affects mucosal surfaces from chemotherapy and head/neck or pelvic radiation. Prevention includes oral hygiene, cryotherapy during bolus 5-FU, palifermin for transplant settings. Treatment focuses on pain management, bland rinses, infection prevention, and nutritional support.

Other common symptoms include constipation (universal with opioids; proactive bowel regimens essential), diarrhea (cause-specific management), anemia (transfusion or growth factors), cytopenias and infection (growth factors, transfusion, antimicrobials), dyspnea (specific cause treatment plus opioids and other measures), cough, lymphedema, hot flashes, pruritus, and insomnia.

Cancer cachexia is a specific wasting syndrome distinct from simple starvation. Multifactorial pathophysiology driven by inflammatory cytokines and metabolic alterations. Management includes nutritional support (oral, enteral, parenteral as appropriate), pharmacologic approaches (megestrol, corticosteroids, olanzapine, anamorelin where available), exercise (resistance training), symptom management addressing barriers to intake, and multidisciplinary care. Goals of care discussions become important as disease progresses.
