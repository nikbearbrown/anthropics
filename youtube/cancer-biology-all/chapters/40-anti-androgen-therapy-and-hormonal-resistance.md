The first chemotherapy that worked was for prostate cancer, and it wasn't a chemotherapy.

Hold the framing. Charles Huggins, working in Chicago in 1941, made an observation that won him the 1966 Nobel Prize. Men with metastatic prostate cancer who underwent surgical castration (bilateral orchiectomy) experienced dramatic regression of their cancers — bone pain reduced, PSA levels dropped (decades before PSA was named), urinary symptoms improved, sometimes patients lived for years. The mechanism was unknown at the time but the clinical effect was unmistakable. Prostate cancer, like breast cancer, depended on a hormone for its growth.

The hormone is testosterone. Prostate cancer cells, like normal prostate cells, express the androgen receptor and respond to androgen signaling with proliferation and survival. Cutting off the androgen supply produces remarkable initial responses. Over time, however, the cancer adapts — becoming *castration-resistant* — and continues to grow despite the absence of testicular androgens. The modern era of prostate cancer therapy has been about understanding and addressing this resistance.

This chapter — the second of two on hormonal therapies — covers anti-androgen therapy for prostate cancer, GnRH-based approaches that produce medical castration, the steroidogenesis inhibitors that block residual androgen production, and the resistance mechanisms that develop with prolonged hormonal therapy. The chapter builds on 23A, which covered anti-estrogen therapy for breast cancer.

Hold the question: *given that prostate cancer adapts to hormonal therapy through specific resistance mechanisms, how do successive generations of drugs continue to be effective?*

## Androgen receptor signaling in prostate cancer

The androgen receptor (AR) is a nuclear receptor analogous to the estrogen receptor. AR resides in the cytoplasm in inactive form, bound to heat shock proteins. Testosterone or its more potent metabolite *dihydrotestosterone (DHT)* — produced from testosterone by 5α-reductase — binds the AR ligand-binding domain. The bound AR dimerizes, translocates to the nucleus, and binds *androgen response elements (AREs)* in the genome to drive transcription of AR-regulated genes.

AR-regulated genes in prostate cancer include:
- *PSA (prostate-specific antigen)*. The classical prostate biomarker. Its serum levels reflect (imperfectly) AR signaling activity and cancer activity.
- *TMPRSS2*. A protease whose expression is regulated by androgen. The *TMPRSS2-ERG fusion*, common in prostate cancer, places the ERG transcription factor under TMPRSS2 androgen-responsive promoter control.
- Multiple genes involved in proliferation, lipid metabolism, and other prostate-specific functions.

Testosterone is produced primarily in the testes (under control of LH from the pituitary, which is controlled by GnRH from the hypothalamus). A smaller fraction is produced in the adrenal glands. Local conversion of dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) and other adrenal precursors to androgens in tissues (including the prostate) contributes additional androgen activity.

Prostate cancer cells often have *intratumoral androgen synthesis* — local conversion of adrenal precursors to potent androgens within the cancer. This becomes important in castration-resistant disease, where elimination of testicular androgens does not eliminate intratumoral androgens.

## Androgen deprivation therapy

The foundational approach to advanced prostate cancer is *androgen deprivation therapy (ADT)* — reducing testosterone to castrate levels (typically <50 ng/dL). This can be achieved several ways:

*Surgical castration (bilateral orchiectomy)*. The original approach. Reduces testosterone to castrate levels within hours. Permanent. Increasingly uncommon as medical alternatives are available, but still used in some settings (cost, compliance, advanced disease).

*GnRH agonists*. Drugs that initially stimulate then desensitize the pituitary GnRH receptor, leading to reduced LH production and reduced testicular testosterone. Examples include leuprolide, goserelin, triptorelin. The drugs cause initial testosterone surge (the "flare phenomenon") before suppression takes effect — this can transiently worsen disease and is typically managed with anti-androgen pretreatment in patients with significant disease burden.

*GnRH antagonists*. Drugs that directly block GnRH receptor, avoiding the initial flare. Examples include degarelix (injectable) and relugolix (oral). The oral formulation has been a substantial convenience advance.

All these approaches reduce testicular androgen production. They don't affect adrenal androgens. Patients on ADT have testosterone levels reduced 90-95% from baseline, but small amounts of androgen activity persist from adrenal sources and intratumoral synthesis.

The clinical effects of ADT:
- *Initial response*. Dramatic PSA drops, often by 90-99 percent. Tumor regression. Symptomatic improvement.
- *Duration of response*. Variable. Some patients respond for many years; others develop castration-resistance within 1-2 years.
- *Side effects*. Hot flashes, fatigue, sexual dysfunction, gynecomastia, loss of bone density (osteoporosis risk), metabolic effects (insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, increased cardiovascular events), loss of muscle mass, cognitive effects, depression.

The side effect burden of ADT is substantial. Many of the effects mimic accelerated aging. Cardiovascular morbidity is increased. Bone protection (bisphosphonates or denosumab) is often needed.

## Anti-androgens (AR antagonists)

Beyond reducing androgen production, the AR itself can be blocked at the receptor level. First-generation anti-androgens (flutamide, bicalutamide, nilutamide) competed with androgens for AR binding but had relatively weak activity and risk of developing into AR agonists in some contexts. They are less commonly used now.

Second-generation anti-androgens have transformed prostate cancer treatment:

*Enzalutamide* (Xtandi, approved 2012). A more potent AR antagonist that also blocks AR nuclear translocation and DNA binding. Active in castration-resistant prostate cancer and increasingly in hormone-sensitive advanced disease. The drug improves overall survival compared to placebo in multiple settings.

*Apalutamide* (Erleada, approved 2018). Similar mechanism to enzalutamide. Approved for non-metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer and metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer.

*Darolutamide* (Nubeqa, approved 2019). Another potent AR antagonist with somewhat better tolerability profile (less CNS penetration, fewer falls and seizures than enzalutamide).

The second-generation anti-androgens are used in combination with ADT (rather than replacing it). The combination produces deeper and longer-lasting suppression of AR signaling than ADT alone.

Clinical applications:
- *Metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer (mHSPC)*. Combination of ADT + second-generation anti-androgen is now standard. Multiple trials (TITAN, ENZAMET, ARCHES, ARASENS) have shown survival benefit.
- *Non-metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (nmCRPC)*. Patients on ADT with rising PSA but no detectable metastases. Anti-androgens have shown metastasis-free survival benefit.
- *Metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC)*. Standard treatment after progression on initial ADT, often combined with other agents.

The toxicities of second-generation anti-androgens add to those of ADT — increased fatigue, falls, fractures, cognitive effects, hypertension. Patient selection requires considering the balance of efficacy and side effects.

## Abiraterone and steroidogenesis inhibition

*Abiraterone* (Zytiga, approved 2011) takes a different approach — inhibiting the enzyme *CYP17A1*, which is essential for androgen synthesis. The drug blocks both testicular and adrenal androgen production, plus intratumoral androgen synthesis.

The mechanism is elegant. CYP17A1 catalyzes 17α-hydroxylase and 17,20-lyase activities in steroidogenesis. The enzyme is required for synthesizing all androgens and many estrogens. Inhibition reduces androgen levels far more profoundly than ADT alone, which only blocks the testicular contribution.

The clinical use:
- *Combined with prednisone*. Because CYP17A1 inhibition also affects cortisol synthesis, the upstream regulation drives ACTH-mediated overproduction of mineralocorticoid precursors. The result is mineralocorticoid excess (hypertension, hypokalemia, fluid retention). Concomitant prednisone provides glucocorticoid replacement and suppresses ACTH, mitigating the mineralocorticoid issues.
- *Combined with ADT*. Abiraterone + prednisone is given on top of ongoing ADT (not as a replacement for it). The combination provides deeper androgen suppression.
- *Multiple settings*. mHSPC (LATITUDE, STAMPEDE trials) and mCRPC (COU-AA-301, COU-AA-302 trials). Substantial survival benefits.

Toxicities include mineralocorticoid effects (managed by prednisone), hepatotoxicity (monitoring), and the cumulative effects with ADT.

*Other steroidogenesis approaches*:
- *Ketoconazole* (an antifungal with off-target inhibition of multiple cytochrome P450 enzymes including CYP17). Used as anti-androgen historically; now largely replaced by abiraterone.
- *Newer CYP17 inhibitors* with different selectivity profiles are in development.

## Timing and sequencing in prostate cancer

Modern prostate cancer hormonal therapy involves multiple agents used in specific sequences. The current framework for advanced disease:

*Localized prostate cancer*. Surgery or radiation as primary treatment, with ADT as neoadjuvant or adjuvant in selected cases.

*Biochemical recurrence after primary treatment*. Rising PSA without detectable metastases. ADT may be initiated; the optimal timing (immediate vs. delayed) has been debated.

*Metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer (mHSPC)*. First-line treatment is now ADT + second-generation anti-androgen OR ADT + abiraterone OR ADT + docetaxel (chemotherapy). Triplet therapy (ADT + abiraterone + docetaxel; PEACE-1 trial) is even more effective for selected high-volume disease. The choice depends on disease burden, patient factors, and access.

*Castration-resistant prostate cancer*. After progression on initial ADT:
- *Pre-docetaxel chemotherapy era*. ADT continued; addition of second-generation hormonal therapy (enzalutamide or abiraterone).
- *Docetaxel*. Chemotherapy remains an option, with overall survival benefit in mCRPC.
- *Sequential hormonal therapy*. Switching among enzalutamide, abiraterone, apalutamide, darolutamide.
- *PARP inhibitors*. For BRCA-mutated or homologous recombination-deficient prostate cancer (olaparib, talazoparib, niraparib).
- *Radium-223*. For symptomatic bone metastases without visceral disease.
- *Lutetium-177-PSMA-617*. Targeted radioligand therapy for PSMA-positive mCRPC progressed on prior therapies.
- *Immunotherapy*. Sipuleucel-T for asymptomatic mCRPC; pembrolizumab for MSI-H disease (rare in prostate cancer).
- *Cabazitaxel*. Chemotherapy for post-docetaxel disease.

The sequencing of these multiple options is one of the more complex aspects of modern oncology. Patient factors, disease characteristics, prior treatments, and biomarker status all influence the decisions.

## Hormonal therapy resistance

Hormone-dependent cancers eventually become hormone-resistant. The resistance mechanisms vary and include:

*In breast cancer*:

*ESR1 mutations*. Activating mutations in the estrogen receptor that produce constitutive (ligand-independent) activity. The mutations (Y537S, D538G, others) emerge under aromatase inhibitor pressure (when estrogen levels are very low, cells with mutations that don't need estrogen are selected). Detected in 30-40% of metastatic breast cancers progressing on AI therapy. These tumors are AI-resistant but may respond to fulvestrant or to newer oral SERDs. Elacestrant was approved specifically for ESR1-mutated disease.

*Loss of ER expression*. Some cancers lose ER expression entirely, becoming truly hormone-independent. The cancer may then behave like triple-negative disease.

*PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway activation*. Mutations in PIK3CA (the most commonly mutated gene in breast cancer overall, present in about 40% of HR-positive disease) and other pathway alterations drive growth bypassing ER signaling. Alpelisib is approved for PIK3CA-mutated HR-positive breast cancer.

*HER2 activation*. Rare in primary HR-positive disease but can emerge in resistance.

*Cell cycle alterations*. Mutations in RB1, CCND1 amplification, CDKN2A loss, others can promote proliferation independent of estrogen signaling.

*Cross-talk with growth factor signaling*. EGFR family, FGFR, IGF-1R, and other pathways can drive estrogen-independent proliferation.

*Tumor microenvironment changes*. CAF and immune cell interactions can support growth.

*In prostate cancer*:

*AR amplification or overexpression*. The cancer cells produce more AR protein to compensate for low androgen levels, recovering sensitivity to residual androgen.

*AR mutations*. Activating mutations in AR. The classical example is mutations that broaden ligand specificity (allowing AR to be activated by adrenal androgens, by progesterone, sometimes by anti-androgens themselves).

*AR splice variants*. Truncated AR variants (AR-V7 in particular) lack the ligand-binding domain but retain the DNA-binding and transactivation domains. They are constitutively active, independent of any androgen. AR-V7 expression predicts resistance to enzalutamide and abiraterone but not to taxane chemotherapy.

*Intratumoral androgen synthesis*. As described, cancer cells can produce their own androgens locally. Abiraterone addresses this; resistance involves escape from abiraterone's effects.

*Bypass signaling*. PI3K/AKT pathway (especially with PTEN loss, common in prostate cancer), MYC, other oncogenic pathways can drive AR-independent proliferation.

*Neuroendocrine differentiation*. Some prostate cancers under prolonged hormonal therapy undergo *neuroendocrine transformation* — they lose AR expression and adopt small-cell-like features. The transformed cells are hormone-independent but also lose responsiveness to hormonal therapy entirely. Treatment shifts to platinum-based chemotherapy similar to small-cell carcinoma.

The pattern of resistance development in both breast and prostate cancer follows similar themes — receptor modification (mutations, amplification, splice variants), bypass signaling, phenotypic transformation, and microenvironmental adaptation. Understanding these mechanisms guides the development of next-generation therapies.

## Newer hormonal therapy approaches

The hormonal therapy field continues to evolve with several promising directions:

*Oral SERDs* (for breast cancer): elacestrant, camizestrant, giredestrant, imlunestrant, others. May replace fulvestrant for many indications. Active in ESR1-mutated disease.

*PROTACs targeting hormonal receptors*. Proteolysis-targeting chimeras that mark target proteins for proteasomal degradation. Multiple PROTACs targeting ER and AR are in clinical development.

*Selective androgen receptor degraders (SARDs)* for prostate cancer. Similar concept to oral SERDs but for AR. In clinical development.

*Combinations with novel targets*. Hormonal therapy combined with PI3K inhibitors, mTOR inhibitors, BTK inhibitors, immune checkpoint inhibitors, and others. The combination space is large and actively being explored.

*Adjuvant CDK4/6 inhibitors* in early-stage breast cancer. Already with abemaciclib FDA approval for high-risk disease (monarchE). Other CDK4/6 inhibitors being tested.

*Adjuvant abiraterone* in high-risk prostate cancer. STAMPEDE and other trials.

*PARP inhibitor combinations* with hormonal therapy. PROpel, MAGNITUDE, TALAPRO-2 trials in metastatic prostate cancer. The combination produces benefits, especially in BRCA-mutated disease.

*Theranostic approaches*. Lutetium-177-PSMA for prostate cancer. PSMA-targeted radioligand therapy for prostate cancer that has progressed on hormonal therapy. Other targeted radioligands are in development.

## Hormonal therapy beyond breast and prostate

Several other cancers respond to hormonal interventions:

*Endometrial cancer*. Estrogen-driven type I endometrial cancers (typically low-grade endometrioid) can respond to progestin therapy. Hysterectomy is curative for early-stage disease; hormonal therapy is used in selected metastatic cases. Aromatase inhibitors have some activity in metastatic endometrial cancer.

*Uterine sarcomas (low-grade)*. Endometrial stromal sarcoma and some other low-grade uterine sarcomas express hormone receptors and can respond to hormonal therapy.

*Thyroid cancer*. Differentiated thyroid cancer is driven by TSH. After thyroidectomy, suppression of TSH with levothyroxine therapy reduces recurrence risk. The principle is unusual — not blocking a growth-driving hormone but suppressing pituitary stimulation of the cancer.

*Neuroendocrine tumors with functional hormone production*. Carcinoid, gastrinoma, insulinoma, glucagonoma, somatostatinoma, VIPoma. The functional manifestations are often more clinically significant than the tumor itself. Somatostatin analogs (octreotide, lanreotide) treat both the hormone-related symptoms and (in some cases) the tumor itself.

*Carcinoid and neuroendocrine tumors generally*. Somatostatin receptor expression makes these tumors amenable to somatostatin analog therapy. PROMID, CLARINET trials established their role.

These hormone-responsive cancers represent diverse biology but share the principle of cancer dependence on specific hormonal signals that can be therapeutically targeted.

## What this chapter gives you

Prostate cancer hormonal therapy is built on the foundation that prostate cancer depends on androgen receptor signaling. Charles Huggins's 1941 demonstration that surgical castration produces dramatic regression of metastatic prostate cancer established the principle that won him the Nobel Prize.

Modern androgen deprivation therapy uses GnRH agonists (leuprolide, goserelin, triptorelin) or antagonists (degarelix, oral relugolix) to achieve medical castration. ADT alone produces responses but limited duration; addition of second-generation anti-androgens (enzalutamide, apalutamide, darolutamide) or steroidogenesis inhibitors (abiraterone) substantially improves outcomes.

The sequencing of prostate cancer hormonal therapy involves:
- *mHSPC*: ADT + second-generation anti-androgen or abiraterone, sometimes with chemotherapy.
- *nmCRPC*: addition of second-generation anti-androgen.
- *mCRPC*: sequential use of remaining hormonal agents, with addition of PARP inhibitors (for BRCA-mutated disease), radium-223, lutetium-177-PSMA, chemotherapy (docetaxel, cabazitaxel), or immunotherapy (sipuleucel-T) depending on context.

Hormonal therapy resistance develops through receptor modifications (ER mutations in breast, AR mutations and splice variants like AR-V7 in prostate), bypass signaling (PI3K, MYC, growth factor pathways), phenotypic transformation (neuroendocrine in prostate, loss of receptor in either), and microenvironmental factors. Understanding resistance mechanisms guides next-generation therapy development.

Newer approaches include oral SERDs (elacestrant approved 2023), PROTACs for receptor degradation, hormonal-therapy/targeted-therapy combinations, theranostics (PSMA-targeted radioligand therapy), and adjuvant approaches with CDK4/6 inhibitors and PARP inhibitors.

Other cancers with hormonal dependence include endometrial cancer (progestin therapy), some uterine sarcomas, thyroid cancer (TSH suppression), and neuroendocrine tumors (somatostatin analog therapy).

Chapter 24 turns to targeted therapies — the small molecules and antibodies that have transformed cancer treatment over the past 25 years by inhibiting specific molecular features that drive cancer growth.
