# Chapter 41 — Targeted Therapies: Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors and Monoclonal Antibodies


## TL;DR

A targeted therapy doesn't kill every cell. It kills the cells that need a specific molecule to survive.

Hold the framing. Conventional chemotherapy damages all cells, with the bias toward dividing cells producing some preferential killing of cancer over normal tissue. The mechanism is broad — DNA damage, mitotic interference, metabolic blockade — and the therapeutic index is therefore narrow. Most chemotherapy side effects come from the same mechanism that kills cancer cells, just operating in normal tissues.

Targeted therapies work differently. They identify a specific molecular vulnerability in cancer cells — a mutated kinase, an amplified receptor, a fusion protein — and they block that specific feature. Cells that don't depend on the targeted feature are unaffected. Cells that do depend on it — the cancer cells, by definition, since the feature drives their growth — are selectively killed. The therapeutic index is much wider. The side effects, when they occur, often reflect the targeted molecule's role in normal cells rather than collateral damage from broad cytotoxicity.

This is the conceptual revolution that has transformed cancer therapy over the past 25 years. Imatinib in 2001 for chronic myeloid leukemia. Trastuzumab in 1998 for HER2-positive breast cancer. The proliferation of tyrosine kinase inhibitors and monoclonal antibodies since. The pace of development has accelerated; new targeted therapies are approved every year, often for molecularly-defined subgroups of cancers that previously had no specific treatment.

This chapter — the first of two on targeted therapies — covers the principles of molecular targeting, the major classes of tyrosine kinase inhibitors, and the monoclonal antibodies (including antibody-drug conjugates and bispecific antibodies). The next — 24B — covers other targeted therapy classes (PARP inhibitors, CDK4/6 inhibitors revisited, angiogenesis inhibitors, others), resistance mechanisms, and patient selection.

Hold the question: *what makes a molecular target druggable, and how does targeted therapy succeed where conventional chemotherapy struggles?*

## The principle of targeted therapy

The conceptual foundation of targeted therapy is *oncogene addiction* — the observation that cancer cells driven by a specific oncogene become dependent on its activity. Blocking the oncogene kills the cell selectively. Normal cells, not addicted to the oncogene, are minimally affected.

For oncogene addiction to be therapeutically useful, several conditions must be met:

*The oncogene must drive the cancer*. The mutation or amplification must be functionally important for cancer cell survival, not just a passenger.

*The oncogene product must be druggable*. There must be a way to inhibit the protein's activity with a small molecule, antibody, or other agent.

*The dependency must be substantial*. Blocking the oncogene must kill the cancer cells, not just slow their growth.

*The toxicity profile must be acceptable*. The drug must not produce intolerable damage to normal tissues that express the target.

*The clinical population must be definable*. The patients with the molecular feature must be identifiable through diagnostic testing.

When these conditions are met, targeted therapy can produce dramatic responses. CML treated with imatinib produces complete cytogenetic responses in over 90% of patients. EGFR-mutant lung cancer treated with osimertinib produces responses in 70-80% of patients. BRAF V600E melanoma treated with BRAF + MEK inhibitors produces responses in 60-70%. The response rates dramatically exceed what conventional chemotherapy can achieve in unselected patients.

The limitations of targeted therapy:
- *Resistance*. Almost always emerges with prolonged treatment. The cancer evolves around the blockade.
- *Limited applicability*. Most patients with most cancers don't have actionable targets. Targeted therapy works for the molecularly-defined subset.
- *Off-target effects*. Even "selective" inhibitors have other activities that can produce side effects.
- *Expensive*. Targeted therapies are typically expensive, creating access disparities.

Despite these limitations, targeted therapy has substantially extended life for millions of patients and continues to be a major area of cancer drug development.

## Tyrosine kinase inhibitors: the imatinib paradigm

The first major success in targeted therapy was *imatinib* (Gleevec) for chronic myeloid leukemia (CML). The story illustrates the targeted therapy paradigm.

CML is caused by the *Philadelphia chromosome*, the t(9;22) translocation that creates the BCR-ABL fusion gene. The BCR-ABL protein is a constitutively active tyrosine kinase that drives CML proliferation. CML cells are dependent on BCR-ABL kinase activity for survival.

Brian Druker and colleagues at Oregon Health Sciences University, working with Nicholas Lydon and Alex Matter at Ciba-Geigy (later Novartis), screened for small molecules that could inhibit ABL kinase activity. They identified STI-571 (later imatinib) as a potent and selective ABL inhibitor. Preclinical studies showed dramatic killing of CML cells.

The clinical trials, beginning in 1998, produced unprecedented responses. Most patients with CML achieved complete cytogenetic remission. The disease that had been uniformly fatal within 5-10 years became a chronic condition managed by a daily pill. Imatinib was approved by the FDA in 2001 — at the time, the most rapid FDA approval ever.

The biology is the proof of concept:
- CML cells depend on BCR-ABL signaling (oncogene addiction).
- Imatinib selectively binds the ATP pocket of ABL kinase, inhibiting its activity.
- BCR-ABL inhibition stops the proliferative and survival signals; cells die.
- Normal cells, not addicted to BCR-ABL, are minimally affected.

The success template — molecular target identification, structure-guided drug development, biomarker-driven clinical trials, dramatic responses — has been repeated for many other targets since.

Second- and third-generation BCR-ABL inhibitors followed:
- *Dasatinib* (Sprycel, 2006). More potent and active against many imatinib-resistant mutations.
- *Nilotinib* (Tasigna, 2007). Similar mechanism, better tolerability for some patients.
- *Bosutinib* (Bosulif, 2012). Dual SRC/ABL inhibition.
- *Ponatinib* (Iclusig, 2012). Active against the T315I mutation that confers resistance to other ABL inhibitors.
- *Asciminib* (Scemblix, 2021). Allosteric inhibitor binding the myristoyl pocket of ABL. Active against T315I and many other resistance mutations.

The sequential development of these drugs illustrates how targeted therapy resistance drives next-generation drug development. Each generation addresses the limitations of its predecessors.

## Major TKI families in clinical use

Tyrosine kinase inhibitors are now a large drug class with many FDA-approved agents. The major families:

*EGFR family inhibitors* in lung cancer:
- *First generation* (gefitinib, erlotinib). Reversible inhibitors. Active against sensitizing EGFR mutations (exon 19 deletions, L858R).
- *Second generation* (afatinib, dacomitinib). Irreversible inhibitors with broader EGFR family activity.
- *Third generation* (osimertinib). Irreversible inhibitor active against T790M (the common first-line resistance mutation) and sensitizing mutations. Now first-line standard for EGFR-mutant non-small-cell lung cancer.
- *Fourth generation* (lazertinib, others in development). Active against C797S and other newer resistance mutations.

*ALK inhibitors* in lung cancer:
- *Crizotinib* (first generation). Original ALK inhibitor; also active against ROS1 and MET.
- *Alectinib, brigatinib, ceritinib* (second generation). More potent; better CNS penetration.
- *Lorlatinib* (third generation). Active against most ALK resistance mutations.

*ROS1 inhibitors*: crizotinib, entrectinib, repotrectinib.

*BRAF inhibitors* (melanoma, thyroid, colorectal, others): vemurafenib, dabrafenib, encorafenib — usually combined with MEK inhibitors (trametinib, cobimetinib, binimetinib).

*MEK inhibitors* (combination with BRAF inhibitors): trametinib, cobimetinib, binimetinib.

*KRAS G12C inhibitors* (lung cancer, others): sotorasib, adagrasib.

*EGFR inhibitors in colorectal cancer* (anti-EGFR antibodies, not TKIs): cetuximab, panitumumab. Only effective in RAS-wild-type tumors.

*BTK inhibitors* (B-cell malignancies): ibrutinib, acalabrutinib, zanubrutinib, pirtobrutinib.

*JAK inhibitors* (myeloproliferative neoplasms): ruxolitinib, fedratinib, momelotinib.

*FLT3 inhibitors* (AML): midostaurin, gilteritinib, quizartinib.

*PI3K inhibitors*: idelalisib (CLL), copanlisib, alpelisib (breast cancer, PIK3CA-mutated), umbralisib.

*PARP inhibitors*: covered in 24B.

*CDK4/6 inhibitors*: palbociclib, ribociclib, abemaciclib. Discussed in Chapter 9B.

*VEGFR inhibitors* and multi-kinase inhibitors: sunitinib, sorafenib, pazopanib, axitinib, cabozantinib, lenvatinib, regorafenib, others. Discussed in Chapter 12B.

*FGFR inhibitors*: pemigatinib, infigratinib, futibatinib (cholangiocarcinoma), erdafitinib (urothelial cancer).

*MET inhibitors*: capmatinib, tepotinib (lung cancer with MET exon 14 skipping).

*RET inhibitors*: selpercatinib, pralsetinib (RET fusions and mutations in lung, thyroid, others).

*NTRK inhibitors*: larotrectinib, entrectinib, repotrectinib (NTRK fusion tumors, tumor-agnostic).

*MEK1/2 inhibitors* beyond melanoma: selumetinib (neurofibromatosis type 1, MEK pathway tumors).

*EZH2 inhibitors*: tazemetostat (follicular lymphoma, epithelioid sarcoma).

*IDH1/IDH2 inhibitors*: ivosidenib, enasidenib, olutasidenib (AML, cholangiocarcinoma, others).

*MENIN inhibitors*: revumenib (MLL-rearranged leukemias).

The list continues to grow. Each year brings approvals for new targets. The therapeutic landscape is increasingly defined by molecular profile rather than tumor type.

## Monoclonal antibodies

Beyond small molecule TKIs, monoclonal antibodies provide another approach to targeted therapy. Antibodies bind their target with high specificity and can:
- *Block the target's function* (e.g., bevacizumab binding VEGF prevents receptor activation).
- *Mark cells for immune destruction* through antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) and complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC).
- *Deliver attached payloads* (chemotherapy in antibody-drug conjugates; radiation in radioimmunotherapy).
- *Engage T cells* against cancer cells (bispecific T-cell engagers).
- *Block immune checkpoints* (covered in Chapter 16B and Chapter 25).

The major antibody categories:

*Anti-HER2 antibodies*. Trastuzumab (Herceptin) was the first transformative antibody therapy, approved in 1998 for HER2-positive metastatic breast cancer. It binds HER2 and inhibits its signaling. Pertuzumab (Perjeta) binds a different epitope; the combination is more effective than either alone. Margetuximab (Margenza) is engineered for enhanced ADCC.

*Anti-EGFR antibodies*. Cetuximab and panitumumab bind EGFR and block ligand binding. Used in colorectal cancer (RAS-wild-type) and head and neck cancer. Cetuximab is also approved with radiation for head and neck cancer.

*Anti-CD20 antibodies*. Rituximab (Rituxan) revolutionized B-cell lymphoma treatment, approved in 1997. Obinutuzumab (Gazyva) is a newer anti-CD20 antibody for CLL and follicular lymphoma. Ofatumumab (Arzerra) is another anti-CD20 option.

*Anti-CD38 antibodies* (myeloma). Daratumumab (Darzalex) and isatuximab (Sarclisa) for multiple myeloma.

*Anti-CD22 antibodies*: inotuzumab ozogamicin (an antibody-drug conjugate for ALL).

*Anti-CD30 antibodies*: brentuximab vedotin (an ADC for Hodgkin lymphoma and anaplastic large cell lymphoma).

*Anti-CD79b antibodies*: polatuzumab vedotin (ADC for diffuse large B-cell lymphoma).

*Anti-BCMA antibodies*: belantamab mafodotin (ADC for multiple myeloma; withdrawn in some markets).

*Anti-VEGF antibodies*: bevacizumab, ramucirumab (anti-VEGFR2). Discussed in Chapter 12B.

*Anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies*: pembrolizumab, nivolumab, atezolizumab, durvalumab, others. Discussed in Chapter 16B and Chapter 25.

*Anti-CTLA-4 antibodies*: ipilimumab, tremelimumab.

The list extends across many cancer types and continues to grow. Monoclonal antibodies have become a major class of cancer therapeutics.

## Antibody-drug conjugates

A particularly important development is *antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs)* — antibodies covalently linked to highly potent cytotoxic agents. The antibody provides targeting; the payload provides cell killing. ADCs deliver chemotherapy specifically to cancer cells expressing the antibody target.

The structural components:
- *Antibody*. Targets the cancer cell antigen.
- *Linker*. Connects the antibody to the payload. The linker must be stable in circulation but releasable in tumors. Several linker chemistries exist (cleavable: cathepsin-cleavable, acid-cleavable, redox-cleavable; non-cleavable: stable linker requiring antibody degradation for payload release).
- *Payload*. The cytotoxic agent. Typically much more potent than conventional chemotherapy (because only small amounts reach cancer cells through ADC delivery). Common payload classes:
  - *Auristatins* (MMAE, MMAF). Microtubule disruptors.
  - *Maytansinoids* (DM1, DM4). Microtubule disruptors.
  - *Calicheamicins*. DNA-damaging agents.
  - *Camptothecins* (deruxtecan, others). Topoisomerase I inhibitors.

The drug-to-antibody ratio (DAR) — number of payload molecules per antibody — affects efficacy and tolerability. Typical DAR is 4-8.

The mechanism of action:
1. ADC binds the target antigen on the cancer cell surface.
2. The ADC-antigen complex is internalized into the cell.
3. The linker is cleaved (or the antibody degraded), releasing the payload.
4. The released payload exerts its cytotoxic effect.
5. *Bystander effect*. With membrane-permeable payloads, free payload can diffuse to adjacent cells (including antigen-negative cells), killing them. This is therapeutically useful in heterogeneous tumors.

Major FDA-approved ADCs:

*Trastuzumab emtansine (T-DM1, Kadcyla)*. Anti-HER2 antibody linked to DM1. Approved for HER2-positive metastatic and high-risk early-stage breast cancer.

*Trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd, Enhertu)*. Anti-HER2 antibody linked to deruxtecan (topoisomerase I inhibitor). Substantially more active than T-DM1, including in HER2-low (not just HER2-positive) breast cancer. Approved for HER2-positive and HER2-low breast cancer, HER2-positive gastric cancer, and HER2-mutant lung cancer.

*Sacituzumab govitecan (Trodelvy)*. Anti-Trop-2 antibody linked to SN-38 (active metabolite of irinotecan). Approved for triple-negative breast cancer and HR-positive HER2-negative breast cancer.

*Datopotamab deruxtecan (Dato-DXd)*. Anti-Trop-2 antibody linked to deruxtecan. In development for breast and lung cancers.

*Brentuximab vedotin (Adcetris)*. Anti-CD30 antibody linked to MMAE. Approved for Hodgkin lymphoma, anaplastic large cell lymphoma, peripheral T-cell lymphoma.

*Inotuzumab ozogamicin (Besponsa)*. Anti-CD22 antibody linked to calicheamicin. Approved for B-cell ALL.

*Gemtuzumab ozogamicin (Mylotarg)*. Anti-CD33 antibody linked to calicheamicin. Approved for AML.

*Polatuzumab vedotin (Polivy)*. Anti-CD79b antibody linked to MMAE. Approved for DLBCL.

*Loncastuximab tesirine (Zynlonta)*. Anti-CD19 antibody linked to a PBD dimer. Approved for relapsed DLBCL.

*Tisotumab vedotin (Tivdak)*. Anti-tissue factor antibody linked to MMAE. Approved for cervical cancer.

*Enfortumab vedotin (Padcev)*. Anti-nectin-4 antibody linked to MMAE. Approved for urothelial cancer.

The ADC field is one of the most rapidly developing in oncology. New targets, better linkers, more potent payloads, and improved engineering are all being pursued. The success of T-DXd in particular — extending HER2-targeted therapy to HER2-low breast cancer and producing dramatic responses in heavily pretreated disease — has validated the platform and accelerated development.

## Bispecific antibodies

Bispecific antibodies bind two different targets simultaneously. The therapeutic concept varies by design:

*T-cell engagers*. Bispecific antibodies that bind a tumor antigen on one arm and CD3 on the T-cell receptor complex on the other arm. The antibody brings T cells into contact with cancer cells, triggering T cell activation and cancer cell killing. The major examples:
- *Blinatumomab* (Blincyto). Anti-CD19/CD3 BiTE for B-cell ALL. The first approved bispecific T-cell engager (2014).
- *Teclistamab* (Tecvayli), *talquetamab*, *elranatamab* — anti-BCMA/CD3 or anti-GPRC5D/CD3 bispecifics for multiple myeloma.
- *Mosunetuzumab* (Lunsumio), *epcoritamab*, *glofitamab* — anti-CD20/CD3 bispecifics for B-cell lymphomas.

*Tarlatamab*. Anti-DLL3/CD3 bispecific for small-cell lung cancer.

*Tebentafusp*. Bispecific fusion of an MHC-restricted TCR (recognizing gp100 peptide on melanoma) with anti-CD3. Different mechanism than antibody-based bispecifics but similar concept. Approved for HLA-A*02:01-positive uveal melanoma.

*Other bispecific designs*. Some bispecific antibodies engage two tumor antigens or two activation signals on T cells. The structural space is large.

The T-cell engagers can produce dramatic responses but also produce cytokine release syndrome (similar to CAR-T) requiring careful management. The mechanism is potent — recruiting cytotoxic T cells to tumor cells — but the recruitment is non-specific (any T cell can engage), distinguishing it from CAR-T where the engineered specificity is in the T cell itself.

## Patient selection and biomarkers

Targeted therapy works only in patients with the right molecular target. Patient selection through biomarker testing is essential.

The major principles:

*Test before treat*. Molecular testing should be done before initiating targeted therapy. For most actionable mutations, the appropriate test is FDA-approved (companion diagnostic) and reimbursed.

*Comprehensive profiling*. Many oncologists now routinely use comprehensive genomic profiling (NGS panels) to identify all targetable alterations at once, rather than sequential single-gene tests.

*Liquid biopsy*. When tissue is insufficient, ctDNA-based testing can identify many targetable alterations.

*Repeat testing at progression*. When patients progress on targeted therapy, repeat biopsy or liquid biopsy can identify resistance mutations and guide next-line therapy.

*Quality of testing matters*. Some tests have higher sensitivity than others. Some labs are more reliable than others. Multi-gene panels vary in what they detect.

The clinical implementation requires:
- Awareness of which biomarkers should be tested in which cancers.
- Access to appropriate testing (cost, insurance coverage, geographic availability).
- Interpretation expertise.
- Treatment access for biomarker-positive patients.

The implementation has improved substantially over the past decade but remains uneven. Cancer centers and large academic medical centers typically have robust infrastructure; community oncology practices and lower-resource settings often have gaps. Closing these gaps is a major equity issue.

## Toxicity profiles of targeted therapy

Targeted therapy toxicities differ from chemotherapy toxicities. Most are *on-target* effects — consequences of inhibiting the targeted molecule in normal cells that also express it.

Common targeted therapy toxicities:

*Skin and nail effects*. EGFR inhibitors produce characteristic acneiform rash (papulopustular eruption on face and trunk), paronychia, and hair changes. The rash actually correlates with anti-tumor activity (more rash = more EGFR inhibition = better response). MEK inhibitors produce skin rash.

*Diarrhea*. Common with many kinase inhibitors (EGFR inhibitors, sorafenib, sunitinib, others) and with MEK inhibitors. Usually manageable with antidiarrheal medication; severe cases require dose modification.

*Fatigue*. Almost universal across targeted therapy classes. Multiple proposed mechanisms (cytokine effects, mitochondrial effects, anemia).

*Hypertension*. Common with VEGF/VEGFR-targeted therapies (bevacizumab, sunitinib, others), through reduced NO signaling.

*Hand-foot syndrome*. Painful erythema and desquamation of palms and soles. Common with VEGFR multi-kinase inhibitors (sorafenib, sunitinib).

*Hepatotoxicity*. Many kinase inhibitors. Monitoring liver function is routine.

*Cardiotoxicity*. Various mechanisms. Trastuzumab causes reversible cardiomyopathy in a small percentage. Some kinase inhibitors prolong QT interval.

*Hypothyroidism*. Common with several multi-kinase inhibitors, especially sunitinib.

*Hyperglycemia*. Common with PI3K and mTOR pathway inhibitors.

*Pulmonary effects*. Some EGFR inhibitors cause interstitial lung disease (rare but serious). Some kinase inhibitors cause pneumonitis.

*Bleeding*. Some VEGF-targeted therapies. Tumor-related bleeding can be precipitated.

*Drug-drug interactions*. Many targeted therapies are CYP3A4 substrates or inhibitors. Careful attention to concomitant medications is needed.

*Immune-related adverse events*. With immune checkpoint inhibitors. Discussed in Chapter 25.

The toxicity profiles allow targeted therapy to be given for prolonged periods (often years) in metastatic disease — something not possible with conventional chemotherapy. The chronic nature of targeted therapy treatment is one of its features.

## What this chapter gives you

Targeted therapy exploits *oncogene addiction* — the dependence of cancer cells on specific molecular features for their growth and survival. Blocking the addicted target kills cancer cells selectively while sparing normal cells that don't depend on the target.

Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) are the dominant small-molecule targeted therapy class. The imatinib paradigm in CML — molecular target identification, structure-guided drug development, biomarker-driven trials, dramatic responses — has been replicated across many oncogenic kinases. Major families include EGFR, ALK, ROS1, BRAF, KRAS G12C, BTK, JAK, FLT3, PI3K, VEGFR, FGFR, MET, RET, NTRK, MEK, EZH2, IDH1/IDH2, and MENIN inhibitors. The list continues to grow.

Monoclonal antibodies bind specific targets with high specificity. They can block function, mark cells for immune destruction, deliver payloads (ADCs), engage T cells (bispecific antibodies), or block immune checkpoints. Major examples include trastuzumab and pertuzumab (HER2), cetuximab and panitumumab (EGFR), rituximab (CD20), daratumumab (CD38), and many others.

Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) combine antibody targeting with potent cytotoxic payloads. Major examples include T-DM1 and T-DXd (HER2), sacituzumab govitecan (Trop-2), brentuximab vedotin (CD30), enfortumab vedotin (nectin-4), and many others. T-DXd has been particularly transformative, extending HER2 targeting to HER2-low cancers.

Bispecific antibodies engage two targets simultaneously. T-cell engagers (anti-CD19/CD3 blinatumomab, anti-BCMA/CD3 teclistamab, others) bring T cells into contact with cancer cells. The class has produced major advances in B-cell and plasma cell malignancies.

Patient selection through biomarker testing is essential for targeted therapy. Comprehensive genomic profiling at diagnosis or at progression identifies actionable alterations. Implementation has improved but remains uneven across settings.

Toxicity profiles of targeted therapy differ from chemotherapy. Most toxicities are on-target effects (consequences of inhibiting the molecular target in normal cells expressing it). Common toxicities include skin and nail effects, diarrhea, fatigue, hypertension, hand-foot syndrome, hepatotoxicity, cardiotoxicity, hypothyroidism, hyperglycemia, and pulmonary effects.
