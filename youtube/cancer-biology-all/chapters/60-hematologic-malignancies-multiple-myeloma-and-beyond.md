# Chapter 60 — Hematologic Malignancies: Multiple Myeloma and Beyond


## TL;DR

Multiple myeloma in 2015 had a median survival of about six years. Today it's closer to ten and rising.

Hold the framing. Among the cancers transformed most dramatically by modern therapy, multiple myeloma deserves particular attention. The disease was historically fatal within 2-3 years of diagnosis. With introduction of high-dose chemotherapy plus autologous stem cell transplant in the 1990s, survival extended to 4-5 years. With introduction of immunomodulatory drugs (thalidomide, lenalidomide, pomalidomide) and proteasome inhibitors (bortezomib, carfilzomib, ixazomib) in the 2000s, survival improved further. With introduction of monoclonal antibodies (daratumumab, isatuximab) in the 2010s, then bispecific antibodies and CAR-T cells in the 2020s, the disease has been transformed into something approaching chronic management for many patients.

The myeloma story illustrates a broader principle. Hematologic malignancies have been the proving ground for many of the most important new cancer therapy modalities. CAR-T cells were proven first in B-cell ALL and lymphomas. Bispecific T-cell engagers were established in leukemias and lymphomas. Antibody-drug conjugates have particularly succeeded in hematologic cancers. The infrastructure for stem cell transplantation, MRD monitoring, and personalized cancer therapy emerged largely from hematologic malignancy treatment.

This chapter — the second of two on hematologic malignancies — covers plasma cell neoplasms (especially multiple myeloma) and broader treatment principles. The chapter complements 33A, which covered leukemias and lymphomas.

## Multiple myeloma

Multiple myeloma is a cancer of plasma cells — terminally differentiated B-cell lineage cells that produce antibodies.

*Biology*. Malignant plasma cells accumulate in bone marrow, producing a monoclonal immunoglobulin (paraprotein, "M protein") that can be detected in blood and urine. The cancer cells produce factors that activate osteoclasts and suppress osteoblasts, causing the characteristic *osteolytic lesions* of myeloma.

The pathogenesis involves accumulation of genetic alterations:
- *Primary translocations*. Recurrent IgH translocations: t(4;14) involving FGFR3 and MMSET; t(11;14) involving cyclin D1; t(14;16) involving MAF; t(14;20) involving MAFB; t(6;14) involving cyclin D3.
- *Hyperdiploidy*. Gain of odd chromosomes; generally favorable prognosis.
- *Secondary alterations*. MYC translocations, TP53 mutations or del(17p), KRAS/NRAS mutations, BRAF mutations.

The disease progresses through stages:
- *Monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS)*. Pre-malignant plasma cell condition with low M protein and minimal plasma cell expansion. Some progress to myeloma at about 1% per year.
- *Smoldering multiple myeloma*. Higher M protein and plasma cell burden than MGUS but no end-organ damage. Higher progression risk (about 10% per year early; less later).
- *Active multiple myeloma*. End-organ damage defining "CRAB" criteria (Calcium elevation, Renal insufficiency, Anemia, Bone lesions) or other myeloma-defining events.

*Epidemiology*. Annual incidence about 7 per 100,000. Median age at diagnosis 70. More common in men. Black populations have approximately twice the incidence of white populations.

*Risk factors*. Age, family history, race (Black > white > Asian), prior MGUS, ionizing radiation, certain occupational exposures.

*Presentation*. Symptoms reflect organ involvement:
- *Anemia*. Often the most common presentation.
- *Bone pain* (especially back pain) from osteolytic lesions; pathologic fractures.
- *Renal insufficiency*. From light chain deposition or hypercalcemia.
- *Hypercalcemia*. From bone resorption.
- *Recurrent infections*. From immunoparesis (suppression of normal immunoglobulin production).
- *Hyperviscosity syndrome*. From very high M protein levels (more common with IgM, occurs in Waldenström macroglobulinemia).
- *Plasmacytomas*. Localized masses of malignant plasma cells.

*Diagnosis*. The workup includes:
- *Serum and urine protein electrophoresis and immunofixation*. Identifies the M protein.
- *Free light chain assay*. Measures kappa and lambda free light chains and their ratio.
- *Bone marrow biopsy*. Quantifies plasma cell percentage and characterizes cytogenetics and FISH for prognostic markers.
- *Imaging*. Whole-body MRI or PET-CT for lesion detection. Skeletal survey historically used; now largely replaced by MRI or PET.
- *Lab studies*. CBC, comprehensive metabolic panel, calcium, β2-microglobulin (prognostic).

*Staging*. International Staging System (ISS) based on β2-microglobulin and albumin, revised in Revised ISS (R-ISS) incorporating LDH and high-risk cytogenetics.

*Treatment*. Multiple myeloma is treated with combination therapy in defined phases. The treatment landscape has been transformed over the past 20 years.

*Initial therapy (induction)*. Modern regimens for transplant-eligible patients typically use 4-drug combinations:
- *VRd*. Bortezomib + lenalidomide + dexamethasone.
- *DRd*. Daratumumab + lenalidomide + dexamethasone.
- *D-VRd*. Daratumumab + bortezomib + lenalidomide + dexamethasone (the GRIFFIN regimen).
- *KRd*. Carfilzomib + lenalidomide + dexamethasone.
- Various other combinations.

For transplant-ineligible patients, similar regimens with possibly different doses and durations.

*Autologous stem cell transplant*. High-dose melphalan followed by autologous stem cell rescue. Standard for transplant-eligible patients (typically up to age 70-75 in good health). Improves response and prolongs PFS. Single versus tandem transplants based on risk and response.

*Consolidation therapy*. Further combination therapy after transplant.

*Maintenance therapy*. Lenalidomide maintenance is standard after transplant. Reduces recurrence and extends survival. Duration may be indefinite or until intolerance/progression.

*Relapsed/refractory myeloma*. Sequential use of available agents based on prior exposures and patient factors:
- *Proteasome inhibitors*. Bortezomib, carfilzomib, ixazomib.
- *Immunomodulatory drugs (IMiDs)*. Lenalidomide, pomalidomide. Newer CELMoDs (mezigdomide, iberdomide) in development.
- *Monoclonal antibodies*. Daratumumab, isatuximab (anti-CD38). Elotuzumab (anti-SLAMF7).
- *XPO1 inhibitor*. Selinexor.
- *Belantamab mafodotin*. Anti-BCMA antibody-drug conjugate. (Withdrawn in some markets after failed trial.)
- *BCMA-directed therapies*:
  - *CAR-T cells*. Idecabtagene vicleucel (Abecma) and ciltacabtagene autoleucel (Carvykti). Substantial response rates in heavily pretreated disease.
  - *Bispecific T-cell engagers*. Teclistamab (anti-BCMA/CD3), elranatamab (anti-BCMA/CD3), talquetamab (anti-GPRC5D/CD3). Approved for relapsed/refractory disease.
  - *Antibody-drug conjugates*. Belantamab mafodotin and newer BCMA ADCs.

The bispecific antibodies and CAR-T cells represent the leading edge of myeloma therapy. The shift from drug-by-drug treatment to multi-modal multi-agent treatment has transformed outcomes.

*MRD monitoring*. Flow cytometry and increasingly NGS-based MRD detection. MRD-negative status correlates with improved outcomes.

*Outcomes*. Median overall survival approaching 10 years in modern era for transplant-eligible patients. Older or unfit patients have shorter survival but still substantially improved over historical outcomes. The disease is being increasingly managed as a chronic condition.

The myeloma treatment landscape continues to expand rapidly. The integration of multiple modalities (proteasome inhibition, immunomodulation, monoclonal antibodies, ADCs, CAR-T, bispecifics) provides multiple sequential treatment options that extend survival substantially.

## Other plasma cell disorders

*Waldenström macroglobulinemia*. Lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma with IgM secretion. Distinct from typical multiple myeloma. Treated with rituximab-based regimens, bortezomib + rituximab, BTK inhibitors (ibrutinib, zanubrutinib).

*AL amyloidosis*. Light chain amyloid deposition in tissues. Treatment includes daratumumab + CyBorD (cyclophosphamide + bortezomib + dexamethasone) and similar regimens. Anti-fibril antibodies in development.

*Solitary plasmacytoma*. Single mass of plasma cells (osseous or extramedullary). Treated with radiation; many eventually progress to myeloma.

*POEMS syndrome*. Rare paraneoplastic syndrome with multiple manifestations and clonal plasma cell disorder. Specialized treatment.

## Myelodysplastic syndromes

MDS are clonal hematopoietic stem cell disorders characterized by ineffective hematopoiesis and dysplastic features. May progress to AML.

*Biology*. Heterogeneous group with various molecular abnormalities. Common alterations:
- *Recurrent mutations*. SF3B1, SRSF2, U2AF1, ZRSR2, TET2, DNMT3A, ASXL1, RUNX1, TP53, others.
- *Cytogenetic abnormalities*. Del(5q), -7/del(7q), trisomy 8, complex karyotype, others.
- *Specific subtypes*:
  - MDS with isolated del(5q). Distinct entity; responds to lenalidomide.
  - MDS with ring sideroblasts. SF3B1 mutation.
  - MDS with biallelic TP53 mutations. Very poor prognosis.

*Risk stratification*. IPSS-R or IPSS-M (molecular) integrate cytogenetics, blast percentage, cytopenias, and (for IPSS-M) mutations.

*Treatment*:
- *Lower-risk MDS*. Supportive care (transfusion support, growth factors), erythropoiesis-stimulating agents, lenalidomide for del(5q), luspatercept for SF3B1-mutated MDS-RS.
- *Higher-risk MDS*. Hypomethylating agents (azacitidine, decitabine) are the standard. Allogeneic stem cell transplant for fit patients. Venetoclax + hypomethylating agent combinations being studied.

*Outcomes*. Highly variable based on risk group. Lower-risk MDS may have years of stable disease. Higher-risk MDS has poor prognosis without transplant.

## Myeloproliferative neoplasms

A group of chronic myeloid disorders with overproduction of one or more blood cell lineages.

*Polycythemia vera*. Overproduction of red blood cells. Almost always driven by JAK2 V617F mutation (or rarely other JAK2 mutations). Treatment includes phlebotomy, low-dose aspirin, hydroxyurea or interferon, ruxolitinib (JAK inhibitor) for refractory cases.

*Essential thrombocythemia*. Thrombocytosis. JAK2, CALR, MPL mutations. Treatment with aspirin, hydroxyurea, anagrelide, interferon.

*Primary myelofibrosis*. Bone marrow fibrosis with cytopenias and constitutional symptoms. Similar mutations as ET. JAK inhibitors (ruxolitinib, fedratinib, momelotinib, pacritinib) for symptom control and splenomegaly. Allogeneic transplant for younger fit patients.

*Mastocytosis*. Mast cell proliferation. KIT mutations (especially D816V). Midostaurin and avapritinib are active.

The MPN field has been transformed by JAK inhibitor therapy (ruxolitinib first) and increasingly by mutation-targeted approaches.

## Stem cell transplantation in hematologic malignancies

Stem cell transplantation (SCT) is a defining feature of hematologic malignancy treatment. Two main types:

*Autologous stem cell transplant*. Patient's own stem cells collected, treatment given (typically high-dose chemotherapy), then stem cells reinfused to rescue from marrow toxicity. Used for myeloma (most common indication), some lymphomas, occasionally others.

*Allogeneic stem cell transplant*. Stem cells from a donor (related, unrelated matched, or umbilical cord blood). Provides both rescue from intensive treatment and a graft-versus-leukemia/lymphoma immune effect. Used for high-risk acute leukemias, MDS, some chronic leukemias, some lymphomas, multiple myeloma in selected cases.

The components of allogeneic SCT:

*Conditioning*. Pre-transplant treatment to:
- Eradicate residual cancer.
- Suppress recipient immunity to prevent graft rejection.
- Create marrow space for engraftment.
Approaches include myeloablative conditioning (high-dose chemotherapy ± TBI) and reduced-intensity conditioning (less intense, relies more on graft-versus-tumor effect).

*Graft sources*:
- *HLA-matched related donor* (usually a sibling).
- *HLA-matched unrelated donor*.
- *Mismatched related donor (haploidentical)*. Half-matched family member. Use has expanded with post-transplant cyclophosphamide GVHD prophylaxis.
- *Umbilical cord blood*. Stem cells from umbilical cord blood at birth.

*Engraftment*. Donor cells reconstitute the bone marrow over weeks. Neutrophils, platelets, then full hematologic recovery.

*Complications*:
- *Graft-versus-host disease (GVHD)*. Donor T cells attack recipient tissues. Acute GVHD (early) and chronic GVHD (late). Major cause of transplant-related morbidity and mortality.
- *Infections*. Bacterial, fungal, viral (CMV reactivation, EBV, others).
- *Veno-occlusive disease*. Hepatic complication of conditioning.
- *Organ toxicities*. From conditioning.
- *Secondary malignancies*. Late complication.
- *Long-term effects*. Endocrine dysfunction, cataracts, cognitive effects, others.

*Outcomes*. Vary by disease, conditioning, donor type, age, and other factors. Allogeneic SCT can cure some cancers that no other therapy can — high-risk acute leukemias in particular.

The transplant field is being affected by CAR-T cells and other novel therapies. For some indications (such as relapsed B-cell lymphoma), CAR-T may be preferred over transplant. For others, transplant remains the most established curative option.

## MRD and the future of hematologic cancer monitoring

Minimal residual disease detection has become standard in hematologic malignancies, with different approaches for different diseases:

*ALL*. Flow cytometry-based MRD or PCR for specific fusion transcripts (BCR-ABL in Ph+ ALL). MRD-positive patients after induction may benefit from intensification including transplant.

*AML*. Flow cytometry, NPM1 PCR for NPM1-mutated AML, increasingly NGS-based approaches. MRD-positive after induction associated with relapse risk. May guide consolidation choices.

*CML*. Quantitative BCR-ABL PCR. Treatment monitoring uses molecular response milestones (BCR-ABL <10% at 3 months, <1% at 6 months, <0.1% at 12 months for major molecular response). Deep molecular response (MR4.0-4.5) may enable treatment-free remission.

*Multiple myeloma*. Flow cytometry, increasingly NGS-based MRD detection. MRD negativity correlates with improved outcomes. Many newer trials use MRD as primary or secondary endpoint.

*CLL*. Flow cytometry-based MRD for measuring response depth. MRD-negative status with venetoclax-based combinations correlates with durable remission.

*Lymphomas*. ctDNA-based MRD increasingly being explored. PCR for translocation-specific markers in some lymphomas.

The MRD framework is reshaping hematologic malignancy management. MRD-guided treatment decisions are increasingly evidence-based. The future likely involves more sensitive MRD detection, broader application across diseases, and integration with treatment decision-making.

## Supportive care in hematologic malignancies

Patients with hematologic malignancies require intensive supportive care:

*Cytopenia management*. Transfusion support (red cells, platelets), growth factors (G-CSF), thrombopoietin agonists.

*Infection prevention and treatment*. Antimicrobial prophylaxis (antibacterial, antifungal, antiviral, anti-Pneumocystis) in high-risk patients. Aggressive treatment of suspected infections. IVIG for hypogammaglobulinemia.

*Tumor lysis syndrome*. Risk in high-burden disease. Prevention with hydration and allopurinol or rasburicase. Recognition and management critical.

*Hyperviscosity*. Can occur in myeloma (high IgM particularly), Waldenström macroglobulinemia. Plasmapheresis may be needed.

*Hypercalcemia*. Common in myeloma. Hydration, bisphosphonates, denosumab. Severe cases may need calcitonin.

*Bone health*. Bisphosphonates or denosumab to prevent skeletal-related events in myeloma. Vertebroplasty for symptomatic compression fractures.

*Neuropathy*. Common with proteasome inhibitors and vinca alkaloids. Dose modification, supportive care.

*Pain management*. Various etiologies — bone pain, nerve pain, post-radiation, treatment-related.

*Psychosocial support*. Hematologic malignancies often involve prolonged treatment, hospitalization, and significant life disruption.

*Long-term effects management*. For survivors, monitoring for late effects of treatment (cardiac toxicity, secondary cancers, endocrinopathies, cognitive effects).

The supportive care infrastructure is substantial. Hematologic malignancies are often treated at specialized centers with comprehensive support services.

## What this chapter gives you

Multiple myeloma has been transformed by modern therapy, with median survival approaching 10 years for transplant-eligible patients (versus 2-3 years historically). The treatment landscape includes proteasome inhibitors (bortezomib, carfilzomib, ixazomib), immunomodulatory drugs (lenalidomide, pomalidomide), monoclonal antibodies (daratumumab, isatuximab), antibody-drug conjugates (belantamab mafodotin), BCMA-targeted CAR-T cells (ide-cel, cilta-cel), bispecific T-cell engagers (teclistamab, elranatamab, talquetamab), and autologous stem cell transplant. Modern multi-drug induction (VRd, D-VRd, KRd) plus transplant plus maintenance has produced unprecedented response depths and durations.

Other plasma cell disorders include Waldenström macroglobulinemia (BTK inhibitor-responsive), AL amyloidosis (daratumumab-based regimens), solitary plasmacytoma, and POEMS syndrome.

Myelodysplastic syndromes are heterogeneous disorders with risk stratification through IPSS-R and IPSS-M. Treatment depends on risk: supportive care, growth factors, lenalidomide (del(5q)), luspatercept (SF3B1) for lower-risk; hypomethylating agents and allogeneic transplant for higher-risk.

Myeloproliferative neoplasms (polycythemia vera, essential thrombocythemia, primary myelofibrosis, mastocytosis) have been transformed by JAK inhibitors (ruxolitinib, fedratinib, momelotinib, pacritinib) and increasingly mutation-targeted approaches.

Stem cell transplantation remains a defining feature of hematologic malignancy treatment. Autologous transplant for myeloma and some lymphomas. Allogeneic transplant provides graft-versus-tumor effect and remains the most established curative option for high-risk acute leukemias, MDS, and some other diseases. CAR-T cells and other novel therapies are reshaping the transplant landscape for some indications.

MRD detection has become central to hematologic malignancy management, with different approaches for different diseases. MRD-guided treatment decisions are increasingly evidence-based and likely to expand.

Supportive care infrastructure (transfusion support, infection prevention, growth factors, bone health, neuropathy management, psychosocial support, long-term effects monitoring) is substantial and essential.

The principles emerging from hematologic malignancies — combination therapy, sequential use of multiple modalities, MRD monitoring, targeted approaches, immunotherapy integration — are being applied increasingly to solid tumors. The pace of progress in hematologic cancers has been remarkable and continues to accelerate.
