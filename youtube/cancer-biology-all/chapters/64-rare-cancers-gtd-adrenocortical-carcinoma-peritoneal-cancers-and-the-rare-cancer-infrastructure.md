The first cancer ever cured by chemotherapy was a rare one.

Hold the framing. In 1956, Min Chiu Li and Roy Hertz at the National Cancer Institute reported the cure of metastatic gestational choriocarcinoma using methotrexate. The case was historic — the first demonstration that a metastatic solid cancer could be cured by chemotherapy alone. Choriocarcinoma is rare. The patient population is small. The biology is unusual (the cancer arises from pregnancy-related tissue and produces β-hCG that serves as both a marker and a window into disease activity). But the lessons learned from this rare cancer reshaped oncology. The principle that chemotherapy could cure cancer, even metastatic cancer, transformed how cancer was approached. The principle that biomarker-guided treatment could refine therapy emerged here. The infrastructure of clinical research in rare cancers was established in part through this work.

Rare cancers have continued to teach oncology lessons beyond their incidence. Pediatric ALL cure rates that informed adult ALL approaches. Testicular cancer chemotherapy regimens that influenced germ cell cancer treatment generally. GIST imatinib approval that demonstrated targeted therapy success outside leukemia. Each rare cancer category, when studied seriously, has produced insights with broader implications.

This chapter — the second of two on rare cancers — covers gestational trophoblastic disease, adrenocortical carcinoma, peritoneal cancers, and the broader infrastructure for rare cancer care. The chapter complements 35A, which covered sarcomas and thymic tumors.

## Gestational trophoblastic disease

A unique spectrum of pregnancy-related neoplasms. Notable for being the first metastatic cancer cured by chemotherapy.

*Spectrum*. Gestational trophoblastic disease (GTD) includes:
- *Hydatidiform mole* (partial and complete). Benign trophoblastic proliferation. Most common form.
- *Invasive mole*. Locally invasive trophoblastic disease.
- *Choriocarcinoma*. Malignant trophoblastic tumor. Highly aggressive.
- *Placental site trophoblastic tumor (PSTT)*. Rare.
- *Epithelioid trophoblastic tumor (ETT)*. Rare.

*Biology*. Arise from placental trophoblast cells. Genetically derived from the conceptus rather than the mother. Some have specific chromosomal patterns (complete mole has paternal genome only; partial mole has triploid).

*Epidemiology*. Hydatidiform mole occurs in about 1 in 1,000 pregnancies in the US. Higher incidence in some regions (Asia, Latin America). Choriocarcinoma is rare (~1 in 40,000 pregnancies in the US; higher in some regions).

*Diagnostics*. Quantitative β-hCG is a remarkable tumor marker — extremely sensitive (can detect single tumor cell), specific (only trophoblastic tissue and normal pregnancy produce hCG), and provides a precise measure of disease burden. The β-hCG level guides:
- Diagnosis of GTD (rising hCG after pregnancy or at unusual levels).
- Treatment response (declining hCG indicates response).
- Surveillance after treatment (rising hCG indicates recurrence).
- Risk stratification.

Imaging — pelvic ultrasound for initial evaluation. CT or MRI for metastatic disease evaluation.

*Treatment by type*:

*Hydatidiform mole*. Suction evacuation of uterine contents. Surveillance with serial hCG to detect persistent disease. Most resolve completely. Persistent gestational trophoblastic neoplasia (PGTN) develops in ~15% of complete moles and ~5% of partial moles.

*Persistent gestational trophoblastic neoplasia (low-risk, FIGO score <7)*. Single-agent methotrexate (with leucovorin rescue) or single-agent actinomycin D. High cure rates (>95%).

*High-risk metastatic disease (FIGO score ≥7)*. EMA-CO regimen (etoposide, methotrexate, actinomycin D, cyclophosphamide, vincristine). Or similar multi-agent regimens. High cure rates (>85%) even in high-risk disease.

*PSTT and ETT*. Often more chemotherapy-resistant. Hysterectomy is the primary treatment for localized disease. EP-EMA (etoposide + platinum + EMA) for advanced disease. Pembrolizumab has shown activity (these tumors can have high mutation burden).

*Surveillance*. Long-term hCG monitoring after treatment. Avoidance of pregnancy during surveillance (since pregnancy hCG would confound monitoring).

*Outcomes*. GTD has remarkable cure rates with appropriate treatment:
- Low-risk PGTN: essentially 100% cure.
- High-risk metastatic disease: >85% cure.
- Even brain metastases often respond to chemotherapy with possible cure.

The combination of an excellent tumor marker (β-hCG) and chemosensitive disease has made GTD one of the most curable metastatic cancers. The history of GTD treatment has profoundly influenced general oncology.

## Adrenocortical carcinoma

A rare and aggressive cancer of the adrenal cortex.

*Epidemiology*. Annual incidence about 1 per 1,000,000 (extremely rare). Bimodal age distribution — children (<5 years) and adults (40-50 years).

*Biology*. Heterogeneous molecular landscape. Common features:
- *TP53 mutations*. Both somatic and germline (Li-Fraumeni syndrome).
- *IGF2 overexpression*. Through loss of imprinting at 11p15.
- *Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation*. CTNNB1 mutations in ~25%.
- *Hereditary syndromes*. Li-Fraumeni, Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome (pediatric), MEN1, Carney complex, others.

*Presentation*. About 60% are functional (produce cortisol, androgens, mineralocorticoids, estrogens, or combinations). Cushing syndrome from cortisol excess is most common. Virilization in women from androgen excess. Hypertension and hypokalemia from mineralocorticoid excess. Feminization in men from estrogen excess.

Non-functional tumors present with abdominal pain, mass, or incidental finding.

*Diagnosis*. Imaging (CT or MRI showing large adrenal mass; size >4 cm raises suspicion). Hormonal workup. Biopsy generally avoided due to bleeding risk and concern for tract seeding. Definitive diagnosis often at surgery.

*Staging*. ENSAT (European Network for the Study of Adrenal Tumors) staging.

*Treatment*:

*Surgery*. Complete resection is the only curative option. Open adrenalectomy preferred over laparoscopic for known/suspected adrenocortical carcinoma (concerns about peritoneal seeding with laparoscopic approach).

*Mitotane*. An adrenolytic agent specific for adrenocortical tissue. Used adjuvantly after surgery (particularly for high-risk disease) and for metastatic disease. Requires specific monitoring (mitotane levels, cortisol replacement may be needed). Has been a foundation of adrenocortical carcinoma treatment for decades.

*Combination chemotherapy*. EDP-M (etoposide + doxorubicin + cisplatin + mitotane) is the standard for advanced disease (FIRM-ACT trial). Median survival with treatment about 14-15 months.

*Targeted therapy*. Limited approved options. Various agents in clinical trials.

*Radiation therapy*. For local control of unresectable disease or recurrence.

*Outcomes*. Localized completely resected disease: 5-year survival 40-60%. Metastatic disease: <15%. Substantial unmet need for better therapies.

## Peritoneal and primary peritoneal cancers

Cancers involving the peritoneum present specific challenges and have unique therapeutic considerations.

### Primary peritoneal carcinoma

Cancer arising primarily in the peritoneal lining. Histologically similar to ovarian cancer (particularly high-grade serous carcinoma).

*Biology*. Closely related to ovarian and fallopian tube cancers. May arise from müllerian-derived tissue or directly from peritoneal cells. BRCA mutations and HRD features common.

*Treatment*. Similar to advanced ovarian cancer:
- *Cytoreductive surgery*. Maximal cytoreduction.
- *Platinum-based chemotherapy*. With or without bevacizumab.
- *PARP inhibitor maintenance*. For HRD-positive disease.

### Peritoneal carcinomatosis from other cancers

Spread of cancer (colorectal, gastric, ovarian, appendiceal, others) to the peritoneum. Different management based on origin:

*Pseudomyxoma peritonei*. Mucinous peritoneal spread typically from appendiceal mucinous neoplasms. Specific management with cytoreductive surgery and HIPEC (heated intraperitoneal chemotherapy).

*Peritoneal metastases from colorectal cancer*. Selected patients may benefit from cytoreductive surgery + HIPEC. Recent trials (PRODIGE 7) have raised questions about routine HIPEC.

*Peritoneal mesothelioma*. Mesothelioma arising in peritoneum (less common than pleural). Cytoreductive surgery + HIPEC is standard for selected patients.

*Gastric cancer peritoneal metastases*. Generally treated systemically. HIPEC role being studied (PIPAC and laparoscopic intraperitoneal approaches in development).

### Hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC)

A specialized approach using heated chemotherapy in the peritoneal cavity at the time of cytoreductive surgery.

*Procedure*. Cytoreductive surgery to remove all visible tumor. Then chemotherapy (typically mitomycin C, cisplatin, or oxaliplatin) heated to 41-43°C circulated through the peritoneal cavity for 60-90 minutes. Heat may enhance chemotherapy effects.

*Indications*. Currently established for:
- Pseudomyxoma peritonei. Standard care.
- Peritoneal mesothelioma. Standard care for selected patients.
- Colorectal peritoneal metastases. Use being refined (PRODIGE 7).
- Selected ovarian and gastric peritoneal disease. Use evolving.

*Outcomes*. Highly variable based on disease, completeness of cytoreduction, and patient factors. Pseudomyxoma peritonei has substantial long-term survival (10-year survival 50-70%). Colorectal peritoneal carcinomatosis has more modest benefits.

The HIPEC field requires substantial expertise. Procedures are concentrated at specialized centers. Patient selection is critical.

## Other rare cancers

Many other rare cancers don't fit specific categories but collectively affect substantial patient numbers:

*Carcinoid and neuroendocrine tumors*. From lung, GI, pancreatic, and other sites. Various differentiation and behavior. Functional tumors produce hormones with paraneoplastic syndromes. Treatment options include surgery, somatostatin analogs, peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (Lu-177-DOTATATE), targeted therapy (everolimus, sunitinib for pancreatic NETs), chemotherapy for high-grade variants.

*Paraganglioma and pheochromocytoma*. Catecholamine-producing tumors. Surgical management primarily. Specific molecular features (SDH mutations, VHL, NF1, RET) guide assessment. Lu-177-DOTATATE for unresectable disease.

*Parathyroid carcinoma*. Rare cancer with hypercalcemia. Surgery primary; limited other options.

*Vulvar and vaginal cancers*. Less common gynecologic cancers. Various histologies. Surgery and radiation primary.

*Penile cancer*. HPV-related in many cases. Surgery primary; chemoradiation alternative.

*Male breast cancer*. Similar biology and treatment to female breast cancer but with specific considerations.

*Salivary gland cancers*. Diverse histologies (mucoepidermoid, adenoid cystic, salivary duct carcinoma, others). Various molecular features. Treatment depends on histology.

*Anal cancer*. HPV-related. Chemoradiation (Nigro regimen) is curative for most localized disease. Surgery reserved for failures.

*Mesothelioma*. Pleural mesothelioma most common (asbestos-related); peritoneal less common. Pleural disease treated with chemotherapy ± immunotherapy (durvalumab + tremelimumab approved); surgery for selected patients.

*Cancer of unknown primary (CUP)*. Discussed in 30A. Diverse entity now managed with molecular profiling-guided treatment.

*Many other rare cancers* (Erdheim-Chester disease, histiocytic neoplasms, various sarcoma subtypes, rare epithelial cancers, etc.).

Each rare cancer typically has its own treatment paradigm. Comprehensive guidelines often refer to expert centers for current management.

## The rare cancer infrastructure

Several specialized infrastructure elements have developed to support rare cancer care and research:

*Specialized rare cancer centers*. Major academic medical centers often have dedicated programs for specific rare cancer categories. NCI Cancer Centers, comprehensive cancer centers, and disease-specific programs.

*Cooperative groups*:
- *Sarcoma Alliance for Research through Collaboration (SARC)*. US sarcoma cooperative group.
- *European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC)*. With specific rare cancer working groups.
- *Children's Oncology Group (COG)*. Pediatric cancers including rare adult-emerging entities.
- *Rare Cancer Genetics Registry*. Cataloging rare cancer molecular features.

*International collaboration*. Many rare cancers require international cooperation to assemble adequate patient numbers for studies. Networks like the European Reference Network on Rare Adult Solid Cancers (EURACAN) facilitate this.

*Tumor registries*. Many rare cancers have specific registries (sarcoma registries, GTD registries, others) collecting data on outcomes, treatments, and biology.

*Patient advocacy organizations*. Disease-specific advocacy groups (Sarcoma Foundation of America, International Society for Trophoblastic Disease, various others) provide patient support and advocate for research funding.

*Tumor-agnostic clinical trials*. Basket trials and master protocols enroll patients across cancer types based on molecular features. Larotrectinib (NTRK fusion), entrectinib, pembrolizumab (MSI-H, TMB-H), and others have entered clinical use through such designs.

*Orphan drug regulatory pathways*. The Orphan Drug Act in the US (1983) and similar provisions in Europe (1999), Japan, and other regions provide incentives for rare disease drug development. The provisions have supported many rare cancer drug approvals.

*Genomic profiling*. Comprehensive genomic profiling has been particularly valuable for rare cancers — identifying actionable alterations that may be common to multiple rare cancers and providing access to tumor-agnostic therapies.

*Real-world evidence*. For very rare cancers, traditional randomized trials may not be feasible. Real-world evidence from registries and electronic health records is increasingly used to inform treatment.

The infrastructure has improved substantially over recent decades but remains imperfect. Many rare cancers still suffer from limited research investment and access to expertise.

## Clinical implications and practical considerations

Several practical considerations apply to rare cancer care:

*Referral to specialized centers*. For most rare cancers, treatment at a specialized center with disease-specific expertise is associated with better outcomes. Practical barriers (geography, cost, insurance) limit referral for some patients.

*Pathology expert review*. Rare cancer diagnoses frequently change on expert review. Pathology consultation at academic centers is often appropriate for confirmation.

*Multidisciplinary assessment*. Rare cancers typically benefit from multidisciplinary tumor board review. The team may need to include subspecialists not routinely available at the patient's home institution.

*Clinical trial consideration*. Clinical trials should be considered for most rare cancer patients given the limited evidence supporting standard treatments. Tumor-agnostic trials may be options when molecular profiling reveals relevant alterations.

*Genetic counseling*. Many rare cancers have hereditary components or are part of hereditary syndromes. Genetic counseling is often warranted.

*Patient advocacy and support*. Connection to disease-specific patient advocacy organizations can provide substantial support — practical, emotional, and informational.

*Off-label drug use*. Rare cancer treatment often uses drugs in off-label contexts based on biological rationale or limited evidence. Reimbursement may be challenging.

*Survivorship*. Survivors of rare cancers may have specific late effects requiring monitoring. Coordination with appropriate specialists.

The care of rare cancer patients requires combining general oncology principles with disease-specific expertise. The trend toward molecular characterization has been particularly helpful for rare cancers, providing access to tumor-agnostic therapies and connection to broader research networks.

## Lessons rare cancers teach

Rare cancers have produced many important lessons for oncology generally:

*Chemotherapy can cure metastatic cancer*. Demonstrated first in gestational choriocarcinoma (1956) and testicular cancer (1970s). Reshaped how cancer was approached.

*Single dominant mutations can drive entire cancers*. CML and GIST established the principle that some cancers are "addicted" to a specific oncogene that can be targeted therapeutically.

*Highly chemosensitive cancers can be curable*. Hodgkin lymphoma, testicular cancer, GTD, pediatric ALL — all are examples of cancers where intensive multi-agent chemotherapy produces high cure rates.

*Biomarkers can guide treatment*. β-hCG in GTD established the principle that tumor markers can guide therapy initiation, response assessment, and surveillance. Now applied broadly with PSA, CA-125, AFP, ctDNA, and many others.

*Molecular profiling can transform care*. NTRK fusions identified across diverse rare cancers can be effectively treated with TRK inhibitors. The model has extended to other tumor-agnostic approaches.

*Cooperative research is essential for rare cancers*. The infrastructure developed for rare cancer research has informed broader cancer cooperative research.

*Patient advocacy can drive research priorities*. Disease-specific patient organizations have been particularly important in rare cancer research funding and advocacy.

These lessons extend beyond rare cancers themselves to inform cancer biology and therapy generally.

## What this chapter gives you

Gestational trophoblastic disease represents one of oncology's success stories. Methotrexate cure of metastatic choriocarcinoma in 1956 established that chemotherapy could cure metastatic solid cancer. The β-hCG tumor marker provides extraordinary diagnostic and monitoring capabilities. Modern treatment achieves >95% cure for low-risk and >85% for high-risk metastatic disease. PSTT and ETT remain more challenging. Pembrolizumab has shown activity in selected cases.

Adrenocortical carcinoma is a rare and aggressive cancer. Functional tumors produce cortisol, androgens, or other hormones with paraneoplastic syndromes. Treatment involves complete surgical resection (open preferred), adjuvant or therapeutic mitotane, and EDP-M chemotherapy for advanced disease. Outcomes remain suboptimal with substantial unmet need for better therapies.

Peritoneal cancers include primary peritoneal carcinoma (treated like ovarian cancer) and peritoneal carcinomatosis from various origins. Cytoreductive surgery + HIPEC is established for pseudomyxoma peritonei and peritoneal mesothelioma; role in other cancers being refined. Specialized centers required for this complex care.

Other rare cancers include neuroendocrine tumors (treated with somatostatin analogs, peptide receptor radionuclide therapy, targeted therapy, chemotherapy depending on subtype), parathyroid carcinoma, vulvar and vaginal cancers, penile cancer, male breast cancer, salivary gland cancers, anal cancer, mesothelioma, and many others.

Rare cancer infrastructure includes specialized centers, cooperative groups (SARC, EORTC, COG, others), international collaboration networks, disease-specific registries, patient advocacy organizations, tumor-agnostic clinical trials, orphan drug regulatory pathways, comprehensive genomic profiling, and real-world evidence. The infrastructure has improved substantially but remains imperfect.

Clinical practical considerations for rare cancers include referral to specialized centers, expert pathology review, multidisciplinary assessment, clinical trial enrollment when possible, genetic counseling for hereditary cancer syndromes, connection to patient advocacy organizations, off-label drug use, and specialized survivorship considerations.

Rare cancers have taught oncology many important lessons that have extended broadly — chemotherapy curing metastatic disease, single-mutation-driven cancers and targeted therapy, biomarker-guided treatment, molecular profiling enabling tumor-agnostic approaches, cooperative research models, and patient advocacy roles. The continued study and treatment of rare cancers benefits both the patients with these diseases and the broader cancer field.
