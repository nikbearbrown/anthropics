A rare cancer is rare to the population but ordinary to the patient who has it.

Hold the framing. About 22% of cancer diagnoses are of "rare" cancers — typically defined as cancers with annual incidence less than 6 per 100,000 (the European definition) or 15 per 100,000 (the US definition). Individually, each rare cancer is uncommon. Collectively, they affect substantial numbers of patients. And for the patient diagnosed with a rare cancer, the disease is no less serious or important than a common cancer would be.

The problem is that rare cancers are systematically under-served by the cancer infrastructure. Clinical trials are harder to conduct (smaller patient populations limit enrollment). Treatment guidelines often have weaker evidence (fewer trials, smaller patient numbers). Specialty expertise is concentrated at very few centers. Drug development is less commercially attractive (smaller markets). Patient advocacy is more dispersed. The result is that rare cancer patients often face delayed diagnoses, inappropriate treatment, and worse outcomes than they would for comparably aggressive common cancers.

The field has been working to address these disparities through international cooperative groups, rare cancer-specific clinical trials, orphan drug regulatory pathways, basket trials that enroll across cancer types, and patient advocacy organizations. The progress has been real but uneven.

This chapter — the first of two on rare and orphan cancers — covers sarcomas (adult sarcomas; pediatric sarcomas were covered in 34A) and thymic tumors. The next — 35B — covers gestational trophoblastic disease, adrenocortical carcinoma, peritoneal cancers, and the broader infrastructure for rare cancer care.

Hold the question: *given that rare cancers face systematic challenges in research and treatment, how do we provide good care for the patients who have them?*

## The rare cancer problem

The rare cancer category is heterogeneous. It includes:

*Pediatric cancers in adults*. Cancers traditionally categorized as pediatric (medulloblastoma, Ewing sarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma) can occur in adults but are individually rare.

*Adult-specific rare cancers*. Cholangiocarcinoma, adrenocortical carcinoma, thymic tumors, mesothelioma, certain sarcomas, certain hematologic malignancies, and many others.

*Rare subtypes of common cancers*. Some specific molecular or histological subtypes of common cancers are themselves rare (HER2-mutated NSCLC, KIT-mutated melanoma, certain breast cancer histologies).

*Cancers of unknown primary* (CUP). Discussed in 30A. Now increasingly managed with molecular profiling rather than empiric chemotherapy.

The challenges include:

*Clinical trial difficulty*. Small patient populations limit trial enrollment. Many rare cancers don't have phase 3 trials supporting current treatment standards. Decisions often rely on case series, small phase 2 studies, or expert consensus.

*Expertise concentration*. Specialized expertise for many rare cancers exists at only a few centers globally. Patient referral to these centers is often essential for optimal care but can be difficult due to geography and resources.

*Drug development*. The Orphan Drug Act in the US and similar provisions in other countries provide incentives for rare disease drug development. The provisions have substantially supported rare cancer drug development, but commercial interest remains lower than for common cancers.

*Awareness*. Even oncologists may not recognize rare cancers in initial presentations, leading to diagnostic delays. Pathologists may misidentify rare cancers.

*Coordination*. Cooperative groups specific to rare cancers (Sarcoma Alliance for Research through Collaboration, Rare Cancers Genetics Registry, European Reference Network on Rare Adult Solid Cancers, others) have developed to address these issues.

## Adult sarcomas

Sarcomas are mesenchymal cancers — arising from connective tissue. Pediatric sarcomas were covered in 34A. Adult sarcomas have substantial diversity.

*Epidemiology*. Adult sarcomas account for about 1% of adult cancers (compared to about 15% of pediatric cancers). Annual incidence about 3-4 per 100,000.

*Classification*. The WHO classification of soft tissue and bone tumors recognizes >100 distinct subtypes. The broad categories:

*Soft tissue sarcomas*:
- *Adipocytic tumors*. Liposarcoma (well-differentiated, dedifferentiated, myxoid, pleomorphic subtypes).
- *Fibroblastic and myofibroblastic tumors*. Dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans (DFSP), solitary fibrous tumor, others.
- *Smooth muscle tumors*. Leiomyosarcoma (uterine and extrauterine).
- *Skeletal muscle tumors*. Rhabdomyosarcoma (mostly pediatric; adult variants exist).
- *Vascular tumors*. Angiosarcoma, Kaposi sarcoma, epithelioid hemangioendothelioma.
- *Peripheral nerve sheath tumors*. Malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor (MPNST), often in neurofibromatosis 1.
- *Uncertain differentiation*. Synovial sarcoma, epithelioid sarcoma, alveolar soft part sarcoma, clear cell sarcoma, others.
- *Undifferentiated sarcoma*.

*Bone sarcomas*:
- *Osteosarcoma*. Most common primary bone cancer in young adults (and adolescents).
- *Chondrosarcoma*. Most common primary bone cancer in adults.
- *Ewing sarcoma*. Bimodal — children/young adults primarily.
- *Chordoma*. Notochordal-derived; usually skull base or sacrum.
- *Giant cell tumor of bone*. Generally benign but can metastasize.

*Gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST)*. Mesenchymal tumor of the GI tract. Specific biology with KIT or PDGFRA mutations. Treated with TKIs as primary therapy.

Each subtype has specific biology and treatment. Sarcoma management requires expertise in subtype-specific approaches.

## Soft tissue sarcoma management

Soft tissue sarcomas are managed at specialized sarcoma centers when feasible due to their complexity.

*Initial evaluation*. Imaging (MRI for extremity tumors, CT for body sites, PET for staging). Image-guided biopsy by experienced personnel — biopsy approach and tract matters for subsequent treatment.

*Pathology*. Expert sarcoma pathology is essential. Many sarcomas have specific molecular features that aid diagnosis (FISH for fusion genes, IHC for specific markers, NGS for molecular profiling).

*Treatment*. Generally individualized by:
- Subtype.
- Grade (Federation Nationale des Centres de Lutte Contre le Cancer — FNCLCC — grading).
- Location (extremity, retroperitoneal, head and neck, others).
- Size.
- Patient factors.

*Local treatment*:

*Surgery*. Wide local excision with adequate margins. Limb-salvage surgery preferred over amputation when feasible. The margin requirement varies by histology — some subtypes need wider margins.

*Radiation therapy*. Often used adjuvantly to reduce local recurrence in extremity sarcomas. Preoperative radiation has advantages for some patients (smaller field, lower dose, easier surgery). Postoperative radiation alternative. Brachytherapy in selected cases.

*Systemic therapy*:

*Adjuvant chemotherapy*. Role is debated. Generally considered for high-grade large deep tumors of extremities, particularly chemosensitive subtypes (synovial sarcoma, high-grade myxoid liposarcoma).

*Standard chemotherapy*. Doxorubicin + ifosfamide is the most active combination across subtypes. AIM (doxorubicin + ifosfamide + mesna), and variations.

*Specific subtype responses*:
- *Synovial sarcoma*. Generally chemotherapy-sensitive.
- *Myxoid/round cell liposarcoma*. Sensitive to trabectedin and conventional chemotherapy.
- *Leiomyosarcoma*. Variable; gemcitabine + docetaxel an option; trabectedin, dacarbazine.
- *Angiosarcoma*. Taxanes active.
- *Dedifferentiated/pleomorphic liposarcoma*. Often chemoresistant.
- *Alveolar soft part sarcoma*. Generally chemoresistant; tyrosine kinase inhibitors and immunotherapy increasingly used.
- *Various others*: subtype-specific approaches.

*Targeted therapy and immunotherapy*:
- *Pazopanib*. Multi-kinase TKI approved for non-adipocytic sarcomas after prior chemotherapy.
- *Trabectedin*. Marine-derived agent active in liposarcoma and leiomyosarcoma.
- *Eribulin*. Active in liposarcoma.
- *Olaratumab*. Anti-PDGFRα antibody (was approved with doxorubicin but withdrawn after confirmatory trial failure — illustrating the accelerated approval framework).
- *Imatinib*. For DFSP (dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans) with COL1A1-PDGFB fusion.
- *NTRK inhibitors* (larotrectinib, entrectinib) for NTRK fusion-positive sarcomas (rare).
- *EZH2 inhibitor* (tazemetostat) for epithelioid sarcoma with SMARCB1 loss.
- *Immune checkpoint inhibitors*. Limited activity in most sarcomas, though alveolar soft part sarcoma and certain other subtypes show responses.
- *Larotrectinib for infantile fibrosarcoma* (NTRK fusion).

*Metastatic disease*. Generally treated palliatively, though some patients with limited lung metastases can be cured with metastasectomy.

*Outcomes*. Highly variable by subtype and stage. Extremity localized high-grade sarcoma 5-year survival 50-70%. Retroperitoneal sarcomas have worse outcomes. Metastatic sarcoma generally 5-year survival <20%. Some indolent subtypes have substantially better long-term outcomes.

## Specific soft tissue sarcoma subtypes

### Liposarcoma

The most common soft tissue sarcoma. Multiple subtypes with distinct biology:

*Well-differentiated liposarcoma*. Indolent. Atypical lipomatous tumor in extremities; well-differentiated liposarcoma in retroperitoneum. MDM2 amplification universal. Treatment is surgical; chemotherapy not active. Selinexor and other approaches in development.

*Dedifferentiated liposarcoma*. Aggressive component arising in well-differentiated liposarcoma. Aggressive behavior. Surgery primary; chemotherapy modest activity. MDM2 amplification with co-amplification of CDK4, HMGA2 in some cases.

*Myxoid/round cell liposarcoma*. Defined by FUS-DDIT3 fusion. Chemosensitive (trabectedin particularly active). Can recur at unusual sites including bone and soft tissue.

*Pleomorphic liposarcoma*. Aggressive. Treated as other high-grade soft tissue sarcomas.

### Leiomyosarcoma

Smooth muscle sarcoma. Uterine and extrauterine variants.

*Uterine leiomyosarcoma*. Often diagnosed postoperatively after hysterectomy for suspected fibroid. Adjuvant chemotherapy considered for high-grade. Metastatic disease treated with chemotherapy (doxorubicin, gemcitabine + docetaxel, trabectedin, eribulin, dacarbazine).

*Extrauterine leiomyosarcoma*. Similar treatment approaches.

### Synovial sarcoma

Despite the name, doesn't arise from synovium. Defined by SS18-SSX fusion. Most common in young adults.

Treatment includes surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy. Generally chemosensitive. Adjuvant chemotherapy often considered. Multi-agent regimens for metastatic disease.

CAR-T cells and TCR-T cells targeting NY-ESO-1 (which is expressed by most synovial sarcomas) are in development. Afami-cel (TCR-T targeting MAGE-A4) approved for HLA-A*02-positive synovial sarcoma in 2024.

### Angiosarcoma

Vascular sarcoma. Various subtypes:
- *Cutaneous angiosarcoma* of head and neck (UV-related).
- *Breast angiosarcoma*. Either primary or post-radiation (after breast cancer treatment).
- *Visceral angiosarcoma*. Aggressive.

Treatment: surgery with wide margins for localized disease. Paclitaxel particularly active for advanced disease. Newer approaches include pazopanib and immunotherapy in some contexts.

### Dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans (DFSP)

Slowly growing skin sarcoma. COL1A1-PDGFB fusion universal. Generally indolent but locally recurrent.

Treatment: wide local excision (Mohs micrographic surgery often used) is the primary approach. Imatinib is active for advanced or unresectable disease (one of the early non-CML/GIST imatinib indications).

### Other subtypes

*Solitary fibrous tumor*. Generally indolent; rare aggressive variants. Pazopanib has activity in advanced disease.

*Epithelioid sarcoma*. SMARCB1 loss universal. EZH2 inhibitor tazemetostat approved.

*Alveolar soft part sarcoma*. ASPSCR1-TFE3 fusion. Generally chemoresistant. Pazopanib, immune checkpoint inhibitors active.

*Malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor (MPNST)*. Often arises in neurofibromatosis 1. Aggressive. Limited treatment options.

The subtype-specific approaches reflect the recognition that sarcomas are not a single disease but many distinct entities sharing only their mesenchymal origin.

## Gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST)

A distinctive sarcoma arising from interstitial cells of Cajal in the GI tract. Has its own treatment paradigm based on the discovery of activating mutations in KIT or PDGFRA.

*Biology*. Driven by activating mutations in:
- *KIT* (~75%). Various exons affected (exon 11 most common, exon 9, others).
- *PDGFRA* (~10%). Various mutations including D842V.
- *Wild-type GIST* (~10-15%). Various other drivers (SDH deficiency, BRAF mutations, NF1 mutations).

*Clinical presentation*. Often incidental finding. May cause bleeding, abdominal pain, obstruction. Most common in stomach.

*Diagnosis*. EUS-guided biopsy or surgical resection. IHC for CD117 (KIT) and DOG1 are characteristic. Molecular profiling essential for treatment selection.

*Treatment*:

*Localized resectable GIST*. Surgery with adequate margins (nodal dissection not required as GIST rarely spreads through lymphatics).

*Risk stratification*. Mitotic count, tumor size, location, and rupture status determine recurrence risk.

*Adjuvant imatinib*. For high-risk GIST after surgery. Typically 3 years duration.

*Metastatic/unresectable GIST*. Imatinib is first-line for KIT/PDGFRA-mutant disease. Dose may be escalated for KIT exon 9 mutations.

*Sequential TKIs for resistant disease*:
- *Sunitinib*. Second-line after imatinib failure.
- *Regorafenib*. Third-line.
- *Ripretinib*. Fourth-line.
- *Avapritinib*. For PDGFRA D842V-mutated GIST (resistant to imatinib).

The GIST sequence of TKIs has transformed outcomes from a uniformly fatal cancer to a chronic condition for most patients. Imatinib approval for GIST in 2002 was one of the first non-CML applications.

*Outcomes*. Localized GIST with adequate surgery and adjuvant imatinib: high cure rates (>80% 5-year survival). Metastatic GIST median survival now several years with sequential TKI therapy.

## Thymic tumors

Thymic tumors are rare cancers arising in the thymus gland. The major types are thymomas and thymic carcinomas.

*Epidemiology*. Annual incidence about 0.15 per 100,000. Mostly diagnosed in adults.

*Subtypes (WHO classification)*:
- *Type A*: medullary thymoma. Indolent.
- *Type AB*: mixed thymoma. Indolent.
- *Type B1*: lymphocyte-rich thymoma.
- *Type B2*: cortical thymoma.
- *Type B3*: well-differentiated thymic carcinoma.
- *Type C*: thymic carcinoma. More aggressive.
- *Neuroendocrine thymic tumors*: separate category.

*Associated conditions*. Thymomas are commonly associated with autoimmune disorders, particularly myasthenia gravis (about 30% of thymoma patients have MG; about 10-15% of MG patients have thymoma). Also associated with pure red cell aplasia, hypogammaglobulinemia, and other autoimmune phenomena.

*Presentation*. Often incidental finding on imaging. May present with myasthenia gravis or other paraneoplastic syndromes. Local symptoms (chest pain, shortness of breath, superior vena cava syndrome) for larger tumors.

*Diagnosis*. CT or MRI imaging. Tissue biopsy for confirmation. Anti-acetylcholine receptor antibodies if myasthenia is suspected.

*Staging*. Masaoka or modern TNM system.

*Treatment*:

*Resectable thymomas*. Surgery (thymectomy or wider resection as needed). Adjuvant radiation for higher-stage disease.

*Unresectable thymoma or thymic carcinoma*. Multimodal therapy including chemotherapy (cisplatin-based regimens), radiation, and sometimes surgery after response.

*Recurrent or metastatic disease*. Chemotherapy options include cisplatin + doxorubicin + cyclophosphamide (PAC), carboplatin + paclitaxel, others. Octreotide for somatostatin receptor-expressing tumors. Various targeted approaches (everolimus, sunitinib) with limited evidence. Pembrolizumab approved for thymic carcinoma based on response data; thymoma immunotherapy is challenging due to risk of severe immune-related adverse events.

*Specific concerns*. Patients with myasthenia gravis require neurology consultation and careful perioperative management. Immune checkpoint inhibitors in thymoma carry substantial risk of triggering autoimmunity (severe myocarditis, myositis, others).

*Outcomes*. Thymoma generally has better prognosis than thymic carcinoma. Type A and B1 5-year survival >90%. Type B3 and thymic carcinoma worse. Metastatic disease has substantially poorer outcomes.

## What this chapter gives you

Rare cancers face systematic challenges in research, treatment expertise, drug development, and awareness. About 22% of cancer diagnoses are of rare cancers, collectively representing substantial patient numbers despite individual rarity. The challenges include clinical trial difficulty, concentrated specialty expertise, drug development barriers, recognition challenges, and need for coordinated care.

Adult sarcomas are a diverse group of mesenchymal cancers with >100 distinct subtypes. Management at specialized sarcoma centers when feasible. The major categories include soft tissue sarcomas (liposarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, synovial sarcoma, angiosarcoma, DFSP, MPNST, others), bone sarcomas (osteosarcoma, chondrosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, chordoma), and GIST.

Treatment approaches are individualized by subtype, grade, location, and stage. Local treatment with surgery (limb-salvage when feasible) and radiation. Systemic treatment with doxorubicin + ifosfamide for most subtypes, with subtype-specific approaches for others (trabectedin in liposarcoma, paclitaxel in angiosarcoma, imatinib in DFSP, tazemetostat in epithelioid sarcoma, etc.).

Specific subtype highlights: liposarcoma (multiple distinct subtypes), leiomyosarcoma (uterine and extrauterine), synovial sarcoma (NY-ESO-1 targetable; afami-cel approved), angiosarcoma (paclitaxel-sensitive), DFSP (imatinib-responsive), epithelioid sarcoma (EZH2 inhibitor-responsive), alveolar soft part sarcoma (pazopanib and immunotherapy active), and others.

Gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST) has its own treatment paradigm based on KIT/PDGFRA mutations. Sequential TKI therapy (imatinib, sunitinib, regorafenib, ripretinib, avapritinib for PDGFRA D842V) has transformed GIST from uniformly fatal to chronic disease.

Thymic tumors include thymomas (multiple WHO types) and thymic carcinomas. Often associated with myasthenia gravis and other autoimmune disorders. Treatment with surgery, radiation, chemotherapy depending on stage. Pembrolizumab for thymic carcinoma. Immunotherapy in thymoma is challenging due to autoimmune risks.
