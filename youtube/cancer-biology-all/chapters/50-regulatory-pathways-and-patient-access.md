# Chapter 50 — Regulatory Pathways and Patient Access


## TL;DR

- A drug that works in trials isn't approved.
- The chapter moves through The FDA approval process, Accelerated approval programs, Companion diagnostics, Post-marketing requirements, and related ideas.
- Read it for the main argument, the vocabulary it introduces, and the practical judgment it asks you to develop.

A drug that works in trials isn't approved. A drug that the FDA says works is approved. The distinction matters.

Hold the framing. The clinical trials covered in 28A produce evidence about whether treatments work. But evidence alone doesn't make a treatment available to patients. The treatment must navigate the regulatory pathway — review by the FDA in the United States, the EMA in Europe, or equivalent bodies elsewhere — that translates trial evidence into approval for clinical use. The regulatory pathway has its own logic, its own standards, its own timeline, and its own consequences for what reaches patients.

The regulatory infrastructure is also imperfect. Drugs are sometimes approved with marginal evidence on accelerated pathways and don't pan out. Drugs that might be useful for specific patients don't get approved because the trials didn't include those patients. The pace of regulatory approval lags behind the science. Patients with terminal disease need access to investigational therapies that haven't completed full review. Each of these tensions has produced specific regulatory mechanisms.

This chapter — the second of two on clinical trials and regulatory pathways — covers the regulatory approval processes (FDA, EMA, and others), the special pathways for serious diseases (accelerated approval, breakthrough designation), the mechanisms for patient access outside trials (compassionate use, expanded access, right to try), and the post-approval requirements that ensure ongoing safety and effectiveness.

Hold the question: *given a clinical trial that shows benefit, how does that benefit reach the patients who need it?*

## The FDA approval process

In the United States, cancer drugs are approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), specifically through the Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER) and the Oncology Center of Excellence (OCE, established 2017 to provide unified oncology drug review).

The traditional pathway:

1. *Preclinical research*. Laboratory and animal studies to characterize the drug, its mechanism, safety profile, and initial dose ranges.

2. *Investigational New Drug (IND) application*. Sponsor submits comprehensive package on the drug to FDA for permission to begin human trials. FDA reviews safety data, manufacturing information, trial design, and protocols. If FDA does not object within 30 days (or affirmatively approves), the IND becomes effective.

3. *Phase 1-3 clinical trials*. Sponsor conducts trials according to the IND-approved protocols. Periodic safety reports to FDA.

4. *New Drug Application (NDA) or Biologics License Application (BLA)*. After completing pivotal trials, sponsor submits comprehensive application including clinical data, manufacturing information, and proposed labeling. The application typically includes hundreds of thousands of pages of documentation.

5. *FDA review*. The FDA review team examines the submission. Standard review timelines are 10 months from filing for standard review and 6 months for priority review.

6. *Advisory committee meeting* (in some cases). For controversial or first-in-class drugs, FDA may convene an advisory committee of external experts to review the data and make recommendations. Advisory committee recommendations are not binding but typically followed.

7. *Approval or Complete Response Letter*. FDA either approves the drug for marketing or issues a Complete Response Letter (CRL) indicating that the application cannot be approved in its current form. The CRL details what additional information or studies are needed.

8. *Post-approval requirements*. Even after approval, sponsors typically have post-marketing commitments — additional studies, safety surveillance, periodic reports.

The standard pathway takes years from IND to approval — typically 7-15 years and $1-2 billion or more in development costs for a successful drug.

## Accelerated approval programs

For serious diseases with unmet medical needs, the FDA has several mechanisms to accelerate the pathway:

*Fast Track*. Designation that facilitates development and review of drugs for serious conditions. Allows more frequent meetings with FDA, rolling review of NDA sections as they're completed, and other interactions designed to accelerate development.

*Breakthrough Therapy Designation*. Established by FDASIA (Food and Drug Administration Safety and Innovation Act, 2012). Granted to drugs that treat serious conditions and have preliminary clinical evidence suggesting substantial improvement over available therapies. Provides intensive FDA guidance and other facilitating measures. The designation has accelerated many oncology approvals.

*Accelerated Approval*. Allows approval based on a surrogate endpoint that is "reasonably likely to predict clinical benefit." Common in oncology — drugs approved based on response rate, progression-free survival, or pathological complete response rather than overall survival. Required post-approval confirmatory trials to verify clinical benefit. If the confirmatory trials fail, the FDA can withdraw the approval.

The accelerated approval pathway has been particularly important in oncology. Many drugs received initial approval based on surrogate endpoints and were later confirmed (or in some cases not confirmed) by follow-up trials. The pathway speeds patient access but accepts some risk of approving drugs that don't ultimately benefit patients.

The confirmatory trial requirement is critical. The FDA has occasionally withdrawn accelerated approvals when confirmatory trials failed (Mylotarg in 2010, later reapproved; bevacizumab for breast cancer in 2011; others). The withdrawal process is contentious — patients on the drug may resist losing access; sponsors may resist withdrawal; the FDA must balance evidence and access.

*Priority Review*. Reduces FDA review timeline from 10 to 6 months for drugs that "offer significant improvements in the safety or effectiveness of the treatment of serious conditions." Doesn't change the evidence requirements but speeds review.

*Real-Time Oncology Review (RTOR)*. Pilot program allowing rolling submission and concurrent review of clinical trial data. Substantial reduction in time from data submission to approval. Has been used for many recent oncology approvals.

These accelerated mechanisms have substantially reduced the time from clinical trial completion to FDA approval for many oncology drugs. The combination of priority review + accelerated approval + RTOR can result in approval within months of pivotal trial completion.

## Companion diagnostics

For biomarker-driven targeted therapies, the FDA often approves the drug together with a *companion diagnostic* — a test that identifies the patients most likely to benefit. The companion diagnostic is essentially required for using the drug.

The regulatory framework for companion diagnostics:

*Class III medical device*. Companion diagnostics are typically Class III devices requiring full FDA approval (Premarket Approval, PMA).

*Co-approval with the drug*. The diagnostic and the drug are reviewed in parallel and approved together. The diagnostic label specifies the patient population eligible for the drug; the drug label specifies the test required for patient selection.

*Multiple companion diagnostics for the same drug*. Sometimes multiple tests are approved for the same drug. Example: HER2 testing for trastuzumab can use IHC (multiple manufacturers) or FISH (multiple manufacturers).

*Class II 510(k) clearance pathway* for some diagnostics. Faster approval pathway for diagnostics that demonstrate equivalence to predicates.

*Comprehensive genomic profiling tests*. Tests covering many genes simultaneously (FoundationOne CDx, Caris, Tempus, others) increasingly receive companion diagnostic indications for multiple drugs through single approval pathways.

*Lab-developed tests (LDTs)*. Tests developed and performed by single laboratories without FDA approval (historically). Recent FDA guidance has signaled increased oversight of LDTs, with implications for the cancer diagnostic landscape.

The companion diagnostic infrastructure is essential for precision oncology but creates implementation challenges. Patients must receive the appropriate testing before drug selection. Tests must be available, reimbursed, and properly interpreted. Workflow integration in clinical practice can lag behind regulatory approval.

## Post-marketing requirements

Approval is not the end of regulatory oversight. Post-marketing requirements include:

*Confirmatory trials* (for accelerated approvals). Trials to confirm the clinical benefit suggested by the surrogate endpoint that supported initial approval.

*Post-marketing surveillance*. Ongoing collection of adverse event reports. The FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) collects spontaneous reports. Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategies (REMS) are required for some drugs with safety concerns.

*Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategies (REMS)*. Required for drugs whose benefits outweigh their risks only with specific safety measures. Examples include monitoring requirements, prescriber certifications, and patient enrollment in registries. Used for many CAR-T cell products, IMiDs, and others.

*Pediatric study requirements*. The Pediatric Research Equity Act (PREA) requires pediatric studies for many drugs. The Best Pharmaceuticals for Children Act (BPCA) provides incentives. Both have produced pediatric oncology data for many drugs originally approved in adults.

*Periodic Safety Update Reports*. Regular reports on safety experience.

*Real-world evidence requirements*. Some recent approvals have included requirements for real-world data collection through registries and other mechanisms.

The post-marketing oversight is one of the longer-term commitments of drug development. The FDA continues to monitor approved drugs throughout their market life.

## EMA and other regulatory bodies

The European Medicines Agency (EMA) regulates drugs in the European Union, with similar but distinct processes:

*Centralized procedure*. EMA-coordinated review with subsequent approval applying to all EU member states. Standard for cancer drugs.

*CHMP (Committee for Medicinal Products for Human Use)*. The scientific committee that conducts the review. Issues recommendations to the European Commission.

*Conditional approval*. Similar to FDA accelerated approval; allows approval with less complete data and post-approval confirmation.

*PRIME (PRIority MEdicines) scheme*. EMA's equivalent of breakthrough therapy designation, with enhanced support for priority drugs.

*Different timelines and requirements*. While EMA and FDA generally reach similar conclusions, the timing and specific evidence requirements can differ.

Other major regulatory bodies include:
- *PMDA (Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency)* in Japan.
- *Health Canada*.
- *TGA (Therapeutic Goods Administration)* in Australia.
- *MFDS (Ministry of Food and Drug Safety)* in South Korea.
- *NMPA (National Medical Products Administration)* in China.
- *CDSCO (Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation)* in India.
- Multiple others.

For global drug development, sponsors typically file with multiple regulators in parallel. Approval timelines and requirements vary. The global harmonization through the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) has reduced some of the differences but substantial variation remains.

## Compassionate use and expanded access

For patients with serious or life-threatening conditions who have exhausted approved options, several mechanisms provide access to investigational therapies outside of trials:

*Expanded Access (Compassionate Use)*. FDA mechanism allowing patients to use investigational drugs outside clinical trials. Requires:
- Serious or life-threatening condition.
- No comparable approved alternative.
- Potential benefit outweighing risks.
- Treatment will not interfere with the clinical development of the drug.

The expanded access programs can be:
- *Individual patient* (single patient INDs).
- *Intermediate-size patient population* (some prespecified groups).
- *Widespread treatment use* (large protocols).

The mechanism allows patient access while the drug is still in development.

*Treatment IND*. For drugs in late-stage trials with substantial supporting evidence, the FDA may permit a treatment IND that provides broader access during the time between trial completion and approval.

*Right to Try* (Federal law since 2018). Provides another pathway for terminally ill patients to access investigational therapies. Allows direct access without FDA approval of the individual treatment, though the manufacturer must agree.

The differences between Expanded Access and Right to Try involve regulatory oversight, FDA involvement, and reporting requirements. Both mechanisms have advocates and critics. The practical impact in oncology has been modest — most patients with cancer can access experimental therapies through clinical trial enrollment, which is generally preferred because it generates evidence.

*Manufacturer participation*. The mechanisms require the manufacturer to provide the drug. Manufacturers may decline based on supply constraints, regulatory concerns, or strategic considerations. This is one of the major practical barriers to expanded access.

*Cost considerations*. Investigational drugs are typically provided free in clinical trials. Expanded access drugs may be free, reduced cost, or full cost — depending on manufacturer policy. Insurance coverage for investigational drugs is variable.

## Compassionate use case-by-case considerations

The decision to provide compassionate access involves multiple stakeholders:

*The patient*. Wants access to potentially helpful treatment despite limited evidence.

*The physician*. Must determine medical appropriateness, manage administration and monitoring, and accept responsibility for using a drug outside its approved context.

*The manufacturer*. Must decide whether to provide the drug, often weighing the individual case against broader development considerations (supply, regulatory implications, precedent).

*The regulator*. Must approve the individual access request (though approval rates are very high — over 99% of FDA expanded access requests are granted).

*The institutional ethics board*. Many institutions require IRB review for compassionate use.

The process has been streamlined over years but remains complex for individual patients. Patient advocacy groups have played significant roles in facilitating access, particularly for rare cancers.

## Special cancer-specific regulatory considerations

Oncology has some unique regulatory features:

*Tumor-agnostic approvals*. Drugs approved based on molecular features regardless of tissue type. Pembrolizumab for MSI-H tumors was the first (2017). Larotrectinib for NTRK fusion tumors followed. BRAF/MEK for BRAF V600E. The approvals require demonstrating that the molecular feature is the key driver of response.

*Orphan Drug Designation*. For cancers affecting fewer than 200,000 people in the US (or other rarity criteria internationally). Provides incentives including tax credits, regulatory exclusivity, and waiver of certain fees. Has supported development of many rare cancer therapies.

*Pediatric exclusivity*. Six additional months of marketing exclusivity for completion of pediatric studies. Has incentivized pediatric cancer drug development.

*Adaptive licensing*. Approval with progressive expansion as more data accumulates. Used in some contexts.

*Real-world evidence*. Increasingly accepted as supporting evidence for label expansion or specific subgroups not well-represented in trials.

*External controls*. Trials using historical or external control groups rather than randomized internal controls. Accepted in some contexts, particularly for rare diseases or where placebo controls would be unethical.

*Single-arm trials for approval*. The FDA has approved drugs based on single-arm trials in some cases, particularly for cancers without good treatment options. Olaratumab in soft-tissue sarcoma was an example of accelerated approval based on single-arm data; the confirmatory trial failed and the approval was withdrawn — illustrating both the speed advantage and the risk of the approach.

## Drug labeling

The FDA-approved drug label is the legal description of how a drug can be used. The label includes:

*Indication*. The specific conditions for which the drug is approved.

*Dosing*. The recommended dose, schedule, and modifications.

*Contraindications*. Conditions where the drug should not be used.

*Warnings and precautions*. Risks and required monitoring.

*Adverse reactions*. The side effect profile.

*Drug interactions*. Effects on or from other medications.

*Special populations*. Considerations for pregnancy, lactation, pediatric, elderly, hepatic/renal impairment.

*Clinical pharmacology*. Mechanism, pharmacokinetics, metabolism.

*Clinical studies*. Summary of the studies supporting approval.

*Patient counseling information*. What to tell patients.

The label governs how the drug can be marketed. Marketing of unapproved uses (off-label promotion) is restricted, though physicians can prescribe off-label based on their clinical judgment. The distinction between manufacturer-promoted use and physician-decided use is regulatorily important.

*Off-label use* is common in oncology. Many cancer drugs are used in indications where they don't have formal FDA approval, based on physician judgment, clinical guidelines, or limited evidence. The off-label use is legal for the prescriber but the manufacturer cannot promote it. Insurance coverage for off-label use varies — some insurers cover NCCN guidelines-supported off-label use; others require FDA approval.

## Pricing and access

The economic dimensions of drug access are increasingly important:

*High costs of cancer drugs*. Modern cancer drugs are expensive — $100,000-300,000+ per year is common for newer agents. The high costs create access barriers.

*Pricing regulation*. Varies dramatically by country. The US has relatively free-market pricing; many other countries negotiate prices with national health systems. The pricing differences result in substantial cost disparities.

*Insurance and reimbursement*. In the US, complex multi-payer system with variable coverage. Outside the US, single-payer systems with negotiated formularies. Both systems have challenges with extremely expensive new drugs.

*Patient assistance programs*. Many manufacturers offer programs to assist uninsured or underinsured patients. The programs have substantial impact for some patients but are unevenly available.

*Generic and biosimilar competition*. As patents expire, generic small molecules and biosimilars become available, typically at 20-80% lower prices. This has substantially reduced costs for older cancer drugs.

*Value-based pricing*. Attempts to link drug prices to demonstrated outcomes. Various models being tested in different settings.

*Drug shortages*. Generic chemotherapy drug shortages have affected cancer care. The 2023 cisplatin and carboplatin shortages caused substantial treatment disruptions.

The economic dimensions of cancer therapy are increasingly part of the regulatory and clinical landscape. Decisions about access, coverage, and use depend on cost considerations alongside efficacy and safety.

## What this chapter gives you

The FDA approval process translates clinical trial evidence into marketed drugs. The traditional pathway proceeds through IND, phase 1-3 trials, NDA/BLA submission, FDA review, and approval. The process takes years and substantial investment but ensures rigorous evidence supporting approved use.

Accelerated approval mechanisms have substantially shortened the timeline for serious disease. Fast Track, Breakthrough Therapy Designation, Accelerated Approval (using surrogate endpoints), Priority Review, and Real-Time Oncology Review work together to bring promising oncology drugs to patients faster.

Companion diagnostics are co-approved with biomarker-driven targeted therapies. The diagnostic identifies patients most likely to benefit, and is essentially required for drug use. The regulatory framework continues to evolve as comprehensive genomic profiling becomes more common.

Post-marketing requirements include confirmatory trials (for accelerated approvals), adverse event surveillance, REMS programs, pediatric studies, and increasing use of real-world evidence.

The EMA, PMDA, Health Canada, and other regulatory bodies provide parallel pathways. International harmonization through ICH has reduced but not eliminated differences in evidence requirements and timelines.

Patient access mechanisms outside trials include Expanded Access (compassionate use), Treatment IND, and Right to Try. These provide options for terminally ill patients who have exhausted approved therapies, though clinical trial enrollment is generally preferred for both patients and the development process.

Tumor-agnostic approvals, Orphan Drug Designation, pediatric exclusivity, real-world evidence acceptance, and other oncology-specific regulatory features support the unique requirements of cancer drug development.

Drug labeling governs how drugs can be marketed and serves as the reference for clinical use. Off-label use is common in oncology but the regulatory boundary between manufacturer promotion and physician-decided use is important.

Pricing and access are increasingly central to the regulatory and clinical landscape. High costs, complex reimbursement, patient assistance programs, generic/biosimilar competition, value-based pricing, and drug shortages all affect what reaches patients in practice.
