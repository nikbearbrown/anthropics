# Chapter 58 — CNS Tumors: Brain Metastases and Multidisciplinary Management

Most brain tumors didn't start in the brain. They came from somewhere else and chose to live there.

Hold the framing. The previous chapter covered primary brain tumors — cancers arising in the central nervous system itself. But primary brain tumors are not the most common brain cancers. *Brain metastases* — cancers that originated elsewhere and traveled to the brain — outnumber primary brain tumors by perhaps 10-to-1. Approximately 20-40% of patients with metastatic cancer eventually develop brain metastases. The trend is increasing as systemic therapy keeps patients alive longer (allowing brain metastases time to develop) and as imaging is more frequently obtained for surveillance.

The biology of brain metastases is distinct from primary brain tumors. The cancer cells came from breast, lung, melanoma, kidney, or colorectal primary sites. They share their primary tumor's molecular features and respond (often) to therapies effective for the primary disease. But they also live in a unique microenvironment — the brain — that creates specific therapeutic challenges. The blood-brain barrier limits drug access. The neuroanatomic constraints limit aggressive surgery. The radiation tolerance of brain tissue limits dose. The management of brain metastases requires balancing systemic and local treatment approaches.

This chapter — the second of two on CNS tumors — covers brain metastases, the multidisciplinary management of all CNS cancers, leptomeningeal disease, treatment-related complications, and future directions.

Hold the question: *given that most brain cancers come from elsewhere in the body, how do we manage the interaction between systemic disease and brain involvement?*

## Epidemiology of brain metastases

Brain metastases occur in 20-40% of patients with metastatic cancer. The frequency varies by primary cancer:

- *Lung cancer*. The most common source. About 40-50% of metastatic lung cancers develop brain metastases. Some lung cancer subtypes (small cell lung cancer; certain molecular subtypes of NSCLC including EGFR-mutated and HER2-mutated) have particularly high rates.
- *Breast cancer*. About 10-30% develop brain metastases. HER2-positive and triple-negative subtypes have higher rates.
- *Melanoma*. Among the highest rates — about 50-60% of metastatic melanomas develop brain involvement.
- *Renal cell carcinoma*. About 10-20%.
- *Colorectal cancer*. Less common, about 1-5%.
- *Other primary sites* (less commonly).

The total incidence of brain metastases in the US is estimated at 100,000-300,000 cases per year, far exceeding the incidence of glioblastoma (about 12,000 per year). The actual numbers may be higher than reported because many brain metastases are not biopsied for definitive diagnosis.

Brain metastases typically occur:
- At the gray-white matter junction (where blood vessels narrow and cells lodge mechanically).
- More often in the frontal lobes (proportional to brain volume) but can occur anywhere.
- Often multiple (rather than single).
- Sometimes with leptomeningeal involvement.

The hemodynamic pattern explains some of the distribution. Tumor cells circulating from primary sites that drain through systemic venous circulation reach the lungs first (where many lodge), then any remaining cells circulate through arterial blood to the brain. This explains why lung cancer brain metastases are so common (cells originate locally) and why other primary cancers' brain metastases often coexist with lung metastases.

## Diagnosis and workup of brain metastases

*Imaging*. MRI with and without contrast is the standard. Brain metastases typically show:
- Multiple enhancing lesions at gray-white junctions.
- Surrounding edema disproportionate to tumor size.
- Variable patterns (ring-enhancing, homogeneous, hemorrhagic for melanoma).
- CT screening only for patients who cannot have MRI.

The number, size, and location of metastases inform treatment. PET-CT for staging extracranial disease.

*Tissue diagnosis*. Often unnecessary if the primary cancer is known and imaging is characteristic. Biopsy reserved for cases where:
- Primary cancer is not known.
- Imaging is atypical (consider other diagnoses).
- Specific molecular characterization is needed that wasn't available from primary tumor.

When biopsied, brain metastases provide molecular profiles that may differ from the primary tumor. Some metastases acquire additional mutations or alterations during evolution.

*Symptoms*. Headaches (especially worsening or with positional component), seizures, focal neurological deficits, cognitive changes, signs of increased intracranial pressure.

*Differential diagnosis* of multiple brain lesions includes brain metastases, primary CNS lymphoma, multifocal glioma (uncommon), abscesses, demyelinating disease, and others.

## Management of brain metastases

The management of brain metastases involves both local treatment (surgery, radiation) and systemic treatment for the underlying cancer. The choice depends on:
- Number of metastases.
- Size and location.
- Primary cancer type and molecular profile.
- Extracranial disease status.
- Patient performance status.
- Prior treatments.

*Local treatment options*:

*Surgery*. For single or few accessible metastases:
- Provides immediate symptom relief and confirmed pathology.
- Standard for large, symptomatic lesions causing mass effect.
- Often combined with subsequent radiation.

*Stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS)*. High-dose, focused radiation to individual lesions:
- Outpatient procedure with no incision.
- Effective for lesions up to 3-4 cm.
- Can treat multiple lesions in a single session (typically up to 10-15 with current technology, sometimes more).
- Standard for limited brain metastases.
- Recent trials have extended SRS use to patients with 5-15 lesions (and even more in some studies).

*Whole-brain radiation therapy (WBRT)*. Radiation to the entire brain:
- Historical standard for multiple brain metastases.
- Has substantial cognitive toxicity in long-term survivors.
- Now used less frequently than previously.
- May be appropriate for patients with very extensive brain disease, leptomeningeal involvement, or limited life expectancy.

*Hippocampal-sparing WBRT*. WBRT with reduced dose to the hippocampi to preserve memory function. Memantine is also used to mitigate cognitive effects.

The shift from WBRT to SRS for limited brain metastases has been one of the major changes in management. SRS provides comparable local control with substantially less cognitive toxicity.

*Systemic therapy for brain metastases*. Increasingly recognized as having activity for brain metastases:

- *Targeted therapy*. Many newer targeted therapies have CNS activity:
  - *EGFR inhibitors* (osimertinib has good CNS penetration; gefitinib, erlotinib less so).
  - *ALK inhibitors* (alectinib, brigatinib, lorlatinib have CNS activity; crizotinib less so).
  - *BRAF/MEK inhibitors* (dabrafenib + trametinib in melanoma).
  - *HER2-targeted therapy* (tucatinib + trastuzumab + capecitabine, T-DXd both have CNS activity in HER2-positive breast cancer).
  - *KRAS G12C inhibitors* (some CNS activity).

- *Immunotherapy*. Checkpoint inhibitors have CNS activity in melanoma, NSCLC, and other cancers. The activity varies; combinations (ipilimumab + nivolumab in melanoma) appear more active than monotherapy in brain metastases.

- *Chemotherapy*. Most conventional chemotherapy has limited CNS penetration; less commonly used for brain metastases alone, but may control extracranial disease.

The integration of systemic therapy with local treatment is one of the key advances. Some patients with limited brain metastases and systemic disease responsive to targeted therapy or immunotherapy can be managed primarily with systemic treatment, with local treatment reserved for progression.

*Treatment selection algorithm*:
1. *Single small (<3 cm) asymptomatic metastasis with effective systemic therapy*. Consider systemic therapy alone with close imaging follow-up.
2. *Limited (1-4) symptomatic or larger metastases*. Surgery (if accessible and providing best symptom control) followed by SRS, or SRS alone, plus systemic therapy.
3. *Multiple (5-15) metastases*. SRS to individual lesions in current practice (was WBRT historically) plus systemic therapy.
4. *Extensive (>15) metastases or leptomeningeal disease*. WBRT (often hippocampal-sparing) plus systemic therapy.
5. *Poor performance status or limited life expectancy*. Palliative care focus; possibly limited radiation for symptom control.

The optimal approach is highly individualized and evolves with new evidence.

## Leptomeningeal disease

Leptomeningeal carcinomatosis is the spread of cancer cells through the cerebrospinal fluid, coating the leptomeninges and infiltrating the surface of the brain and spinal cord. It is a particularly difficult complication of metastatic cancer.

*Frequency*. About 5-10% of patients with metastatic solid tumors. Most common in breast cancer, lung cancer, and melanoma.

*Presentation*. Symptoms vary based on location of CSF involvement:
- *Cerebral symptoms*. Cognitive changes, seizures, headaches.
- *Cranial nerve involvement*. Visual changes, facial weakness, hearing loss, swallowing problems.
- *Spinal symptoms*. Weakness, sensory changes, bowel/bladder dysfunction, pain.

The symptoms often progress rapidly without treatment.

*Diagnosis*. MRI of brain and spine with contrast may show:
- Nodular leptomeningeal enhancement.
- Cranial nerve enhancement.
- "Hydrocephalus" from CSF outflow obstruction.

CSF analysis through lumbar puncture:
- Cytology to detect malignant cells (the gold standard for diagnosis).
- Cell counts, protein, glucose typically abnormal.
- Sometimes multiple LPs needed (sensitivity of single LP is about 50-70%).
- Flow cytometry for hematologic malignancies.
- Molecular testing (ctDNA in CSF) emerging.

*Treatment options*:

*Intrathecal chemotherapy*. Methotrexate, cytarabine (liposomal cytarabine — DepoCyt — was used but discontinued), thiotepa, others. Administered via Ommaya reservoir or lumbar puncture. Provides direct CNS drug delivery.

*Whole-brain or whole-CNS radiation*. For symptomatic disease and to bulky deposits.

*Systemic therapy*. May provide some CNS activity. Specific examples:
- *EGFR inhibitors*. Osimertinib has activity in EGFR-mutated lung cancer leptomeningeal disease.
- *Tucatinib + trastuzumab + capecitabine*. Active in HER2-positive breast cancer leptomeningeal disease.
- *T-DXd*. Activity in HER2-positive breast cancer leptomeningeal disease.
- *Pembrolizumab*. Activity in melanoma leptomeningeal disease.
- *Various others*.

*Outcomes*. Historically poor — median survival 4-8 weeks with no treatment, 2-6 months with treatment. Improving with newer systemic therapies that have CNS activity in specific molecular subtypes. Some patients with appropriate molecular targets and systemic therapy may have substantially longer survival.

The management of leptomeningeal disease requires individualization and often multidisciplinary care.

## Treatment complications and long-term effects

CNS cancer treatment produces specific complications that require careful management:

*Radiation-related complications*:

*Acute effects*. Skin reactions, fatigue, alopecia in the radiation field, sometimes mild headaches.

*Subacute effects (weeks to months)*. "Somnolence syndrome" — fatigue, sleepiness, sometimes mild cognitive changes. Usually self-limited.

*Late effects (months to years)*:
- *Radiation necrosis*. Brain tissue necrosis in the radiation field. Can mimic tumor progression on imaging. Diagnosis can be challenging; advanced imaging (MR perfusion, MR spectroscopy, PET) helps. Management may include corticosteroids, bevacizumab, hyperbaric oxygen, or surgical resection.
- *Cognitive decline*. Particularly with whole-brain radiation. Various severity. May progress over years.
- *Endocrinopathies*. Hypopituitarism after radiation to pituitary region.
- *Secondary malignancies*. Rare but possible. Meningiomas, gliomas, sarcomas in radiation field after years to decades.
- *Cerebrovascular disease*. Increased risk of stroke after CNS radiation, particularly when combined with chemotherapy.

*Surgical complications*. Infection, bleeding, neurological deficits from resection, CSF leak, seizures. Most can be minimized with experienced neurosurgical care.

*Drug-related complications*:
- *Chemotherapy effects*. Vary by drug. Methotrexate can cause leukoencephalopathy. Bevacizumab can cause hemorrhage and hypertension.
- *Targeted therapy effects*. Various.
- *Immunotherapy effects*. Immune-mediated encephalitis, hypophysitis, and other CNS irAEs.

*Tumor-related complications*:
- *Seizures*. Common in brain tumors and metastases. Antiepileptic drugs (levetiracetam preferred; avoid CYP-inducing drugs that interfere with chemotherapy).
- *Cerebral edema*. Often managed with corticosteroids (dexamethasone). Hyperosmolar therapy (mannitol, hypertonic saline) for acute crisis.
- *Hydrocephalus*. May require ventriculoperitoneal shunt placement.
- *Deep vein thrombosis*. Cancer patients with brain tumors have particularly high VTE risk. Anticoagulation balancing thrombosis prevention with bleeding risk.

The management of complications is a substantial part of CNS cancer care, requiring expertise across multiple specialties.

## Multidisciplinary CNS tumor care

Brain tumor care is intrinsically multidisciplinary. The team typically includes:

*Neuro-oncology*. Medical oncologist with neuro-oncology specialization. Coordinates systemic therapy and overall management.

*Neurosurgery*. Performs biopsies, resections, and emergency procedures. Plays central role in initial management.

*Radiation oncology*. Plans and delivers stereotactic radiosurgery, whole-brain radiation, fractionated radiation. CNS radiation requires specialized expertise.

*Neuropathology*. Provides diagnostic pathology with molecular characterization.

*Neuroradiology*. Interpretation of complex CNS imaging including advanced techniques (perfusion, spectroscopy, fMRI for surgical planning).

*Rehabilitation medicine*. Physical, occupational, speech therapy for treatment-related functional deficits.

*Neuropsychology*. Cognitive assessment and rehabilitation.

*Palliative care*. Symptom management and end-of-life care.

*Neurology*. Management of seizures and other neurological complications.

*Endocrinology*. Management of pituitary dysfunction and endocrine complications.

*Genetics*. Counseling for hereditary tumor syndromes.

*Social work and patient advocacy*. Practical support for patients and families.

The brain tumor patient often requires substantial coordinated care over a sustained period. Major academic centers have dedicated brain tumor programs with all these specialists. Community settings often have less coordinated care, with implications for outcomes.

The tumor board concept is particularly important in neuro-oncology. Complex cases benefit from multidisciplinary review. Treatment decisions integrate input from all relevant specialties.

## Specific CNS cancer challenges in clinical practice

Several issues recur in CNS cancer management:

*Pseudoprogression versus progression*. After radiation therapy (especially temozolomide + radiation in glioblastoma) or immunotherapy, imaging may show apparent worsening that doesn't represent true tumor progression. Distinguishing pseudoprogression from real progression can be challenging. Advanced imaging, repeat imaging at intervals, and sometimes biopsy may be needed.

*Radiation necrosis versus recurrence*. Late post-radiation imaging changes can mimic tumor recurrence. Distinguishing them affects treatment decisions. PET imaging, MR perfusion, MR spectroscopy can help.

*Steroid management*. Corticosteroids (especially dexamethasone) are widely used to manage edema. They produce significant side effects (insulin resistance, immunosuppression, mood changes, infection risk, myopathy, others) that accumulate with prolonged use. Tapering steroids while maintaining symptom control is an ongoing challenge.

*Quality of life*. Brain tumors affect cognition, personality, function, family dynamics in profound ways. Quality of life considerations are central to treatment decisions, particularly as treatment options are limited and survival is often short.

*Driving and capacity*. Brain tumors and treatment effects can compromise the patient's ability to drive and make medical decisions. Assessment of these capacities is part of the clinical care.

*End-of-life planning*. Given the prognosis of many CNS cancers, early advance care planning is important. Discussions about goals of care, advance directives, and hospice transitions are part of standard practice.

## Future directions in CNS cancer treatment

Several emerging approaches may improve outcomes:

*Tumor-treating fields (TTFields)*. Already approved for glioblastoma (added to maintenance temozolomide) and for mesothelioma. Being tested in other settings. The mechanism involves disruption of mitotic spindle formation through low-intensity electric fields.

*Personalized neoantigen vaccines*. Various trials in glioblastoma. Combinations with checkpoint inhibitors being explored.

*Oncolytic virotherapy*. PVS-RIPO (polio virus), DNX-2401 (adenovirus), and others have shown some signals of activity in glioblastoma. Combinations being tested.

*CAR-T cell therapy for CNS tumors*. EGFRvIII-targeted, B7-H3-targeted, IL-13Rα2-targeted, GD2-targeted, claudin-6-targeted CAR-T cells in trials. The blood-brain barrier limitation requires local delivery or specific approaches. Early signals in glioblastoma and diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma.

*Convection-enhanced delivery*. Direct catheter-based intratumoral delivery of drugs, bypassing the blood-brain barrier. Has shown some activity for selected drugs and may enable approaches not feasible with systemic delivery.

*Blood-brain barrier opening with focused ultrasound*. Selectively opening the BBB at tumor sites to allow drug penetration. Being developed for various therapeutic agents.

*IDH inhibitors for IDH-mutant gliomas*. Vorasidenib approved 2024. Ivosidenib for selected indications. The success may extend to additional IDH-mutant glioma populations.

*Targeted therapy for specific molecular subgroups*. BRAF-mutant tumors, NTRK fusions, RET fusions, others. The molecular profiling of brain tumors increasingly reveals targetable alterations.

*Improved radiation techniques*. Proton therapy for selected pediatric and adult CNS tumors. Better radiation planning to spare critical structures. Adaptive radiotherapy as tumor and anatomy change during treatment.

*MRD detection in CNS cancer*. Liquid biopsy approaches for CSF and blood to detect recurrent disease early.

The CNS cancer field continues to develop, with substantial unmet need driving research investment. The translation has been slower than other cancer types due to the unique challenges of CNS biology, but progress continues.

## What this chapter gives you

Brain metastases are more common than primary brain tumors, occurring in 20-40% of patients with metastatic cancer. Lung cancer is the most common primary source, followed by breast cancer, melanoma, renal cell carcinoma, and others.

Diagnosis uses MRI as the standard imaging modality, with tissue biopsy reserved for selected cases. Symptoms include headaches, seizures, focal neurological deficits, and cognitive changes.

Management of brain metastases involves both local treatment (surgery for accessible/symptomatic lesions, stereotactic radiosurgery for limited metastases, whole-brain radiation for extensive disease — increasingly with hippocampal sparing) and systemic treatment. Many newer targeted therapies and immunotherapy have meaningful CNS activity, expanding options for systemic management.

Leptomeningeal disease is a particularly difficult complication, treated with intrathecal chemotherapy, CNS radiation, and systemic therapies with CNS activity. Outcomes are historically poor but improving with newer molecular-targeted approaches.

Treatment complications include radiation effects (acute, subacute, and late, including radiation necrosis and cognitive decline), surgical complications, drug-related effects, and tumor-related complications (seizures, edema, hydrocephalus, VTE).

Multidisciplinary care involves neuro-oncology, neurosurgery, radiation oncology, neuropathology, neuroradiology, rehabilitation, neuropsychology, palliative care, and several other specialties. Tumor board review is particularly important in neuro-oncology.

Specific clinical challenges include distinguishing pseudoprogression from progression, radiation necrosis from recurrence, steroid management, quality of life considerations, and end-of-life planning.

Future directions include tumor-treating fields (expanding use), personalized neoantigen vaccines, oncolytic virotherapy, CAR-T cell therapy for CNS, convection-enhanced delivery, focused ultrasound BBB opening, IDH inhibitors for IDH-mutant gliomas, additional targeted therapies for specific molecular subgroups, improved radiation techniques, and MRD detection in CNS cancer.
