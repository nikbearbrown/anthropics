The cancer that's curable in Boston is fatal in Lagos.

Hold the framing. The advances of modern oncology — molecular profiling, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, CAR-T cells, sophisticated radiation, multidisciplinary care — are concentrated in high-income countries. The infrastructure to deliver these advances costs hundreds of millions of dollars per cancer center. The drugs cost tens to hundreds of thousands of dollars per patient. The expertise requires years of specialized training. The implementation requires functioning health systems, reliable supply chains, accurate diagnostics, surgical capability, radiation facilities, intensive care support.

Most of these elements are missing or limited in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where about 70% of cancer cases globally now occur. Cancer mortality is disproportionately concentrated in these settings — a much higher case fatality rate than in high-income countries for the same cancer types. A child with leukemia in the US has a >90% chance of cure. The same child in a low-income African country has perhaps a 20% chance of survival. A patient with HER2-positive breast cancer in Europe receives trastuzumab and lives many years. The same patient in many countries cannot access trastuzumab and dies within 1-2 years.

This is not because cancer biology is different across regions. It's because cancer care infrastructure is different. The gap is one of the largest health disparities globally and represents an enormous opportunity for improvement through targeted interventions.

This chapter — the second of two on health economics and access — covers global disparities in cancer care, the ethical and policy dimensions of cancer treatment, and approaches to addressing inequities. The chapter completes the book's coverage by examining the broader system-level issues that determine what cancer care actually looks like for patients globally.

Hold the question: *given that we have powerful cancer therapies, what does it take to make them accessible globally, and what ethical principles should guide our priorities?*

## The global cancer burden

Cancer is becoming a global health priority:

*Incidence patterns*. Annual cancer incidence globally is approximately 20 million cases (2022 GLOBOCAN data). The distribution:
- High-income countries: ~30% of cases.
- Middle-income countries: ~50% of cases.
- Low-income countries: ~20% of cases.

*Mortality patterns*. Annual cancer deaths approximately 9.7 million. The distribution is more weighted toward low- and middle-income countries — about 70% of cancer deaths occur in LMICs.

*Why the disparity in mortality*. Same cancers, very different outcomes:
- *Late diagnosis*. In LMICs, cancers are typically diagnosed at later stages (when treatment is less effective). Limited screening infrastructure. Limited awareness. Delayed care-seeking due to costs and access.
- *Limited treatment access*. Many cancer drugs and therapies not available. Surgical and radiation capacity limited. Specialized care concentrated in few centers.
- *Treatment abandonment*. Even when treatment is started, costs and access issues lead to abandonment. Estimates of treatment abandonment in pediatric cancer in LMICs range from 20-60% in various settings.
- *Less effective treatment*. When drugs are available, may be older agents rather than current standards. Counterfeit or substandard medications. Inadequate supportive care.
- *Comorbidities and competing risks*. Other health problems affect cancer outcomes.

*Cancer types by region*. Different patterns:
- *High-income countries*. Breast, prostate, lung, colorectal cancers dominant.
- *LMICs*. Higher relative burden of infection-related cancers (cervical, liver, stomach), plus increasing rates of "Western" cancers as lifestyle and aging shift epidemiology.

The disparity is enormous and not biological — it's structural. The same cancer that has 90% 5-year survival in a high-income country may have 20% 5-year survival in a low-income country, due entirely to access and care quality differences.

## Specific disparities by region

The cancer care disparities have specific patterns globally:

*Sub-Saharan Africa*:
- Limited number of oncologists (some countries have <1 oncologist per million population, compared to ~50 per million in high-income countries).
- Limited radiation therapy capacity (some countries have 1-2 linear accelerators serving entire populations).
- Limited pathology infrastructure for accurate diagnosis.
- Limited access to cancer drugs.
- Cervical cancer, breast cancer in young women, hepatocellular carcinoma (HBV-related), AIDS-related cancers all have particularly poor outcomes.
- Recent improvements in some countries with concerted international support (Rwanda, others).

*South Asia*:
- India has substantial cancer burden but variable access across regions.
- Tobacco-related cancers (oral, lung) common.
- Cervical cancer and breast cancer in women.
- Pediatric cancer outcomes substantially worse than high-income countries.
- Some major cancer centers but limited geographic distribution.

*Southeast Asia*:
- Hepatocellular carcinoma (HBV, HCV).
- Stomach cancer.
- Lung cancer (especially in non-smoking women — environmental factors).
- Variable access and outcomes.

*Latin America*:
- Wide range of outcomes by country.
- Cervical cancer remains substantial issue in some countries.
- Breast cancer increasing.
- Some countries (Argentina, Brazil, Chile) have substantial cancer infrastructure; others have major gaps.

*Eastern Europe and Central Asia*:
- Transitional health systems.
- Variable infrastructure.
- Specific cancer patterns related to industrial history and lifestyle.

*Middle East and North Africa*:
- Mixed picture — substantial cancer care in wealthy Gulf countries; limited in others.
- War and political instability affecting care in some areas.

*Oceania*:
- Substantial cancer infrastructure in Australia and New Zealand.
- Limited services in many Pacific Island nations.

The geographic patterns of cancer outcomes reflect health system development, political stability, economic resources, and policy decisions — not biology.

## Within-country disparities

Beyond country-level differences, substantial within-country disparities exist:

*Race and ethnicity*. In the US:
- Black Americans have higher cancer mortality than white Americans for most cancer types, even controlling for stage.
- Native Americans have specific cancer patterns and outcomes.
- Hispanic and Asian American outcomes vary by specific group and cancer.
- Multiple contributing factors: historical discrimination, current racism in care delivery, structural disparities, social determinants of health, sometimes biological factors.

*Income and socioeconomic status*. Lower-income patients have worse cancer outcomes across countries:
- Less screening.
- Later diagnosis.
- Less access to specialty care.
- More treatment abandonment.
- Worse comorbidities.
- Less psychosocial support.

*Geographic disparities*. Rural patients often have worse outcomes than urban:
- Distance from specialty cancer centers.
- Limited oncology workforce in rural areas.
- Limited access to clinical trials.
- Travel burden for treatment.

*Insurance status*. In US and other systems with insurance-based care:
- Uninsured patients have worse outcomes.
- Insurance gaps during cancer treatment can be devastating.
- Coverage variability across insurance types.

*Education*. Health literacy affects cancer care engagement and outcomes.

*Language*. Limited English (or local language) proficiency creates barriers in many systems.

*LGBTQ+ status*. Specific care barriers and disparities in some settings.

*Disability*. Cancer patients with disabilities face additional access barriers.

The within-country disparities compound the between-country disparities. A wealthy patient in a low-income country may have better cancer access than a poor patient in a wealthy country. The disparities are complex and require multifaceted approaches.

## Initiatives addressing global cancer disparities

Multiple initiatives aim to address global cancer disparities:

*WHO Global Initiative for Childhood Cancer*. Launched 2018. Aims to improve childhood cancer survival globally to 60% by 2030 (from current ~30%). Strategy includes:
- National childhood cancer programs.
- Treatment protocol harmonization.
- Essential medicine access.
- Training and workforce development.
- Health system strengthening.

*WHO Cervical Cancer Elimination Initiative*. Aims to eliminate cervical cancer as a public health problem globally. Strategy:
- 90% HPV vaccination coverage by age 15.
- 70% screening coverage by ages 35 and 45.
- 90% treatment of precancerous lesions and invasive cancers.

*International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) cancer programs*. Support for radiation therapy infrastructure in LMICs through PACT (Programme of Action for Cancer Therapy) and related initiatives.

*Union for International Cancer Control (UICC)*. Global membership organization for cancer organizations. World Cancer Day (Feb 4). Advocacy and education.

*National Cancer Institute (NCI) Center for Global Health*. US government engagement in global cancer initiatives.

*National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Harmonized Guidelines*. Cancer treatment guidelines adapted for resource-constrained settings.

*Global Action Plan for the Prevention and Control of Noncommunicable Diseases*. WHO framework that includes cancer.

*Disease-specific global initiatives*. Various initiatives for HPV vaccination, hepatitis B vaccination, *H. pylori* eradication, tobacco control, breast cancer awareness, and many other cancer-related causes.

*Pharmaceutical access programs*. Some drug companies provide reduced-price or donated cancer drugs through global access programs. Various NGO-pharmaceutical partnerships.

*Twinning programs*. Partnerships between cancer centers in high-income and low-income countries to share expertise, training, and resources.

*Workforce development*. Training programs to build oncology workforce in LMICs. Some focused on specific specialties (radiation oncology, pediatric oncology).

*Telemedicine and telehealth*. Extending specialty expertise to underserved areas through telecommunications. Particularly important in resource-limited settings.

The initiatives have produced incremental progress but the gap remains enormous. Closing the gap substantially would require sustained, coordinated investment over decades.

## Ethical issues in cancer care

Cancer care involves multiple ethical considerations:

*Resource allocation*. Cancer care consumes substantial healthcare resources. Decisions about how to allocate between cancer and other health priorities, and between different types of cancer care, are inherently ethical.

*Cost-effectiveness vs. individual care*. Tension between making the best decision for the individual patient and decisions that maximize population health within fixed resources.

*Equity vs. equality*. Should the same care be available to all? Or should care be allocated based on potential benefit, ability to pay, or other criteria? Different ethical frameworks suggest different answers.

*Access to expensive treatments*. As cancer drugs become more expensive, fewer patients globally can access them. Questions about whether countries should subsidize unlimited access (likely unsustainable) or have explicit rationing (politically and ethically challenging).

*Clinical trial access*. Patients with limited treatment options sometimes have access to trials that provide promising experimental therapy. But trial access is geographically and demographically uneven. The "ZIP code lottery" of trial access creates disparities.

*Informed consent and capacity*. Cancer patients are often making decisions while distressed and may have impaired capacity. Standards for informed consent and surrogate decision-making.

*End-of-life decisions*. When to stop active treatment. Hospice transition. Physician-assisted dying (legal in some jurisdictions). Religious and cultural considerations.

*Confidentiality and disclosure*. Genetic information from cancer testing affects family members. Genetic privacy issues.

*Research ethics*. Conduct of research in vulnerable populations, in LMICs, in pediatric patients. Post-trial drug access for participants.

*Industry conflicts of interest*. Drug company influence on cancer research, education, and clinical practice.

*Vulnerable populations*. Specific protections for children, pregnant women, prisoners, those with cognitive impairment, others.

*Distributive justice*. How should cancer care benefits be distributed across society?

*Procedural justice*. Fair decision-making processes for cancer care policy.

These ethical considerations underlie much of cancer policy and clinical decision-making. Different ethical frameworks (utilitarian, deontological, virtue-based, communitarian) provide different answers. Real-world decisions integrate multiple considerations.

## Policy considerations

Cancer care is substantially shaped by policy decisions:

*Drug pricing policy*. As discussed in 38A. The single most consequential policy area for cancer care costs.

*Insurance and coverage policy*. Universal health coverage, single-payer systems, employer-based insurance, individual marketplaces — each shapes cancer care delivery.

*Cancer screening policy*. Recommendations and reimbursement for cancer screening. Public health screening programs.

*Vaccination policy*. HPV vaccination, hepatitis B vaccination, and other cancer-preventing vaccines. Mandates, school requirements, insurance coverage.

*Tobacco control policy*. Tax policy, marketing restrictions, smoke-free environments, cessation support. Single most important cancer prevention policy area.

*Environmental policy*. Air pollution standards, carcinogen regulation, occupational exposure limits, sun protection education.

*Food and lifestyle policy*. Tax policy on unhealthy foods, food labeling, urban design that supports physical activity.

*Workplace policy*. Paid sick leave, family medical leave, cancer-related accommodations.

*Research policy*. Public funding for cancer research. International research collaboration. Open data and publication policies.

*Health system organization*. Centralization vs. distribution of cancer care. Cancer center designation systems. Quality measurement and reporting.

*Workforce policy*. Training programs for oncology workforce. Specialty distribution.

*Specific cancer initiatives*. Cancer Moonshot, Cancer Prevention and Research Institutes, national cancer plans.

*Global health policy*. International cooperation, foreign aid for cancer, intellectual property rules affecting drug access.

Policy decisions across these areas substantially determine what cancer care looks like for patients. Many policy decisions have long lag times before effects on cancer outcomes manifest, making long-term planning essential but politically challenging.

## The drug development ecosystem

The cancer drug development ecosystem has specific features:

*Academic research*. Substantial NIH and other government funding supports basic cancer research at academic institutions. The discoveries from this research often form the basis for drug development.

*Biotech and pharmaceutical industry*. Industry-funded development brings drugs from concept through clinical trials and regulatory approval. Industry investment in cancer R&D is substantial.

*Venture capital*. Funds biotech startup development of cancer therapies.

*Academic-industry partnerships*. Increasingly common. Academic discoveries licensed to companies for development. Joint research programs.

*Regulatory pathways*. FDA, EMA, and other regulators provide pathways for cancer drug approval, with accelerated pathways for serious diseases.

*Patient advocacy*. Cancer patient advocacy organizations influence research priorities, regulatory decisions, and policy. Has been increasingly impactful.

*International cooperation*. Drug development often spans multiple countries. International coordination through ICH and other bodies.

The ecosystem produces many new cancer drugs but with the pricing patterns discussed in 38A. Reform proposals span various dimensions:

*Public-sector drug development*. More government funding for drug development to reduce reliance on private sector.

*Compulsory licensing*. In specific circumstances, allowing generic production of patented drugs.

*Patent reform*. Limiting evergreening and patent extensions.

*Transparency requirements*. Pricing, development costs, profit margins.

*Direct government drug manufacturing*. Various proposals.

Each reform proposal has supporters and opponents. The current ecosystem has substantial inertia.

## The future of cancer care access

Several trends will shape cancer care access in coming years:

*Continued drug price pressure*. Drug pricing will remain a major political and policy issue. Various reform efforts will continue.

*Biosimilar and generic expansion*. As key cancer drugs lose patent protection, biosimilars and generics will reduce prices for some indications.

*New therapeutic approaches*. Continued development of targeted therapy, immunotherapy, cellular therapy. Some may be more expensive than current options; others may be more cost-effective.

*Precision oncology infrastructure*. Continued expansion of molecular profiling and biomarker-driven care. Implementation gaps in many settings.

*Digital health and telemedicine*. May expand access to cancer expertise in underserved areas.

*Global cancer initiatives*. Continued efforts to improve cancer care in LMICs. WHO targets and national programs.

*Climate change*. Will affect cancer epidemiology (UV exposure changes, environmental carcinogen exposure changes, displacement and resource issues affecting healthcare).

*Pandemic preparedness*. COVID-19 demonstrated the fragility of cancer care during public health emergencies. Future pandemics could similarly disrupt care.

*Health equity initiatives*. Increasing focus on addressing within-country disparities. Various policy and clinical interventions.

*Patient-driven advocacy*. Continued growth of patient and survivor advocacy organizations influencing research, policy, and clinical practice.

The future of cancer care access depends substantially on choices made in coming years — about drug pricing, health system organization, global cooperation, equity priorities, and resource allocation. The current trajectory has produced both remarkable scientific advances and substantial access inequities. Where the field goes from here will depend on values, politics, and policy as much as on continued scientific progress.

## Conclusion to the textbook

This textbook has traced cancer biology from molecular foundations through clinical management and access considerations. The journey has covered:

- *Foundations*: cell biology, genetics, epidemiology, mutational processes.
- *Cancer biology*: oncogenes, tumor suppressors, epigenetics, cell cycle, apoptosis, metabolism, angiogenesis, invasion and metastasis, microenvironment, heterogeneity, immunology.
- *Diagnosis and characterization*: clinical workup, imaging, pathology, molecular profiling, classification.
- *Treatment modalities*: surgery, radiation, chemotherapy, hormonal therapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, gene therapy, oncolytic virotherapy, nanotechnology.
- *Cancer types*: solid tumors by organ system, CNS tumors, hematologic malignancies, pediatric cancers, rare cancers.
- *Care infrastructure*: clinical trials, regulatory pathways, supportive care, palliative care, survivorship, health economics.

The picture that emerges is one of remarkable progress and persistent challenges. Cancer biology has been illuminated to a depth that would have been unimaginable a few decades ago. Treatment options have expanded dramatically, with cures or substantial life extension for many cancers that were once uniformly fatal. The biology informs increasingly precise treatments. The infrastructure for delivering modern care has developed substantially.

At the same time, cancer remains a leading cause of death globally. Many cancers still kill most patients within years of diagnosis. Treatment costs create access barriers. Disparities in outcomes by geography, race, income, and other factors are substantial. The science of cancer biology continues to advance faster than our ability to translate advances into equitable access.

The student finishing this textbook should have a comprehensive framework for understanding cancer — the biology, the clinical management, the broader context. The specific facts will evolve as cancer biology continues to develop. The frameworks will largely persist.

For the patient or family member dealing with cancer, the textbook provides background for informed engagement with their care. For the clinician, the foundation for ongoing learning in oncology. For the researcher, the context for contributions to advancing the field. For the policy maker or advocate, the understanding to engage with cancer-related issues at the system level.

The remaining work — for science, for medicine, for policy, for advocacy — is to convert the biological understanding into reliable, equitable cures for everyone affected by cancer. We are partway there. The continuing journey is the work of generations.
