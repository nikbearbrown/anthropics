A cancer survivor is someone whose treatment never quite ends.

Hold the framing. The word "survivor" is used in oncology to describe anyone from the day of diagnosis onward — encompassing patients in active treatment, those in remission, and those decades past their cancer. The National Coalition for Cancer Survivorship adopted this broad definition deliberately. It recognized that the cancer experience doesn't have clean boundaries. The person diagnosed with cancer carries the disease and its aftermath through the rest of their life, in ways that change but rarely disappear.

The numbers matter. There are roughly 18 million cancer survivors in the United States as of 2024, projected to reach 22 million by 2030. About two-thirds are 5+ years past diagnosis. Many are decades out from treatment. They are not "cured and done" — they live with the consequences of having had cancer and having been treated for it.

The consequences are real. Cancer treatment, particularly the aggressive regimens used to achieve cure, produces long-term effects on organ systems, function, and risk of subsequent disease. Cardiotoxicity from anthracyclines may manifest twenty years after treatment. Secondary cancers from radiation appear decades later. Cognitive effects persist after the treatment that caused them is long over. Fertility, growth, sexual function, endocrine health — all may be permanently affected. The survivor population is large enough now that survivorship is a distinct subspecialty.

This chapter — the first of two on cancer survivorship — covers the long-term physical effects of cancer treatment across organ systems. The next — 37B — covers psychosocial dimensions, rehabilitation, reintegration, and quality of life considerations.

Hold the question: *given that cancer treatment increasingly produces cures, what does the rest of the survivor's life look like, and how do we optimize it?*

## The framework of survivorship care

Survivorship care addresses the long-term consequences of cancer and its treatment. The framework includes:

*Surveillance for cancer recurrence*. Disease-specific monitoring based on the original cancer type and stage.

*Surveillance for second cancers*. Detection of new cancers that develop years or decades after the original treatment.

*Management of late effects*. Treatment-related effects on multiple organ systems that develop or persist after treatment completion.

*Health promotion*. Lifestyle interventions (exercise, diet, smoking cessation) that improve cancer outcomes and general health.

*Psychosocial support*. Ongoing attention to fear of recurrence, identity, relationships, finances, work.

*Coordination of care*. Between oncology, primary care, and specialty providers.

*Patient education*. Helping survivors understand their cancer history, current health status, and ongoing surveillance needs.

The Institute of Medicine 2005 report "From Cancer Patient to Cancer Survivor: Lost in Transition" highlighted gaps in survivorship care and established the framework for modern survivorship programs. Major cancer centers now have dedicated survivorship clinics; the Children's Oncology Group Long-Term Follow-Up Guidelines provide evidence-based recommendations for pediatric cancer survivors.

The challenges of survivorship care are real:

*Workforce limitations*. Oncology providers don't have capacity to provide indefinite follow-up for all survivors. Primary care physicians often lack expertise in late effects management.

*Communication gaps*. Survivors moving between providers may have incomplete information transfer.

*Insurance and access*. Survivorship surveillance and management may not be well-covered.

*Geographic concentration*. Specialized survivorship programs are at major centers, with limited access in rural and underserved areas.

*Survivorship care plans* (documents summarizing the cancer history, treatment received, surveillance recommendations, and survivor-specific health concerns) are designed to address communication gaps but implementation has been variable.

## Cardiovascular effects of cancer treatment

Cardiovascular disease is increasingly recognized as one of the most consequential long-term effects of cancer treatment.

*Anthracycline cardiotoxicity*. Doxorubicin, daunorubicin, idarubicin, epirubicin produce cumulative, dose-dependent cardiotoxicity. The mechanism involves topoisomerase IIβ inhibition in cardiomyocytes, generation of reactive oxygen species, and direct mitochondrial effects.

Risk increases with cumulative dose. The classical doxorubicin dose limit is 450-550 mg/m² lifetime; higher doses produce substantial risk. Other risk factors include radiation to chest, prior cardiovascular disease, hypertension, female sex, age extremes.

Clinical manifestations:
- Asymptomatic decline in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF).
- Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction.
- Atrial and ventricular arrhythmias.
- Can manifest acutely during treatment, subacutely months later, or chronically decades later.

Surveillance: Echocardiography (or cardiac MRI) before treatment, during treatment for high-dose regimens, after treatment completion. Frequency depends on cumulative dose, baseline cardiac function, and other risk factors.

Prevention and management:
- Cumulative dose limits.
- Dexrazoxane as cardioprotectant for high-dose anthracyclines.
- Liposomal doxorubicin (reduces cardiotoxicity for equivalent anti-cancer effect).
- ACE inhibitors and beta-blockers for established cardiomyopathy.
- Aggressive cardiovascular risk factor management.

*Anti-HER2 cardiotoxicity*. Trastuzumab can cause LVEF decline, typically reversible. Compound with anthracyclines (synergistic effect). Specific monitoring during trastuzumab therapy.

*Anti-VEGF cardiotoxicity*. Bevacizumab and VEGFR-targeted TKIs cause hypertension, occasional cardiomyopathy, arterial thromboembolism. Aggressive BP management.

*Tyrosine kinase inhibitor cardiotoxicity*. Various TKIs cause different cardiac effects. Some prolong QT interval (specific monitoring with ECG). Some cause LVEF decline.

*Immune checkpoint inhibitor cardiotoxicity*. Rare but often severe immune-mediated myocarditis. Mortality up to 50% in severe cases. Particularly with anti-CTLA-4 + anti-PD-1 combinations. Requires high vigilance.

*Radiation cardiotoxicity*. Chest radiation (for Hodgkin lymphoma, breast cancer, lung cancer, others) causes:
- Coronary artery disease (often with unusual distribution, sometimes silent until myocardial infarction).
- Valvular disease.
- Pericardial disease.
- Cardiomyopathy.

The risk depends on radiation dose, fields, fractionation, and time from treatment. Substantial risk persists decades after treatment. Hodgkin lymphoma survivors treated with mantle radiation in the 1970s-80s have high rates of cardiovascular disease.

*Surveillance for radiation cardiotoxicity*. Echocardiography, cardiac stress testing, coronary CT angiography or calcium scoring, ECG. Frequency based on radiation exposure and risk factors.

*General cardiovascular risk management*. All cancer survivors benefit from aggressive cardiovascular risk factor management:
- Blood pressure control.
- Lipid management (statins for appropriate risk profiles).
- Diabetes management.
- Smoking cessation.
- Exercise.
- Weight management.

The intersection of cardio-oncology and survivorship has produced specialized programs at many cancer centers. Cardiologists trained in cardio-oncology coordinate care with survivors at risk.

## Endocrine effects

Many cancer treatments affect endocrine function.

*Cranial radiation*. Causes:
- Growth hormone deficiency. Particularly significant in children. May require GH replacement.
- Central hypothyroidism. From pituitary effects.
- Central adrenal insufficiency. Requires corticosteroid replacement.
- Hypogonadotropic hypogonadism. Affects fertility and sex hormone levels.

*Neck radiation*. Causes:
- Primary hypothyroidism (most common late effect of neck radiation).
- Thyroid cancer (radiation-induced).
- Hyperparathyroidism (rare).

*Pelvic radiation*. Causes:
- Ovarian failure in women.
- Testicular failure in men.
- Affects fertility and sex hormone production.

*Chemotherapy effects*:
- *Alkylating agents* (cyclophosphamide, ifosfamide, busulfan, others). Often cause gonadal failure. Risk varies by age and cumulative dose.
- *Other chemotherapy*. Variable gonadotoxicity.

*Targeted therapies*:
- *Sunitinib and other multi-kinase inhibitors*. Often cause primary hypothyroidism.
- *Immune checkpoint inhibitors*. Various immune-mediated endocrinopathies (thyroiditis, hypophysitis, adrenal insufficiency, type 1 diabetes). Often permanent.

*Survivorship endocrine care*:
- Annual thyroid function tests for at-risk survivors.
- Sex hormone monitoring; replacement when appropriate (with cancer-specific considerations for hormone replacement therapy).
- Growth hormone testing and replacement in pediatric survivors.
- Bone density monitoring (DEXA scans) for survivors with risk factors.
- Diabetes screening.

The endocrine effects can be subtle initially but produce significant long-term consequences if untreated.

## Fertility and reproductive effects

Cancer and its treatment frequently affect fertility. Pre-treatment fertility counseling and preservation should be offered to patients of reproductive age.

*Male fertility*:
- *Sperm cryopreservation* before treatment for patients of reproductive age.
- Some men recover spermatogenesis after treatment (depends on regimen).
- Testicular tissue cryopreservation for prepubertal boys (experimental).

*Female fertility*:
- *Oocyte cryopreservation*. Mature oocytes harvested after ovarian stimulation, then frozen.
- *Embryo cryopreservation*. If patient has partner, embryos can be created and frozen. Established technique.
- *Ovarian tissue cryopreservation*. Ovarian cortex removed, frozen, and later reimplanted. Particularly important for prepubertal girls and patients who cannot delay treatment for ovarian stimulation.
- *Ovarian suppression during chemotherapy*. GnRH agonists may have some protective effect, but evidence is limited.

*Pregnancy after cancer*:
- Generally considered safe after treatment, with disease-specific recommendations for timing.
- Breast cancer: typically waiting 2 years after treatment; longer for some patients.
- Hormone receptor-positive breast cancer: complex considerations about interrupting endocrine therapy for pregnancy (POSITIVE trial provided some data).
- Pregnancies in survivors should be managed by high-risk obstetrics with awareness of cancer-related considerations.

*Sexual function*:
- Often significantly affected by cancer treatment.
- Erectile dysfunction after prostate cancer treatment.
- Vaginal dryness, dyspareunia after pelvic radiation or chemotherapy-induced menopause.
- Body image issues affecting sexual function.
- Often underdiscussed in cancer care.

Survivors of childhood cancer have specific fertility concerns. The Childhood Cancer Survivor Study has documented elevated rates of infertility among childhood cancer survivors compared to siblings.

## Neurologic and cognitive effects

Cancer treatment can produce diverse neurologic effects:

*Peripheral neuropathy*. Common with platinum agents (especially cisplatin and oxaliplatin), taxanes, vinca alkaloids, bortezomib, brentuximab vedotin, others. Manifests as:
- Sensory symptoms (numbness, tingling, pain) — most common.
- Motor symptoms (weakness) — less common.
- Autonomic symptoms — uncommon.

Often dose-cumulative. May be partially reversible after treatment but often persistent.

Management:
- Dose modification or treatment discontinuation when severe.
- Duloxetine has evidence for chronic chemotherapy-induced neuropathy.
- Topical agents (lidocaine, menthol).
- Physical therapy.
- Avoidance of additional neurotoxic agents.

*Cognitive effects ("chemo brain")*. Affects 30-50% of cancer survivors. Manifests as:
- Memory impairment (particularly working memory).
- Attention difficulties.
- Executive function impairment.
- Processing speed slowing.
- Word-finding difficulties.

Mechanisms include direct treatment effects on brain tissue, inflammation, hormonal effects, fatigue, sleep disruption, depression, and chronic stress.

Management:
- Cognitive rehabilitation strategies.
- Exercise (has cognitive benefits).
- Cognitive behavioral therapy.
- Addressing reversible contributors (sleep, depression, anemia).
- Stimulants (methylphenidate) in selected patients.

*Brain effects of CNS-directed therapy*. Cranial radiation, intrathecal chemotherapy, and high-dose methotrexate can cause more severe and progressive cognitive effects, particularly in children. Modern approaches have reduced CNS-directed therapy intensity to mitigate this.

*Hearing loss*. Cisplatin causes ototoxicity, often permanent. Affects high frequencies first. More severe in children. Sodium thiosulfate approved for prevention in pediatric patients.

*Other neurologic effects*. Radiation-induced encephalopathy, treatment-related strokes, neuropathies from specific drugs.

## Pulmonary effects

Several treatments affect lung function:

*Bleomycin pulmonary toxicity*. Can cause pulmonary fibrosis. Cumulative dose-related. Exacerbated by high inspired oxygen (relevant for anesthesia management). Limits use, particularly in adults with concerning pulmonary function.

*Other chemotherapy*. Busulfan, BCNU, nitrosoureas, methotrexate, gemcitabine — various pulmonary effects.

*Radiation pneumonitis*. Acute pulmonary inflammation 1-3 months after radiation. Can progress to fibrosis. Depends on radiation field and dose. Higher risk with concurrent chemotherapy.

*Immune checkpoint inhibitor pneumonitis*. Immune-mediated lung inflammation. Variable severity. Requires prompt recognition and treatment with corticosteroids.

*Targeted therapy pulmonary effects*. EGFR inhibitors can cause interstitial lung disease. Various other drugs have specific pulmonary effects.

*Pneumonectomy or lobectomy*. Affects exercise capacity and pulmonary reserve.

Survivorship management:
- Pulmonary function testing for at-risk survivors.
- Smoking cessation.
- Avoidance of additional pulmonary insults.
- Treatment of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.
- Vaccinations (influenza, COVID-19, pneumococcal).

## Renal effects

*Cisplatin nephrotoxicity*. Cumulative kidney damage. Hydration during treatment reduces incidence. Long-term renal dysfunction in some patients.

*Other nephrotoxic chemotherapy*. Ifosfamide, methotrexate (especially high-dose, with inadequate hydration or urinary alkalinization), pemetrexed, others.

*Targeted therapy renal effects*. Specific patterns from various drugs.

*Radiation*. Pelvic or abdominal radiation can affect kidney function.

*Surgical effects*. Nephrectomy reduces renal reserve.

Survivorship management:
- Annual creatinine and estimated GFR.
- BP management.
- Avoidance of additional nephrotoxic agents.
- Hydration awareness for survivors with reduced renal reserve.

---

## Gastrointestinal effects

*Surgical effects*. Bowel resections, ostomies, gastric resections all have long-term consequences.

*Radiation enteritis and proctitis*. Pelvic radiation causes chronic GI effects in some patients. Manifests as diarrhea, bleeding, strictures, malabsorption.

*Chemotherapy-induced enteritis*. Variable.

*Liver effects*. Various drugs and radiation can cause chronic liver effects.

Survivorship management:
- Nutritional support.
- Ostomy care.
- Management of radiation enteritis (medications, sometimes hyperbaric oxygen).
- Hepatic function monitoring.

## Bone health

Multiple factors affect bone health in survivors:

*Hormone-deprivation therapy*. Aromatase inhibitors (breast cancer), ADT (prostate cancer), GnRH agonists with chemotherapy — all accelerate bone loss.

*Steroids*. Long-term use causes osteoporosis.

*Radiation*. Radiation to bone-bearing fields affects bone structure and density.

*Chemotherapy-induced hypogonadism*. Affects bone metabolism.

*Immobility during treatment*. Contributes to bone loss.

Survivorship management:
- DEXA scans for survivors with risk factors.
- Calcium and vitamin D supplementation.
- Bisphosphonates or denosumab when indicated.
- Weight-bearing exercise.

## Secondary malignancies

Cancer survivors have elevated rates of subsequent cancers. The Childhood Cancer Survivor Study and other long-term cohorts have characterized the risks.

*Specific risks*:

*Radiation-associated*:
- *Breast cancer* after chest radiation (especially in young women treated for Hodgkin lymphoma).
- *Thyroid cancer* after neck radiation.
- *Sarcomas* in radiation fields.
- *Lung cancer* after chest radiation.
- *Various other site-specific cancers*.

*Chemotherapy-associated*:
- *Therapy-related AML and MDS*. After alkylating agents (typically 5-10 years post-treatment) or topoisomerase II inhibitors like etoposide (typically 2-5 years post-treatment).
- Various solid tumors.

*Hereditary syndrome-associated*. Patients with hereditary cancer syndromes face elevated risks for multiple cancers.

*HPV-associated cancers* in HPV-driven cancer survivors. Additional HPV-related cancers can occur.

*Surveillance and prevention*:

- *Risk-based screening*. Beyond standard population screening, survivors with specific exposure histories may need additional screening (e.g., earlier and more frequent breast cancer screening in women treated with chest radiation in adolescence).

- *Children's Oncology Group Long-Term Follow-Up Guidelines* provide structured recommendations for pediatric survivor surveillance.

- *Behavioral interventions*. Smoking cessation particularly important; sun protection; HPV vaccination; healthy lifestyle.

- *Specific cancer prevention*. Aspirin in selected populations, hormonal interventions in specific contexts.

The secondary cancer risk is one of the most important reasons that modern treatment protocols aim to reduce cumulative exposures while maintaining cure rates.

## What this chapter gives you

Cancer survivors face long-term physical effects across multiple organ systems. The framework of survivorship care addresses surveillance for cancer recurrence and second cancers, management of late effects, health promotion, psychosocial support, coordination of care, and patient education.

Cardiovascular effects are increasingly recognized as among the most consequential long-term effects. Anthracycline cardiotoxicity is cumulative and dose-related. Anti-HER2 therapy, anti-VEGF agents, various TKIs, immune checkpoint inhibitors, and radiation all have specific cardiac toxicities. Surveillance with echocardiography or cardiac MRI for at-risk survivors. Aggressive general cardiovascular risk factor management important for all survivors.

Endocrine effects from cranial radiation (growth hormone deficiency, central hypothyroidism, central adrenal insufficiency, hypogonadotropic hypogonadism), neck radiation (primary hypothyroidism, thyroid cancer), pelvic radiation (gonadal failure), and various chemotherapies. Multi-kinase inhibitors cause hypothyroidism. Immune checkpoint inhibitors cause various immune-mediated endocrinopathies. Annual thyroid function tests and other endocrine monitoring for at-risk survivors.

Fertility and reproductive effects require pre-treatment counseling and preservation strategies (sperm cryopreservation, oocyte/embryo cryopreservation, ovarian tissue cryopreservation). Sexual function often affected by cancer treatment and frequently underdiscussed.

Neurologic and cognitive effects include peripheral neuropathy (cumulative, often persistent), cognitive effects ("chemo brain" — affecting 30-50% of survivors with variable mechanisms), brain effects of CNS-directed therapy (more severe with cranial radiation), hearing loss (cisplatin), and other neurologic effects.

Pulmonary effects from bleomycin (cumulative fibrosis), other chemotherapy, radiation pneumonitis (acute and chronic), immune checkpoint inhibitor pneumonitis, EGFR inhibitor ILD, and surgical resections.

Renal effects from cisplatin and other nephrotoxic chemotherapy, radiation, and nephrectomy. Long-term renal function monitoring important.

Gastrointestinal effects include surgical sequelae, radiation enteritis/proctitis, chemotherapy effects, and liver effects.

Bone health affected by hormone-deprivation therapy, steroids, radiation, chemotherapy-induced hypogonadism, and immobility. DEXA monitoring and management with bisphosphonates or denosumab when indicated.

Secondary malignancies are a significant survivor risk — radiation-associated (breast, thyroid, sarcoma, lung), chemotherapy-associated (therapy-related AML/MDS), hereditary syndrome-associated, and HPV-associated. Risk-based surveillance and behavioral interventions.
