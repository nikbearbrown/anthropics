A cure that the patient cannot afford is no cure at all.

Hold the framing. Modern oncology has produced an unprecedented expansion of therapeutic options. Drugs that extend life by months in metastatic disease. Drugs that increase cure rates in adjuvant therapy. Drugs that transform diseases like CML, ALL, and many lymphomas from uniformly fatal to often curable. The biological achievement has been remarkable.

The economic cost has been remarkable too. A typical course of treatment for metastatic cancer can cost $150,000-300,000. A year of newer targeted therapies often exceeds $200,000. CAR-T cells cost $400,000-500,000 per infusion. Even with insurance, patient out-of-pocket costs can be substantial — sometimes financially devastating. Without insurance, many of these therapies are inaccessible. The dramatic improvements in cancer outcomes have been accompanied by dramatic increases in financial barriers to care.

This is one of the central paradoxes of modern oncology. We have more effective treatments than ever before, and many patients cannot access them. The disparity between what science can do and what economics permits has become a defining feature of the field. Patient assistance programs, insurance complexity, drug pricing negotiations, value-based care experiments — all are responses to this paradox, with mixed results.

This chapter — the first of two on health economics in cancer — covers drug pricing and affordability. The next — 38B — covers global disparities and broader ethical and policy considerations.

Hold the question: *given that modern cancer care is increasingly expensive, what determines who has access to it and what should we do about the inequities?*

## The cancer drug pricing problem

Cancer drug prices have risen dramatically over the past several decades. Specific patterns:

*Newer drug prices*. Cancer drugs approved in recent years routinely cost $100,000-300,000+ per year. The list price for many newer therapies is much higher than what most patients or insurance systems can afford without negotiation.

*Trajectory of pricing*. Cancer drug launch prices have increased roughly 10% per year on average for two decades, far outpacing inflation and clinical benefit. A 2010 cancer drug launched at $5,000-10,000 per month was considered expensive; today's average is closer to $15,000-25,000 per month.

*Old vs new pricing*. Older generic chemotherapy drugs are relatively cheap. Newer brand-name targeted therapies and immunotherapies are extremely expensive. The price-benefit relationship is variable — some expensive new drugs provide modest benefits over much cheaper alternatives.

*Combinations multiply costs*. Modern combination therapies (immunotherapy + chemotherapy, multiple targeted therapies, etc.) multiply costs. A treatment combination may exceed $400,000-500,000 per year.

*CAR-T and cellular therapy costs*. CAR-T cells cost $400,000-500,000 per infusion for the drug itself, plus hospitalization, monitoring, and complication management. Total costs frequently exceed $1 million for a single treatment course.

*Variations across countries*. Drug prices vary enormously by country. The same drug typically costs substantially less in countries that negotiate drug prices through national health systems. The US, with its largely free-market drug pricing, has the highest cancer drug prices globally.

The pricing pattern has been criticized as unsustainable. Multiple analyses have concluded that pricing has substantially exceeded the value provided (the relationship between price and clinical benefit has weakened over time).

## Why cancer drugs cost so much

Multiple factors contribute to high cancer drug prices:

*Development costs*. Bringing a new cancer drug to market involves substantial costs. R&D, preclinical studies, multiple phases of clinical trials, regulatory submissions. Estimates vary widely ($1-3 billion is often cited for successful drugs that include allocation of failed drug costs).

*Risk and failure rates*. Most drugs in development don't reach approval. The successful drugs must cover development costs of the failures. The cancer drug development success rate is around 5-10% from preclinical to approval.

*Pricing-to-what-the-market-will-bear*. In the US, drug companies set prices based on what insurance systems and patients can pay. No price negotiation occurs at the federal level for most drugs (though this is beginning to change with the Inflation Reduction Act).

*Patent protection and exclusivity*. New drugs have patent protection (typically ~20 years from filing, with patent restoration extending market exclusivity). Drug companies maximize pricing during exclusivity period.

*Lack of price negotiation*. The US Medicare program was historically prohibited from negotiating drug prices. The Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) of 2022 changed this for some drugs starting in 2026. Most countries with national health systems negotiate prices.

*Limited generic competition for some drugs*. Even when patent expires, biologics often face limited biosimilar competition. Cancer drugs are sometimes maintained as high-cost even years after generic availability.

*Distribution costs*. Specialty pharmacy distribution, complex logistics for some products (especially cellular therapies) add substantial costs.

*Marketing and administrative costs*. Significant components of drug company budgets.

*Profit margins*. Drug companies are for-profit and target substantial returns to investors.

The factors interact. Defenders of current pricing argue that high prices are necessary to fund innovation; critics argue that pricing has exceeded what's needed for sustainable innovation and produces substantial inequities.

## Insurance and reimbursement systems

Different countries have different approaches to drug pricing and coverage:

*United States*. Largely free-market pricing with multiple payers. Major payers include:
- *Medicare*. Federal health insurance for 65+ and selected younger patients with disabilities. Covers ~50% of cancer patients in US.
- *Medicaid*. State-federal program for low-income patients.
- *Commercial insurance*. Through employers or individual purchase. ACA marketplaces.
- *VA*. Veterans Affairs health system.

Each negotiates separately. Drug pricing varies based on negotiations. Patients face copays, coinsurance, deductibles that vary by plan. Out-of-pocket maximums (per ACA) limit catastrophic costs but can still be substantial.

The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 has begun changing this:
- Medicare drug price negotiation for selected drugs starting 2026.
- Out-of-pocket cap for Medicare Part D ($2,000 starting 2025).
- Insulin price cap.
- Other provisions.

*European Union and similar systems*. National or regional health systems negotiate prices. Drug companies must negotiate to enter markets. NICE in the UK, HAS in France, IQWiG in Germany, and similar bodies in other countries assess clinical value and cost-effectiveness to inform coverage decisions.

*Australia*. Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS) provides government-subsidized drug coverage. Drugs must demonstrate cost-effectiveness to be listed.

*Canada*. Patented Medicine Prices Review Board regulates drug prices. Provincial coverage varies.

*Other countries*. Diverse approaches with substantial variation in drug availability and patient costs.

The pricing differences across systems result in dramatic disparities in drug availability and patient out-of-pocket costs. The same drug may cost $200,000 in the US, $80,000 in many European countries, and be entirely unavailable in many low- and middle-income countries.

## The patient experience of cancer drug costs

The financial impact on cancer patients is substantial:

*Out-of-pocket costs*. Even with insurance, patients face:
- Deductibles before insurance kicks in.
- Copays and coinsurance throughout treatment.
- Specialty drug tier copays.
- Out-of-network charges.
- Procedures and tests not covered.

Annual out-of-pocket costs for cancer treatment frequently exceed $5,000-10,000 even with good insurance, and can be much higher.

*Financial toxicity*. As discussed in 37B, the financial burden of cancer care is itself harmful:
- Bankruptcy rates 2-3x higher in cancer patients than general population.
- Worse treatment adherence (skipping doses, splitting pills, abandoning treatment).
- Worse quality of life.
- Possible worse mortality.

*Sources of patient financial assistance*:

*Manufacturer patient assistance programs*. Most drug manufacturers offer programs for uninsured or underinsured patients. Substantial programs provide drugs free or at reduced cost for qualifying patients. Eligibility based on income.

*Foundation assistance*. Multiple foundations provide cancer-related financial assistance. Patient Advocate Foundation, CancerCare, Leukemia & Lymphoma Society, Susan G. Komen, others.

*Hospital charity care*. Many hospitals have charity care policies for uninsured or low-income patients.

*Insurance navigation services*. Help patients understand and access insurance benefits.

*Disease-specific organizations*. Many cancer-specific groups provide financial assistance, navigation, or advocacy.

The financial assistance infrastructure is substantial but fragmented. Many patients don't access available resources due to lack of awareness or navigation difficulty. Some patients experience financial toxicity despite available resources due to gaps in coverage or specific situations not addressed by available programs.

## Cost-effectiveness analysis

Cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) is the systematic evaluation of how much a treatment costs relative to the clinical benefit provided.

*Standard CEA approach*:
- *Quality-adjusted life years (QALYs)*. Combine life expectancy with quality of life. 1 QALY = 1 year in perfect health.
- *Incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER)*. Cost per QALY gained from a new treatment compared to standard.
- *Cost-effectiveness threshold*. The "willingness to pay" threshold used to judge whether a treatment is cost-effective. Often $50,000-150,000 per QALY in US analyses; lower in some other countries.

*Application to cancer drugs*. Many newer cancer drugs have ICERs exceeding $200,000 per QALY — beyond traditional cost-effectiveness thresholds. The Institute for Clinical and Economic Review (ICER) in the US has assessed many cancer drugs and frequently found that prices substantially exceed value.

*International cost-effectiveness frameworks*:
- NICE in the UK uses thresholds typically £20,000-30,000 per QALY (about $25,000-40,000), with flexibility for specific conditions.
- PBS in Australia uses similar approach.
- Many other systems use cost-effectiveness analysis.

*US perspective*. The US has been resistant to formal cost-effectiveness frameworks in drug coverage decisions. Concerns about "rationing care," uncomfortable association with denying treatment, and political dynamics have limited formal CEA use. This is beginning to change with IRA Medicare negotiations and other developments.

*Criticisms of CEA*:
- Reduces complex clinical situations to single numbers.
- May undervalue innovative drugs that provide modest benefits to many patients.
- Doesn't capture all aspects of treatment value.
- Methodological challenges in measuring quality of life.

*Defenses of CEA*:
- Provides systematic framework for resource allocation.
- Forces explicit consideration of value.
- Identifies treatments with substantially poor value relative to alternatives.
- Used effectively in many other countries.

The tension between recognizing constrained resources and providing the best possible cancer care remains unresolved in the US system.

## Value-based care experiments

Various approaches aim to better align drug prices with value:

*Outcomes-based contracts*. Manufacturers receive different reimbursement based on patient outcomes. If patients respond well, full payment; if not, reduced payment. Several implementations in oncology.

*Indication-based pricing*. Same drug priced differently for different indications based on the value provided. Implementation challenges include the complexity of tracking patient indications.

*Risk-sharing agreements*. Manufacturers share financial risk if drugs don't achieve expected outcomes.

*Reference pricing*. Setting drug prices based on prices for similar drugs or alternative treatments.

*Bundled payments*. Single payment for an episode of cancer care rather than fee-for-service.

*Medicare's Oncology Care Model (OCM)*. Aimed to align incentives in oncology. Mixed results. Successor models being developed.

These approaches have had mixed success. The complexity of cancer care, difficulty measuring outcomes, and resistance from various stakeholders have limited their impact.

## Drug shortages

Beyond high prices, cancer treatment is affected by drug shortages of older generic drugs:

*Recent major shortages*. Cisplatin and carboplatin shortages in 2023 affected millions of patients globally. Methotrexate and other essential agents have had shortages. The shortages have caused real harm — treatment delays, regimen substitutions with potentially inferior alternatives, patient anxiety.

*Causes of shortages*:
- Manufacturing problems (FDA inspections, quality issues, plant closures).
- Limited number of manufacturers (consolidation has reduced redundancy).
- Low profit margins for generics making them less attractive to produce.
- Supply chain vulnerabilities (especially for drugs manufactured in single locations).
- Quality control failures.

*Responses*:
- FDA expedited review of new manufacturers.
- Importation from foreign approved facilities.
- Reformulation when needed.
- Rationing and prioritization protocols at hospitals.
- Policy proposals for supply chain redundancy and incentives for generic manufacturing.

The shortages illustrate that even cheap, established cancer drugs can become inaccessible. The fragility of the generic drug supply chain has been a wake-up call. Policy responses are developing but slowly.

## Specialty drug pharmacy and access

Cancer drugs are increasingly distributed through specialty pharmacy channels rather than traditional retail or hospital pharmacies:

*Specialty pharmacy characteristics*:
- Handle complex, high-cost drugs.
- Cold chain management, specific storage.
- Patient education and adherence support.
- Coordination with insurance and patient assistance programs.

*Issues*:
- Limited number of specialty pharmacies providing specific drugs.
- "Restricted distribution" programs for some drugs.
- Coordination challenges with hospital systems.
- Geographic access issues.

*Hub services*. Drug companies often provide "hub" services that coordinate patient access, insurance verification, prior authorization, and patient assistance for their drugs. Substantial infrastructure but adds complexity.

The specialty pharmacy ecosystem has developed substantially over recent decades. It enables access to complex therapies but adds layers of administration that affect patients.

## Approaches to reducing cancer drug costs

Various approaches aim to reduce cancer drug costs:

*Generic and biosimilar competition*. As drugs lose patent protection, competition from generics (for small molecules) and biosimilars (for biologics) reduces prices. Biosimilar entry for several cancer biologics (trastuzumab, rituximab, bevacizumab) has reduced their prices substantially. Generic entry for older targeted therapies (imatinib became generic in 2016 and prices dropped substantially).

*Importation*. Limited authorized importation from countries with lower drug prices. Politically controversial.

*Medicare price negotiation*. Beginning 2026 under the IRA. Initial drugs selected for negotiation include cancer drugs (Imbruvica/ibrutinib, others).

*International reference pricing*. Setting US prices in relation to prices in other developed countries. Various proposals; political resistance.

*Pharmacy benefit manager (PBM) reform*. PBMs are intermediaries between drug manufacturers and insurance plans. Their practices have been controversial; reform proposals could affect pricing.

*Patent reform*. Limiting patent extensions and "evergreening" strategies that maintain patent protection beyond original innovation.

*Government-funded drug development*. Some proposals would have government funding play a larger role in drug development, with resulting drugs available at lower prices.

*Compulsory licensing*. In some emergency situations, governments can allow generic production of patented drugs. Limited use in cancer but a tool.

*Hospital and provider purchasing*. Group purchasing organizations negotiate prices. 340B program in US provides discounts to qualifying providers.

Each approach has limitations and faces political and industry resistance. The overall trajectory has been increasing cancer drug costs despite various interventions.

## What this chapter gives you
