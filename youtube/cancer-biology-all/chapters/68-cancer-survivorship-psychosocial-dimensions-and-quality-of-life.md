The hardest part of having cancer might be the part that comes after you don't have it anymore.

Hold the framing. Patients in active cancer treatment have something concrete to focus on. There are appointments. There are scans. There are decisions about therapy. There are conversations about prognosis. The disease is in the foreground. The structure is provided by the medical care.

When treatment ends — when the patient is told they're cured, or in remission, or done with active therapy — that structure dissolves. The patient is told to "live their life" again, but their life has been irreversibly changed. Their body is different. Their relationships are different. Their priorities are different. Their identity has been rearranged. And underneath everything is the awareness that the cancer might come back — and that this time, treatment might not work.

This experience of survivorship has been described by patients as profoundly difficult and underrecognized. The medical system that intensively cared for them during treatment often steps back during survivorship. Friends and family may assume that "cured" means "back to normal." The survivor may feel pressure to perform recovery, to demonstrate gratitude, to move on. The complex emotional and psychological reality of being a cancer survivor is often invisible to those around them.

This chapter — the second of two on cancer survivorship — covers the psychosocial dimensions of being a cancer survivor: emotional and psychological aspects, social and relational issues, work and finances, rehabilitation, reintegration, and quality of life considerations. The chapter complements 37A, which covered physical late effects.

Hold the question: *given that surviving cancer is its own challenge separate from having it, how do we support survivors in living well after their disease?*

## The emotional and psychological landscape of survivorship

Survivorship brings specific psychological challenges that may differ from those during active treatment:

*Fear of recurrence*. The most pervasive survivorship issue. Affects essentially all survivors to some degree. Manifests as:
- Anxiety around surveillance appointments and tests.
- Heightened attention to physical symptoms (interpreting normal symptoms as possible cancer return).
- Worry about prognosis and "What if?" thinking.
- Avoidance of cancer-related reminders.

Severity varies enormously. Some survivors experience mild background concern; others have significant anxiety that impairs function. Time generally reduces fear of recurrence but it rarely disappears entirely.

Management:
- Education about realistic recurrence risk.
- Cognitive behavioral therapy specifically for fear of recurrence (FORT, ConquerFear, others have evidence).
- Mindfulness-based approaches.
- Acceptance-based approaches.
- Connection with other survivors.

*Post-treatment depression*. Affects 15-25% of survivors. Can manifest unexpectedly when treatment ends. May reflect:
- Loss of treatment structure and the support of the cancer care team.
- Realization of the cumulative impact of the cancer experience.
- Loss of identity associated with being "in treatment."
- Survivor's guilt (when others didn't survive).
- Persistent physical symptoms that limit return to prior function.

Treatment with antidepressants, psychotherapy, support groups, exercise.

*Anxiety disorders*. Generalized anxiety, panic disorder, specific phobias related to cancer (claustrophobia in scanners, needle phobia, others). Treatment with CBT, SSRIs/SNRIs, exposure therapies as appropriate.

*Post-traumatic stress*. Cancer experience can produce PTSD-like symptoms. Re-experiencing through intrusive thoughts and dreams. Avoidance of cancer reminders. Hyperarousal and hypervigilance. Negative cognitive and mood changes. Particularly common after particularly intensive treatments, traumatic events during treatment, or major life disruptions. Treatment with trauma-focused therapies (EMDR, prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy).

*Identity and meaning*. Cancer often profoundly affects identity. "Cancer survivor" becomes part of self-concept. Some survivors integrate this positively; others struggle with it. Meaning-making work can be valuable. Existential concerns about purpose, legacy, mortality may be heightened.

*Survivor's guilt*. Why did I survive when others didn't? Particularly common when patients have shared cancer journeys with others (support groups, treatment cohorts) who have died.

*Body image*. After surgery (mastectomy, ostomy, limb amputation), hair loss, weight changes, scarring, lymphedema — body image can be substantially affected. Affects intimate relationships and self-image.

*Sexual function*. Often affected by treatment and emotional aspects. Underdiscussed in survivorship. Specific interventions (specialized sex therapy, pharmacologic approaches, devices) can help.

*Cognitive concerns*. Beyond measurable cognitive impairment, survivors often report subjective cognitive complaints that affect work and daily life. Validating these experiences and providing support is important.

## Social and relational impacts

Cancer affects relationships in complex ways:

*Family relationships*. Spouses, partners, children, parents — all affected. Some relationships strengthen through the cancer experience; others strain. Communication challenges around the cancer, asymmetric experiences (survivor vs. caregiver), changes in roles and responsibilities.

*Caregiver effects*. Family members and friends who provided care often have lasting effects. Caregiver burden during treatment can extend into survivorship. Post-cancer caregiver syndrome with depression, anxiety, physical effects. Caregiver support is part of comprehensive survivorship care.

*Friendships*. Some friends provide tremendous support; others can't handle the cancer experience and withdraw. Social networks often reshape during and after cancer.

*Dating and new relationships*. Cancer history can be challenging in new relationships. When and how to disclose. Concerns about fertility, sexual function, body image. Specific issues for adolescent and young adult survivors.

*Children of survivors*. Long-term effects on children whose parents had cancer. Worry about parental mortality. Sometimes overprotective. Sometimes anxiety persisting into adulthood.

*Community*. Connection with cancer community can be valuable but also a reminder. Some survivors stay engaged with cancer advocacy; others want distance from cancer-related activities.

*Workplace and professional life*. Re-entering work after cancer treatment can be challenging. Disclosure to employers and colleagues. Accommodations. Stigma. Changed capacity. Some survivors return to prior work; others change directions.

The social impacts are real but variable. Some survivors describe their cancer experience as profoundly positive overall — strengthened relationships, clarified priorities, deeper appreciation for life. Others describe primarily negative experiences. Most have mixed experiences with both positive and negative elements.

## Financial impacts and "financial toxicity"

The economic dimension of cancer has become increasingly recognized as a form of toxicity affecting survivors.

*Direct cancer costs*:
- Out-of-pocket costs even with insurance (copayments, deductibles, non-covered expenses).
- Drug costs (especially with new expensive therapies).
- Travel and accommodation for treatment.

*Indirect costs*:
- Lost wages during treatment.
- Reduced earning capacity afterward (some survivors can't return to prior work).
- Disability for those unable to return to work.

*Insurance issues*:
- Loss of insurance with job loss.
- Pre-existing condition concerns (largely addressed in US by ACA).
- Coverage gaps for specific treatments.
- High deductibles and out-of-pocket maximums.

*Long-term financial effects*:
- Reduced retirement savings due to treatment costs and lost wages.
- Increased insurance premiums.
- Bankruptcy (cancer patients have 2.6x higher bankruptcy rates than general population in US).
- Reduced wealth accumulation throughout post-cancer life.

*Effects on outcomes*. Patients experiencing financial toxicity have:
- Worse adherence to treatment (skipping doses, splitting pills, delaying refills).
- Worse quality of life.
- Higher symptom burden.
- Possibly worse mortality.

*Management*:
- Financial counseling as part of cancer care.
- Patient assistance programs from manufacturers, foundations, hospitals.
- Insurance navigation.
- Reducing low-value care.
- Policy advocacy for coverage improvements.

Financial toxicity is increasingly recognized as a clinically important issue requiring systematic attention.

## Rehabilitation in cancer survivorship

Cancer rehabilitation addresses the functional consequences of cancer and its treatment.

*Physical therapy*. For:
- Lymphedema management.
- Post-surgical mobility and strength.
- Balance issues from neuropathy.
- General reconditioning after deconditioning during treatment.
- Specific functional limitations from radiation or surgery.

*Occupational therapy*. For:
- Activities of daily living difficulties.
- Return-to-work challenges.
- Adaptive equipment.
- Cognitive rehabilitation strategies.
- Energy conservation techniques.

*Speech-language pathology*. For:
- Communication issues after head and neck cancer surgery or laryngeal cancer.
- Swallowing difficulties.
- Cognitive-linguistic concerns.

*Cardiac rehabilitation*. For survivors with cardiovascular effects of treatment.

*Pulmonary rehabilitation*. For survivors with significant pulmonary effects.

*Pelvic floor therapy*. For survivors with pelvic dysfunction from prostate, gynecologic, or colorectal cancer treatment.

*Lymphedema management*. Specialized therapy including manual lymphatic drainage, compression garments, exercises, sometimes surgical interventions (lymphaticovenular anastomosis, lymph node transfer).

*Sexual rehabilitation*. Specialized counseling, pharmacologic treatments, devices, partner involvement.

*Vocational rehabilitation*. Helping survivors return to work or transition to new vocational activities.

*Survivorship-specific rehabilitation programs*. Increasingly available at major cancer centers. Integrate multiple disciplines for comprehensive survivor needs.

The cancer rehabilitation field has substantial evidence supporting its interventions but is underutilized. Many survivors don't access rehabilitation services that could improve their function and quality of life. Insurance coverage varies. Awareness among oncology providers about rehabilitation resources is limited in many settings.

## Reintegration into ordinary life

After cancer treatment, returning to "ordinary life" is its own process:

*Resuming work*. Some survivors return to full prior work; others need accommodations or modifications. Some change to less demanding work. Some retire earlier than planned. Some find their work has become unsatisfying after the cancer experience and make career changes.

Workplace accommodations may be needed:
- Reduced hours (especially initially).
- Flexible scheduling for medical appointments.
- Modified duties.
- Accommodation for fatigue, cognitive effects, or physical limitations.

The Americans with Disabilities Act and similar legislation in other countries provides protections, though implementation varies.

*Resuming family roles*. Caregivers who took on additional responsibilities during treatment may continue them or transition responsibilities back. Children's experiences during the cancer treatment shape their needs going forward.

*Resuming social activities*. Some social activities are easier to resume; others may be permanently changed. Travel, sports, social events.

*Health behaviors*. Survivorship is often a time of renewed focus on healthy lifestyle:
- Exercise. Substantial evidence of benefit for cancer outcomes and general health.
- Diet. Generally healthful patterns; specific cancer-specific recommendations for some cancers (low-fat for breast cancer, plant-based for various cancers).
- Smoking cessation (essential).
- Limited alcohol.
- Sun protection.
- Sleep hygiene.

*Cancer-related behaviors*:
- Self-examination practices (skin, breast).
- Adherence to surveillance schedules.
- Vaccination (HPV vaccination for survivors not previously vaccinated; recommended vaccines).

*Survivorship as new normal*. Many survivors describe finding a new equilibrium that is neither the pre-cancer life nor the cancer-treatment period. The "new normal" incorporates ongoing health concerns, treatment effects, modified priorities, and continued life trajectory.

## Quality of life assessment

Systematic assessment of quality of life is increasingly part of survivorship care.

*Quality of life domains*:
- *Physical*. Symptoms, function, energy.
- *Emotional*. Mood, anxiety, distress.
- *Social*. Relationships, social activities.
- *Functional*. Activities of daily living, work, hobbies.
- *Existential/spiritual*. Meaning, purpose, peace.

*Assessment tools*:
- *FACT* (Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy) and disease-specific subscales.
- *EORTC QLQ-C30* and modules.
- *SF-36* and SF-12 generic health measures.
- *PROMIS* (Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System).

*Patient-reported outcomes integration*. Increasingly, cancer care incorporates regular collection of patient-reported outcomes. Multiple studies have shown that systematic PRO collection during cancer treatment improves outcomes (Basch et al., 2017 JAMA). The approach is extending into survivorship.

The shift toward patient-reported outcomes recognizes that traditional clinical assessment misses important aspects of the survivor experience. Validated PRO measures provide structured ways to track survivor concerns and identify those needing additional support.

## Special populations in survivorship

Different survivor populations face specific challenges:

*Pediatric cancer survivors* (covered in 34B). Decades of life ahead. Specific late effects. Specialized long-term follow-up needed.

*Adolescent and young adult survivors*. Cancer interrupts critical life stages (education, career development, relationship formation, family planning). AYA survivor programs address specific needs.

*Older survivors*. Cancer in the context of multiple comorbidities. Cancer treatment effects compound age-related issues. Functional decline acceleration.

*Long-term survivors* (>5 years from treatment). Continued late effects management. Different needs from short-term survivors.

*Metastatic disease survivors*. Patients living with cancer as a chronic condition. Different from cured survivors. Ongoing treatment with its own effects. Uncertainty about future. Some metastatic disease patients live many years with controlled disease.

*Cancer survivors with disabilities*. Pre-existing disabilities affect cancer treatment and survivorship. Cancer-related disabilities may compound existing conditions.

*Survivors with mental illness*. Specific challenges in coordinating mental health and cancer care.

*Survivors with limited English proficiency*. Language barriers in survivorship care.

*Underserved survivors*. Racial/ethnic minority survivors, low-income survivors, rural survivors all face additional challenges. Survivorship care disparities exist alongside treatment disparities.

Each population has specific needs that survivorship programs increasingly try to address.

## Survivorship care delivery models

Multiple models exist for delivering survivorship care:

*Oncology-led survivorship*. Survivors continue follow-up with their oncology team. Advantage: continuity, oncology expertise. Disadvantages: limits oncology capacity for new patients, late effects expertise may be limited.

*Specialized survivorship clinics*. Dedicated programs with multidisciplinary teams. Advantage: focused expertise. Disadvantages: separate from cancer treatment team, geographic access varies.

*Primary care-based survivorship*. Survivors return to primary care for routine surveillance. Advantage: capacity, holistic care. Disadvantages: primary care provider expertise varies, communication challenges.

*Shared care models*. Oncology and primary care share responsibility. Advantage: distributes workload appropriately. Disadvantages: coordination challenges.

*Risk-stratified models*. High-risk survivors get specialty follow-up; low-risk return to primary care. Most rational approach but implementation challenges.

*Survivorship care plans* are documents intended to facilitate communication between oncology and primary care. Implementation has been variable; their value depends on integration into clinical workflows.

The optimal model depends on the survivor's specific situation, resources available, and health system context. No single approach works for all situations.

## Specific survivor concerns by cancer type

Some cancers have particular survivorship considerations:

*Breast cancer survivors*. Hormonal therapy side effects (hot flashes, bone effects, mood). Lymphedema. Body image and reconstruction issues. Fertility after treatment. Risk of contralateral breast cancer. Specific surveillance for hormonal therapy-related effects.

*Prostate cancer survivors*. Urinary, sexual, bowel function. Hormonal therapy effects (hot flashes, bone effects, metabolic effects, cognitive effects). Body composition changes.

*Colorectal cancer survivors*. Bowel function changes. Ostomy management. Diet adjustments. Surveillance for recurrence and second primaries.

*Lung cancer survivors*. Pulmonary function. Smoking cessation. Continued surveillance.

*Head and neck cancer survivors*. Speech, swallowing, dental health. Cosmetic concerns. Persistent symptoms (xerostomia, others).

*Childhood cancer survivors*. Comprehensive long-term effects management. Educational and developmental considerations. Fertility. Specific second cancer risks.

*Hematologic malignancy survivors*. Long-term immunologic effects. Stem cell transplant late effects (GVHD, organ effects, secondary cancers).

*Brain cancer survivors*. Cognitive and functional effects. Endocrine effects from radiation. Quality of life often more affected than overall survival improvement.

Each cancer type's survivor population has specific needs that survivorship programs address.

## Looking forward

The future of cancer survivorship involves:

*Better risk stratification*. Identifying which survivors need intensive follow-up versus those who can transition to primary care.

*Personalized late effects prediction*. Using genetic and other factors to predict individual risk for specific late effects.

*Better interventions*. For specific late effects (cardiotoxicity, cognitive impairment, fatigue, others).

*Digital health tools*. Apps, wearables, remote monitoring to support survivors and detect issues.

*Survivor-centered research*. Including survivor perspectives in research priorities and outcomes.

*Policy advances*. Insurance coverage, workplace protections, financial assistance.

*Workforce development*. Training healthcare providers in survivorship care, both within oncology and primary care.

*Global survivorship*. As cancer outcomes improve globally, survivorship infrastructure needs to develop in low- and middle-income countries.

The cancer survivor population continues to grow as more patients survive cancer. Survivorship will remain a major area of growth in oncology over coming decades.

## What this chapter gives you

The psychosocial dimensions of cancer survivorship are substantial and often underrecognized. Common challenges include fear of recurrence (essentially universal, varying severity), post-treatment depression, anxiety disorders, post-traumatic stress, identity and meaning concerns, survivor's guilt, body image issues, sexual function challenges, and subjective cognitive concerns. Each has specific evidence-based interventions when severe enough to require treatment.

Social and relational impacts affect families, caregivers, friendships, dating relationships, children of survivors, community connections, and workplace life. The impacts vary widely — some survivors experience profoundly positive changes; others experience primarily negative effects; most have mixed experiences.

Financial toxicity is increasingly recognized as clinically important. Direct cancer costs, indirect costs (lost wages, reduced earning capacity), insurance issues, and long-term financial effects all contribute. Financial toxicity affects treatment adherence, quality of life, and possibly survival. Management includes financial counseling, patient assistance programs, insurance navigation, and policy advocacy.

Cancer rehabilitation addresses functional consequences through physical therapy, occupational therapy, speech-language pathology, cardiac rehabilitation, pulmonary rehabilitation, pelvic floor therapy, lymphedema management, sexual rehabilitation, and vocational rehabilitation. The field has substantial evidence but is underutilized.

Reintegration involves resuming work (often with accommodations), family roles, social activities; adopting healthy behaviors (exercise, diet, smoking cessation); engaging in cancer-related self-management (surveillance adherence, self-examinations); and finding a "new normal" that incorporates the cancer experience.

Quality of life assessment using validated tools (FACT, EORTC QLQ-C30, SF-36, PROMIS) provides structured measurement. Systematic patient-reported outcomes collection improves outcomes in cancer care.

Special populations include pediatric, AYA, older, long-term, metastatic disease, disabled, mentally ill, limited English proficient, and underserved survivors. Each faces specific challenges.

Survivorship care delivery models include oncology-led, specialized clinics, primary care-based, shared care, and risk-stratified approaches. Optimal model varies by situation. Survivorship care plans facilitate transitions between providers.

Specific survivor concerns vary by cancer type — breast, prostate, colorectal, lung, head and neck, childhood, hematologic malignancy, brain — each with particular issues requiring specialized expertise.

The future of cancer survivorship involves better risk stratification, personalized predictions, improved interventions for specific late effects, digital health tools, survivor-centered research, policy advances, workforce development, and global expansion.
