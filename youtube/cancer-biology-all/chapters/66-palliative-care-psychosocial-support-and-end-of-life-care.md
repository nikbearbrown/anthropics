The patients in cancer trials who do best are the ones in the palliative care arm.

Hold the framing. In 2010, Jennifer Temel and colleagues published a landmark trial in the New England Journal of Medicine. Patients with newly diagnosed metastatic non-small-cell lung cancer were randomized to either standard oncology care alone or standard oncology care plus early integration of palliative care. The palliative care arm showed better quality of life, less depression, less aggressive end-of-life care, and — most strikingly — longer overall survival despite receiving less intensive cancer treatment near the end of life.

The trial reframed how oncology thinks about palliative care. Once considered something to introduce when active treatment was no longer effective — almost a "giving up" — palliative care is now understood as care that should be integrated from the start of cancer treatment, alongside disease-directed therapy. The discipline focuses on quality of life, symptom management, and patient-centered decision-making. When done well, it doesn't shorten life; it often extends it. And it dramatically improves the experience of cancer treatment for patients and families.

This chapter — the second of two on supportive and palliative care — covers the psychosocial dimensions of cancer care, the principles of palliative care, and end-of-life care. The chapter complements 36A, which covered symptom management and nutritional support.

Hold the question: *given that cancer affects not just bodies but lives, families, and communities, how do we provide care that addresses the whole person?*

## The psychosocial impact of cancer

Cancer affects more than the body. The diagnosis is one of the most psychologically and socially disruptive events many people experience.

*Common psychological responses*:

*Acute distress*. Initial reactions at diagnosis include shock, disbelief, anxiety, fear, anger. Most patients experience significant distress in the days to weeks after diagnosis.

*Anxiety*. Pervasive throughout the cancer experience. Anxiety about diagnosis, treatment, prognosis, recurrence, family impact. About 20-40% of patients meet criteria for anxiety disorders during cancer treatment.

*Depression*. Affects about 15-25% of patients. Higher in advanced disease. May be missed by clinicians focused on physical symptoms. Both situational depression and clinical major depression can occur. Important to distinguish from cancer-related fatigue or symptom-related dysphoria.

*Adjustment disorders*. Difficulty adjusting to cancer-related changes. Common and may not meet criteria for specific disorders.

*Post-traumatic stress*. Cancer experience can produce PTSD-like symptoms, particularly after intensive treatment.

*Demoralization*. Loss of meaning, hopelessness, sense of failure. Distinct from depression and important to recognize.

*Fear of recurrence*. Pervasive among cancer survivors. Major source of ongoing distress even after successful treatment.

*Cognitive symptoms*. "Chemo brain" — cognitive impairment from cancer or its treatment. Memory, attention, processing speed. Often persistent.

*Body image issues*. From surgery, hair loss, weight changes, ostomy, mastectomy, others. Substantial impact on identity and relationships.

*Sexual dysfunction*. From surgery, hormonal therapy, chemotherapy, radiation. Often underdiscussed.

*Social impacts*:

*Relationships*. Marriages, partnerships, friendships all affected. Some strengthen; some strain. Communication challenges around cancer.

*Family roles*. Sick family members may not be able to fulfill prior roles. Caregivers take on new responsibilities. Children of patients have specific concerns and may need support.

*Work and finances*. Cancer treatment disrupts work. Loss of income compounded by treatment costs. Financial toxicity is increasingly recognized as a significant aspect of cancer treatment.

*Identity*. Cancer becomes part of identity, sometimes dominantly. "Cancer patient," "survivor" — labels that affect self-concept.

*Existential and spiritual concerns*:

*Meaning-making*. Patients often seek meaning in cancer experience. Questions about purpose, legacy, mortality.

*Spirituality and religion*. Important for many patients. May provide comfort or be a source of distress.

*Mortality awareness*. Cancer often forces engagement with mortality. Reactions vary widely.

Comprehensive cancer care must address these dimensions.

## Psychosocial assessment and intervention

*Systematic distress screening*. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network recommends systematic distress screening for all cancer patients. The "distress thermometer" (rating distress 0-10 with a problem list) is a brief tool. Patients with significant distress (typically >4) warrant further evaluation.

*Specific psychiatric assessment*. For patients with significant symptoms — structured assessment for depression (PHQ-9), anxiety (GAD-7), other specific concerns.

*Multidisciplinary support team*. Cancer centers have psychosocial oncology programs typically including:
- *Social workers*. Often the first-line for psychosocial issues. Practical support, navigation, family support, counseling.
- *Psychologists and psychiatrists*. For specific psychiatric conditions and psychotherapy.
- *Chaplains*. Spiritual care.
- *Patient navigators*. Help patients access resources and coordinate care.
- *Support groups*. Disease-specific or general cancer groups. Peer support is valuable for many patients.

*Psychotherapeutic interventions*:

*Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)*. Effective for cancer-related anxiety, depression, fatigue, sleep disorders, pain.

*Mindfulness-based interventions*. Mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) and other approaches. Evidence of benefit for multiple cancer-related outcomes.

*Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT)*. For chronic conditions including cancer.

*Meaning-centered psychotherapy*. Specifically developed for cancer patients facing advanced disease.

*Dignity therapy*. Brief intervention focused on legacy and meaning for advanced cancer patients.

*Couples and family therapy*. For cancer-related relationship and family issues.

*Pharmacotherapy*:

*Antidepressants*. SSRIs, SNRIs, mirtazapine, others. Specific drug selection considers interactions with cancer treatments. Tamoxifen and paroxetine/fluoxetine interaction (CYP2D6) is one concern. Mirtazapine useful for patients with insomnia and weight loss.

*Anxiolytics*. SSRIs for chronic anxiety. Benzodiazepines for acute situations, with care given dependence concerns.

*Sleep medications*. For specific insomnia. Trazodone, melatonin, others.

*Stimulants*. Methylphenidate sometimes used for fatigue, cognitive impairment.

*Other supportive interventions*:

*Exercise*. Effects on mood and fatigue.

*Yoga, tai chi, qigong*. Mind-body practices with evidence of benefit.

*Music therapy, art therapy*. Selected applications.

*Acupuncture*. For some symptoms.

*Support groups*. Disease-specific or general. Peer support valuable for many.

The integration of psychosocial care with cancer treatment is increasingly emphasized. Cancer centers are evaluated partly on psychosocial care quality.

## What palliative care is

The understanding of palliative care has evolved substantially. Modern definition (WHO):

*Palliative care* is an approach that improves quality of life for patients and families facing problems associated with life-threatening illness, through prevention and relief of suffering by means of early identification, impeccable assessment, and treatment of pain and other physical, psychosocial, and spiritual problems.

Key features:
- *Applies throughout cancer trajectory*. Not just end-of-life care.
- *Provided alongside disease-directed treatment*. Not an alternative to active treatment.
- *Focuses on quality of life*. Symptom management, function, well-being.
- *Multidimensional*. Physical, psychological, social, spiritual aspects.
- *Patient and family-centered*. Patient values and family context central.
- *Interdisciplinary*. Physician, nurse, social worker, chaplain, others.

*Distinction from hospice*. Hospice is a specific Medicare benefit (in the US) for patients with prognosis ≤6 months who are foregoing curative-intent treatment. Palliative care is broader — applies to any patient with serious illness, at any stage, alongside any treatment.

*Models of palliative care delivery*:

*Primary palliative care*. Basic palliative care skills practiced by the primary treatment team (oncologists, primary care physicians, nurses). All clinicians caring for cancer patients should have some palliative care competence.

*Specialty palliative care*. Specialized palliative care teams. Often consulted for complex cases — refractory symptoms, complex psychosocial issues, advance care planning, transitions of care.

*Inpatient palliative care consultation*. Hospital-based specialty consultation.

*Outpatient palliative care*. Increasingly available at major cancer centers.

*Community palliative care*. Home-based palliative care services.

*Hospice*. Specific program for end-of-life care.

The 2010 Temel trial mentioned earlier established the value of *early integrated palliative care*. Patients newly diagnosed with metastatic NSCLC who received palliative care alongside oncology care from the start had better outcomes than those who received palliative care only when standard cancer treatment failed. Subsequent trials have confirmed the value of early palliative care across multiple cancer types.

The shift in oncology thinking has been substantial. Major cancer centers now have outpatient palliative care clinics integrated with their oncology programs. Many institutions automatically refer patients with metastatic disease for palliative care assessment at diagnosis.

## The components of palliative care

Specialty palliative care addresses multiple dimensions:

*Symptom management*. The most concrete component. Pain, nausea, dyspnea, fatigue, and other symptoms. Palliative care specialists have particular expertise in refractory symptoms and complex symptom combinations.

*Communication and advance care planning*:
- *Goals of care discussions*. What is the patient hoping to achieve? What are the trade-offs they're willing to make?
- *Advance directives*. Documents specifying preferences for future care if patient becomes unable to decide.
- *POLST/MOLST forms* (Physician/Medical Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment). Translate advance directives into actionable medical orders.
- *Durable power of attorney for healthcare*. Designates surrogate decision-maker.
- *Discussions about CPR, mechanical ventilation, artificial nutrition, dialysis, ICU admission*. As specific scenarios become more likely.

*Caregiver support*. Cancer affects families. Caregivers experience significant burden and distress. Specific support for caregivers improves both patient and caregiver outcomes.

*Coordination of care*. Across specialists, settings (inpatient, outpatient, home, hospice), and over time.

*Transitions*. From active treatment to symptom-focused care. From outpatient to hospice. From hospital to home. Each transition requires thoughtful management.

*Spiritual and existential care*. Chaplains and other spiritual care providers. Important for many patients.

*Psychological care*. Coordinated with psychiatric and psychological services.

*Bereavement support*. For families after death. Often provided through hospice programs.

The interdisciplinary nature of palliative care means teams include physicians, nurses, social workers, chaplains, psychologists, and others. Team composition varies by setting.

## End-of-life care

The dying process and care during it require specific skills and attention.

*Recognizing the dying process*. Signs that death is approaching (days to weeks):
- Decreasing performance status.
- Decreased oral intake.
- Increased sleep.
- Changes in breathing patterns.
- Mottled extremities.
- Decreased urine output.
- Increasing confusion.
- "Terminal restlessness" or terminal delirium.

*Symptom management at end of life*:

*Pain*. Continue opioid management with route adjustment (often transition from oral to subcutaneous as oral intake decreases).

*Dyspnea*. Opioids (low doses often effective). Position changes. Fan therapy. Oxygen for symptomatic benefit even when oxygenation adequate.

*Secretions* ("death rattle"). Anticholinergics (scopolamine patch, glycopyrrolate). Repositioning. Family education that this is not distressing to the patient.

*Restlessness/delirium*. Underlying causes addressed when reversible. Haloperidol or other antipsychotics. Benzodiazepines for anxiety component but may worsen delirium.

*Constipation*. Continue bowel regimen as long as it provides relief.

*Mouth care*. Comfort measures.

*Discontinuation of non-essential medications*. As patient is dying, medications not contributing to comfort can be stopped. Diuretics, antihypertensives, cholesterol medications, others.

*Nutrition and hydration at end of life*. Often misunderstood. Forcing food and fluid in actively dying patients doesn't extend life and may add discomfort. Family education that decreasing appetite and intake is part of the dying process.

*Spiritual and emotional care*. Patient and family needs vary widely. Some patients want particular religious or spiritual practices. Others want peace and quiet. Others want family presence. Honoring patient preferences.

*Family support*. Family members experiencing anticipatory grief and witnessing the dying process need substantial support. Education about what to expect. Presence and bereavement support.

*Place of death*. Many patients prefer to die at home; others prefer hospital or hospice facility. Specific arrangements vary by health system, family resources, and patient circumstances.

*Hospice care*. In the US, the Medicare hospice benefit provides comprehensive care for terminally ill patients (typically prognosis ≤6 months) who have elected to forego curative-intent treatment. Hospice provides:
- *Medical management* of symptoms.
- *Nursing support* with regular visits.
- *Home health aide* assistance.
- *Social work*.
- *Chaplaincy*.
- *Bereavement support* for family for 13 months after death.
- *Medical equipment and supplies* related to terminal diagnosis.
- *Volunteer support*.

The hospice benefit has been extraordinarily valuable for end-of-life care but has limitations:
- *Prognosis requirement*. The 6-month prognosis can be hard to estimate accurately.
- *Forgoing curative treatment*. Some patients may want both palliative comfort and continuing cancer treatment, which is challenging under traditional hospice rules.
- *Underuse*. Many patients enroll in hospice only in the last days of life, missing the broader benefits.

*Palliative sedation*. For refractory symptoms (pain, dyspnea, agitation) not controlled by other means at end of life. Sedation to alleviate suffering, distinct from physician-assisted dying. Ethically distinct framework.

*Physician-assisted dying*. Legal in some jurisdictions. Allows physicians to prescribe lethal medications for terminally ill patients to self-administer. Controversial. Distinct from palliative sedation, euthanasia, and refusing treatment. The ethical and legal frameworks vary by jurisdiction.

## Communication in serious illness

Communication is central to good cancer and palliative care.

*Delivering serious news*. Frameworks like SPIKES (Setting, Perception, Invitation, Knowledge, Empathy, Strategy/Summary) provide structure for difficult conversations.

*Prognostic communication*. Patients vary in what they want to know about prognosis. Some want detailed numbers; others prefer general framing. Communication should be tailored. Honesty is important; uncertainty should be acknowledged.

*Goals of care conversations*. Open-ended questions about what matters most. "What are you hoping for?" "What worries you most?" "What would be most important to you if your situation worsened?"

*Discussing transitions to comfort-focused care*. Often difficult. Should not be framed as "giving up" but as appropriate focus on comfort and quality. The dichotomy between "fighting" and "giving up" is often unhelpful.

*Cultural and individual variation*. Different cultures, religions, and individuals have different preferences for how serious illness should be discussed. Specific cultural traditions may inform decisions and communication.

*Family meetings*. Particularly important during transitions. Facilitator role for clinicians. Goals: ensure understanding, address concerns, support decisions.

*Documentation*. Goals of care, code status, advance directives should be clearly documented and updated as circumstances change.

Skilled communication in serious illness is teachable and learnable. Programs like VitalTalk provide formal training. Many cancer centers offer communication skills training for clinicians.

## Survivorship care

For patients cured of cancer, survivorship raises specific issues:

*Surveillance for recurrence and second cancers*. Disease-specific surveillance schedules based on cancer type and stage.

*Long-term and late effects management*. As discussed throughout this book. Treatment-related cardiotoxicity, cognitive effects, fertility issues, endocrine effects, secondary malignancies, and others require systematic surveillance and management.

*Health behavior counseling*. Cancer survivors benefit from healthy lifestyle (exercise, diet, weight management, smoking cessation, limited alcohol).

*Psychosocial follow-up*. Fear of recurrence, post-cancer identity, relationships, work issues — all may continue to require support.

*Survivorship care plans*. Documents summarizing the cancer treatment received and ongoing surveillance recommendations. Provided to patient and primary care physician.

*Transition to primary care*. As patients move from active oncology follow-up to surveillance, transitioning care to primary care physicians (with continued oncology involvement as needed) is increasingly emphasized.

*Special survivorship populations*:
- *Childhood cancer survivors*. Decades of life ahead with specific late effects monitoring needs.
- *Adolescent and young adult survivors*. Specific issues including fertility, education, career.
- *Older survivors*. Cancer treatment in the context of multiple comorbidities.
- *Long-term survivors* (>5 years from treatment). Continued late effects management.

Comprehensive survivorship programs address these dimensions. The infrastructure has been growing as the number of cancer survivors has increased substantially.

## What this chapter gives you

Psychosocial care addresses the substantial impact of cancer beyond the disease itself. Common responses include acute distress, anxiety, depression, adjustment disorders, post-traumatic stress, demoralization, fear of recurrence, cognitive symptoms, body image issues, and sexual dysfunction. Social impacts include relationships, family roles, work and finances, identity changes. Existential concerns include meaning-making, spirituality, mortality awareness.

Comprehensive psychosocial care includes systematic distress screening, multidisciplinary support teams (social workers, psychologists, psychiatrists, chaplains, patient navigators), psychotherapeutic interventions (CBT, mindfulness-based interventions, ACT, meaning-centered psychotherapy, dignity therapy), pharmacotherapy when indicated, and other supportive interventions (exercise, yoga, music therapy, support groups).

Palliative care is care focused on quality of life for patients with serious illness, provided alongside disease-directed treatment from the time of diagnosis. The Temel 2010 trial established the value of early integrated palliative care, showing better quality of life, less depression, less aggressive end-of-life care, and longer survival in early-integration patients. Modern oncology increasingly integrates palliative care from the start.

Palliative care components include symptom management, communication and advance care planning, caregiver support, coordination of care, transitions management, spiritual and existential care, psychological care, and bereavement support. Models include primary palliative care (by primary teams), specialty palliative care (specialized teams), inpatient consultation, outpatient palliative care, community palliative care, and hospice.

End-of-life care includes recognizing the dying process, symptom management with route adjustments, discontinuation of non-essential medications, appropriate adjustment of nutrition and hydration support, spiritual and emotional care, family support, and decisions about place of death. The Medicare hospice benefit provides comprehensive end-of-life care for patients with terminal illness. Palliative sedation for refractory symptoms is distinct from physician-assisted dying.

Communication in serious illness includes delivering serious news (using frameworks like SPIKES), prognostic communication (tailored to patient preferences), goals of care conversations, discussing transitions to comfort-focused care without framing as "giving up," cultural and individual variation considerations, family meetings, and clear documentation. Communication skills are teachable through training programs.

Survivorship care addresses surveillance for recurrence and second cancers, long-term and late effects management, health behavior counseling, psychosocial follow-up, survivorship care plans, and transition to primary care. Special survivorship populations (childhood survivors, AYA survivors, older survivors, long-term survivors) have specific needs.

This chapter concludes the cancer biology textbook's coverage of cancer biology, diagnosis, treatment, and supportive care. The chapters together provide a comprehensive framework for understanding cancer from molecular biology through clinical management and survivorship.
