These three cancers together kill more people than any other ten cancers combined.

Hold the number. Breast cancer, lung cancer, and colorectal cancer account for roughly 30% of all cancer deaths globally. They are the most common cancers in many countries. They are the cancers that drive most of cancer research investment. They are the cancers where treatment paradigms have been most extensively developed and refined. The therapeutic frameworks for these three cancers are templates that other cancer types follow, often with adaptations for specific biology.

Each of these three has its own complex story. Breast cancer is now multiple distinct diseases — hormone receptor-positive, HER2-positive, triple-negative — with very different treatments and outcomes. Lung cancer has been transformed by molecular profiling and targeted therapy, with treatment depending on the driver mutation more than the histology in many cases. Colorectal cancer is moving through an era of MRD-guided adjuvant therapy and molecular-driven subgroup management.

This chapter — the first of two on specific solid tumors — covers these three high-impact cancers. The next — 31B — covers genitourinary, gynecologic, GI (beyond colorectal), head and neck, and skin cancers.

## Breast cancer

Breast cancer is the most common cancer in women globally (2.3 million new cases in 2022) and one of the leading causes of cancer death. The biology spans multiple distinct diseases.

*Epidemiology and risk factors*. Major risk factors include age, family history (BRCA1/2 and other hereditary syndromes account for 5-10% of cases), reproductive factors (early menarche, late menopause, nulliparity, late first pregnancy, lack of breastfeeding), hormone exposure (postmenopausal hormone therapy, oral contraceptives), lifestyle (obesity, alcohol, sedentary), and breast density.

*Screening and early detection*. Mammography is the standard screening modality. Different guidelines recommend starting at 40-50 with variable intervals. High-risk women (BRCA mutation carriers, etc.) receive MRI plus mammography starting earlier. Risk-based screening is increasingly used.

*Diagnosis and workup*. Diagnostic mammogram, ultrasound, MRI, image-guided biopsy. Histology classification (ductal, lobular, special types). Grading (Nottingham/Elston-Ellis). Receptor testing (ER, PR, HER2). For locally advanced or metastatic disease, additional staging imaging (CT, bone scan, sometimes PET).

*Subtypes by receptor status*. The clinical classification:

- *Hormone receptor-positive (HR+) HER2-negative*. About 70-75% of cases. Generally better prognosis. Treated with hormonal therapy and (for higher-risk disease) chemotherapy. CDK4/6 inhibitors with hormonal therapy for metastatic disease.

- *HER2-positive*. About 15-20% of cases. Historically had worst prognosis; transformed by HER2-targeted therapy. Trastuzumab, pertuzumab, T-DM1, T-DXd, lapatinib, neratinib, tucatinib, margetuximab — multiple HER2-targeted agents. Can be HR-positive or HR-negative.

- *Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC)*. About 15% of cases. Lacks ER, PR, HER2. Historically had limited targeted treatment options; now has multiple emerging approaches. PD-1 inhibitors (pembrolizumab) approved for PD-L1-positive metastatic disease. PARP inhibitors for BRCA-mutated TNBC. Sacituzumab govitecan (Trop-2 ADC). Trodelvy and other ADCs.

*Molecular subclassification*. PAM50 intrinsic subtypes (Luminal A, Luminal B, HER2-enriched, Basal-like) provide additional information. Oncotype DX recurrence score guides adjuvant chemotherapy decisions in HR-positive early-stage disease.

*Local treatment*. Surgery (lumpectomy with radiation or mastectomy, with sentinel lymph node biopsy or axillary dissection). Radiation therapy. Reconstruction options.

*Systemic therapy by stage*:

*Early-stage HR-positive disease*. Adjuvant endocrine therapy (tamoxifen for premenopausal, AI for postmenopausal, often with ovarian suppression for high-risk premenopausal). Duration 5-10 years. Adjuvant chemotherapy for higher-risk disease (Oncotype DX recurrence score guides). Adjuvant abemaciclib (CDK4/6 inhibitor) for high-risk early disease.

*Early-stage HER2-positive disease*. Adjuvant chemotherapy + trastuzumab (often + pertuzumab for higher-risk). Neoadjuvant pertuzumab + trastuzumab + chemotherapy, then surgery, then completion of HER2-targeted therapy. T-DM1 adjuvant for residual disease after neoadjuvant therapy.

*Early-stage triple-negative disease*. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy + pembrolizumab (KEYNOTE-522 regimen), then surgery, then completion of pembrolizumab. Adjuvant chemotherapy for residual disease. Olaparib adjuvant for BRCA-mutated disease.

*Metastatic HR-positive disease*. CDK4/6 inhibitor + endocrine therapy as first-line. Sequential endocrine therapies. PI3K inhibitor (alpelisib) for PIK3CA-mutated disease. Everolimus + endocrine therapy. Eventually chemotherapy or ADCs.

*Metastatic HER2-positive disease*. T-DXd is reshaping treatment. Trastuzumab + pertuzumab + taxane. T-DM1, tucatinib-based combinations.

*Metastatic triple-negative disease*. Sacituzumab govitecan, T-DXd (for HER2-low), pembrolizumab + chemotherapy (PD-L1-positive), PARP inhibitors (BRCA-mutated), chemotherapy.

The treatment landscape is rapidly evolving with multiple new approvals each year. Breast cancer is among the cancers with the most extensive modern treatment options.

*Outcomes*. Five-year survival exceeds 90% for stage I-II disease in countries with good screening and treatment access. Stage IV survival has improved dramatically with modern treatment, with median survival of 3-5+ years in some subgroups. Triple-negative metastatic disease historically had the shortest survival but has benefited from recent approvals.

## Lung cancer

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer death globally (1.8 million deaths in 2022). The biology has been transformed by molecular profiling and targeted therapy.

*Epidemiology and risk factors*. Tobacco smoking is the dominant risk factor, accounting for about 90% of cases. Other factors include radon exposure (second leading cause), occupational exposures (asbestos, arsenic, chromium, others), air pollution, family history, and HIV (modest increase). About 10-15% of lung cancers occur in never-smokers, with different biology.

*Screening and early detection*. Low-dose CT screening for heavy smokers (USPSTF: 50-80 years old, ≥20 pack-year history, currently smoking or quit within 15 years). The screening reduces lung cancer mortality but uptake has been limited.

*Histological classification*:

*Non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC)*. About 85% of cases. Major subtypes:
- *Adenocarcinoma*. The most common subtype, especially in non-smokers.
- *Squamous cell carcinoma*. Strongly tobacco-related.
- *Large cell carcinoma*.
- *Less common subtypes*.

*Small-cell lung cancer (SCLC)*. About 15% of cases. Aggressive neuroendocrine carcinoma, strongly tobacco-related.

*Carcinoid tumors and other neuroendocrine variants*. Less common.

*Molecular profiling*. Standard for all advanced NSCLC. Major actionable alterations:
- *EGFR mutations* (20-30% of adenocarcinomas, higher in Asian populations and non-smokers).
- *ALK rearrangements* (3-7%).
- *KRAS mutations* (25-30%, including G12C in about 13%).
- *ROS1 rearrangements* (1-2%).
- *BRAF V600E* (1-2%).
- *MET exon 14 skipping* (3-4%).
- *RET fusions* (1-2%).
- *NTRK fusions* (rare).
- *HER2 mutations and amplifications*.
- *NRG1 fusions*.

Plus PD-L1 expression, tumor mutational burden, and other features.

*Staging*. TNM staging based on tumor size and location, lymph node involvement, and distant metastases. PET-CT is standard for staging. EBUS-TBNA for mediastinal lymph node staging.

*Treatment by stage*:

*Stage I NSCLC*. Surgery (lobectomy with mediastinal lymph node dissection). Adjuvant chemotherapy for stage IB or II with high-risk features. Adjuvant osimertinib (EGFR-mutated) or atezolizumab (PD-L1≥1%) for selected patients.

For medically inoperable patients, stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) provides excellent local control.

*Stage II NSCLC*. Surgery with adjuvant chemotherapy. Adjuvant immunotherapy or targeted therapy as for stage I.

*Stage III NSCLC*. Concurrent chemoradiation followed by consolidation durvalumab (PACIFIC regimen) for unresectable disease. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy + immunotherapy followed by surgery for selected patients.

*Stage IV NSCLC*. Treatment guided by molecular profile:
- *EGFR-mutated*. Osimertinib first-line.
- *ALK-rearranged*. Alectinib, lorlatinib, or other ALK inhibitor.
- *ROS1-rearranged*. Crizotinib, entrectinib, or repotrectinib.
- *BRAF V600E*. Dabrafenib + trametinib.
- *KRAS G12C*. Sotorasib or adagrasib.
- *MET exon 14*. Capmatinib or tepotinib.
- *RET fusion*. Selpercatinib or pralsetinib.
- *NTRK fusion*. Larotrectinib or entrectinib.
- *No targetable driver*. Treatment by PD-L1 status:
  - PD-L1 ≥50%: pembrolizumab monotherapy or chemotherapy combinations.
  - PD-L1 <50%: chemotherapy + pembrolizumab.
  - Squamous histology: chemotherapy + pembrolizumab.

The treatment landscape is sophisticated, with first-, second-, and later-line options that depend on molecular profile and prior treatments.

*Small-cell lung cancer treatment*. Limited stage: concurrent chemoradiation + atezolizumab or durvalumab consolidation. Extensive stage: chemotherapy (platinum + etoposide) + immunotherapy (atezolizumab or durvalumab). Maintenance therapy. Second-line topotecan, lurbinectedin, or other options.

*Outcomes*. NSCLC stage I-II five-year survival 50-90% depending on stage. Stage III about 30%. Stage IV historically <5% but has improved with modern targeted therapy and immunotherapy — selected molecular subgroups (EGFR, ALK) now have median survival of years. SCLC outcomes remain poor despite incremental improvements.

## Colorectal cancer

Colorectal cancer is one of the most common cancers worldwide (1.9 million new cases globally in 2022). The treatment paradigm has been substantially refined through screening, surgical advances, and molecular-driven approaches.

*Epidemiology and risk factors*. Major risk factors include age, family history (Lynch syndrome, FAP, MUTYH-associated polyposis account for 5-10%), inflammatory bowel disease, obesity, processed meat and red meat consumption, alcohol, sedentary lifestyle, smoking. Early-onset colorectal cancer (under 50) has been increasing for unclear reasons.

*Screening*. Multiple options including colonoscopy, FIT, sigmoidoscopy, CT colonography, stool DNA testing. USPSTF recommends starting at age 45 in average-risk individuals. Lynch syndrome carriers and other high-risk groups receive intensive surveillance.

*Histology*. Adenocarcinoma is the dominant histology (95%+). Subtypes include mucinous adenocarcinoma, signet ring cell carcinoma, medullary carcinoma. Right-sided versus left-sided cancers have different biology and prognoses.

*Molecular profiling*. Standard for advanced disease and increasingly for early-stage:
- *RAS status* (KRAS, NRAS mutations) — determines anti-EGFR antibody use.
- *BRAF V600E* — different prognosis and treatment.
- *Mismatch repair status / microsatellite instability* — guides immunotherapy.
- *HER2 amplification* — increasingly relevant for treatment.
- *NTRK fusions* — rare but actionable.
- *Other actionable alterations*.

*Staging*. TNM with stages I-IV. Stage I: T1-T2N0; stage II: T3-T4N0; stage III: any T with N+; stage IV: M+.

*Treatment by stage*:

*Stage I*. Surgery alone (segmental colectomy with adequate lymphadenectomy). High cure rate.

*Stage II*. Surgery with adjuvant chemotherapy considered for high-risk features (T4, poor differentiation, vascular invasion, perforation, obstruction, MSI-stable). MSI-high stage II doesn't benefit from fluorouracil-based adjuvant.

*Stage III*. Surgery with adjuvant chemotherapy. CAPOX or FOLFOX for 3-6 months. The IDEA collaboration showed that 3 months is non-inferior to 6 months for many lower-risk patients.

*Stage IV*. Systemic therapy as primary treatment, with metastasectomy for selected oligometastatic disease (especially liver metastases).

First-line therapy: chemotherapy backbone (FOLFOX or FOLFIRI) plus targeted therapy:
- *RAS wild-type, left-sided*: chemotherapy + anti-EGFR antibody (cetuximab or panitumumab).
- *RAS wild-type, right-sided*: chemotherapy + bevacizumab.
- *RAS mutated*: chemotherapy + bevacizumab.
- *BRAF V600E*: BRAF + MEK + anti-EGFR (encorafenib + cetuximab in second-line).
- *HER2 amplified*: tucatinib + trastuzumab or T-DXd.
- *MSI-high*: immunotherapy (pembrolizumab) — substantially better than chemotherapy for MSI-high disease (KEYNOTE-177).

Second-line and subsequent: TAS-102 (trifluridine/tipiracil), regorafenib, fruquintinib (recently approved), other targeted options.

*MRD-guided adjuvant therapy*. The DYNAMIC trial demonstrated that ctDNA-based detection of MRD after surgery can guide adjuvant chemotherapy decisions — MRD-positive patients benefit from chemotherapy while MRD-negative patients may not need it. This is being incorporated into guidelines and is one of the major recent advances.

*Rectal cancer specifics*. Total mesorectal excision is the standard surgery. Neoadjuvant chemoradiation or short-course radiation for locally advanced. *Total neoadjuvant therapy (TNT)* involves all chemotherapy plus radiation before surgery — has improved outcomes in some studies. *Watch-and-wait* approach for patients achieving complete response — some patients can avoid surgery entirely.

*Outcomes*. Stage I 5-year survival around 90%. Stage III around 70-75%. Stage IV around 14% historically but improving. The shift toward MRD-guided adjuvant therapy and biomarker-driven systemic therapy is improving outcomes across stages.

## What this chapter gives you

Breast, lung, and colorectal cancers — the three most common cancers worldwide — have been heavily studied and have produced sophisticated treatment paradigms that serve as models for other cancers.

*Breast cancer* is multiple distinct diseases by receptor status:
- HR-positive: treated with endocrine therapy + CDK4/6 inhibitors; adjuvant chemotherapy guided by Oncotype DX
- HER2-positive: treated with HER2-targeted therapy (trastuzumab, pertuzumab, T-DM1, T-DXd, others)
- Triple-negative: limited targeted options but expanding (pembrolizumab + chemo, PARP inhibitors for BRCA-mutated, sacituzumab govitecan)

The treatment landscape continues to evolve with new approvals. Five-year survival exceeds 90% for early stages with good screening and treatment access.

*Lung cancer* has been transformed by molecular profiling. NSCLC treatment now depends heavily on driver mutation profile (EGFR, ALK, ROS1, BRAF, KRAS G12C, MET, RET, NTRK, others) and PD-L1 status. Multiple FDA-approved targeted therapies for each major molecular subgroup. SCLC has more limited but improving options with addition of immunotherapy. Early-stage screening with low-dose CT reduces mortality but uptake is limited.

*Colorectal cancer* treatment depends on molecular profile (RAS status, BRAF status, MSI status, HER2 status). MRD-guided adjuvant therapy using ctDNA is reshaping early-stage management. Rectal cancer increasingly uses total neoadjuvant therapy with watch-and-wait for complete responders. Stage IV outcomes have improved substantially with biomarker-driven combinations.

The three cancers together illustrate the principles of modern cancer care — integrated histological and molecular classification, biomarker-driven treatment selection, multimodality therapy, and increasingly personalized approaches.
