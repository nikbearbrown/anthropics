A nanoparticle is small enough to find a tumor that a pill can't.

Hold the framing. Conventional cancer drugs face a delivery problem. Most are distributed throughout the body via the bloodstream, exposing every tissue to whatever effects the drug produces. Cancer cells receive the drug; so do bone marrow cells, gut epithelium, hair follicles, immune cells, and every other tissue. The toxicity that limits chemotherapy dosing comes mostly from this collateral exposure. The fundamental engineering challenge is to deliver the drug to cancer cells preferentially — to concentrate it where it's needed and minimize it where it isn't.

Nanotechnology offers tools to address this challenge. Particles small enough to circulate in blood but large enough to behave differently from individual molecules can be designed with specific properties. They can carry drug payloads in higher concentrations than free drugs. They can accumulate preferentially in tumors (through leaky tumor vasculature in the "enhanced permeability and retention" effect or through active targeting). They can release their payload selectively (in the acidic tumor microenvironment, in response to enzymes, on demand from external triggers). They can incorporate imaging functions to track their distribution and effect. They can combine therapy with diagnostics in the same particle — theranostics.

The conceptual promise has been clear for decades. The clinical translation has been harder than expected. Many nanotechnology approaches have produced disappointing results when tested in patients — modest benefits over conventional formulations, manufacturing complexities, regulatory challenges, cost issues. But the field continues to develop, with multiple FDA-approved nanotherapeutics in clinical use and a substantial pipeline of innovative approaches.

This chapter — the first of two on cancer nanotechnology — covers the principles of nanoparticle drug delivery and the nanomaterial-based imaging approaches. The next — 27B — covers theranostics, multifunctional platforms, and emerging technologies.

Hold the question: *if a nanoparticle can be designed to behave differently from a free drug molecule, what can we engineer it to do for cancer?*

## What nanoparticles are and what makes them useful

Nanoparticles are particles with dimensions in the nanometer range — typically 1-1000 nanometers, with 10-200 nm being most clinically relevant. To put the scale in context: a water molecule is about 0.3 nm. A virus is 20-300 nm. A red blood cell is 7000 nm. Cells are 10,000-100,000 nm. Nanoparticles sit in the range where they are much larger than individual molecules but much smaller than cells.

This size range matters because it produces specific behaviors:

*Tumor accumulation through EPR effect*. The *enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect* refers to the tendency of macromolecules and nanoparticles to accumulate in tumors more than in normal tissues. The mechanism involves two features of tumor vasculature: *increased permeability* (tumor vessels have leaky walls with gaps that allow larger molecules to escape into tumor tissue, while normal capillaries don't permit this) and *poor lymphatic drainage* (tumors typically have impaired lymphatic clearance, so molecules that enter remain longer). The result: nanoparticles accumulate preferentially in tumors compared to normal tissues.

The EPR effect was first characterized by Maeda and colleagues in the 1980s. It is the basis for "passive targeting" of nanoparticles to tumors. The magnitude of the EPR effect varies substantially across tumor types and patients (some tumors have more permeable vasculature than others), and its clinical impact has been less dramatic than initial enthusiasm predicted.

*Long circulation times*. Nanoparticles can be designed (typically with surface modification using polyethylene glycol or "PEGylation") to evade clearance by the reticuloendothelial system, allowing them to circulate longer than small molecules. Longer circulation provides more opportunity for tumor accumulation.

*Drug protection*. Nanoparticles can protect their drug payload from degradation in circulation. Drugs that would be metabolized or cleared quickly as free molecules can be delivered effectively when encapsulated.

*Combined functions*. A single nanoparticle can incorporate multiple functions — drug payload, imaging agent, targeting ligand, environment-responsive triggers. The combination allows complex behavior not possible with simple drug molecules.

*Active targeting*. Surface modification with targeting ligands (antibodies, peptides, aptamers) can direct nanoparticles to specific cells or tissues beyond what passive accumulation achieves.

## Liposomes: the most established nanocarriers

Liposomes are spherical vesicles formed by lipid bilayers, similar in structure to cell membranes. They were the first nanocarriers developed clinically and remain the most established class.

The structure: phospholipid bilayer enclosing an aqueous interior. Hydrophilic drugs can be encapsulated in the interior; hydrophobic drugs in the bilayer membrane.

Approved liposomal cancer drugs:

*Doxil (doxorubicin HCl liposome injection)*. Pegylated liposomal doxorubicin. Approved 1995 for HIV-related Kaposi sarcoma; later expanded to ovarian cancer, multiple myeloma, breast cancer. The PEGylation provides long circulation times. The encapsulation reduces cardiac toxicity compared to free doxorubicin (a major clinical advantage).

*DaunoXome (liposomal daunorubicin)*. For HIV-related Kaposi sarcoma.

*Marqibo (liposomal vincristine)*. For Philadelphia chromosome-negative ALL.

*Onivyde (irinotecan liposome injection)*. Nanoliposomal irinotecan. Approved for metastatic pancreatic cancer in combination with 5-FU and leucovorin. The encapsulation improves pharmacokinetics and reduces toxicity.

*Vyxeos (daunorubicin and cytarabine liposome injection)*. Liposome containing both drugs at a fixed 1:5 molar ratio. Approved for treatment-related AML and AML with myelodysplasia-related changes. The synchronized release of both drugs to leukemia cells provides superior cell killing.

*Mepact (mifamurtide)*. Liposomal muramyl tripeptide phosphatidylethanolamine. For osteosarcoma.

The clinical benefits of liposomal formulations vary. Doxil provides clear toxicity advantages over conventional doxorubicin. Onivyde and Vyxeos provide efficacy advantages. Other liposomal formulations have provided more modest benefits.

The limitations of liposomal drugs:
- *Variable EPR effect*. Tumor accumulation is inconsistent across patients and tumor types.
- *Reticuloendothelial system clearance*. Despite PEGylation, eventual clearance by liver and spleen limits duration.
- *Cost*. Liposomal formulations are typically more expensive than conventional ones.
- *Storage and stability*. Liposomes can be unstable, with shorter shelf lives than conventional drugs.

Despite these limitations, liposomes remain the most clinically successful nanotechnology platform.

## Polymeric nanoparticles

Polymeric nanoparticles use synthetic polymers to encapsulate drugs. The polymers can be biodegradable (releasing drug as they break down) or non-biodegradable (releasing drug through diffusion).

Major polymer types:

*PLGA (poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid))*. The most common biodegradable polymer for drug delivery. Breaks down to lactic and glycolic acid, both naturally occurring metabolites. Used in various drug delivery applications, including some emerging cancer applications.

*Poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG)*. Used both as a polymer for nanoparticles and as a surface modification (PEGylation) of other nanoparticles to evade immune clearance.

*Dextran and other natural polymers*. Polysaccharides used in some formulations.

*Polysaccharides like chitosan*. Used for some specialized applications, particularly those requiring mucoadhesion.

Approved polymeric nanotherapeutics:

*Abraxane (nab-paclitaxel)*. Albumin-bound paclitaxel nanoparticles. The 130-nm albumin nanoparticles carry paclitaxel non-covalently. Albumin facilitates tumor entry through interactions with the albumin-binding glycoprotein SPARC (secreted protein acidic and rich in cysteine), which is often overexpressed in tumors. Nab-paclitaxel avoids the solvent (Cremophor EL) used in conventional paclitaxel formulation, eliminating the hypersensitivity reactions that require premedication. Approved for metastatic breast cancer, non-small-cell lung cancer, and pancreatic cancer. One of the most successful nanotechnology cancer drugs.

*Onpattro (patisiran)*. Lipid nanoparticle-formulated siRNA. Approved for hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis (not cancer, but demonstrates the technology that scaled into mRNA vaccines).

*COVID-19 mRNA vaccines (Pfizer/BNT162b2; Moderna/mRNA-1273)*. Lipid nanoparticle-delivered mRNA. The massive global rollout has dramatically scaled LNP manufacturing capacity, with implications for cancer mRNA vaccines (Chapter 25B).

The polymeric nanoparticle field continues to develop with multiple emerging candidates in clinical trials.

## Antibody-drug conjugates as targeted nanoscale therapy

Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), discussed in Chapter 24A, can be considered a form of targeted nanoscale therapy. The antibody serves as the targeting moiety; the cytotoxic payload provides the therapeutic effect; the linker connects them with specific cleavage properties.

The success of T-DXd (trastuzumab deruxtecan) in HER2-positive and HER2-low breast cancer, plus many other ADCs across cancer types, demonstrates how molecular targeting combined with carrier-based payload delivery can produce dramatic clinical benefits.

ADCs differ from typical nanoparticles in some ways:
- *Smaller size*. ADCs are about 10 nm (the size of a typical antibody) compared to 100-200 nm for typical liposomes.
- *Single targeting moiety*. Each ADC has one antibody (or sometimes engineered antibody fragment), versus multivalent ligand display on nanoparticles.
- *Defined payload-to-carrier ratio*. The DAR (drug-to-antibody ratio) is typically 4-8 for ADCs versus thousands of drug molecules per nanoparticle.

The general principles, however, are similar — targeted delivery, payload release, and cell killing. ADCs are arguably the most successful targeted drug delivery technology in oncology.

## Inorganic nanoparticles

Inorganic nanoparticles use materials like gold, iron oxide, silica, or quantum dots. They have specific properties useful for various applications.

*Gold nanoparticles*. Have optical properties that make them useful for imaging (photoacoustic, surface-enhanced Raman scattering) and for photothermal therapy (absorbing near-infrared light and converting it to heat to ablate tumors). *Aurimune* (CYT-6091) was a gold-based nanoparticle therapy that reached clinical trials but didn't achieve FDA approval.

*Iron oxide nanoparticles*. Have magnetic properties useful for MRI contrast and for magnetic hyperthermia (heating in alternating magnetic fields). Approved iron oxide products include Feridex (now discontinued), Resovist, and others for MRI contrast. NanoTherm is an iron oxide product approved in Europe for thermal therapy in glioblastoma.

*Silica nanoparticles*. Used as carriers for drugs and imaging agents. Mesoporous silica with high surface area can carry substantial drug payloads.

*Quantum dots*. Semiconductor nanocrystals with unique optical properties (size-dependent fluorescence). Used in research but with toxicity concerns (heavy metal content) that have limited clinical translation.

*Carbon-based nanomaterials*. Carbon nanotubes, graphene, fullerenes. Have unique structural and electronic properties. Various research applications; limited clinical translation.

The inorganic nanoparticle field has produced extensive research but fewer FDA-approved clinical products than lipid- and polymer-based platforms. The challenges include biocompatibility, clearance, and manufacturing reproducibility.

## Nanomaterials for cancer imaging

Beyond drug delivery, nanomaterials provide tools for cancer imaging. The imaging applications include:

*MRI contrast enhancement*. Iron oxide nanoparticles (smaller than ~50 nm SPIONs — superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles, and larger USPIOs — ultrasmall superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles) provide strong T2 contrast. Different formulations have been used for liver imaging (Feridex), lymph node imaging, and tumor detection. Some products have been discontinued; others remain in development.

Gadolinium-based nanoparticles can provide T1 contrast with potentially improved tumor accumulation through EPR effects.

*Fluorescence imaging*. Fluorescent dye-loaded nanoparticles allow tumor imaging through near-infrared fluorescence. Particularly useful for intraoperative imaging — guiding surgical resection by visualizing tumor boundaries that are hard to see by white light. *Indocyanine green (ICG)* in various formulations is widely used clinically. Newer formulations with longer circulation times and tumor-specific accumulation are in development.

*CT contrast*. Gold nanoparticles, iodine-based nanoparticles, and others provide CT contrast. Can be combined with targeting moieties for specific tumor imaging.

*PET imaging*. Radioisotope-labeled nanoparticles for PET. Used in research more than routine practice.

*Photoacoustic imaging*. Gold nanoparticles, melanin-based particles, and others provide photoacoustic contrast — the conversion of absorbed light to acoustic signal allows imaging combining optical contrast with ultrasound-like spatial resolution.

*Surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS)*. Gold nanoparticles with attached Raman reporter molecules can be detected by their characteristic spectral signatures. Highly sensitive detection but technically demanding.

*Multimodal imaging*. Single nanoparticles incorporating multiple imaging functions (MRI + fluorescence; PET + MRI; etc.) allow combined imaging approaches.

The clinical translation of nanoparticle imaging has been modest compared to research activity. Most current cancer imaging uses conventional contrast agents and modalities. Specific applications (fluorescence-guided surgery, magnetic resonance imaging with specialized contrast) have been clinically successful in selected contexts.

## Active targeting strategies

Beyond passive accumulation through EPR effects, nanoparticles can be designed with active targeting — surface modification with ligands that bind specific cancer cell surface markers.

The targeting strategy:

1. *Identify a cancer-specific (or cancer-enriched) surface marker*. Examples: HER2, EGFR, transferrin receptor (highly expressed on proliferating cells), folate receptor (enriched on some cancers), CD44 (enriched on cancer stem cells).

2. *Design a targeting ligand*. Antibodies, antibody fragments (Fab, scFv), peptides, aptamers, small molecules (folic acid for folate receptor).

3. *Attach the ligand to the nanoparticle surface*. Various chemistries for stable conjugation.

4. *Validate targeting*. Confirm specific binding to target-expressing cells and accumulation at target sites.

Examples of active targeting in clinical or near-clinical use:

*Trastuzumab-decorated nanoparticles for HER2-positive cancers*. Various formulations in development.

*Folate-targeted nanoparticles*. EC145 (a folate-camptothecin conjugate) was tested in folate receptor-positive cancers but did not achieve approval. Mirvetuximab soravtansine (an ADC with folate receptor α targeting) was approved for ovarian cancer.

*Transferrin-targeted nanoparticles*. Multiple research formulations.

*Peptide-targeted nanoparticles*. Various peptide ligands explored.

*Aptamer-targeted nanoparticles*. Aptamers (short nucleic acids selected for target binding) provide alternatives to antibodies with potential advantages in manufacturing and engineering.

The active targeting approach has not always produced the dramatic improvements over passive targeting that initial enthusiasm predicted. Multiple factors contribute:
- The fraction of injected dose reaching the tumor is often limited by clearance and circulation factors, regardless of targeting.
- Tumor heterogeneity means that not all cells express the targeted marker.
- The "protein corona" that forms on nanoparticles in vivo can mask targeting ligands.
- The targeting may improve cellular uptake at the tumor but not overall tumor accumulation.

Despite these challenges, active targeting remains an important strategy and continues to be refined.

## Stimuli-responsive nanoparticles

A more sophisticated approach is to design nanoparticles that release their payload in response to specific stimuli, providing further selectivity.

*pH-responsive nanoparticles*. The tumor microenvironment is more acidic (typically pH 6.5-6.8) than normal tissue (pH 7.4). Nanoparticles designed to release payload at low pH preferentially deliver drug to tumor regions.

*Enzyme-responsive nanoparticles*. Tumors often have elevated proteases (matrix metalloproteinases, cathepsins). Linkers cleavable by these enzymes release payload preferentially in tumors.

*Redox-responsive nanoparticles*. The intracellular environment has higher glutathione than extracellular. Disulfide-linked nanoparticles release payload upon cellular uptake.

*Temperature-responsive nanoparticles*. Designed to release payload above body temperature. Combined with external heating (focused ultrasound, magnetic heating, NIR laser), allows on-demand release.

*Light-responsive nanoparticles*. Photochemical mechanisms release payload upon illumination. Useful for accessible tumors.

*Magnetic-responsive nanoparticles*. Magnetic fields can guide nanoparticle accumulation, trigger release, or heat for hyperthermia.

*Ultrasound-responsive nanoparticles*. Various ultrasound-triggered release mechanisms.

The stimulus-responsive approaches add complexity to nanoparticle design but can provide additional selectivity for tumor delivery and release. Most are in research or early clinical development.

## Manufacturing and quality considerations

Nanoparticles are more complex to manufacture than small molecule drugs. The manufacturing challenges include:

*Size and uniformity control*. Therapeutic activity often depends on having particles within a specific size range. Manufacturing must produce uniform particles, batch after batch.

*Drug loading and encapsulation efficiency*. The amount of drug per particle and the consistency of loading must be controlled.

*Stability*. Nanoparticles must remain stable during storage and administration. Some formulations require special storage (cold chain, protection from light).

*Surface chemistry*. Surface modifications (PEGylation, ligand attachment) must be reproducible.

*Regulatory considerations*. Nanoparticle drugs are more complex regulatory products than small molecules. Demonstrating bioequivalence between batches, characterizing structure, and establishing safety profiles requires extensive analysis.

*Scale-up*. Manufacturing at clinical and commercial scale requires reliable production processes. Some research-grade nanoparticles have failed because they couldn't be scaled.

*Cost*. Nanoparticle drugs are typically more expensive than conventional formulations.

These challenges have contributed to the slower-than-expected clinical translation of many promising nanotechnology approaches. Successful clinical nanomedicines (Doxil, Abraxane, T-DM1, T-DXd) have demonstrated that the manufacturing challenges can be solved, but the bar is substantial.

## What this chapter gives you

Nanotechnology offers tools for cancer drug delivery and imaging through particles in the 10-200 nm range that behave differently from individual drug molecules. The key advantages include passive tumor accumulation through the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, long circulation times, drug protection, combined functions, and active targeting capabilities.

Liposomes are the most established nanocarriers, with multiple FDA-approved products including Doxil (PEGylated liposomal doxorubicin), Onivyde (liposomal irinotecan), Vyxeos (liposomal daunorubicin + cytarabine), and others. The clinical benefits come primarily from improved pharmacokinetics, reduced toxicity (especially cardiac for Doxil), and synchronized drug release for combination products.

Polymeric nanoparticles include nab-paclitaxel (Abraxane), one of the most successful nanotechnology cancer drugs, plus a substantial pipeline. The use of lipid nanoparticles for siRNA (Onpattro) and mRNA delivery (COVID-19 vaccines and personalized cancer vaccines) has dramatically scaled this technology.

Antibody-drug conjugates can be considered a form of targeted nanoscale therapy. The clinical success of multiple ADCs (T-DXd, sacituzumab govitecan, enfortumab vedotin, and others) demonstrates the power of targeted carrier-based delivery.

Inorganic nanoparticles (gold, iron oxide, silica, quantum dots, carbon-based) have specific properties useful for various applications but have produced fewer FDA-approved clinical products than lipid and polymer platforms.

Nanomaterials for imaging include MRI contrast (iron oxide, gadolinium), fluorescence (NIR dyes), CT contrast, PET, photoacoustic imaging, SERS, and multimodal combinations. Clinical translation has been modest compared to research activity, with specific successes in selected applications.

Active targeting through surface ligands (antibodies, peptides, aptamers, small molecules) provides additional selectivity beyond passive accumulation, with mixed results across approaches.

Stimuli-responsive nanoparticles (pH, enzyme, redox, temperature, light, magnetic, ultrasound) add another layer of selectivity by releasing payload in response to specific tumor characteristics or external triggers.

Manufacturing and quality challenges have contributed to slower clinical translation than initial enthusiasm predicted, but successful nanomedicines demonstrate that these challenges can be solved.
