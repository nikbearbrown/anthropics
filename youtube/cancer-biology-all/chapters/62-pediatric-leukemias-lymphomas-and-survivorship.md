# Chapter 62 — Pediatric Leukemias, Lymphomas, and Survivorship


## TL;DR

- The child cured of cancer in 1970 was an exception.
- The chapter moves through Pediatric acute lymphoblastic leukemia, Pediatric acute myeloid leukemia, Pediatric lymphomas, Hodgkin lymphoma, and related ideas.
- Read it for the main argument, the vocabulary it introduces, and the practical judgment it asks you to develop.

The child cured of cancer in 1970 was an exception. The child cured today has eight decades of life ahead, and what we do to them now matters for all of it.

Hold the framing. Pediatric oncology has produced some of the most dramatic treatment successes in cancer medicine. Childhood acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) cure rates have risen from <10% in 1960 to >90% today. Hodgkin lymphoma in children is essentially curable. Wilms tumor has >90% cure rates. The successes are the result of decades of systematic refinement through cooperative group trials, with treatments progressively optimized to maximize cures while reducing toxicity.

But the children cured today face decades of life ahead, and the long-term consequences of cancer treatment matter substantially. Cardiotoxicity from anthracyclines. Cognitive effects from CNS-directed therapy. Secondary cancers from radiation and certain chemotherapies. Endocrine effects from CNS or pelvic radiation. Infertility from gonadotoxic therapies. Growth effects. Psychosocial effects. The cured cancer survivor needs lifelong specialty follow-up to manage and prevent these consequences.

This chapter — the second of two on pediatric cancers — covers pediatric leukemias and lymphomas (the most common pediatric cancers) and broader principles of pediatric oncology including survivorship.

Hold the question: *given that pediatric cancer treatment increasingly produces cures, how do we maximize quality of long-term outcomes?*

## Pediatric acute lymphoblastic leukemia

Childhood ALL is the most common childhood cancer and one of the most successful treatment stories in oncology.

*Epidemiology*. The most common cancer in children, accounting for about 25% of all pediatric cancers. Peak incidence at age 2-5. Slight male predominance. Higher rates in Hispanic and white children than Black children.

*Subtypes*. Based on lineage and molecular features (extending the framework from 33A):
- *B-cell ALL* (the majority).
- *T-cell ALL* (~15%).

Molecular subtypes within B-ALL include:
- *Hyperdiploid* (gain of chromosomes). Favorable.
- *ETV6-RUNX1 (t(12;21))*. Favorable, common in children.
- *TCF3-PBX1 (t(1;19))*. Intermediate.
- *KMT2A (MLL)-rearranged*. Poor prognosis, especially in infants.
- *iAMP21*. Poor prognosis.
- *Hypodiploid*. Poor prognosis.
- *BCR-ABL (Ph+)*. Was poor prognosis; transformed by TKI addition to chemotherapy.
- *Ph-like ALL*. Various kinase activations resembling Ph+; poor prognosis without targeted therapy.

*Risk stratification*. Combines age (especially infants and adolescents), white blood cell count at presentation, immunophenotype, cytogenetics/molecular features, CNS involvement, and response to therapy (especially MRD).

*Diagnosis*. Bone marrow aspirate/biopsy with comprehensive workup (flow cytometry, cytogenetics, FISH, molecular testing). Peripheral blood often shows lymphoblasts. CSF analysis for CNS involvement.

*Treatment*. Multi-agent chemotherapy in defined phases over 2-3 years total duration:

1. *Induction*. Several weeks (typically 4-6) of intensive chemotherapy to achieve initial remission. Standard agents include vincristine, anthracycline (daunorubicin), corticosteroids (prednisone or dexamethasone), asparaginase, sometimes cyclophosphamide. Modern induction includes targeted therapy additions (TKI for Ph+ ALL; blinatumomab being incorporated for selected patients).

2. *Consolidation*. Several months of intensive multi-agent therapy. Various drug combinations.

3. *CNS prophylaxis*. Intrathecal chemotherapy (methotrexate, cytarabine, hydrocortisone). High-dose intravenous methotrexate. Cranial radiation rarely used now (replaced by intrathecal therapy and high-dose methotrexate) due to long-term cognitive effects.

4. *Delayed intensification*. Additional intensive treatment phases.

5. *Maintenance*. 6-mercaptopurine + methotrexate (with periodic vincristine and corticosteroid pulses) for 2-3 years total treatment duration.

The regimens have become increasingly risk-adapted. Lower-risk patients receive less intensive treatment to minimize toxicity; higher-risk patients receive more intensive treatment.

*MRD-guided therapy*. MRD assessment (flow cytometry or PCR) after induction and consolidation guides treatment intensification. MRD-positive patients receive intensified treatment or transplant; MRD-negative patients may have de-intensification of certain components.

*Targeted therapy and immunotherapy additions*:
- *TKIs for Ph+ ALL*. Imatinib, dasatinib added to chemotherapy. Has eliminated the historical poor prognosis of Ph+ ALL.
- *Blinatumomab*. Anti-CD19/CD3 BiTE. Used in consolidation for MRD-positive patients and for relapsed disease. AALL1731 and similar studies are incorporating blinatumomab into frontline therapy.
- *Inotuzumab ozogamicin*. Anti-CD22 ADC. Used for relapsed disease.
- *CAR-T cells*. Tisagenlecleucel approved for relapsed/refractory pediatric and young adult B-ALL. Brexucabtagene autoleucel for adults.

*Stem cell transplant*. For high-risk disease or relapsed disease with poor response. Allogeneic transplant from matched donor.

*Outcomes*. Overall 5-year survival for pediatric ALL exceeds 90% in modern protocols. Specific subgroups have variable outcomes:
- ETV6-RUNX1: >95%.
- Hyperdiploid: >90%.
- Ph+ ALL with TKI: now >70%.
- Infant ALL (especially KMT2A-rearranged): worse, <50%.
- T-cell ALL: variable, generally 80%.
- Relapsed ALL: variable, depending on timing and prior treatment.

The childhood ALL story illustrates how systematic cooperative group trials, integration of new agents, MRD-guided risk stratification, and refined supportive care can transform a uniformly fatal cancer into one with >90% cure rates.

## Pediatric acute myeloid leukemia

Childhood AML is less common than ALL but more aggressive.

*Epidemiology*. About 15-20% of childhood acute leukemias. Median age across the pediatric population (different distribution from adult AML).

*Subtypes*. Similar molecular classification to adult AML but with different distributions:
- *Core binding factor leukemias* (RUNX1-RUNX1T1, CBFB-MYH11). Better prognosis. More common in children.
- *Acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL)*. Treated with ATRA + arsenic trioxide; high cure rates.
- *KMT2A-rearranged*. Common in infants. Poor prognosis with conventional therapy; menin inhibitors (revumenib) emerging.
- *FLT3-ITD*. Poor prognosis; treated with FLT3 inhibitor addition.
- *NUP98 rearrangements*. Common in children. Poor prognosis.

*Treatment*. Intensive multi-agent chemotherapy. Cytarabine + anthracycline (similar to adult 7+3 but with adjustments). Multiple consolidation cycles. APL treated with ATRA + ATO. Allogeneic transplant for high-risk disease.

*Outcomes*. Overall 5-year survival 70-75%. APL >95%. Core binding factor leukemias ~80%. KMT2A-rearranged and TP53-mutated have poor prognosis.

## Pediatric lymphomas

Pediatric lymphomas are biologically and clinically different from adult lymphomas.

### Hodgkin lymphoma

The most common pediatric lymphoma. Classical Hodgkin lymphoma is the dominant subtype.

*Presentation*. Painless cervical or mediastinal lymphadenopathy. B symptoms in some patients.

*Treatment*. Risk-stratified treatment to maximize cure while minimizing long-term effects:
- *Early-stage favorable*. Reduced chemotherapy (typically 2-4 cycles of ABVD-like regimens) with low-dose involved-field radiation. Modern approaches aim to minimize radiation exposure.
- *Early-stage unfavorable*. More chemotherapy with radiation.
- *Advanced stage*. Multi-cycle chemotherapy ± radiation.

PET-adapted approaches use interim PET response to guide treatment intensity.

Brentuximab vedotin + AVD regimens are emerging for advanced disease. Checkpoint inhibitors very active in relapsed disease.

*Outcomes*. 5-year survival >95% for early-stage; 80-90% for advanced. The challenge is balancing cure with long-term toxicity (cardiotoxicity from anthracyclines, secondary cancers from radiation, fertility effects).

### Non-Hodgkin lymphoma in children

Different distribution than adult NHL:
- *Burkitt lymphoma*. The most common pediatric NHL. Highly aggressive but highly curable with intensive treatment.
- *Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma*. Less common in children than adults.
- *Lymphoblastic lymphoma*. Often T-cell lineage. Treated similarly to T-ALL.
- *Anaplastic large cell lymphoma (ALCL)*. Often ALK-positive (better prognosis). Treated with chemotherapy ± brentuximab vedotin.

*Treatment*. Intensive multi-agent chemotherapy regimens specific to lymphoma subtype. CNS prophylaxis often needed. Stem cell transplant for high-risk or relapsed disease. Brentuximab vedotin for CD30-positive disease. Crizotinib for ALK-positive ALCL.

*Outcomes*. Generally excellent. Burkitt lymphoma 5-year survival >85% with modern intensive therapy. ALCL outcomes vary by stage but generally good.

## Survivorship and long-term effects

Childhood cancer survivors face decades of life ahead and are at risk for late effects of treatment.

*Major late effects*:

*Cardiotoxicity*. Anthracyclines (doxorubicin, daunorubicin) cause cumulative cardiotoxicity that can manifest years to decades after treatment. The Childhood Cancer Survivor Study has shown elevated rates of heart failure, coronary disease, cardiomyopathy. Prevention: cumulative dose limits, cardioprotective strategies (dexrazoxane for high-dose anthracyclines), cardiac surveillance.

*Secondary malignancies*. Survivors have elevated rates of second cancers, especially:
- After radiation therapy: solid tumors in the radiation field (breast cancer after chest radiation, thyroid cancer after neck radiation, sarcomas, others).
- After alkylating agents and etoposide: hematologic malignancies (MDS, AML) and others.
- Hereditary syndrome carriers (e.g., Li-Fraumeni, retinoblastoma): elevated risk for multiple cancers.

Risk-based screening guidelines (Children's Oncology Group Long-Term Follow-Up Guidelines) provide structured surveillance.

*Cognitive effects*. CNS-directed therapy (cranial radiation, intrathecal chemotherapy, high-dose methotrexate) can produce cognitive impairment manifesting years after treatment. Particularly significant in young children receiving cranial radiation. Has motivated efforts to reduce CNS-directed therapy intensity.

*Endocrine effects*. Cranial radiation can cause growth hormone deficiency, hypothyroidism, hypogonadism, premature ovarian failure, central adrenal insufficiency. Pelvic radiation affects gonadal function. Total body irradiation affects multiple endocrine systems.

*Fertility effects*. Many cancer treatments cause infertility. Pre-treatment fertility preservation (semen cryopreservation, oocyte/embryo cryopreservation, ovarian tissue cryopreservation) should be discussed for adolescent and young adult patients. Children cannot consent to fertility preservation, but ovarian tissue cryopreservation can sometimes be done prophylactically.

*Growth effects*. Cancer and treatment can affect growth. Radiation to growth plates. Growth hormone deficiency from cranial radiation. Spinal radiation. Skeletal abnormalities from treatment fields.

*Renal effects*. Cisplatin causes long-term renal dysfunction. Nephrectomy in Wilms tumor.

*Pulmonary effects*. Bleomycin, busulfan, certain radiation can cause pulmonary fibrosis.

*Auditory effects*. Cisplatin causes hearing loss; can be substantial in young children. Sodium thiosulfate approved for prevention in pediatric cancer patients.

*Dental effects*. Chemotherapy and head/neck radiation affect dental development.

*Psychosocial effects*. Educational, social, employment, family planning effects throughout life.

*Surveillance*. Long-term follow-up clinics provide systematic surveillance. The Children's Oncology Group Long-Term Follow-Up Guidelines provide evidence-based surveillance recommendations based on treatment exposures.

*Lifelong consequences mindset*. Pediatric oncology increasingly emphasizes treatment selection that minimizes long-term effects while maintaining cure rates. This has driven:
- Reduced cranial radiation in ALL.
- Reduced radiation in Hodgkin lymphoma.
- Risk-adapted treatment intensity.
- Use of targeted therapy and immunotherapy where possible.
- Reduced cumulative doses of toxic agents.

The challenge is balancing cure (the immediate goal) with quality of decades of subsequent life.

## Cancer in adolescents and young adults

A particular subgroup is *adolescent and young adult (AYA)* cancer patients — typically defined as ages 15-39. AYA cancer has been recognized as a distinct category with specific challenges:

*Mixed cancer types*. AYA cancers span pediatric-type cancers (some sarcomas, lymphomas) and adult-type cancers (early-onset breast and colorectal cancer, melanoma, thyroid cancer, testicular cancer, others).

*Worse outcomes than expected*. Historically, AYA patients have had less improvement in survival than younger children or older adults. The reasons are multiple — clinical trial enrollment is lower; care is split between adult and pediatric oncology with sometimes inappropriate placement; psychosocial issues are complex; biological features may be distinct.

*Treatment considerations*. Some pediatric cancers may benefit from pediatric-protocol treatment even in young adults (ALL is the classic example — adolescents and young adults treated on pediatric protocols have better outcomes than those treated on adult protocols).

*Specific psychosocial concerns*. Educational disruption, employment, dating and relationships, family planning, financial concerns, body image issues. Often less family support than younger children.

*Fertility preservation*. Particularly important for this group.

*Survivorship*. Long expected life span makes long-term effects management essential.

AYA oncology programs at specialized centers address these unique needs.

## Pediatric cancer in low- and middle-income countries

The global pediatric cancer picture is unequal:

*Outcome disparities*. Pediatric cancer 5-year survival in high-income countries exceeds 80%; in low-income countries, survival is often <20%. The gap represents access to modern treatment and supportive care, not biological differences.

*Major factors*. Late diagnosis. Limited access to specialized centers. Limited drug availability. Limited transfusion support. High treatment abandonment rates due to costs and access issues. Limited diagnostic infrastructure.

*Global initiatives*. The Global Initiative for Childhood Cancer (WHO, St. Jude Children's Research Hospital) aims to improve global pediatric cancer survival to 60% by 2030. Strategies include treatment protocol harmonization, access programs for essential drugs, training and infrastructure development, regional cooperation.

The gap between rich and poor countries in pediatric cancer outcomes is one of the largest health disparities globally. Closing it is a major focus of international cancer organizations.

## Pediatric oncology infrastructure

Effective pediatric cancer care requires substantial specialized infrastructure:

*Pediatric oncology centers*. Comprehensive children's cancer programs with specialized expertise. Major institutions in the US include St. Jude, Memorial Sloan Kettering, Children's Hospital of Philadelphia, Texas Children's, Boston Children's, Seattle Children's, and others. Internationally, similar specialized centers exist in major cities.

*Cooperative groups*. Children's Oncology Group (COG) in the US coordinates pediatric cancer research and protocols. Similar groups internationally (SIOP — Société Internationale d'Oncologie Pédiatrique — globally). The cooperative group infrastructure has been essential to pediatric oncology progress.

*Specialized supportive care*. Pediatric oncology nursing, child life services, pediatric pain management, pediatric palliative care, school services, family support.

*Long-term follow-up clinics*. For surveillance of late effects in survivors.

*Family-centered care*. Pediatric oncology recognizes that the patient is a family unit. Family support, sibling involvement, and family resource provision are integral.

*Multidisciplinary teams*. Pediatric oncologists, surgeons, radiation oncologists, neurosurgeons, pathologists, radiologists, nurses, social workers, child life specialists, chaplains, psychologists, nutritionists, physical and occupational therapists, school liaison services, all working together.

The infrastructure is concentrated. Most pediatric cancer care happens at relatively few specialized centers. This concentration enables higher quality care but creates access barriers for families far from major centers.

## What this chapter gives you

Pediatric acute lymphoblastic leukemia is one of the most successful stories in cancer medicine. Multi-agent chemotherapy in defined phases (induction, consolidation, CNS prophylaxis, delayed intensification, maintenance) over 2-3 years total duration produces >90% cure rates. MRD-guided risk stratification, targeted therapy additions (TKIs for Ph+ ALL, blinatumomab), and CAR-T cells for relapsed disease have further refined outcomes.

Pediatric acute myeloid leukemia is more aggressive but biologically diverse. APL is highly curable with ATRA + arsenic trioxide. Core binding factor leukemias have favorable outcomes. KMT2A-rearranged disease has historically poor prognosis with menin inhibitors emerging. Overall 5-year survival around 70%.

Pediatric Hodgkin lymphoma is highly curable with chemotherapy + reduced radiation, increasingly using PET-adapted approaches to balance cure with long-term toxicity reduction.

Pediatric non-Hodgkin lymphomas have different distribution than adult NHL — Burkitt lymphoma dominant, plus lymphoblastic lymphoma, anaplastic large cell lymphoma, and others. Treatment uses subtype-specific intensive regimens. Generally excellent outcomes.

Survivorship is a major emphasis in pediatric oncology. Late effects include cardiotoxicity, secondary malignancies, cognitive effects, endocrine effects, fertility effects, growth effects, renal effects, pulmonary effects, auditory effects, dental effects, and psychosocial effects. The Children's Oncology Group Long-Term Follow-Up Guidelines provide structured surveillance. Treatment is increasingly designed to minimize long-term effects while maintaining cure rates.

Adolescent and young adult (AYA) cancer is a distinct category with mixed cancer types, historically worse outcomes than younger or older patients, and specific psychosocial concerns. Pediatric-protocol treatment may be appropriate for selected diagnoses (ALL in particular). Specialized AYA programs address unique needs.

Global pediatric cancer outcomes are highly unequal, with 5-year survival exceeding 80% in high-income countries versus <20% in some low-income countries. International initiatives (Global Initiative for Childhood Cancer) aim to close the gap.

Pediatric oncology infrastructure includes specialized centers, cooperative groups (COG, SIOP), specialized supportive care, long-term follow-up clinics, family-centered care, and multidisciplinary teams. The infrastructure is concentrated at relatively few centers, enabling high quality but creating access challenges.
