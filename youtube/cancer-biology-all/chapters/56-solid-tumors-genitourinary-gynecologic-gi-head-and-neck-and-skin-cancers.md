# Chapter 56 — Solid Tumors: Genitourinary, Gynecologic, GI, Head and Neck, and Skin Cancers


## TL;DR

The cancers that don't get the headlines are the ones that often determine whether oncology actually works.

Hold the framing. The cancers covered in 31A — breast, lung, colorectal — are the most studied, the most funded, and the most refined in treatment paradigms. They are also large categories that affect millions of patients. But oncology's reach extends far beyond these. The genitourinary cancers (prostate, kidney, bladder, testicular) affect more men than breast cancer affects women. The gynecologic cancers (ovarian, endometrial, cervical) include some of the most lethal cancers in women. The GI cancers beyond colorectal include pancreatic cancer (one of the most lethal cancers overall), gastric cancer, esophageal cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, biliary tract cancers. Head and neck cancers, skin cancers — each major category has its own biology, its own treatment paradigms, and its own evolution.

This chapter — the second of two on specific solid tumors — covers these additional cancer categories. The approach is necessarily abbreviated; entire textbooks have been written about each. But the principles covered throughout this book apply, and the specific implementations illustrate how the principles translate to patient care.

Hold the question: *how do the molecular and treatment paradigms developed in major cancers extend to the broader landscape of solid tumors?*

## Genitourinary cancers

The genitourinary cancers include prostate, kidney, bladder, and testicular cancer, with substantial diversity of biology and treatment.

### Prostate cancer

The most common cancer in men in many countries. Hormone-driven biology with androgen receptor signaling central to disease (Chapter 23B).

*Epidemiology*. Increases with age. Family history, BRCA1/2 mutations, Lynch syndrome, and African ancestry all affect risk. PSA-based screening has substantially affected epidemiology — increasing detection of indolent disease while reducing late presentation.

*Screening controversy*. PSA-based screening detects many cancers but produces overdiagnosis of indolent disease. Current USPSTF guidelines recommend individualized decision-making in ages 55-69 (Chapter 17B).

*Diagnosis and risk stratification*. PSA testing, digital rectal exam, prostate MRI, transrectal ultrasound-guided biopsy. Newer approaches use multiparametric MRI to target biopsies. Risk stratification based on PSA, Gleason score, T-stage, and increasingly molecular features (PAM50 in prostate cancer, PTEN loss, others).

*Treatment by risk and stage*:

*Low-risk localized prostate cancer*. Active surveillance is increasingly the preferred approach — close monitoring with intervention only if disease progresses. Avoids the morbidity of definitive treatment for cancers that may never cause symptoms.

*Intermediate-risk localized*. Radical prostatectomy, external beam radiation therapy, or brachytherapy. Each has different toxicity profiles (urinary, sexual, bowel). The choice depends on patient factors and preferences.

*High-risk localized*. Radiation + androgen deprivation therapy for 18-36 months. Or surgery + adjuvant radiation in selected cases.

*Biochemical recurrence after definitive treatment*. Rising PSA without clinical disease. Treatment options include salvage radiation, ADT, observation, or imaging-guided targeted therapy for oligometastatic recurrence.

*Metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer*. ADT + second-generation anti-androgen (enzalutamide, apalutamide, darolutamide) OR ADT + abiraterone OR ADT + docetaxel. Triplet therapy (ADT + abiraterone + docetaxel) for selected high-volume disease.

*Castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC)*. Sequential use of: abiraterone + prednisone; enzalutamide, apalutamide, darolutamide; docetaxel; cabazitaxel; PARP inhibitors for BRCA-mutated; radium-223 for symptomatic bone metastases; lutetium-177-PSMA-617 (Pluvicto) for PSMA-positive; sipuleucel-T; pembrolizumab for MSI-H (rare).

*Outcomes*. Localized prostate cancer has excellent outcomes (10-year survival >95% for low-risk; >80% for intermediate-risk). Metastatic disease median survival has extended from 1-2 years a decade ago to 3-5+ years with modern therapy.

### Renal cell carcinoma

About 80% of kidney cancers. Multiple molecular subtypes with different biology.

*Histological subtypes*. Clear cell RCC (75%, VHL-driven), papillary RCC (10-15%), chromophobe RCC (5%), collecting duct carcinoma, renal medullary carcinoma, others.

*Risk factors*. Smoking, obesity, hypertension, end-stage renal disease, von Hippel-Lindau syndrome, Birt-Hogg-Dubé syndrome, hereditary leiomyomatosis and RCC, others.

*Diagnosis*. Often incidental on imaging done for other reasons. Imaging (CT, MRI) is highly accurate for diagnosis. Biopsy used selectively.

*Local treatment*. Partial nephrectomy when feasible (preserving kidney function); radical nephrectomy for larger tumors. Active surveillance for small (<3-4 cm) renal masses in older patients. Ablation (RFA, cryoablation) for small renal masses in selected patients.

*Adjuvant therapy*. Pembrolizumab approved as adjuvant after surgery for high-risk resected RCC. Pembrolizumab + axitinib being studied for adjuvant.

*Metastatic RCC*. The treatment paradigm has been transformed since 2005:
- *Pre-2005*. High-dose IL-2 was the only therapy with durable response potential, in 10-15% of patients with substantial toxicity.
- *2005-2015*. Multi-kinase TKIs (sunitinib, sorafenib, pazopanib) with response rates around 30% and PFS around 11 months.
- *2015-present*. Combinations with checkpoint inhibitors. Pembrolizumab + axitinib, nivolumab + cabozantinib, pembrolizumab + lenvatinib, ipilimumab + nivolumab — each with substantial survival improvements over monotherapy.

Current first-line standard depends on IMDC risk score (favorable, intermediate, poor risk based on clinical factors).

Belzutifan (HIF-2α inhibitor, Chapter 11A) approved for VHL-disease-associated RCC and recently expanded to advanced clear cell RCC after prior therapy.

*Outcomes*. Stage I 5-year survival >90%. Stage IV historically 8-15%; now 30%+ with modern combinations.

### Bladder cancer

Urothelial cancer of the bladder is the most common type. Squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma occur less frequently.

*Risk factors*. Smoking (the dominant cause), occupational exposures (aromatic amines, others), Schistosoma haematobium infection (in endemic areas), chronic urinary tract infection, prior pelvic radiation, certain medications.

*Presentation*. Painless hematuria is the most common presentation. Urinary frequency, urgency, dysuria.

*Diagnosis and staging*. Cystoscopy with biopsy. Cytology of urine. Imaging (CT urogram for upper tract evaluation). EUA (examination under anesthesia) for staging muscle invasion.

*Staging categories*:
- *Non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC)*. Confined to mucosa or lamina propria. About 70-75% of new diagnoses. Treated with TURBT (transurethral resection) + intravesical therapy.
- *Muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC)*. Invades muscularis propria. About 20% of new diagnoses. Treated with radical cystectomy (often with neoadjuvant chemotherapy) or bladder-preserving chemoradiation.
- *Metastatic bladder cancer*. Systemic therapy.

*NMIBC treatment*. TURBT + intravesical therapy with BCG (the standard) or chemotherapy (mitomycin C, gemcitabine, others). Maintenance BCG. Risk-stratified intensity. Failure of BCG can lead to cystectomy or salvage intravesical therapy (pembrolizumab for BCG-unresponsive in some cases).

*MIBC treatment*. Neoadjuvant cisplatin-based chemotherapy followed by radical cystectomy with urinary diversion. Or chemoradiation with bladder preservation. Enfortumab vedotin-based regimens are emerging.

*Metastatic bladder cancer*. First-line therapy is now enfortumab vedotin + pembrolizumab (EV-302 trial showed substantial OS benefit). Cisplatin-based chemotherapy for selected patients. Second-line includes additional immunotherapy, enfortumab vedotin, sacituzumab govitecan, erdafitinib (for FGFR3-mutated disease).

*Outcomes*. NMIBC: 5-year survival >90% but high recurrence requiring ongoing surveillance. MIBC: 5-year survival 50-60% with multimodal treatment. Metastatic: historically poor; substantially improved with recent EV + pembrolizumab approval.

### Testicular cancer

Less common but historically important as a model for cancer cure.

*Epidemiology*. Most common cancer in young men (15-35). Cryptorchidism increases risk.

*Histology*. Seminomatous (40-45%) and non-seminomatous (55-60%) germ cell tumors. The non-seminomatous category includes embryonal carcinoma, yolk sac tumor, choriocarcinoma, teratoma, and mixed.

*Diagnosis*. Testicular ultrasound, tumor markers (AFP, β-HCG, LDH). Inguinal orchiectomy is both diagnostic and therapeutic.

*Treatment*. Stage I disease typically managed with surveillance after orchiectomy; some patients receive adjuvant carboplatin (seminoma) or BEP chemotherapy (non-seminoma) for high-risk features.

Stage II-III disease: BEP chemotherapy (bleomycin, etoposide, cisplatin) is the standard. Cures most patients.

*Outcomes*. Testicular cancer has 5-year survival >95% across stages with modern therapy. It is one of the most curable cancers, including in metastatic disease. The combination of effective chemotherapy + the natural history of the disease + young patient population produces exceptional outcomes.

## Gynecologic cancers

### Ovarian cancer

The most lethal gynecologic cancer due to typically late presentation.

*Histology and subtypes*. The vast majority are epithelial ovarian cancers, with subtypes:
- *High-grade serous carcinoma (HGSC)*. The most common and most aggressive. Often arises from fallopian tube fimbriae. Universal TP53 mutations; high rates of HRD; sensitive to chemotherapy and PARP inhibitors.
- *Low-grade serous carcinoma*. Less aggressive but more chemotherapy-resistant.
- *Endometrioid carcinoma*. Often associated with endometriosis.
- *Clear cell carcinoma*. Often resistant to chemotherapy.
- *Mucinous carcinoma*. Distinctive biology, often more chemotherapy-resistant.

Germ cell tumors and sex cord-stromal tumors occur less frequently.

*Risk factors*. BRCA1/2 mutations (substantial risk), Lynch syndrome, family history, nulliparity, endometriosis (clear cell), older age.

*Screening*. No effective screening for ovarian cancer in the general population. High-risk women (BRCA carriers) may undergo TVUS + CA-125 surveillance, but the value is debated. Prophylactic salpingo-oophorectomy is recommended for BRCA carriers after childbearing.

*Presentation*. Often vague symptoms (bloating, pelvic pain, urinary frequency, early satiety) that lead to late diagnosis. Most patients present with stage III or IV disease.

*Diagnosis and staging*. Imaging (CT, MRI, sometimes PET), CA-125, surgical staging through exploratory laparotomy with cytoreduction.

*Treatment*. Primary cytoreductive surgery to remove as much disease as possible (residual disease should be <1 cm or microscopic). Adjuvant or neoadjuvant chemotherapy (carboplatin + paclitaxel) is the standard.

For recurrence, treatment depends on platinum sensitivity (defined by interval since last platinum):
- *Platinum-sensitive (>6 months since platinum)*: platinum-based regimens.
- *Platinum-resistant (<6 months)*: non-platinum chemotherapy.

PARP inhibitors (olaparib, niraparib, rucaparib) have transformed treatment, especially as maintenance after platinum response. Olaparib + bevacizumab maintenance for HRD-positive disease. Mirvetuximab soravtansine (folate receptor α-targeted ADC) approved for platinum-resistant FRα-positive disease.

*Outcomes*. Stage I 5-year survival >90%. Stage III about 40%. Stage IV about 20%. Modern maintenance therapy has extended PFS and OS in BRCA-mutated and HRD-positive disease substantially.

### Endometrial cancer

The most common gynecologic cancer in developed countries.

*Histology and molecular subtypes*. The TCGA molecular classification:
- *POLE ultramutated*. Very high mutation burden, excellent prognosis, responds well to immunotherapy.
- *Microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H)*. About 25-30%, responds to immunotherapy.
- *Copy-number low*. Endometrioid histology, hormone-receptor-positive, generally good prognosis.
- *Copy-number high*. Includes serous carcinoma; aggressive; TP53-mutated.

The molecular classification is increasingly integrated with histological diagnosis.

*Risk factors*. Obesity (major risk factor), unopposed estrogen exposure, nulliparity, late menopause, tamoxifen exposure, Lynch syndrome, family history.

*Presentation*. Postmenopausal bleeding is the classic presentation.

*Diagnosis and staging*. Endometrial biopsy. Surgical staging through hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy and lymphadenectomy.

*Treatment*. Surgery is the primary treatment. Adjuvant therapy based on stage and risk:
- Low-risk: surgery alone.
- High-risk: adjuvant chemotherapy and/or radiation.

For advanced or recurrent disease:
- *MSI-H or POLE-mutated*: pembrolizumab or dostarlimab.
- *Other subtypes*: chemotherapy (carboplatin + paclitaxel) ± targeted therapy. Lenvatinib + pembrolizumab approved for advanced disease.

*Outcomes*. Stage I 5-year survival 90-95%. Stage IV around 20%.

### Cervical cancer

Now substantially preventable through HPV vaccination and screening.

*Etiology*. Caused by persistent HPV infection (Chapter 8B), particularly HPV-16 and HPV-18.

*Screening*. Cervical cancer screening with Pap smear and/or HPV testing detects precancerous lesions for treatment (Chapter 17B). The screening has dramatically reduced cervical cancer incidence and mortality in countries with effective screening programs.

*Histology*. Squamous cell carcinoma (most common). Adenocarcinoma (increasing relative frequency).

*Treatment*. Stage I (microinvasive to small tumors): cervical conization or radical hysterectomy (with pelvic lymphadenectomy for invasive cancers). Stage II-IV (advanced local or regional): concurrent chemoradiation (cisplatin-based) with brachytherapy. Recurrent or metastatic: chemotherapy + pembrolizumab or bevacizumab; tisotumab vedotin for selected refractory disease.

*Outcomes*. Stage I 5-year survival >90%. Stage IV poor (15-20%). Global cervical cancer remains a major cause of female cancer death in countries with limited screening — illustrating the impact of public health infrastructure.

## Gastrointestinal cancers (beyond colorectal)

### Pancreatic cancer

One of the most lethal cancers. Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) accounts for 95%+.

*Risk factors*. Smoking, family history (BRCA, Lynch, FAMMM, hereditary pancreatitis), chronic pancreatitis, obesity, diabetes (both risk factor and consequence).

*Presentation*. Often late. Vague abdominal symptoms, weight loss, painless jaundice (for tumors in head of pancreas). Most patients present with locally advanced or metastatic disease.

*Diagnosis*. CT or MRI for diagnosis and staging. EUS-guided biopsy is standard. CA 19-9 as biomarker.

*Treatment*. Only 15-20% present with resectable disease. Surgery (Whipple procedure for pancreatic head, distal pancreatectomy for body/tail) is the only curative option. Adjuvant chemotherapy (modified FOLFIRINOX, gemcitabine + capecitabine) extends survival.

For metastatic disease: FOLFIRINOX (younger fit patients) or gemcitabine + nab-paclitaxel. KRAS G12C inhibitors (sotorasib, adagrasib) for the 1-3% with G12C mutations. PARP inhibitors (olaparib) for BRCA-mutated disease. NTRK or other rare targetable alterations.

The pancreatic cancer treatment landscape has changed less dramatically than other cancers. Most patients have no actionable mutations beyond KRAS G12D and G12V (still not druggable in 2026), and chemotherapy remains the foundation.

*Outcomes*. Stage I-II 5-year survival 25-30% (much better than historical due to better adjuvant therapy and surgical care). Stage IV around 3-5%. Overall 5-year survival around 12% — one of the worst across cancer types.

### Hepatocellular carcinoma

Liver cancer typically arising in cirrhotic livers.

*Risk factors*. Hepatitis B and C, alcoholic cirrhosis, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, aflatoxin exposure, hereditary hemochromatosis, others.

*Screening*. Patients with cirrhosis undergo screening with alpha-fetoprotein and abdominal ultrasound every 6 months. Effectiveness depends on adherence.

*Diagnosis*. Often based on imaging (CT or MRI) characteristics; biopsy not always necessary. AFP, AFP-L3, DCP as biomarkers.

*Staging*. BCLC (Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer) staging integrates tumor characteristics with liver function and performance status.

*Treatment*:
- *Very early/early stage*: surgical resection, ablation (radiofrequency, microwave), or liver transplantation.
- *Intermediate stage*: transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) or transarterial radioembolization (TARE).
- *Advanced stage*: systemic therapy. Atezolizumab + bevacizumab is first-line standard (IMbrave150 trial). Tremelimumab + durvalumab is alternative (STRIDE regimen). Sorafenib and lenvatinib are TKI options. Second-line: cabozantinib, regorafenib, ramucirumab.

*Outcomes*. Stage I 5-year survival can exceed 70% with optimal treatment. Stage IV 5-year survival <15%. Improvements with atezolizumab + bevacizumab in metastatic disease have been substantial.

### Gastric and gastroesophageal junction cancer

Adenocarcinoma is the dominant histology globally; squamous cell carcinoma of the esophagus is more common in some Asian and African populations.

*Risk factors*. *H. pylori* infection (major), GERD with Barrett esophagus (for adenocarcinoma of esophagus and GEJ), tobacco, alcohol, salt and processed meat consumption, family history.

*Molecular subtypes (TCGA gastric cancer subtypes)*:
- *EBV-positive*. Often PD-L1-high; responds to immunotherapy.
- *MSI-high*. Responds to immunotherapy.
- *Genomically stable*. Diffuse-type histology often; CDH1 mutations.
- *Chromosomally unstable*. TP53 mutations; often HER2 amplification.

*Treatment*. Early-stage: surgery (gastrectomy or esophagectomy with appropriate lymphadenectomy). Adjuvant or perioperative chemotherapy (FLOT regimen for gastric/GEJ).

Locally advanced: neoadjuvant chemotherapy or chemoradiation; surgery; adjuvant therapy.

Metastatic: chemotherapy backbone (platinum + fluoropyrimidine) plus targeted therapy:
- HER2-positive: trastuzumab + chemo first-line; T-DXd second-line (DESTINY-Gastric trials).
- MSI-H: pembrolizumab.
- PD-L1-positive: pembrolizumab + chemotherapy or nivolumab + chemotherapy first-line.
- CLDN18.2-positive: zolbetuximab + chemotherapy approved in 2024.

*Outcomes*. Stage I 5-year survival 65-70%. Stage IV around 5-10%. Improving with multiple recent approvals.

### Cholangiocarcinoma and other biliary cancers

Cancers of the bile ducts and gallbladder. Generally aggressive with limited treatment options historically.

*Risk factors*. Primary sclerosing cholangitis, hepatitis B/C, liver flukes (in endemic areas), choledochal cysts.

*Subtypes*. Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, perihilar (Klatskin) cholangiocarcinoma, distal cholangiocarcinoma. Gallbladder cancer.

*Treatment*. Surgery for resectable disease (often complex due to anatomic location). Adjuvant chemotherapy (capecitabine).

Advanced disease: gemcitabine + cisplatin ± durvalumab first-line. Molecular profiling reveals targetable alterations in 30-40%:
- *FGFR2 fusions*: pemigatinib, infigratinib, futibatinib.
- *IDH1 mutations*: ivosidenib.
- *BRAF V600E*: dabrafenib + trametinib.
- *HER2 amplification*: trastuzumab-based therapy.
- *NTRK fusions*: larotrectinib or entrectinib.

The molecular targeting has substantially expanded options for advanced cholangiocarcinoma.

*Outcomes*. Resected cholangiocarcinoma 5-year survival 30-40%. Metastatic disease 5-year survival <5% historically; improving with molecular-targeted therapy.

### Other GI cancers

*Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma*. Treated with chemoradiation (definitive or preoperative); checkpoint inhibitors approved for advanced disease (pembrolizumab + chemo; nivolumab + chemo).

*Anal cancer*. Mostly HPV-driven squamous cell carcinoma. Treated curatively with chemoradiation (mitomycin C + 5-FU + radiation — the Nigro regimen). Surgery only for failures.

*GI stromal tumors (GIST)*. Mesenchymal tumors with KIT or PDGFRA mutations. Treated with imatinib (first-line), sunitinib (second-line), regorafenib (third-line), ripretinib (fourth-line), avapritinib (PDGFRA D842V-mutant). The TKI sequence has transformed outcomes.

*Neuroendocrine tumors*. Diverse group with diverse behaviors. Treatment depends on subtype, grade, location, and functional status. Somatostatin analogs (octreotide, lanreotide), everolimus, sunitinib, lutetium-177-DOTATATE (theranostic approach), capecitabine + temozolomide, chemotherapy for high-grade variants.

## Head and neck cancers

A diverse group including squamous cell carcinomas of the oral cavity, oropharynx, larynx, hypopharynx, nasopharynx, sinonasal tract, and salivary glands. Plus thyroid cancer (often classified separately).

*Squamous cell carcinoma risk factors*. Tobacco, alcohol, HPV (particularly for oropharyngeal cancer — increasingly the dominant cause), Epstein-Barr virus (for nasopharyngeal cancer), betel nut chewing (for oral cancer in some regions).

*HPV-positive vs HPV-negative oropharyngeal cancer*. Substantially different biology. HPV-positive disease has better prognosis and may be candidate for de-escalated treatment to reduce toxicity.

*Treatment*:
- *Early-stage*. Surgery or radiation as primary treatment.
- *Locally advanced*. Concurrent chemoradiation (cisplatin + radiation; cetuximab + radiation as alternative). Multidisciplinary care essential.
- *Recurrent/metastatic*. First-line: pembrolizumab ± chemotherapy depending on PD-L1 expression. Second-line and beyond: cetuximab, chemotherapy, additional immunotherapy options.

*Thyroid cancer*. Papillary thyroid cancer (most common, generally indolent), follicular, medullary (RET-driven), anaplastic (aggressive). Treatment: thyroidectomy ± radioactive iodine. Targeted therapy for advanced disease: lenvatinib, sorafenib, selpercatinib (RET-mutated MTC), dabrafenib + trametinib (BRAF V600E anaplastic).

*Salivary gland cancers*. Diverse histology with different prognoses and treatments. NTRK fusions in secretory carcinoma; HER2 amplification in some; androgen receptor expression in salivary duct carcinoma allowing anti-androgen therapy.

*Outcomes*. HPV-positive oropharyngeal cancer 5-year survival 70-85%. HPV-negative 40-60%. Thyroid cancer overall >95%. Anaplastic thyroid cancer historically <10%.

## Skin cancers

The most common cancers worldwide but the most preventable.

### Melanoma

The most lethal common skin cancer.

*Risk factors*. UV exposure (especially sunburns), fair skin, family history, dysplastic nevi, immunosuppression, prior melanoma.

*Biology*. BRAF mutations (50%), NRAS mutations (15-20%), NF1 mutations, c-KIT mutations (acral and mucosal). High mutation burden in UV-driven melanoma.

*Diagnosis*. ABCDE features (asymmetry, border, color, diameter, evolution) guide clinical suspicion. Excisional biopsy with margins. Pathological diagnosis with Breslow thickness, ulceration, mitotic rate.

*Treatment*:
- *Early-stage*. Wide local excision with margins (1-2 cm depending on Breslow thickness). Sentinel lymph node biopsy for Breslow >0.8 mm or with high-risk features.
- *Stage III (nodal disease)*. Completion lymphadenectomy versus observation (MSLT-II showed observation is often acceptable). Adjuvant immunotherapy (pembrolizumab, nivolumab, ipilimumab + nivolumab) or targeted therapy (dabrafenib + trametinib for BRAF V600E).
- *Stage IV*. Immunotherapy as foundation: ipilimumab + nivolumab, nivolumab + relatlimab, or anti-PD-1 monotherapy. BRAF + MEK inhibitors for BRAF V600E. Targeted therapy for other rare alterations.

*Outcomes*. Stage I 5-year survival 95%+. Stage III 50-80% depending on N-stage. Stage IV historically <10%; now 50%+ with combination immunotherapy.

### Basal cell and squamous cell carcinoma

The most common skin cancers. Generally good prognosis with treatment.

*Treatment*. Surgical excision (Mohs micrographic surgery for difficult locations). Cryotherapy or topical therapy for some superficial lesions. Hedgehog pathway inhibitors (vismodegib, sonidegib) for advanced or metastatic basal cell carcinoma. Cemiplimab approved for advanced cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma.

*Outcomes*. Very high cure rates with appropriate treatment for both.

### Merkel cell carcinoma

A rare but aggressive neuroendocrine skin cancer.

*Etiology*. About 80% caused by Merkel cell polyomavirus; 20% UV-driven (high mutation burden).

*Treatment*. Surgery for localized disease. Adjuvant radiation often. For advanced/metastatic disease, avelumab or pembrolizumab — Merkel cell carcinoma has been remarkably responsive to immunotherapy.

*Outcomes*. Stage I 60-70% 5-year survival. Stage IV historically very poor; now substantially improved with immunotherapy.

## What this chapter gives you

The solid tumors beyond breast, lung, and colorectal cancer represent a substantial portion of cancer burden globally with diverse biology and management.

*Genitourinary cancers* include prostate (hormone-driven, with sophisticated sequential therapy), renal cell carcinoma (transformed by immunotherapy + TKI combinations), bladder cancer (recent transformation with enfortumab vedotin + pembrolizumab), and testicular cancer (highly curable, often >95% survival).

*Gynecologic cancers* include ovarian (typically late presentation, BRCA/HRD-driven biology with PARP inhibitor maintenance), endometrial (molecular subtypes increasingly guide treatment including pembrolizumab for MSI-H), and cervical (largely preventable through HPV vaccination and screening).

*Gastrointestinal cancers beyond colorectal* include pancreatic cancer (highly lethal, limited treatment advances), hepatocellular carcinoma (atezolizumab + bevacizumab transforming metastatic treatment), gastric/GEJ (HER2-targeted therapy, immunotherapy, CLDN18.2 expansion), cholangiocarcinoma (molecular profiling reveals targetable alterations in 30-40%), esophageal cancer, anal cancer (curative chemoradiation), GIST (TKI sequence has transformed outcomes), and neuroendocrine tumors.

*Head and neck cancers* depend heavily on HPV status (HPV-positive disease has better prognosis), with treatment typically chemoradiation for locally advanced disease and immunotherapy ± chemotherapy for advanced disease. Thyroid cancer has diverse biology with effective treatment for most subtypes.

*Skin cancers* include melanoma (transformed by immunotherapy with substantial improvements in metastatic survival), basal and squamous cell carcinomas (generally curable), and Merkel cell carcinoma (responsive to immunotherapy despite aggressive biology).

The pattern across these diverse cancers illustrates the principles covered throughout this book — integrated molecular and histological characterization, biomarker-driven treatment selection, multimodality therapy, immune checkpoint inhibitors expanding across cancer types, targeted therapy where actionable alterations exist, and continuing advances driven by molecular understanding.

This chapter concludes the specific cancer type discussions. The remaining chapters cover hematological malignancies (32-33), comprehensive special topics, and the book's synthesis.
