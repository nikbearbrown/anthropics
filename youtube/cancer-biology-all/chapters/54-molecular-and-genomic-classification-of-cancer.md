A breast cancer in 2026 isn't a single diagnosis. It's a histological category plus a molecular profile plus a clinical risk assessment, and the molecular profile is increasingly the part that determines treatment.

Hold the framing. Chapter 30A covered histological and anatomical classification — the foundation that has organized cancer medicine for over a century. The framework remains essential, but the past 25 years have added a second axis of classification: molecular and genomic features that often matter more for treatment than histology alone.

The reframing has been profound. Two breast cancers that look identical under the microscope can have completely different optimal treatments based on hormone receptor status, HER2 status, and mutation profile. A lung adenocarcinoma with an EGFR mutation gets a different first-line treatment than one with an ALK rearrangement, KRAS G12C mutation, or no targetable alteration. A microsatellite-instability-high cancer responds to pembrolizumab regardless of tissue of origin. The histological diagnosis tells us what the cancer looks like; the molecular profile tells us what to do about it.

This chapter — the second of two on cancer classification — covers molecular and genomic classification, the integration with histological frameworks, and the emerging multi-dimensional approaches that increasingly characterize modern oncology.

Hold the question: *given that molecular features now drive much of cancer treatment, how do we organize and integrate molecular classification with the traditional anatomic and histological frameworks?*

## The major molecular classification dimensions

Modern cancer classification incorporates multiple molecular dimensions:

*Driver mutations*. Specific genetic alterations that drive cancer growth. Examples:
- *EGFR mutations* in lung adenocarcinoma.
- *KRAS mutations* (especially G12C) in lung cancer, colorectal cancer, pancreatic cancer.
- *BRAF V600E* in melanoma, thyroid cancer, others.
- *TP53 mutations* in many cancers (most commonly mutated gene overall).
- *PIK3CA mutations* in breast cancer, endometrial cancer, others.
- *Many others*.

*Copy number alterations*. Gene amplifications or deletions:
- *HER2 amplification* in breast cancer, gastric cancer.
- *MYC amplification* in many cancers.
- *MET amplification* in some lung cancers.
- *CDKN2A deletion* in many cancers.

*Gene fusions and rearrangements*. Chromosomal translocations creating fusion proteins:
- *BCR-ABL* (Philadelphia chromosome) in CML.
- *EML4-ALK* and other ALK fusions in lung cancer.
- *ROS1 fusions* in lung cancer.
- *RET fusions* in lung and thyroid cancer.
- *NTRK fusions* in various cancers.
- *FGFR fusions* in cholangiocarcinoma and others.
- *Many others*.

*Hormone receptor status*. ER, PR, AR expression in hormone-driven cancers.

*Mismatch repair status*. dMMR/MSI-H status in many cancers.

*Tumor mutational burden*. Total mutations per megabase.

*Homologous recombination deficiency*. Through BRCA1/2 mutations, HRD genomic scars, or both.

*PD-L1 expression*. For immunotherapy decision-making.

*Methylation patterns*. Particularly important in CNS tumors and some others.

*Gene expression profiles*. Including signatures like Oncotype DX recurrence score, PAM50 intrinsic subtypes, MammaPrint, others.

*Specific molecular subtypes*. Comprehensive molecular classifications for specific cancers, integrating multiple features.

The combination of these dimensions provides far richer characterization than histology alone. Modern cancer diagnosis ideally integrates molecular and histological information to optimize treatment decisions.

## Comprehensive molecular subtyping by cancer

Several cancer types have well-developed molecular subtype classifications that complement histological diagnosis:

*Breast cancer intrinsic subtypes (PAM50)*. Based on gene expression patterns:
- *Luminal A*. ER-positive, lower proliferation, generally better prognosis.
- *Luminal B*. ER-positive, higher proliferation, more aggressive than Luminal A.
- *HER2-enriched*. HER2 amplification, often ER-negative.
- *Basal-like*. Often triple-negative, share features with basal epithelial cells.
- *Normal-like*. Rare; characteristics resemble normal breast.

The PAM50 classification correlates with prognosis and treatment response. Commercial assays (Prosigna) provide molecular subtyping clinically.

*Colorectal cancer consensus molecular subtypes (CMS)*. Four major subtypes:
- *CMS1*. Microsatellite-instability, immune-rich, often right-sided.
- *CMS2*. Canonical (Wnt/MYC-driven), classical adenocarcinoma.
- *CMS3*. Metabolic dysregulation.
- *CMS4*. Mesenchymal, EMT-high, often worst prognosis.

The CMS classification has prognostic value and may predict treatment response.

*Lung adenocarcinoma molecular subtypes*. Based on driver mutations and other features:
- *EGFR-mutant*. Treated with EGFR TKIs.
- *ALK-rearranged*. Treated with ALK inhibitors.
- *ROS1-rearranged*. Treated with ROS1 inhibitors.
- *BRAF V600E*. Treated with BRAF/MEK inhibitors.
- *KRAS G12C*. Treated with sotorasib or adagrasib.
- *MET exon 14 mutations*. Treated with capmatinib or tepotinib.
- *RET fusions*. Treated with selpercatinib or pralsetinib.
- *NTRK fusions*. Treated with larotrectinib or entrectinib.
- *Others without specific actionable mutations*. Treated with chemo, immunotherapy, or combinations.

The classification has fundamentally reshaped lung cancer management.

*Glioblastoma molecular subtypes*. The WHO 2021 classification heavily integrates molecular features:
- *IDH-wildtype glioblastoma*. The "classical" adult glioblastoma. Subtypes include classical, mesenchymal, proneural.
- *IDH-mutant astrocytoma*. Better prognosis than IDH-wildtype.
- *IDH-mutant oligodendroglioma* (with 1p/19q codeletion). Best prognosis.
- *Pediatric glioma subtypes*. Different biology and treatment.

The molecular classification has substantially refined glioma diagnosis and treatment.

*AML molecular subtypes*. The 2022 ICC and 5th edition WHO Classification organize AML primarily by molecular features:
- *AML with specific genetic abnormalities* (RUNX1-RUNX1T1, CBFB-MYH11, PML-RARA, KMT2A rearrangements, MECOM rearrangements, NPM1 mutations, CEBPA mutations, others).
- *AML with myelodysplasia-related changes*.
- *AML with mutated TP53*.
- *Therapy-related AML*.
- *AML, NOS (not otherwise specified)*.

The molecular classification informs treatment decisions and prognosis. For example, FLT3-mutated AML is treated with FLT3 inhibitors; IDH-mutated AML with IDH inhibitors; APL (PML-RARA) with ATRA + arsenic trioxide.

*B-cell lymphoma subtypes*. DLBCL has been reclassified based on cell of origin (GCB vs ABC vs unclassified) and genetic subtypes (MCD, BN2, EZB, ST2, N1, A53). The classifications correlate with prognosis and may guide treatment.

*Other cancers* with well-developed molecular subtyping include ovarian cancer (HRD vs non-HRD), gastric cancer (TCGA subtypes — EBV-positive, MSI-H, genomically stable, chromosomally unstable), pancreatic cancer (classical vs basal-like), and others.

The trend is clear: molecular subtyping is becoming integral to cancer classification across cancer types. The implementation requires substantial diagnostic infrastructure.

## The Cancer Genome Atlas and large-scale genomics

The systematic molecular characterization of cancer received major impetus from the *Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)* project (2006-2018), a multi-institutional collaboration that comprehensively profiled over 11,000 tumors across 33 cancer types.

TCGA produced comprehensive molecular characterization for each cancer type, including:
- Whole-exome sequencing.
- Copy number profiling.
- DNA methylation profiling.
- mRNA expression profiling.
- microRNA expression.
- Protein expression (RPPA).
- Histopathologic and clinical correlation.

The data have been an enormous resource for cancer research. Multiple subsequent projects have extended the work:

*International Cancer Genome Consortium (ICGC)*. Global expansion with similar approaches.

*Pan-Cancer Analysis of Whole Genomes (PCAWG)*. Whole-genome sequencing of TCGA tumors with deeper structural variant analysis.

*GENIE (Genomics Evidence Neoplasia Information Exchange)*. AACR-led project sharing molecular and clinical data from cancer centers worldwide.

*Pan-Cancer Atlas*. Integrated analyses across TCGA cancer types.

*HMF (Hartwig Medical Foundation)*. Dutch initiative providing comprehensive whole-genome sequencing for metastatic cancer patients.

These resources have produced fundamental insights into cancer biology and have informed clinical practice and drug development. The data are publicly available, enabling researchers worldwide to use them.

The molecular insights from these projects include:

*The cancer driver landscape*. Identifying recurrently mutated genes across cancer types. The number of recurrent cancer drivers is finite — perhaps 100-300 genes accounting for most driver mutations across cancers.

*Mutational signatures*. Patterns of mutations that reflect specific mutagenic processes (UV light, tobacco smoke, alkylating agents, mismatch repair deficiency, APOBEC, others). The signatures provide insight into cancer etiology and can guide diagnosis (the Cosmic mutational signatures database).

*Pan-cancer pathways*. Recognition that disparate cancer types share common pathway alterations. PI3K/AKT pathway, RTK signaling, cell cycle dysregulation, p53 pathway, chromatin modification — all hit across many cancer types.

*Cancer hallmarks at molecular level*. The Hanahan-Weinberg hallmarks correlate with specific molecular alterations.

*Tumor heterogeneity*. Within and between tumors, including subclonal architecture.

The molecular characterization continues to deepen. Whole-genome sequencing, single-cell technologies, spatial methods, and multi-omic integration are all extending the picture.

## Practical molecular classification in 2026

For an individual patient with cancer in 2026, the molecular classification involves several components:

*Comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP)*. NGS-based profiling of 300+ cancer-related genes. Standard for most metastatic cancers and increasingly for early-stage cancers being considered for adjuvant therapy. Provides:
- Point mutations and small indels.
- Copy number alterations.
- Structural variants (fusions, rearrangements).
- Tumor mutational burden.
- Microsatellite instability status.
- Sometimes homologous recombination deficiency.
- Quality metrics for the assay.

*Specific biomarker testing* (additional to or instead of CGP):
- *Hormone receptor and HER2 status* in breast cancer (IHC/FISH).
- *PD-L1 expression* (IHC) when relevant.
- *Mismatch repair status* (IHC or MSI testing).
- *Specific mutation testing* for some specific clinical decisions.

*Liquid biopsy*. ctDNA-based profiling when tissue is insufficient or for monitoring.

*Pathology integration*. The molecular results are integrated with histological diagnosis and clinical findings.

*Molecular tumor board*. Multidisciplinary review of complex cases.

*Treatment selection*. Based on the integrated profile, with specific drugs matched to specific alterations.

*Monitoring*. Serial liquid biopsies during treatment to detect resistance.

The full workflow takes 1-3 weeks from biopsy to actionable molecular information. The infrastructure is more developed at academic centers; community oncology practices have variable access.

## Integrated histological-molecular classification

The integration of histological and molecular classifications has produced disease definitions that go beyond either dimension alone:

*WHO Classification 5th edition (rolling out)*. Disease-specific volumes that integrate molecular features into pathological diagnosis. The CNS tumor classification (2021) was the first major revision; others have followed.

*Breast cancer classification*. Combines histological subtype (ductal, lobular, special types), grade, with hormone receptor and HER2 status, plus increasingly molecular features (PAM50 intrinsic subtype, Oncotype DX, BRCA status, PIK3CA mutations, others).

*Lung adenocarcinoma*. Histological growth patterns combined with driver mutation profile, PD-L1 status, TMB. The "comprehensive lung cancer classification" includes both dimensions.

*Glioma*. The 2021 WHO classification fundamentally integrates molecular features (IDH status, 1p/19q codeletion, TERT promoter, CDKN2A/B deletion, others) with histological grade. A diagnosis like "diffuse astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, 1p/19q intact, CDKN2A/B retained, grade 2" provides comprehensive characterization.

*AML*. The 2022 ICC and 5th WHO classifications heavily integrate molecular features.

*Mature B-cell lymphomas*. Genetic and gene expression subtypes increasingly integrated with traditional classifications.

*Sarcoma*. Many sarcoma subtypes are now defined by specific molecular features (specific gene fusions for Ewing sarcoma, synovial sarcoma, dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans, GIST KIT/PDGFRA mutations, others).

The integrated classifications provide:
- *Better diagnostic precision*. Distinguishing biologically distinct entities that look histologically similar.
- *Treatment guidance*. Molecular features often determine treatment selection.
- *Prognostic refinement*. Molecular features add to traditional prognostic markers.
- *Research organization*. Better-defined entities for clinical research.

The challenge is implementation. Integrated classifications require both pathological expertise and molecular testing capability. The implementation infrastructure is uneven, particularly outside major academic centers.

## Tumor-agnostic classifications

A particularly important development is *tumor-agnostic classification* — defining cancers by molecular features regardless of tissue of origin.

The major tumor-agnostic categories with FDA approvals:

*Microsatellite-instability-high (MSI-H) / mismatch repair deficient (dMMR) tumors*. Treated with pembrolizumab, dostarlimab, or other immune checkpoint inhibitors regardless of tissue type.

*NTRK fusion-positive tumors*. Treated with larotrectinib or entrectinib regardless of tissue type.

*High tumor mutational burden tumors* (TMB ≥10 mut/Mb). Treated with pembrolizumab.

*BRAF V600E-positive tumors*. Treated with dabrafenib + trametinib across solid tumors (approved 2022).

*RET-altered tumors*. Selpercatinib and pralsetinib for RET fusions and certain mutations.

The tumor-agnostic approach represents a fundamental shift in how cancers are classified for treatment purposes. The molecular alteration is the actionable feature; the tissue of origin is secondary. The list of tumor-agnostic approvals continues to grow.

The classification implications:
- *A patient is classified by both tissue (lung, breast, etc.) AND by actionable molecular features*. The latter may dominate treatment selection.
- *Comprehensive molecular profiling* is essential for identifying tumor-agnostic-relevant alterations.
- *Treatment decisions* increasingly depend on molecular profile across cancer types.

The tumor-agnostic framework is one of the major directions in oncology. It will likely continue to expand as more molecular-based therapies are approved.

## Classification challenges and limitations

Several challenges affect modern molecular classification:

*Heterogeneity within tumors*. Single biopsies may not capture the full molecular landscape. Spatial heterogeneity and clonal evolution complicate classification.

*Variant interpretation*. Distinguishing pathogenic mutations from benign variants and variants of unknown significance (VUS). The interpretation requires expertise and evolving knowledge bases.

*Implementation gaps*. Access to molecular profiling varies. Cost, reimbursement, infrastructure, and expertise all limit broader implementation.

*Rapidly evolving knowledge*. The classification frameworks change as new information emerges. Keeping current is challenging.

*Workforce limitations*. Specialized expertise (molecular pathology, bioinformatics, oncologists with molecular medicine training) is limited.

*International variability*. Different countries have different testing access and different classifications used. International research and care coordination requires translation.

*Tumor evolution over time*. Cancers change during treatment. The classification at diagnosis may not reflect the cancer at progression.

*Confidence intervals*. Many classifications have inherent uncertainty. The boundary between subtypes may not be sharp.

These challenges have driven ongoing development of better classification systems, AI-based interpretation tools, and improved implementation infrastructure.

## Future directions in cancer classification

Several trends will shape cancer classification over the next decade:

*Integration of multi-omic data*. Combining genomic, transcriptomic, epigenomic, proteomic, and metabolomic data for comprehensive cancer characterization.

*Spatial information*. Spatial transcriptomics and proteomics adding location-specific molecular information to traditional histology.

*Single-cell information*. Cellular heterogeneity within tumors becoming part of standard characterization.

*Microbiome data*. Tumor and gut microbiome composition as additional dimensions.

*AI-driven classification*. Machine learning approaches integrating diverse data types to produce optimal classifications.

*Dynamic classifications*. Classifications that update over time as cancer evolves, supported by liquid biopsy monitoring.

*Trans-cancer classifications*. Beyond tumor-agnostic approaches, classifications based on molecular and biological features regardless of histology.

*Patient-derived model integration*. Functional drug testing results integrated into classification for treatment selection.

*Outcomes-based refinement*. Classifications validated by their utility in predicting outcomes, with ongoing refinement.

*Implementation tools*. Decision support, clinical workflows, and accessibility improvements to make molecular classification universally available.

The next decade will likely bring increasingly sophisticated, multi-dimensional, and clinically actionable cancer classifications. The implementation infrastructure must keep pace with the science.
