A child's cancer is biologically different from an adult's cancer, and that difference is what allows us to cure most of them.

Hold the framing. Cancer in children is rare — about 16,000 new diagnoses per year in the United States, compared to nearly 2 million adult cancers. But the impact is enormous. Every child diagnosed represents a life that could be cut short or could be saved with decades to live. The biology is distinct. The treatments are different. The institutional and emotional infrastructure is different. The success in pediatric oncology has been one of the great achievements of modern medicine — childhood cancer cure rates have risen from <30% in 1960 to >80% today.

The difference matters because pediatric cancers reflect different biological origins than adult cancers. Most childhood cancers arise from developmental cells (neuroblasts, primitive renal cells, immature lymphocytes) rather than from accumulated mutations in adult tissues. They often have specific genetic drivers (gene fusions, single dominant alterations) rather than the complex mutation landscapes of adult cancers. They respond differently to chemotherapy — generally better, with higher cure rates per dose. They also create different long-term consequences — childhood cancer survivors live for decades after treatment, and treatment toxicities have lifelong implications in ways that don't apply to most adult cancers.

This chapter — the first of two on pediatric cancers — covers the major pediatric solid tumors: neuroblastoma, Wilms tumor, medulloblastoma and other CNS tumors, sarcomas, and other solid cancers of childhood. The next — 34B — covers pediatric leukemias and lymphomas, plus broader principles of pediatric oncology including survivorship.

## The pediatric cancer landscape

Pediatric cancer differs from adult cancer in several important ways:

*Different biology*. Most childhood cancers arise from developmental tissues. The cell of origin is often a primitive cell that has not yet fully differentiated. The genetic drivers are frequently single dominant alterations (gene fusions, specific mutations) rather than the accumulated mutation landscapes of adult cancers.

*Different distribution*. Pediatric cancer types are different from adult cancers:
- *Leukemias* (~30% of pediatric cancers, predominantly ALL). Most common.
- *CNS tumors* (~25%). Brain and spinal cord tumors.
- *Lymphomas* (~10%). Hodgkin and non-Hodgkin.
- *Neuroblastoma* (~5-7%). The most common extracranial solid tumor in young children.
- *Wilms tumor* (~5%). Renal tumor of children.
- *Soft tissue sarcomas* (~5%). Rhabdomyosarcoma and other types.
- *Bone tumors* (~3-4%). Osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma.
- *Germ cell tumors* (~3%).
- *Retinoblastoma* (~3%).
- *Hepatic tumors* (~1-2%). Hepatoblastoma.
- *Carcinomas* (~5%). The "adult-type" cancers are uncommon but increase with age.

The list contrasts sharply with adult cancer epidemiology, where carcinomas dominate.

*Different responses to treatment*. Many pediatric cancers are more chemotherapy-sensitive than adult cancers. Cure rates with multi-agent chemotherapy are often dramatically higher.

*Different long-term considerations*. Children who survive cancer have decades of life ahead. Treatment-related effects (cardiotoxicity, secondary cancers, infertility, growth and developmental effects, cognitive effects) have lifelong implications.

*Different infrastructure*. Pediatric oncology is concentrated at specialized centers (children's hospitals with comprehensive cancer programs). Clinical trial enrollment is high — about 50-60% of children with cancer are enrolled in trials, compared to <5% of adults. The collaborative infrastructure (Children's Oncology Group in the US, similar groups internationally) has enabled systematic refinement of treatment.

*Different ethical considerations*. Consent involves parents/guardians and (when appropriate) the child. Long-term effects must be weighed differently. The emotional impact on families is profound.

## Neuroblastoma

The most common extracranial solid tumor in young children and one of the most biologically intriguing pediatric cancers.

*Biology*. Arises from primitive sympathetic nervous system cells (neuroblasts) that normally give rise to adrenal medulla and sympathetic ganglia. Tumors can occur anywhere in the sympathetic nervous system distribution — adrenal glands (~40%), paraspinal ganglia (cervical, thoracic, abdominal, pelvic), with adrenal location most common.

Neuroblastoma displays remarkable biological heterogeneity:
- Some neuroblastomas spontaneously regress (especially stage 4S in infants).
- Some undergo differentiation to ganglioneuroma (benign).
- Some are aggressive metastatic disease.

The clinical behavior correlates with molecular features:
- *MYCN amplification*. Strong adverse prognostic factor. ~20-25% of cases. Targets the MYC family transcription factor.
- *11q deletion*. Adverse prognosis without MYCN amplification.
- *17q gain*. Frequent.
- *ALK mutations*. Recurrent activating mutations. Targetable.
- *ATRX mutations*. Adverse prognosis (alternative lengthening of telomeres).
- *TERT rearrangements*. Adverse prognosis.

*Epidemiology*. Annual incidence in children <15 is about 10 per million. Median age at diagnosis is 17 months. Most diagnosed before age 5.

*Risk stratification* combines age, stage, MYCN status, histology, DNA ploidy, and other features. The International Neuroblastoma Risk Group (INRG) system classifies patients as very low-risk, low-risk, intermediate-risk, or high-risk.

*Presentation*. Variable based on location:
- *Abdominal*. Mass, pain, hypertension (from catecholamine production).
- *Thoracic*. Often asymptomatic; found on imaging for other reasons.
- *Paraspinal with intraspinal extension*. Neurological symptoms.
- *Metastatic disease*. Bone pain, hepatomegaly, raccoon eyes (from periorbital metastases), skin nodules (in infants).
- *Paraneoplastic*. Opsoclonus-myoclonus syndrome (jerky eye movements and myoclonus, often with cerebellar ataxia).

*Diagnosis*. Imaging (CT, MRI, MIBG scan which uses radiolabeled metaiodobenzylguanidine — taken up by neuroblastoma cells). Tissue biopsy with molecular characterization. Urinary catecholamine metabolites (VMA, HVA) elevated in most cases. Bone marrow biopsy and aspirate to assess marrow involvement.

*Treatment by risk*:

*Very low-risk and low-risk*. Surgery alone may be sufficient. Some stage 4S patients in infants are observed (may spontaneously regress).

*Intermediate-risk*. Chemotherapy + surgery. Reduced-intensity treatment.

*High-risk*. Aggressive multimodal therapy:
1. *Induction chemotherapy*. Intensive multi-agent chemotherapy.
2. *Surgery* to resect primary tumor.
3. *Consolidation*. High-dose chemotherapy with autologous stem cell rescue, sometimes tandem transplants.
4. *Radiation* to the primary site.
5. *Differentiation therapy*. Isotretinoin (13-cis retinoic acid).
6. *Immunotherapy*. Anti-GD2 monoclonal antibody (dinutuximab) with GM-CSF and IL-2.

The integration of dinutuximab into high-risk neuroblastoma treatment has substantially improved outcomes. GD2 (a disialoganglioside expressed on neuroblastoma) is the target.

*Targeted therapy for ALK-mutated neuroblastoma*. Crizotinib, lorlatinib, and ALK-specific TKIs have activity. Increasing use in relapsed/refractory ALK-mutated disease.

*GD2-targeted therapies in development*. Beyond dinutuximab, additional GD2-targeted approaches include CAR-T cells, ADCs, and bispecific antibodies.

*MIBG therapy*. Radioiodine-labeled MIBG (131-I MIBG) selectively taken up by neuroblastoma cells. Therapeutic application uses high-dose 131-I MIBG for relapsed/refractory disease.

*Outcomes*. Highly variable based on risk:
- Low-risk: 5-year survival >95%.
- Intermediate-risk: 5-year survival >90%.
- High-risk: 5-year survival 50-70% with modern multimodal treatment. Substantial improvement from <20% in earlier eras.

The high-risk neuroblastoma improvements illustrate the power of multimodal therapy.

## Wilms tumor

Wilms tumor (nephroblastoma) is a renal tumor of childhood. One of the most successful examples of cooperative group treatment refinement.

*Biology*. Arises from primitive renal tissue (metanephric blastema). Various molecular drivers:
- *WT1 mutations*. Wilms tumor 1 gene; associated with Denys-Drash syndrome and WAGR syndrome.
- *CTNNB1 mutations*. β-catenin pathway.
- *Loss of imprinting at 11p15* (IGF2 locus). Associated with Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome.
- *Anaplastic histology*. Adverse prognosis.

*Epidemiology*. Most common renal cancer of childhood. Annual incidence about 8 per million children <15. Median age at diagnosis around 3-4 years.

*Risk factors*. Hereditary syndromes (WAGR, Denys-Drash, Beckwith-Wiedemann) and family history. Most cases are sporadic.

*Presentation*. Asymptomatic abdominal mass is the most common presentation, often noticed by parents during bathing. Other features: abdominal pain, hematuria, hypertension, fever.

*Diagnosis*. Abdominal ultrasound for initial evaluation; CT or MRI for further characterization and staging. Chest imaging to assess for lung metastases (most common metastatic site). Definitive diagnosis at surgery; biopsy is generally avoided as it can spread disease.

*Staging*. National Wilms Tumor Study staging system (I-V) based on extent of disease.

*Treatment*. The cooperative group approach has refined treatment to balance cure with reducing toxicity:

*Lower-stage disease*. Surgery (nephrectomy) followed by chemotherapy. The chemotherapy intensity and duration depends on stage and histology. Many lower-stage patients can be treated with brief chemotherapy without radiation.

*Higher-stage disease*. Pre-operative chemotherapy (in some protocols, particularly SIOP in Europe) or upfront surgery (COG approach in the US) followed by chemotherapy ± radiation. Multi-agent regimens with vincristine, actinomycin D, doxorubicin, others.

*Anaplastic histology*. More intensive treatment needed; worse prognosis.

*Bilateral disease*. About 5% of cases. Nephron-sparing surgery to preserve renal function whenever possible.

*Outcomes*. Wilms tumor has one of the best survival rates among pediatric cancers — overall 5-year survival >90% across stages. Favorable histology stage I-II nearly 100% cure. Even anaplastic and metastatic disease have substantial cure rates with modern multimodal therapy.

The Wilms tumor story exemplifies how systematic cooperative group trials can refine treatment — gradually decreasing chemotherapy intensity for lower-risk patients while increasing for higher-risk, achieving high cures with minimal long-term effects.

## Medulloblastoma

The most common malignant brain tumor of childhood.

*Biology*. Arises from cerebellar primitive cells. Four major molecular subgroups (WHO 2021 classification):
- *WNT-activated*. ~10% of cases. Best prognosis. Older children. CTNNB1 mutations.
- *SHH-activated*. ~30% of cases. Two subgroups: TP53-wildtype (mostly favorable) and TP53-mutant (poor prognosis). Common in infants and adolescents.
- *Group 3*. ~25%. Often MYC amplification. Poor prognosis. More common in infants and young children.
- *Group 4*. ~35%. Most heterogeneous. Variable prognosis. Common in school-age children.

The molecular classification has substantially refined risk stratification and treatment selection.

*Epidemiology*. Annual incidence about 5 per million in children. Median age at diagnosis 5-7 years.

*Risk factors*. Genetic syndromes (Gorlin syndrome — SHH-activated; Li-Fraumeni syndrome — TP53-mutant SHH; Turcot syndrome — WNT-activated).

*Presentation*. Headaches, vomiting (especially morning vomiting from increased intracranial pressure), ataxia, cranial nerve deficits. Symptoms often progress over weeks to months.

*Diagnosis*. MRI of brain and spine (medulloblastoma can spread through CSF; staging requires spine imaging and CSF cytology). Surgical resection provides tissue for diagnosis and molecular classification.

*Staging*. M staging based on dissemination:
- M0: no dissemination.
- M1: positive CSF cytology only.
- M2-3: gross dissemination.

*Treatment*. Multimodal approach:

1. *Maximal safe surgical resection*.
2. *Craniospinal radiation* (after age 3; younger children have severe long-term effects from CNS radiation).
3. *Chemotherapy*. Multi-agent regimens.

Risk-adapted treatment:
- *Average-risk* (older children, M0, complete resection, favorable histology): standard craniospinal radiation dose and chemotherapy.
- *High-risk* (younger children, metastatic disease, anaplastic histology, residual tumor): higher-intensity treatment.
- *Infants* (under 3): treatment without radiation when possible due to severe long-term effects. High-dose chemotherapy with stem cell rescue may be used.

Molecular subgroup-specific treatment is emerging. WNT-activated tumors may receive reduced-intensity treatment given excellent prognosis. SHH-activated tumors can be treated with hedgehog pathway inhibitors (vismodegib, sonidegib) in selected contexts. Group 3 and Group 4 tumors with adverse features receive more intensive treatment.

*Outcomes*. Overall 5-year survival around 70-80% with substantial variation by subgroup:
- WNT-activated: >95%.
- SHH-activated (TP53 wildtype, older children): 80%.
- SHH-activated TP53-mutant: poor.
- Group 3 with MYC amplification: ~30-50%.
- Group 4: variable, often 70-80%.

Long-term effects of treatment are substantial — cognitive deficits, endocrine dysfunction, growth effects, secondary cancers, others.

## Other pediatric CNS tumors

*Diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma (DIPG)*. Highly aggressive brainstem glioma. Driven by H3K27M mutations in most cases. Universally fatal with median survival <12 months. Current trials include CAR-T cells (GD2, B7-H3), oncolytic viruses, and other novel approaches.

*Ependymoma*. Pediatric ependymomas have specific biology. Posterior fossa ependymomas in young children are particularly challenging. Treatment includes surgery and radiation.

*Atypical teratoid/rhabdoid tumor (AT/RT)*. Aggressive embryonal tumor with SMARCB1 (INI1) loss. Treated with intensive multimodal therapy. EZH2 inhibitors (tazemetostat) show activity in some AT/RT.

*Pilocytic astrocytomas*. Generally benign pediatric tumors. BRAF alterations are common. BRAF inhibitors and MEK inhibitors active in unresectable or recurrent disease. Selumetinib (MEK inhibitor) approved for neurofibromatosis type 1-associated plexiform neurofibromas.

*Diffuse hemispheric gliomas of childhood*. Different from adult gliomas in driver mutations and treatment.

## Sarcomas in pediatric oncology

Sarcomas are disproportionately common in pediatric oncology compared to adult oncology.

### Rhabdomyosarcoma

The most common soft tissue sarcoma of childhood. Arises from cells differentiating toward skeletal muscle.

*Subtypes*:
- *Embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma*. The most common subtype. Often head/neck or genitourinary location.
- *Alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma*. Driven by PAX3-FOXO1 or PAX7-FOXO1 fusion proteins. More aggressive.
- *Spindle cell/sclerosing rhabdomyosarcoma*. Various subtypes.

*Treatment*. Multimodal therapy with chemotherapy (VAC — vincristine, actinomycin D, cyclophosphamide — and variations), surgery, and radiation. Cure rates vary by stage and subtype.

*Outcomes*. Localized embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma 5-year survival 80-90%. Alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma has worse prognosis. Metastatic disease has poor outcomes despite intensive treatment.

### Ewing sarcoma

A bone or soft tissue sarcoma defined by characteristic gene fusions (EWSR1-FLI1 most commonly, EWSR1-ERG and other variants less commonly).

*Treatment*. Multi-agent chemotherapy alternating VDC (vincristine + doxorubicin + cyclophosphamide) and IE (ifosfamide + etoposide), with local control through surgery and/or radiation. Stem cell transplant for high-risk disease.

*Outcomes*. Localized disease 5-year survival 70-80%. Metastatic disease 20-30%.

Targeted therapy approaches for Ewing sarcoma (targeting the fusion protein, EWS-FLI1 transcriptional effects, others) are in development but have been challenging.

### Osteosarcoma

The most common primary bone cancer in adolescents.

*Treatment*. Multi-agent chemotherapy (MAP regimen — methotrexate, doxorubicin, cisplatin) with surgery for primary tumor. Limb-salvage surgery preferred over amputation when feasible.

*Outcomes*. Localized osteosarcoma 5-year survival 70%. Metastatic disease 20-30%. Limited progress in metastatic osteosarcoma over decades despite intensive efforts.

### Other pediatric sarcomas

Synovial sarcoma, infantile fibrosarcoma (NTRK fusion-driven, responds to larotrectinib or entrectinib), various others.

## Retinoblastoma

A cancer of the developing retina; the prototype hereditary cancer syndrome (RB1 gene; Chapter 4 and 6).

*Genetics*. RB1 mutations. Hereditary form has germline RB1 mutation (often bilateral, multifocal). Sporadic form has somatic RB1 inactivation.

*Presentation*. Leukocoria (white pupillary reflex), strabismus. Often detected by family members noticing absent red reflex in photos.

*Treatment*. Approach has evolved toward eye-preserving therapy:
- *Enucleation* (removal of eye) for advanced unilateral disease.
- *Intra-arterial chemotherapy* (selective ophthalmic artery infusion of melphalan and other drugs).
- *Intravitreal chemotherapy* for vitreous seeding.
- *Cryotherapy or laser therapy* for small lesions.
- *External beam radiation* (used less due to second cancer risk in hereditary cases).

*Outcomes*. Survival rates >95% in countries with access to modern treatment. Visual outcomes vary. Hereditary cases have substantially elevated risk of second cancers (osteosarcoma, soft tissue sarcoma, melanoma) requiring lifelong surveillance.

---

## Hepatoblastoma

A primary liver cancer of young children. Distinct from adult hepatocellular carcinoma.

*Treatment*. Surgery and chemotherapy (cisplatin-based). Liver transplant for unresectable disease.

*Outcomes*. 5-year survival 70-80% overall, with stage and histology-dependent variation.

---

## Germ cell tumors

Childhood germ cell tumors include gonadal (testicular, ovarian) and extragonadal (sacrococcygeal, mediastinal, intracranial) variants.

*Treatment*. Surgery for resectable disease; chemotherapy (cisplatin-based, similar to adult germ cell tumors) for advanced or recurrent disease.

*Outcomes*. Generally excellent. Cure rates >90% across stages.

## What this chapter gives you

Pediatric cancer differs from adult cancer in biology, distribution, treatment response, long-term considerations, infrastructure, and ethical considerations. Most childhood cancers arise from developmental cells with specific genetic drivers, respond well to multi-agent chemotherapy, and require attention to lifelong effects of treatment.

The major pediatric solid tumors include:

*Neuroblastoma*. Arises from primitive sympathetic nervous system cells. Highly heterogeneous behavior from spontaneous regression to aggressive disease. Risk stratification based on age, stage, MYCN amplification, and other features. High-risk treatment uses multimodal therapy with chemotherapy, surgery, transplant, radiation, retinoid, and anti-GD2 immunotherapy. Outcomes substantially improved with multimodal approaches.

*Wilms tumor*. Renal tumor of young children. Successful cooperative group refinement has produced excellent outcomes (>90% overall 5-year survival) while reducing toxicity. Bilateral and anaplastic variants require specialized approaches.

*Medulloblastoma*. The most common malignant childhood brain tumor. Four molecular subgroups (WNT, SHH, Group 3, Group 4) with different prognoses and treatment approaches. Treatment includes surgery, craniospinal radiation, and chemotherapy. Average-risk vs high-risk treatment. Infants require special consideration due to radiation effects.

Other pediatric CNS tumors include DIPG (highly aggressive H3K27M-mutated brainstem glioma), ependymomas, AT/RT (SMARCB1-deficient), pilocytic astrocytomas (BRAF-altered, MEK inhibitor-responsive), and diffuse hemispheric gliomas.

Sarcomas are disproportionately common in pediatric oncology — rhabdomyosarcoma (embryonal and alveolar subtypes), Ewing sarcoma (EWSR1-FLI1 fusion), osteosarcoma, and others. Multi-agent chemotherapy with local control through surgery and/or radiation. Treatment-resistant in many cases despite intensive efforts.

Retinoblastoma is the prototype hereditary cancer syndrome. Modern eye-preserving treatment uses intra-arterial and intravitreal chemotherapy. Hereditary cases require lifelong second cancer surveillance.

Hepatoblastoma, germ cell tumors, and other pediatric solid tumors have specific treatment approaches with generally favorable outcomes for localized disease.
