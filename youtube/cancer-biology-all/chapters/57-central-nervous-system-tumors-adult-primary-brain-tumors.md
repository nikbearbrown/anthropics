A brain tumor doesn't have to leave the brain to kill you.

Hold the framing. Most cancers kill through metastasis — escape from the primary tumor, colonization of distant organs, eventual organ failure or systemic effects. Primary brain tumors mostly don't metastasize outside the central nervous system. Yet they remain among the most lethal cancers. Glioblastoma kills most patients within 15-18 months despite aggressive multimodal treatment. The reason is that the brain is the organ. A tumor that occupies expanding space in a rigid skull, infiltrates the very tissue that controls cognition, movement, breathing, and consciousness, cannot grow much before producing devastating consequences. The cancer doesn't need to leave the brain. The brain is unforgiving enough territory.

The biology of CNS tumors is also distinct. The blood-brain barrier limits drug access to most systemic therapies. The brain microenvironment is immunologically privileged in ways that complicate immunotherapy. Surgical resection is constrained by the need to preserve neurological function. Radiation therapy must spare critical structures while reaching infiltrating tumor. Each modality faces challenges unique to the CNS.

This chapter — the first of two on CNS tumors — covers adult primary brain tumors, particularly glioblastoma (the most common and most aggressive primary brain cancer) and meningiomas (the most common benign brain tumor). The next — 32B — covers brain metastases (more common than primary brain tumors), multidisciplinary management, and the future directions of CNS cancer therapy.

Hold the question: *given the unique biology of the CNS, what makes brain cancer treatment different from other cancers?*

## The brain as a treatment environment

The CNS poses specific challenges for cancer treatment:

*Blood-brain barrier (BBB)*. The vascular endothelium of brain capillaries has tight junctions that exclude most molecules from the brain parenchyma. The BBB is essential for normal brain function — protecting from toxins, regulating metabolite exchange, maintaining brain homeostasis. For cancer therapy, the BBB blocks delivery of most chemotherapy drugs, many targeted therapies, and antibody-based treatments. Drugs that cross the BBB (temozolomide, certain TKIs designed for CNS penetration, others) are more effective for brain tumors than those that don't.

The BBB can be partially disrupted in tumors (especially in glioblastoma), allowing some drug penetration. But the disruption is heterogeneous — different regions of the same tumor have different BBB integrity. Surrounding the contrast-enhancing tumor core, there is invariably a halo of infiltrating tumor cells with intact BBB that drugs poorly reach.

*Limited regenerative capacity*. The brain has minimal regenerative capacity in adults. Treatment damage to brain tissue is generally permanent. This limits how aggressive surgery and radiation can be without producing devastating side effects.

*Functional importance of every region*. Different brain regions control different functions. Removing tumor from motor cortex causes paralysis; from language areas causes aphasia; from visual cortex causes blindness. Surgery must balance complete tumor resection with preservation of function.

*Immunological privilege*. The brain has unique immune privilege — limited entry of peripheral immune cells, lack of conventional lymphatic drainage (though glymphatic system provides some), specialized resident immune cells (microglia). These features have complicated immunotherapy approaches in the CNS.

*Cerebrospinal fluid dynamics*. Tumors can spread through CSF, particularly with cells that lift off into CSF (leptomeningeal disease). This route of spread is particularly challenging therapeutically.

*Pressure and herniation risks*. Tumors and edema in the rigid skull can produce pressure that herniates brain tissue, causing rapid neurological deterioration and death. Managing intracranial pressure is often a critical part of treatment.

These features shape every aspect of brain tumor management — diagnosis, surgical approach, radiation planning, drug selection, and supportive care.

## Adult primary brain tumor categories

Primary brain tumors arise from cells within the central nervous system. The major categories:

*Gliomas*. Arise from glial cells. The dominant primary brain cancer in adults. Subtypes include astrocytomas (including glioblastoma), oligodendrogliomas, ependymomas.

*Meningiomas*. Arise from meningeal cells. Usually benign but can be invasive. The most common primary brain tumor overall when including benign types.

*Pituitary adenomas*. Usually benign. May produce hormones (functional adenomas) or compress surrounding structures (non-functional adenomas).

*Vestibular schwannomas (acoustic neuromas)*. Benign Schwann cell tumors of cranial nerve VIII.

*Other tumors*. Hemangioblastomas, pineal region tumors, choroid plexus tumors, ependymomas, various rare types.

*Primary CNS lymphoma*. Lymphoma arising primarily in the brain. Usually B-cell origin, often associated with immunosuppression.

*Medulloblastoma and other embryonal tumors*. Primarily pediatric; covered in Chapter 34A.

Brain metastases (from cancers originating elsewhere) are actually the most common brain tumors but covered separately in 32B.

The 2021 WHO Classification of CNS Tumors (5th edition) heavily integrates molecular features into diagnosis. The classification has been substantially revised from prior editions to reflect the molecular understanding.

## Glioblastoma

The most common and most aggressive primary brain cancer in adults.

*Epidemiology*. Annual incidence around 3-5 per 100,000. Median age at diagnosis around 64. Slightly more common in men. Generally not associated with strong genetic predispositions or environmental factors (unlike many other cancers).

*Biology and molecular classification*. The 2021 WHO classification defines glioblastoma as a high-grade astrocytic tumor with IDH-wildtype status, plus specific molecular features:
- TERT promoter mutation, AND/OR
- EGFR amplification, AND/OR
- Gain of chromosome 7 / loss of chromosome 10.

The "IDH-mutant astrocytomas, grade 4" that were previously called secondary glioblastomas are now classified as IDH-mutant astrocytomas (grade 4 if appropriate), reflecting their better prognosis.

Other relevant molecular features in glioblastoma:
- *MGMT promoter methylation* (~40% of cases). MGMT is a DNA repair enzyme that removes alkylating damage. Methylation silences MGMT expression, making tumors more sensitive to temozolomide. MGMT-methylated tumors have substantially better outcomes than unmethylated.
- *TP53 mutations* (common).
- *PTEN loss* (common).
- *CDKN2A/B deletion* (common).
- *NF1 loss* (specific subgroup).
- *PDGFRA amplification*.
- *Various transcriptomic subtypes* (classical, mesenchymal, proneural — though clinical utility is debated).

*Presentation*. Headaches, seizures, focal neurological deficits, cognitive changes, personality changes, signs of increased intracranial pressure (nausea, vomiting, papilledema). The presentation often progresses over weeks to months.

*Diagnosis and imaging*. MRI with and without contrast is the standard. Glioblastoma typically shows:
- Contrast enhancement (often ring-enhancing).
- Surrounding edema.
- Central necrosis.
- Mass effect.
- Often crosses the corpus callosum (the "butterfly" appearance).

Differential diagnosis includes brain metastases, primary CNS lymphoma, abscesses, and other lesions. MR spectroscopy, perfusion imaging, and PET imaging help differentiate.

*Treatment*. Standard of care for newly diagnosed glioblastoma is the Stupp protocol:
1. *Maximal safe surgical resection*. Greater extent of resection correlates with better outcomes, but surgeons must balance against neurological function.
2. *Concurrent chemoradiation*. Radiation (60 Gy in 30 fractions) with concurrent temozolomide (75 mg/m²/day during radiation, then 5/28 cycles after).
3. *Adjuvant temozolomide*. 6 cycles or more after concurrent treatment.

Tumor Treating Fields (TTFields, Optune) — a wearable device delivering low-intensity electric fields to disrupt mitotic spindle formation — provides additional survival benefit when added to maintenance temozolomide (EF-14 trial). Use varies based on patient preference and access.

*Outcomes*. Median overall survival is approximately 15-18 months with the Stupp protocol; the 5-year survival is approximately 5-10%. MGMT-methylated tumors have median survival around 21-23 months; MGMT-unmethylated around 12-14 months. Elderly patients with poor performance status have worse outcomes regardless.

*Recurrent disease*. Almost all glioblastomas recur. Treatment options include:
- *Repeat surgery* for selected patients with focal recurrence and good performance status.
- *Bevacizumab* (anti-VEGF antibody). FDA-approved for recurrent glioblastoma but does not improve overall survival, though improves imaging response and quality of life.
- *Lomustine* or temozolomide re-challenge.
- *Tumor Treating Fields*.
- *Clinical trials*. Glioblastoma is an area of intense research.

The treatment paradigm has been remarkably stable for the past 15 years despite extensive research efforts. Many promising approaches (anti-angiogenic agents, EGFR-targeted therapy, IDO inhibitors, multiple immunotherapy approaches, oncolytic viruses) have failed in glioblastoma trials. The reasons include the blood-brain barrier, the diffuse infiltrative growth pattern, the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, and the inherent biology of glial cells.

*Emerging approaches*. Oncolytic viruses (PVS-RIPO, DNX-2401, others) have shown some signals. CAR-T cells targeting EGFRvIII or B7-H3 are in trials. Personalized neoantigen vaccines. Convection-enhanced delivery for direct intratumoral drug delivery. Selective inhibitors for IDH-mutant gliomas (now approved for IDH-mutant lower-grade gliomas — voranigo/vorasidenib).

## IDH-mutant astrocytomas and oligodendrogliomas

The 2021 WHO classification separated IDH-mutant gliomas from glioblastoma based on the recognition that they have very different biology and prognosis.

*IDH-mutant astrocytoma* (grades 2-4):
- IDH1 R132H is the most common mutation; IDH2 mutations less frequent.
- TP53 mutations and ATRX loss are typical.
- Better prognosis than IDH-wildtype glioblastoma.
- Grade 2: median survival 5-10 years.
- Grade 3: median survival 5-7 years.
- Grade 4: median survival 3-5 years.

*Oligodendroglioma* (IDH-mutant, 1p/19q-codeleted):
- IDH mutation plus 1p/19q codeletion are required for diagnosis.
- TERT promoter mutations common.
- Best prognosis of the glioma subtypes.
- Median survival 10-15 years or longer.
- Particularly responsive to chemotherapy.

*Treatment of IDH-mutant gliomas*:

*Low-grade gliomas (grade 2)*. Maximal safe resection. For high-risk patients (older age, large tumor, poor performance status, IDH-mutant astrocytoma but not oligodendroglioma), adjuvant radiation + chemotherapy (PCV regimen — procarbazine, lomustine, vincristine — or temozolomide).

*High-grade IDH-mutant gliomas (grade 3-4)*. Multimodal therapy similar to glioblastoma though tailored.

*IDH inhibitor therapy*. Vorasidenib (Voranigo, approved 2024) was the first IDH-targeted therapy approved for grade 2 IDH-mutant gliomas after surgery. The INDIGO trial showed substantial PFS benefit. Ivosidenib is also approved for IDH1-mutant gliomas. The approach represents the first targeted therapy for gliomas based on molecular features.

The IDH-mutant glioma story illustrates how molecular classification can dramatically refine treatment. Patients previously treated with the same approach as IDH-wildtype glioblastoma now have access to specifically targeted therapy.

## Meningiomas

Meningiomas arise from arachnoid cap cells of the meninges. The most common primary brain tumor when including benign types — about 30% of all primary CNS tumors. Most are benign, slow-growing, and often incidental.

*Epidemiology*. More common in women (2:1 ratio). Incidence increases with age. Often incidentally discovered on imaging done for other reasons.

*Risk factors*. Prior radiation therapy (especially scalp), neurofibromatosis type 2, female hormones (some are progesterone-receptor-positive and may grow during pregnancy).

*WHO grading*:
- *Grade 1 (benign)*. ~80%. Slow-growing, well-circumscribed. Includes meningothelial, fibrous, transitional, psammomatous, angiomatous, and other variants.
- *Grade 2 (atypical)*. ~15-20%. More aggressive features (increased mitotic activity, focal necrosis, certain other features). Higher recurrence risk.
- *Grade 3 (anaplastic/malignant)*. ~1-3%. Frankly malignant histology. Aggressive behavior with high recurrence rates and possible metastases.

*Diagnosis*. MRI shows characteristic features: extra-axial location (outside the brain itself), broad dural attachment, "dural tail" enhancement, homogeneous contrast enhancement. May be classified by location (parasagittal, falx, sphenoid wing, posterior fossa, spinal, etc.).

*Treatment*:

*Observation*. For small asymptomatic meningiomas without growth, particularly in older patients or those with comorbidities. Many meningiomas remain stable over years.

*Surgical resection*. The standard for symptomatic, growing, or large tumors. The Simpson grading scale describes the completeness of resection (grade 1 complete removal including dural attachment; grades 2-5 with progressively less complete removal). Lower Simpson grades correlate with lower recurrence risk.

*Radiation therapy*. For tumors not fully resectable, after subtotal resection, for higher-grade meningiomas, or as primary treatment for skull base tumors. Stereotactic radiosurgery for smaller meningiomas. Conventional fractionated radiation for larger or atypical/malignant variants.

*Systemic therapy*. Limited role. Bevacizumab has been used for refractory cases. Newer molecular-targeted approaches are in development for specific molecular subtypes.

*Outcomes*. Grade 1 meningiomas have excellent prognosis with complete resection (>90% 10-year survival). Grade 2 has higher recurrence and somewhat worse prognosis. Grade 3 is aggressive with often poor outcomes.

## Primary CNS lymphoma

Lymphoma arising primarily in the brain or spinal cord, without systemic involvement.

*Epidemiology*. Annual incidence about 0.5 per 100,000. Associated with immunosuppression (HIV, transplant, congenital immunodeficiencies, autoimmune disease treatment). Median age in immunocompetent patients around 60.

*Histology*. Almost always B-cell (most commonly DLBCL).

*Presentation*. Cognitive changes, focal neurological symptoms, vision changes (if periventricular involvement), seizures. Often more rapid onset than glioma.

*Diagnosis*. MRI shows enhancing lesions, often periventricular or near corpus callosum, with little surrounding edema. Definitive diagnosis requires biopsy (stereotactic biopsy is usual; CSF cytology and flow cytometry may aid).

*Treatment*. Differs substantially from systemic lymphoma:
- *High-dose methotrexate-based chemotherapy*. The cornerstone. Multiple regimens incorporate high-dose methotrexate with other agents (rituximab, vincristine, procarbazine, others).
- *Consolidation therapy*. Either high-dose chemotherapy + autologous stem cell transplant or whole-brain radiation therapy (the latter has fallen out of favor due to cognitive toxicity in long-term survivors).
- *Salvage therapy*. For relapsed/refractory disease — high-dose chemotherapy + transplant, ibrutinib, lenalidomide, CAR-T cells (emerging).

*Outcomes*. Complete remission in 50-70% with initial treatment. Long-term survival has improved with modern approaches; 5-year survival around 30-40% in immunocompetent patients.

## Other CNS tumors

*Pituitary adenomas*. Generally benign. Treatment depends on whether they produce hormones (functional) or compress structures (non-functional). Functional adenomas may respond to medical therapy (dopamine agonists for prolactinomas, somatostatin analogs for growth hormone-producing tumors). Surgery (typically transsphenoidal) for tumors that fail medical therapy or are non-functional. Radiation for incomplete resections.

*Vestibular schwannomas*. Benign tumors of the vestibulocochlear nerve. Treatment options include observation (for small asymptomatic tumors), surgical resection, or stereotactic radiosurgery. Decision based on tumor size, symptoms, and patient factors. Hearing preservation is a key consideration.

*Hemangioblastomas*. Highly vascular tumors, often cerebellar. Associated with von Hippel-Lindau syndrome. Treatment is surgical resection.

*Ependymomas*. Tumors of ependymal cells lining ventricles and spinal canal. Various subtypes with different biology. Surgery + radiation often used. Pediatric posterior fossa ependymomas have specific biology and treatment.

*Choroid plexus tumors, pineal region tumors, primary spinal cord tumors*. Less common entities with specific management approaches.

The CNS tumor field has been substantially refined through molecular classification. The 2021 WHO classification represents a major shift toward integrated diagnosis. Treatment decisions increasingly depend on specific molecular features rather than histology alone.

## What this chapter gives you

Central nervous system tumors pose unique therapeutic challenges due to the blood-brain barrier, limited regenerative capacity, functional importance of every brain region, immunological privilege, CSF dynamics, and pressure considerations.

Adult primary brain tumors include gliomas (the dominant category), meningiomas, pituitary adenomas, vestibular schwannomas, hemangioblastomas, primary CNS lymphoma, ependymomas, and various rare types. The 2021 WHO Classification heavily integrates molecular features into diagnosis.

Glioblastoma is the most common and most aggressive primary brain cancer. Molecular features (IDH-wildtype required for diagnosis; TERT promoter mutation, EGFR amplification, +7/-10 also defining) and MGMT methylation status guide prognosis. Treatment follows the Stupp protocol (maximal safe surgery + concurrent chemoradiation + adjuvant temozolomide) with addition of Tumor Treating Fields. Despite extensive research, median survival remains 15-18 months. Recurrent disease has limited options.

IDH-mutant astrocytomas and oligodendrogliomas have different biology from glioblastoma with substantially better prognosis. Molecular classification separates these entities. Vorasidenib (approved 2024) was the first IDH-targeted therapy for grade 2 IDH-mutant gliomas — a major recent advance.

Meningiomas are the most common primary brain tumor when benign types are included. Grade 1 (benign) tumors have excellent prognosis. Treatment ranges from observation to surgery to radiation depending on grade, size, location, and symptoms.

Primary CNS lymphoma is treated with high-dose methotrexate-based chemotherapy and consolidation with stem cell transplant or radiation. Outcomes have improved substantially over decades.

Pituitary adenomas, vestibular schwannomas, hemangioblastomas, ependymomas, and other rare entities have specific management approaches.
