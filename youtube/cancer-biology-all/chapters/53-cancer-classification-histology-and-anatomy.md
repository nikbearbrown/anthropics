# Chapter 53 — Cancer Classification: Histology and Anatomy

Pathology used to be how we knew what a cancer was. It still is, but now it's only the first chapter.

Hold the framing. For most of cancer medicine's history, the answer to "what is this cancer?" came from a pathologist looking at tumor tissue under a microscope. The pathologist examined cell morphology, tissue architecture, and immunohistochemical features to assign a diagnosis — adenocarcinoma of the colon, ductal carcinoma of the breast, squamous cell carcinoma of the lung, glioblastoma multiforme. The diagnosis identified the cancer's tissue of origin and its general behavior class. Treatment selection flowed from this classification.

The histological classification is still the foundation, but it has become the first layer of a multi-dimensional characterization. Modern cancer classification integrates histology with anatomic stage (where the cancer is), molecular features (what mutations drive it), and increasingly with imaging characteristics, genomic signatures, and other features. Two patients with histologically identical breast adenocarcinomas may have very different treatments and prognoses based on hormone receptor status, HER2 status, mutation profile, and other features that don't show on H&E staining.

The classification framework matters because it organizes everything else in oncology. Treatment guidelines, clinical trials, research databases, and individual patient care all depend on assigning cancers to defined categories. As the categories have evolved — from purely anatomic and histologic to integrated molecular-clinical — the practice of oncology has evolved with them.

This chapter — the first of two on cancer classification — covers histological and anatomical classification systems. The next — 30B — covers molecular and genomic classification, and the integration that increasingly characterizes modern cancer medicine.

Hold the question: *what information does a cancer classification need to capture, and how do different classification approaches serve different purposes?*

## The purposes of cancer classification

Cancer classification serves multiple purposes:

*Prognosis*. Different cancer types have different natural histories. Classification informs expected outcomes.

*Treatment selection*. Treatments are studied and approved by cancer type. Classification determines which treatments apply.

*Research and trials*. Clinical trials enroll patients by classification. Research databases organize information by classification.

*Communication*. Standardized classification enables clear communication among clinicians, researchers, and patients.

*Epidemiology*. Cancer incidence, mortality, and other statistics are reported by classification. The classifications must be consistent across time and place.

*Insurance and reimbursement*. Coverage decisions use classification.

*Registry tracking*. Cancer registries collect data by classification for surveillance and research.

The same cancer may be classified by different systems for different purposes. The classification approaches complement each other.

The major classification dimensions:

*Anatomic location*. Where in the body the cancer arises (breast, lung, colon, etc.).

*Histologic type*. The microscopic appearance and presumed tissue of origin (adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, sarcoma, lymphoma, etc.).

*Differentiation*. How much the cancer resembles its normal counterpart (well, moderately, poorly differentiated).

*Stage*. The extent of disease (TNM system, summarized as stages 0-IV).

*Grade*. The biological aggressiveness (typically grades 1-3 or 1-4 depending on cancer type).

*Molecular features*. Genetic and biological characteristics (hormone receptor status, HER2 status, mutations, etc.).

*Clinical features*. Symptoms, performance status, age, comorbidities.

The full classification of a cancer typically integrates multiple dimensions. "Stage III, grade 2, ER-positive, HER2-negative, PIK3CA-mutated, invasive ductal carcinoma of the breast" specifies the disease at several levels.

## Histological classification: the foundation

The histological classification of cancers is based on the microscopic appearance and the presumed cell of origin. The classification has been refined over many decades and is codified by the World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Tumors series.

The major histological categories of cancer:

*Carcinomas*. Cancers arising from epithelial cells. Comprise about 85-90% of all cancers in adults. Subcategories:

- *Adenocarcinomas*. From glandular epithelium. Breast, colon, lung adenocarcinoma, prostate, pancreas, stomach, kidney, thyroid (papillary, follicular), ovarian, endometrial, and many others.

- *Squamous cell carcinomas*. From flat surface epithelium. Skin squamous cell carcinoma, oral cavity, pharynx, larynx, esophagus (upper portion), lung squamous cell carcinoma, cervical, anal.

- *Transitional cell carcinomas (urothelial carcinomas)*. From urothelium (transitional epithelium). Bladder, ureter, renal pelvis, urethra.

- *Basal cell carcinomas*. From basal layer of skin epithelium. Common skin cancer.

- *Small cell carcinomas*. Neuroendocrine carcinomas with small cell morphology. Lung small cell carcinoma is the prototype; also other anatomic sites.

- *Neuroendocrine carcinomas* (non-small-cell). Various differentiations and anatomic locations.

- *Hepatocellular carcinoma*. From hepatocytes.

- *Cholangiocarcinoma*. From bile duct epithelium.

- *Adenoid cystic carcinoma*, *acinic cell carcinoma*, *mucoepidermoid carcinoma*. Specific salivary gland and other epithelial cancers.

*Sarcomas*. Cancers arising from mesenchymal cells. About 1% of adult cancers but disproportionately common in children. Subcategories:

- *Soft tissue sarcomas*. Many subtypes by cell of origin. Liposarcoma (fat), leiomyosarcoma (smooth muscle), rhabdomyosarcoma (skeletal muscle, primarily pediatric), angiosarcoma (vascular), synovial sarcoma, MPNST (malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor), GIST (gastrointestinal stromal tumor — though biologically distinct), and many others.

- *Bone sarcomas*. Osteosarcoma (the most common primary bone cancer in children and adolescents), chondrosarcoma (cartilage), Ewing sarcoma (a specific genetically defined sarcoma usually affecting bone), chordoma (notochord-derived).

*Leukemias*. Cancers of hematopoietic stem cells circulating in blood and bone marrow. Subcategories:

- *Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL)*. B-cell and T-cell variants. Most common childhood cancer.
- *Acute myeloid leukemia (AML)*. Multiple subtypes based on cell of origin and genetics.
- *Chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL)*. B-cell malignancy of small mature lymphocytes.
- *Chronic myeloid leukemia (CML)*. BCR-ABL-driven myeloid proliferation.
- *Hairy cell leukemia*, *prolymphocytic leukemia*, others.

*Lymphomas*. Cancers of lymphocytes forming solid masses, primarily in lymph nodes. Subcategories:

- *Hodgkin lymphoma*. Characterized by Reed-Sternberg cells. Several subtypes.
- *Non-Hodgkin lymphomas*. A diverse group. Major subtypes include diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL — most common), follicular lymphoma, mantle cell lymphoma, marginal zone lymphoma, Burkitt lymphoma, peripheral T-cell lymphomas, anaplastic large cell lymphoma, NK/T-cell lymphomas, and many others.

*Plasma cell neoplasms*. Multiple myeloma, plasmacytoma, and related disorders.

*Myeloproliferative neoplasms*. Chronic myeloid leukemia, polycythemia vera, essential thrombocythemia, primary myelofibrosis.

*Myelodysplastic syndromes*. Disorders of hematopoietic differentiation that can progress to AML.

*Central nervous system tumors*. Brain and spinal cord tumors. Major subtypes include:

- *Gliomas*. Astrocytomas (including glioblastoma — the most aggressive primary brain tumor), oligodendrogliomas, ependymomas. Classification has been heavily revised in recent years with incorporation of molecular features.
- *Medulloblastomas*. Pediatric brain tumors. Multiple molecular subtypes.
- *Meningiomas*. From meningeal cells. Usually benign but can be aggressive.
- *Schwannomas, neurofibromas*. Peripheral nerve sheath tumors.
- *Pituitary adenomas*. Usually benign but can be functional.

*Germ cell tumors*. Cancers arising from germ cells (egg or sperm precursors). Mostly in testes or ovaries; rarely extragonadal (mediastinum, retroperitoneum, pineal gland). Subtypes:

- *Seminomas* (in testes) and *dysgerminomas* (in ovaries).
- *Non-seminomatous germ cell tumors* (embryonal carcinoma, yolk sac tumor, choriocarcinoma, teratoma).

*Melanomas*. From melanocytes. Cutaneous, mucosal, uveal subtypes.

*Mesotheliomas*. From mesothelial cells (pleura, peritoneum, pericardium). Strongly associated with asbestos exposure.

*Other specialized tumors*. Adrenocortical carcinoma, pheochromocytoma, parathyroid carcinoma, thymic neoplasms, and others.

The histological classification is the foundation of cancer diagnosis. Pathology reports specify the histologic type and subtype. Treatment decisions and clinical trial eligibility typically use these classifications.

## Histological subclassification within cancer types

Within each major cancer type, histological subclassification provides additional information. Examples:

*Breast cancer histology*:
- Invasive ductal carcinoma (the most common, ~70% of cases).
- Invasive lobular carcinoma (~10-15%).
- Special subtypes: tubular, mucinous, medullary, papillary, cribriform, micropapillary, metaplastic, others.
- *Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS)*. Non-invasive but a precursor lesion.
- *Lobular carcinoma in situ (LCIS)*. A risk indicator more than a true precancerous lesion.

Each subtype has somewhat different clinical behavior. Lobular cancers often have different growth patterns; mucinous and tubular cancers often have better prognosis; metaplastic cancers tend to behave more aggressively.

*Lung cancer histology*:
- *Non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC)* — about 85% of cases. Subtypes:
  - Adenocarcinoma (the most common NSCLC subtype, including lepidic, acinar, papillary, micropapillary, solid patterns).
  - Squamous cell carcinoma.
  - Large cell carcinoma.
  - Adenosquamous carcinoma.
- *Small cell lung cancer (SCLC)* — about 15% of cases. A neuroendocrine carcinoma.
- *Other neuroendocrine carcinomas*. Typical and atypical carcinoid, large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma.

The histology affects both prognosis and treatment. NSCLC and SCLC have very different management; adenocarcinoma versus squamous cell histology in NSCLC affects molecular testing requirements and some treatment selections.

*Colorectal cancer histology*:
- Adenocarcinoma (95%+). With various differentiation patterns and special features.
- Mucinous adenocarcinoma.
- Signet ring cell carcinoma.
- Medullary carcinoma.
- Neuroendocrine tumors and carcinomas.
- Other rare subtypes.

The histology can suggest underlying biology (e.g., mucinous and medullary subtypes have higher rates of mismatch repair deficiency).

*Lymphoma histology*. Extensive subclassification within Hodgkin and non-Hodgkin lymphoma categories, with substantial implications for treatment.

*Sarcoma histology*. Multiple subtypes with very different biologies, requiring expert pathology review for accurate classification.

*Glioma histology*. Has been heavily revised in recent years to integrate molecular features (covered in 30B).

The histological subclassification provides information beyond the major type. Treatment guidelines and clinical trials often use specific subtypes.

## Grading: differentiation as biological aggressiveness

Tumor grade reflects how much the cancer resembles its normal tissue counterpart, with implications for biological aggressiveness.

The grading concept:
- *Well-differentiated (grade 1)*. Tumor cells closely resemble their normal counterparts. Generally less aggressive.
- *Moderately differentiated (grade 2)*. Some loss of normal features.
- *Poorly differentiated (grade 3)*. Substantial loss of normal features. More aggressive.
- *Undifferentiated (grade 4)*. Cells barely resemble any specific tissue type. Most aggressive.

The grading systems vary by cancer type:

*Breast cancer grading (Nottingham/Elston-Ellis grade)*. Three components scored 1-3 each:
- Tubule formation.
- Nuclear pleomorphism.
- Mitotic count.
The scores are summed for total of 3-9, then grouped: 3-5 = grade 1, 6-7 = grade 2, 8-9 = grade 3.

*Prostate cancer grading (Gleason score and Grade Groups)*. Based on architectural patterns:
- Patterns scored 1-5 based on architectural appearance.
- Sum of the two most common patterns (primary + secondary) gives the Gleason score (lowest 6, highest 10).
- Recently grouped into Grade Groups 1-5 to better correlate with prognosis.

*Glioma grading*. WHO grades 1-4 based on:
- Cellular density.
- Nuclear pleomorphism.
- Mitotic activity.
- Microvascular proliferation.
- Necrosis.
Now integrated with molecular features (IDH status, 1p/19q codeletion, TERT promoter mutation, others).

*Sarcoma grading (FNCLCC system)*. Three components:
- Tumor differentiation.
- Mitotic count.
- Necrosis.

Different histological subtypes use different grading systems. The general principle — higher grade indicates more aggressive behavior — applies across cancers, but the specific systems and thresholds vary.

The grade is determined by the pathologist on examination of the tumor specimen. It is part of the standard pathology report and informs treatment decisions and prognosis.

## Anatomic staging: TNM and beyond

Anatomic staging describes the extent of disease. The TNM system is the dominant framework:

*T (tumor)*. Size and local invasion of the primary tumor. Specific T categories vary by cancer type.

*N (nodes)*. Regional lymph node involvement. Numbers and locations vary.

*M (metastasis)*. Distant metastasis. M0 (no metastasis) or M1 (metastasis present, sometimes with further subdivisions).

The TNM categories combine into stage groupings (0, I, II, III, IV) that vary by cancer type. The American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) maintains the staging manual, currently in its 8th edition (2017) with the 9th edition in preparation.

Key TNM principles:

*Pathological staging (p)*. Based on surgical specimen evaluation. The gold standard for solid tumors that are resected.

*Clinical staging (c)*. Based on clinical and imaging information before surgery. Used when surgery isn't performed or before surgery.

*Post-neoadjuvant staging (y)*. After neoadjuvant therapy. May differ substantially from pre-treatment staging.

*Recurrent staging (r)*. For recurrence after curative treatment.

*Autopsy staging (a)*. Post-mortem assessment.

The TNM system has evolved to incorporate non-anatomic features in some cancers:

*Breast cancer prognostic stage groups*. Incorporate ER, PR, HER2 status, and Oncotype DX recurrence score alongside anatomic TNM. The integration substantially refines prognosis.

*Prostate cancer staging*. Incorporates PSA at diagnosis and Gleason score alongside TNM. Different staging is used for clinical decisions.

*Melanoma staging*. Includes Breslow thickness, ulceration, and mitotic rate.

*Glioma classification*. Has moved heavily toward molecular classification (Chapter 30B).

*Lymphoma staging*. Uses Lugano classification (modified Ann Arbor) with specific features for lymphoma biology.

The integration of biological features into anatomic staging reflects the recognition that anatomic extent alone doesn't fully capture cancer behavior. The trend toward biology-informed staging will continue.

## Anatomic location and primary site identification

For most cancers, the anatomic site of the primary tumor is clear. For some, it isn't:

*Cancer of unknown primary (CUP)*. Patients present with metastatic disease without an identifiable primary tumor. The differential diagnosis requires:

- Extensive imaging.
- Endoscopic procedures.
- Comprehensive pathology with immunohistochemistry panels.
- Molecular profiling to suggest tissue of origin.

The classification of CUP has evolved. Historically, CUP was treated empirically with a chemotherapy regimen for "carcinoma of unknown primary." Modern approaches use molecular profiling to assign likely tissue of origin and select therapy accordingly. The CUPISCO trial showed that molecular profiling-guided therapy improved outcomes versus empiric treatment in CUP.

*Squamous cell carcinoma vs adenocarcinoma in CUP*. Different chemotherapy preferences and prognoses.

*Neuroendocrine cancer of unknown primary*. Specific subgroup with different management.

*Primary site identification through molecular profiling*. Several commercial assays (CancerTYPE ID, others) use gene expression patterns to predict tissue of origin. Sensitivity and specificity are improving.

*Anatomic vs molecular classification*. In some cases, the molecular features (specific mutations, gene expression patterns) better predict therapy response than anatomic origin. The trend toward tumor-agnostic therapies (Chapter 24A) reflects this.

The challenge of CUP illustrates the limits of pure anatomic classification. Some cancers don't fit cleanly into the anatomic framework, and molecular classification provides complementary information.

## ICD-O and other classification systems

Beyond the WHO classification, several other systems organize cancer information:

*ICD-O (International Classification of Diseases for Oncology)*. The standardized coding system for cancer diagnoses. Maintained by WHO. Used by cancer registries worldwide. Provides codes for both topography (anatomic site) and morphology (histologic type).

*ICD-10 and ICD-11*. The general disease classification, with codes for cancer that are less detailed than ICD-O. Used for clinical billing and general health statistics.

*SEER classification*. Used by the US Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results program for cancer surveillance and statistics.

*WHO Classification of Tumours*. The "blue books" — disease-specific volumes (CNS tumors, hematopoietic tumors, breast tumors, etc.) that define histological and molecular classifications. The current 5th edition is being rolled out.

*NCCN Guidelines*. National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines use specific classification frameworks for treatment recommendations.

*Cancer registry classifications*. National and regional cancer registries use standardized classifications for reporting.

Different classifications serve different purposes. ICD-O for registry coding. ICD-10/11 for clinical billing. WHO Classification for diagnostic pathology. NCCN for treatment guidelines. They are designed to be compatible but require translation in some contexts.

## Classification challenges

Several challenges affect cancer classification:

*Heterogeneity within cancer types*. Two patients with the same histological diagnosis may have very different biology. The classification must capture this heterogeneity to be clinically useful.

*Reclassification as biology is understood*. New molecular and biological insights regularly require reclassification. The 2021 WHO Classification of CNS tumors integrated molecular features for major changes in glioma classification.

*Borderline cases*. Some cancers don't fit cleanly into established categories. New classifications are sometimes needed.

*International variation*. Different countries and regions sometimes use different classifications, complicating international research and care.

*Pathology workforce limitations*. Accurate histological classification requires expert pathology. The workforce is limited in many settings.

*Inter-observer variability*. Even expert pathologists can disagree on grading and some classification decisions. The variability has been a concern, particularly for borderline lesions.

*Communication with patients*. Classification terminology is often technical. Communicating diagnoses to patients requires translation.

These challenges have driven the integration of molecular features (Chapter 30B), the development of AI-based pathology tools, and ongoing refinement of classification systems.

## What this chapter gives you

Cancer classification provides the framework for organizing diagnosis, prognosis, treatment, research, and surveillance. Different classification dimensions (anatomic, histologic, grade, stage, molecular, clinical) capture different aspects of the cancer; modern oncology integrates multiple dimensions.

Histological classification — based on microscopic appearance and presumed cell of origin — is the foundation. The major categories are carcinomas (most common adult cancers, with adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, transitional cell carcinoma, and other subtypes), sarcomas (mesenchymal cancers, with extensive subtypes), leukemias (blood cell cancers), lymphomas (lymphatic cancers), plasma cell neoplasms, myeloproliferative neoplasms, myelodysplastic syndromes, central nervous system tumors, germ cell tumors, melanomas, mesotheliomas, and other specialized tumors.

Within each cancer type, histological subclassification provides additional information about expected behavior and treatment. Breast cancer subtypes (ductal, lobular, special types), lung cancer histology (adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, small cell carcinoma), and lymphoma subtypes are examples.

Tumor grading reflects differentiation and biological aggressiveness, with different systems for different cancers (Nottingham/Elston-Ellis for breast, Gleason/Grade Groups for prostate, WHO grades for glioma, FNCLCC for sarcoma).

Anatomic staging through TNM provides standardized description of disease extent. Pathological, clinical, post-neoadjuvant, recurrent, and autopsy staging address different clinical contexts. Modern staging increasingly incorporates biological features (HER2 status in breast cancer, PSA and Gleason in prostate, ulceration in melanoma).

Cancer of unknown primary illustrates the limits of pure anatomic classification, and molecular profiling for tissue of origin is increasingly used.

Various classification systems (ICD-O, ICD-10/11, SEER, WHO Classification of Tumours, NCCN, registry classifications) serve different purposes within the broader infrastructure.

Challenges include heterogeneity within cancer types, reclassification as biology is understood, borderline cases, international variation, workforce limitations, inter-observer variability, and patient communication.
