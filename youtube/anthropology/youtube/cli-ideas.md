## Candidate 01 — "Research the Four Species Definitions with Claude"
- Source: anthropology/chapters/04-biological-evolution-and-early-human-evidence.md
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: Biology has not settled on a single definition of "species" — it routinely uses four, and the definitions sometimes disagree. Researching where they converge and where they diverge with Claude reveals that the disagreement is not sloppiness: it is the data.

- Prompt seed: `claude "Explain the four major species definitions used in biology and anthropology: biological, ecological, phylogenetic, and mate-recognition. For each: state what the definition measures, give its strongest application domain, and identify one specific case where it fails or produces a problematic verdict. Do all four definitions agree on whether these are one species or two?"`
- Read / check: Biological species definition should name Mayr and identify fossil/asexual organisms as failure cases. Phylogenetic should name derived characteristics as the criterion. Sulawesi macaque case: biological definition says one species (fertile offspring), ecological says two (different niches), phylogenetic is ambiguous, mate-recognition says two (different signals). The brief should explicitly note the disagreement.
- Human supplies: Nothing — fully synthetic. Spot-check the Sulawesi macaque hybridization claim against published primatology (Macaca maura × M. tonkeana hybrids are documented in the literature).
- Output medium: Manim (a 4-panel scorecard grid fills in column by column: each definition scores the Sulawesi case YES/NO/AMBIGUOUS; the disagreement pattern is the visual payoff)
- The change: Ask Claude to apply all four definitions to the Neanderthal-Homo sapiens case — genetic evidence shows 2–4% Neanderthal DNA in non-African modern humans. Which definition says they were the same species and which says they were different?
- Teardown angle: When four definitions disagree, you have not finished thinking — you have found the edge of the tool. The four definitions are four answers to four different questions. The Sulawesi case is hard because it asks all four questions at once.
- Exclusions: Full hominin phylogeny, dating methods — separate cards.

- Teardown angle: Natural selection is precise and testable. Social Darwinism is an error about what "fitness" means. The two have never belonged in the same argument — but they have been conflated for 150 years, and the conflation is always in service of someone's politics.

## Candidate 05 — "Research Intersectionality with Claude"
- Source: anthropology/chapters/09-social-inequalities.md
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: A Black woman in a 1989 workplace discrimination case did not fit the legal categories for race discrimination (because Black men were hired) or sex discrimination (because white women were hired). Kimberlé Crenshaw named what happened: intersecting axes of inequality produce outcomes that neither axis alone predicts. Researching the framework through Claude makes the insight applicable beyond its legal origin.
- The artifact: A sourced 3-case brief applying Crenshaw's intersectionality framework: (1) the original 1989 GM factory case, (2) the São Paulo bank associate from the chapter (Black woman in investment banking — race × gender × class), (3) the Indian Dalit nurse (caste × gender × education) — each case showing which combination of axes produces the specific outcome, and why single-axis analysis would miss it.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Explain Kimberlé Crenshaw's intersectionality framework: what is it, what problem does it solve that single-axis discrimination analysis cannot, and what was the 1989 General Motors case that motivated it? Then apply the framework to two contemporary cases: (1) a Black woman entering investment banking in Brazil who faces exclusions that neither Black male colleagues nor white female colleagues face in the same way, (2) a Dalit woman nurse in rural India who faces caste-based informal exclusions in a professional setting. For each case: identify which axes of inequality are intersecting, what outcome each produces, and why a race-only or gender-only analysis would miss what is happening."`
- Read / check: The GM case: Black women were not protected by race discrimination law (because Black men were hired in factory jobs) OR sex discrimination law (because white women were hired in office jobs) — the intersection created a gap. São Paulo case: race + gender + professional setting produces "onlyness" exclusion. Dalit nurse: caste + gender + credential creates a soft exclusion that is durable without active malice. The framework should be distinguished from "adding up" oppressions.
- Human supplies: Nothing — fully synthetic. Spot-check Crenshaw's 1989 paper citation (Demarginalizing the Intersection of Race and Sex, University of Chicago Legal Forum).
- Output medium: Manim (a Venn diagram animation: two overlapping circles (race, gender) — the overlap zone lights up and is labeled with the specific outcome; then a third circle (class/caste) added; the three-way overlap zone shown as the intersectionality region)
- The change: Ask Claude to apply intersectionality to a case where the intersection produces an advantage rather than a disadvantage — are there cases where combining axes confers privilege that single-axis analysis would also miss?
- Teardown angle: Intersectionality is not additive — it is multiplicative, and sometimes the product is qualitatively different from either axis alone. The framework was invented to see the gap between the categories, not to describe people as walking bundles of disadvantage.
- Exclusions: Full critical race theory debate, specific legislation — tangents.

## Candidate 07 — "Research Phylogenetic Trees with Claude"

- Hook: A phylogenetic tree is not a ladder — it is a branching diagram, and every misreading of it as a ladder produces bad arguments about evolutionary "progress." - The artifact: A sourced 3-part brief: (1) how to read a phylogenetic tree — nodes as common ancestors, branches as lineages, time as the y-axis, (2) the hominin tree from Australopithecus through Homo sapiens with key species (A. afarensis, A. africanus, H. habilis, H. erectus, H. neanderthalensis, H. sapiens) placed with approximate dates, (3) the three most common misreadings of phylogenetic trees (ladder = progress, side branches = failures, modern humans descended from Neanderthals) each corrected.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Explain how to read a phylogenetic tree — what nodes represent, what branches represent, how time is shown, and what the tree does and does not claim about evolutionary relationships. Then sketch the major hominin species from Australopithecus afarensis (3.9–2.9 million years ago) through Homo sapiens, placing each on the tree with approximate dates and key anatomical features. - The change: Ask Claude how ancient DNA evidence (aDNA from Neanderthal and Denisovan specimens) has changed the hominin tree in the past 15 years — what relationships did aDNA confirm or overturn?
- Teardown angle: The tree is not a story of inevitable progress toward modern humans. It is a branching pattern of experimentation, most of which ended — and the one lineage that made it to today had many cousins it lived alongside and occasionally interbred with.

## Candidate 08 — "Research Sapir-Whorf in Weak Form with Claude"
- Source: anthropology/chapters/06-language-and-communication.md
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)

- The artifact: A sourced 3-study brief: (1) Berlin and Kay's color-term universals (basic color terms appear in a predictable sequence across languages), (2) Lera Boroditsky's Russian blue experiment (Russian speakers with separate terms for light-blue and dark-blue distinguish shades faster than English speakers), (3) the Pirahã language (no color terms, no number words, no recursion) — each showing what the weak form predicts and what it does not.
- Prompt seed: `claude "What is the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis, and what is the difference between its strong and weak forms? Which version survives the current empirical evidence? Give me three specific empirical studies that bear on the question: Berlin and Kay's color universals, Lera Boroditsky's Russian color perception research, and the Pirahã language. For each study: what was tested, what was found, and whether the finding supports the strong form, the weak form, or neither. Close with 150 words on what the weak form implies about the relationship between translation and thought — can you translate the Pirahã concept of 'approximately two days from now' into English without losing something?"`
- Read / check: Berlin and Kay: 11 basic color terms appear universally in a predictable sequence (white/black → red → yellow/green/blue → ...) — supports universals over linguistic relativity. Boroditsky: Russian speakers faster on blue-shade discrimination when distinction crosses a lexical boundary — supports weak form. Pirahã: no numbers, no color terms, no recursion (Everett 2005, contested) — potentially supports strong form for numerical cognition. The translation brief should acknowledge the weak form: not "can't translate" but "the conceptual handle is different."
- Human supplies: Nothing — fully synthetic. Note that the Pirahã claims are contested in linguistics (Everett vs. generativists) — Claude should flag this.
- Output medium: Manim (a spectrum of color terms animates: English (11 basic terms) vs. Russian (12, including separate siniy/goluboy) vs. a language with fewer terms; the reaction-time difference illuminates the weak-form effect)
- The change: Ask Claude to find a concept from a non-English language that has no single-word English equivalent (saudade, Schadenfreude, hiraeth) and apply the weak-form framework: does the English speaker who learns the word think about that domain differently?
- Teardown angle: The strong form is wrong and the weak form is subtle. Language does not trap thought in a cage — but it does build some footpaths more worn than others.
- Exclusions: Full Chomsky-Everett debate on recursion, language evolution neurobiology — separate cards.

## Candidate 10 — "Research the Tragedy of the Commons Across Cultures with Claude"
- Source: anthropology/chapters/07-work-life-and-value-economic-anthropology.md; anthropology/chapters/02-methods-cultural-and-archaeological.md
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: Garrett Hardin said the tragedy of the commons is inevitable. Elinor Ostrom spent a career showing it is not — when communities meet certain conditions. Anthropological fieldwork from fishing communities, irrigation systems, and alpine pastures shows that cultural governance mechanisms can solve the commons problem without markets or government. Researching the cases with Claude reveals what the conditions are and when they fail.
- The artifact: A sourced 3-case brief: (1) the Newfoundland cod collapse (tragedy as predicted — open access, no governance), (2) Ostrom's Nobel-winning Swiss alpine pasture case (successfully governed commons — small group, mutual monitoring, graduated sanctions), (3) a modern digital commons failure (a Wikipedia category that was vandalized to uselessness before moderation was introduced) — each evaluated against Ostrom's eight conditions.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Elinor Ostrom's 2009 Nobel Prize was for showing that communities can govern shared resources without privatization or government regulation. Summarize her eight conditions for successful commons governance. Then evaluate three cases: (1) the Newfoundland cod collapse of the early 1990s — which of Ostrom's conditions were absent? (2) Traditional Swiss alpine pasture management — which conditions were present? (3) A Wikipedia category that collapsed into vandalism before moderation was introduced — does Ostrom's framework apply to digital commons, and what modifications are needed?"`
- Read / check: Ostrom's eight conditions should be accurately listed (clearly defined boundaries, congruence between rules and local conditions, collective-choice arrangements, monitoring, graduated sanctions, conflict-resolution mechanisms, recognition by external authorities, nested enterprises). Newfoundland collapse: open access (no boundary), no local governance mechanism, external regulation that failed to hold. Swiss alpine: centuries of evolved local rules, boundary definition, monitoring by herders, graduated fines.
- Human supplies: Nothing — fully synthetic. Spot-check Ostrom's conditions against her 1990 book Governing the Commons.
- Output medium: Manim (an 8-condition checklist animates: each condition appears as a checkmark or X for each of the three cases; the pattern of successes and failures is the visual argument)
- The change: Ask Claude to apply Ostrom's conditions to a contemporary commons governance challenge: ocean fisheries under international law, or global carbon emissions. Which conditions are present and which are absent?
- Teardown angle: The tragedy of the commons is not a natural law — it is a specific combination of conditions. Ostrom found the conditions. The question for any commons is not "will it collapse?" but "which conditions are absent, and can they be built?"
- Exclusions: Full game theory of commons, specific environmental policy — tangents.
- Score: 7/10
