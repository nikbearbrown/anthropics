# Chapter 17 — Medical Anthropology

## Suggested titles

- *The Body Between Two Worlds: Medical Anthropology*
- *Disease, Illness, Sickness — Why the Distinctions Matter*
- *When Medical Systems Meet: Medical Anthropology*

## TL;DR

Health is biological and cultural at once. Medical anthropology pulls apart the strands — *disease* (biological abnormality), *illness* (the patient's experience), *sickness* (the social role) — then traces how every culture builds its own ethnomedical system, biomedicine included. The chapter walks through the Hmong-American case of Lia Lee, the framework of medical pluralism, and the way structural violence shapes who gets sick and who gets cured.

## 1. Lia Lee

Merced, California, in 1982. A three-month-old girl named Lia Lee has her first seizure. Her parents, Foua Yang and Nao Kao Lee, are Hmong refugees who fled the secret war in Laos. They take Lia to Merced Community Medical Center. The doctors diagnose epilepsy and prescribe a complex regimen of anticonvulsant medications. The Lees diagnose *qaug dab peg* — *the spirit catches you and you fall down* — a condition the Hmong believe is caused by a wandering soul that has been frightened from the body. The condition is also a sign that the afflicted person is a future *txiv neeb*, a shaman, marked from infancy.

The doctors and the Lees do not speak the same language. They do not share the same medical framework. They do not even agree on what is happening to Lia. The doctors see a brain disorder treatable by phenobarbital and anticonvulsants. The Lees see a soul-loss event that requires animal sacrifice, soul-calling rituals, and the particular care due to a child the spirits have selected. The doctors find the medication regimen — twenty pills a day across multiple changing prescriptions — straightforward; the Lees find it incomprehensible, dangerous, and culturally insulting. The Lees often do not give the medicine. The doctors order Lia removed from her parents and placed in foster care. After fifteen months, Lia is returned. She suffers a major seizure that causes brain death. She lives in a vegetative state for another twenty-six years, cared for by her family at home. She dies in 2012.

Anne Fadiman's 1997 book *The Spirit Catches You and You Fall Down* tells the story in detail. The book has been required reading for medical students for two decades because it does what medical anthropology does: shows that medicine is not just biology and pharmacology but also culture, language, power, family structure, refugee experience, and the assumption — held by both Hmong and American doctors — that one's own framework is the universal one. *Both sides loved Lia. Both sides wanted her to live. Both sides held frameworks the other could not see.*

The Lia Lee case is not unique. Every clinical encounter in which the patient and the provider come from different cultural traditions reproduces some version of the problem. The provider's framework is what doctors call *biomedicine*; the patient's framework is what anthropologists call *ethnomedicine*. The chapter walks through the analytical apparatus that lets you see what is actually going on when the two systems meet.

## 2. Concept 1 — Disease, illness, sickness, and the social construction of health

### The four words

The conceptual move that founds medical anthropology is a careful pulling-apart of words English uses interchangeably. *Disease*, *illness*, *sickness*, *malady* — in everyday speech, near-synonyms. In medical anthropology, four distinct things.

**Disease** is the biological condition — the viral infection, the broken bone, the malignant tumor, the seizure-prone brain. Disease is, in principle, what biomedical instruments can detect. A blood test, a biopsy, an MRI scan, an EEG.

**Illness** is the patient's experience of the disease. The fever, the pain, the fatigue, the worry about missing class, the disappointment of a canceled trip, the question of whether to call the doctor. Illness is *sociocultural* — shaped by what the culture teaches the patient to feel, name, and report.

**Sickness** is the social perception of ill health and the social roles it triggers. The spouse who calls in sick to work for the patient. The schoolteacher who excuses absences. The friends who bring soup. The state that issues a disability pension. Sickness is *interpersonal* — what others do because they perceive the patient as ill.

**Malady** is the umbrella term anthropologists use for all three together, when the analytical question does not require separating them.

The distinctions are not pedantic. Two patients with the same disease may have radically different illness experiences, depending on what their culture teaches them about the disease's meaning. A patient may have a serious disease and almost no illness experience (the asymptomatic infection); a patient may have an intense illness experience without a detectable disease (the chronic-pain patient who cannot get a diagnosis); a patient may have severe sickness without anyone in their network treating it as such (the depressed worker whose colleagues see only laziness). Disease, illness, and sickness move in loose coordination, and *the analytical work of medical anthropology starts with refusing to collapse them*.

### Boas, race, and the foundation

The social-construction-of-health move has a specific intellectual lineage. Franz Boas (Chapter 1) conducted a 1912 study of immigrant families in New York City. The dominant racial-anthropology view of his time held that head shape, bodily proportions, and cognitive capacity were biologically fixed traits passed down within ethnic groups — and therefore good evidence for the racial hierarchy that justified U.S. immigration policy and Jim Crow. Boas showed something the field did not want to see. The children of Italian, Jewish, and Slavic immigrants, raised in New York on different diets and different child-rearing practices than their parents had experienced in Europe, had different head shapes, different growth curves, and different developmental outcomes than their parents. The supposedly fixed biological traits were not fixed. *Biology was responsive to culture, generation by generation*. The racial hierarchy was not biological destiny; it was a social arrangement using biology as cover.

Boas's empirical work cleared the ground for medical anthropology. If biology and culture interact at the scale of one immigrant generation, then any clinical encounter is also a cultural encounter, and any health outcome is also a social outcome. The four-field framework (biological + cultural + linguistic + archaeological anthropology) collapses into one question when applied to bodies: *what do biology and culture do together*?

### Sick role

Talcott Parsons, the mid-20th-century American sociologist, codified the **sick role** in 1951. To be socially recognized as sick, a person must do four things. They must be exempt from normal social responsibilities (not expected to work, attend class, fulfill family obligations at the usual rate). They must be expected to *want* to get well (illness as something to recover from, not something to embrace). They must be expected to seek competent help (the doctor, the healer, the elder). They must be expected to cooperate with that help (take the medicine, follow the regimen).

The framework is contested but useful. The contestation is that Parsons's model is built around an idealized middle-class American patient who can take time off work, has health insurance, has a primary-care physician, and has socially accepted permission to be unwell. Many people across many cultures and many U.S. demographics do not have those conditions. The framework's *contested edges* are exactly where medical anthropology does its work — where a Black mother is not granted the sick role and so her chest pain is read as drug-seeking; where a Hmong family does not recognize the doctor as the *competent help* and so does not cooperate with phenobarbital; where the chronically ill patient is not expected to want to get well because their condition is incurable and so falls outside the framework entirely.

### The body as cultural object

The body is not neutral biology with culture added on top. The body is itself culturally shaped, all the way down. Posture, gait, weight, scarification, circumcision, dental work, tattoos, hair styling, dress, athletic conditioning, chronic-disease management — all are biology-and-culture together. The Tibetan farmer and the Wall Street trader have different bodies not because their genomes differ much (they do not) but because their cultural lives have built different bodies out of similar biological starting materials. *Medical anthropology takes the body as a cultural artifact, the way archaeology takes a pot.*

## 3. Concept 2 — Ethnomedicine, biomedicine, medical pluralism

### Ethnomedicine

**Ethnomedicine** is a culture's body of knowledge about health, healing, and the management of malady. Ethnomedicine includes theories of what causes disease, classifications of disease types, identification of healing specialists, training and apprenticeship pathways for those specialists, treatment techniques, materia medica (what substances treat what), ritual procedures, and the social arrangements that make healing possible (who pays the healer, who consults whom, who holds authority).

Every culture has an ethnomedical system. Industrial Western societies have one. Pre-colonial Andean communities had one. Contemporary urban Beijing has multiple operating in parallel. The ethnographic record documents thousands of distinct ethnomedical systems, with some patterns recurring (most have herbal pharmacopeias, most have specialists, most identify some illness as supernaturally caused) and some patterns culturally specific (the precise classification of *susto* in Latin America; the *yin-yang* balance framework of traditional Chinese medicine; the humoral framework of medieval European medicine).

**Traditional ecological knowledge (TEK)** is the local, accumulated, place-specific knowledge of plants, animals, and other natural substances used in ethnomedicine. The Amazonian populations Victoria Reyes-García studied along the Maniqui River carry the names, locations, seasons, preparation methods, dosages, and cautions for hundreds of medicinal plants. The TEK is empirical — it has been tested across generations on actual bodies and refined accordingly — and its empirical character is what makes it interesting to contemporary pharmaceutical research. The 1897 isolation of acetylsalicylic acid from willow bark by Felix Hoffmann at Bayer — yielding aspirin — is the canonical case. The willow-bark ethnobotanical knowledge was already there; biomedicine extracted and patented the active compound.

The pharmaceutical-extraction relationship has its own politics. The TEK belongs, in any reasonable accounting, to the source community. The extracted compound is patented by a corporation. The source community typically receives nothing. The 1992 Convention on Biological Diversity and various subsequent benefit-sharing agreements were attempts to address the inequality. The agreements have been more honored in form than in substance.

### Biomedicine as one ethnomedical system

The most consequential analytical move in medical anthropology is the recognition that **biomedicine** — the science-based system practiced in hospitals, prescribed by physicians trained at medical schools, structured by health-insurance markets and pharmaceutical regulation — is *itself an ethnomedical system*. Not the universal medicine to which all other ethnomedical systems are imperfect approximations. One specific ethnomedical system, with a specific history (European Enlightenment science, 19th-century germ theory, 20th-century pharmaceutical industry), specific cultural assumptions (mind-body dualism, individual rather than collective patients, treatment focused on disease rather than on illness or sickness), and specific blind spots.

This is not a relativist claim that biomedicine is *just one tradition among many* with no special claim to truth. Biomedicine has one very large empirical advantage. Its claims are systematically tested, its interventions are evaluated through controlled trials, and its models update on evidence. Acetylsalicylic acid actually does relieve headaches in any human body that takes it. Antibiotics actually do kill the bacteria causing a particular infection. Vaccines actually do confer immunity to specific pathogens. The empirical core of biomedicine — the part that survives trial-by-trial — is the most-tested medical knowledge in human history, and it has produced the largest and fastest health gains any species has experienced.

The medical-anthropology critique is not that biomedicine's empirical core is wrong. The critique is that *biomedicine often sells more than it can deliver*. It claims to be a culture-free science when much of its practice is culturally specific. It treats disease while sometimes ignoring illness and sickness. It assumes patient compliance when patient compliance depends on whether the patient's framework recognizes the prescribing doctor as competent help. It universalizes its categories (the DSM-5's diagnostic system) when many of its categories do not travel cleanly across cultures. The Lia Lee case shows the gap: the medication regimen made biological sense, but the prescribing doctors did not see that they were asking the Lees to operate inside a framework the Lees did not share.

The contemporary discipline asks biomedical practitioners to recognize biomedicine *as* an ethnomedical system — a practice that is empirical at its core but culturally embedded in its delivery — and to treat the patient's ethnomedical framework as something to be engaged rather than overridden.

### Medical pluralism

**Medical pluralism** is the situation in which multiple ethnomedical systems coexist within a single society, used by the same people for different conditions, often simultaneously. Contemporary China is the textbook case. Most Chinese consult both biomedical doctors and traditional Chinese medicine practitioners depending on the condition — biomedicine for a broken bone or an acute infection, traditional Chinese medicine for chronic complaints, balance issues, or maintenance. The state has built parallel medical infrastructures for both, with traditional Chinese medicine receiving formal institutional support. Most patients see no contradiction in using both.

Medical pluralism is the rule, not the exception. The contemporary U.S. is medically plural — biomedicine alongside chiropractic, acupuncture, homeopathy, herbalism, faith healing, yoga and Ayurveda for stress, essential oils, the supplement industry. The U.K. is medically plural — biomedicine alongside the formally regulated osteopathy and chiropractic. India is medically plural — biomedicine alongside Ayurveda, Unani medicine, Siddha medicine, homeopathy, and various regional traditions. Most patients in most places navigate plural systems strategically.

The privilege of biomedicine in pluralistic systems varies. In the U.S., biomedicine has the formal authority of insurance, hospital admitting privileges, the FDA, and state licensing; other systems exist alongside but not on equal regulatory footing. In China, traditional Chinese medicine has near-equal institutional standing. In Hawaiʻi, the precolonial *lāʻau lapaʻau* tradition was actively suppressed for over a century and is now being revitalized — Juliet McMullin's *Healthy Ancestor* (2010) traces both the suppression and the revival.

Patients in pluralistic systems often consult multiple practitioners simultaneously without telling each one about the others. The chiropractor is not told about the chemotherapy. The biomedical oncologist is not told about the herbal tea. The friction is sometimes harmful (some herbs interact dangerously with chemotherapy drugs) and sometimes harmless. *The patient's strategy is rational: use what works for what.* The biomedical practitioner who takes the time to ask about other treatments — and not in a tone of incredulity — gets the information needed to coordinate care.

## 4. Concept 3 — Inequality, structural violence, and culture-bound distress

### Critical medical anthropology

The discipline's most-developed theoretical strand is **critical medical anthropology** (CMA), sometimes called political-economic medical anthropology (PEMA). The framework, developed in the 1980s and 1990s through the work of Merrill Singer, Hans Baer, and others, treats health inequalities not as accidental or biological but as the predictable products of social structure. Who gets sick, who gets treated, who survives, who dies — these are sorted by class, race, gender, citizenship, and geography in ways that biomedicine alone cannot explain.

The foundational concept is **structural violence**, a term Johan Galtung introduced in 1969 and Paul Farmer made central to medical anthropology in the 1990s and 2000s. Structural violence is the systematic harm done to specific populations by social arrangements that limit their access to resources, treatment, dignity, and life itself. Structural violence does not require an individual perpetrator. The arrangement is the violence. The Black New Yorker who has a stroke and dies waiting for an ambulance that takes twice as long to arrive in their neighborhood as in a white neighborhood — there is no specific malicious actor, but the systemic disparity has killed them. The structural violence is the disparity.

The Tuskegee syphilis study (1932–1972) is a different kind of case — direct violence by a research arm of the U.S. Public Health Service, in which 399 Black men in Macon County, Alabama, with syphilis were observed but not treated for 40 years, even after penicillin became standard care in 1947. The men were not told they had syphilis. They were told they had *bad blood*. Many died of the disease. Wives and children contracted it. The study was halted in 1972 only after an Associated Press exposé. President Bill Clinton apologized in 1997. The Tuskegee study is not just historical; it is a continuing reason that Black Americans have measurably lower trust in the U.S. medical system, with real consequences for vaccine uptake, clinical-trial participation, and the willingness to seek care. *The structural violence and the Tuskegee study together explain a substantial portion of the racial health gap that biomedicine alone cannot explain.*

### Syndemics

Merrill Singer's concept of **syndemics** — the *synergistic interaction of two or more diseases or health conditions in a population, amplified by social conditions* — extends the CMA framework. Most chronic-disease patients in marginalized communities have not one condition but several, mutually reinforcing. The diabetic with depression with food insecurity with housing instability is not four separate problems; it is one syndemic, and treating any one of the four without addressing the others tends to fail.

Singer's work on the SAVA syndemic — *substance abuse, violence, and AIDS* — in low-income U.S. urban populations is the canonical case. The three conditions are not independent; they accelerate one another. Substance use makes risky sexual behavior more likely; violence creates trauma that drives substance use; HIV transmission concentrates in the populations most exposed to both. Treating substance use alone, or treating HIV alone, without addressing the underlying violence and the structural conditions producing all three, produces the modest results that decades of public health data confirm.

The syndemic framework reframes what seems like multiple diseases into one social problem. *The same arrangement is producing all of them*. The implication for clinical practice is that treating chronic conditions in marginalized populations requires more than pharmaceutical management; it requires addressing the social arrangements producing the syndemic. The implication for policy is that public-health spending isolated from housing, education, and labor policy treats symptoms while preserving the disease.

### Culture-bound syndromes and cultural concepts of distress

A different kind of inequality lives in the diagnostic categories themselves. **Culture-bound syndromes** (the older term) and **cultural concepts of distress** (the DSM-5's contemporary preferred term, abbreviated CCD) are conditions whose presentation and meaning are specific to particular cultural contexts.

*Susto* (Spanish, *fright*) appears in Latin American populations across Mexico, Central America, and parts of South America. The condition is precipitated by a frightening or traumatic event; symptoms include weight loss, fatigue, restlessness, depression, and sometimes a sense that the soul has temporarily left the body. Arthur Rubel, Carl O'Nell, and Rolando Collado-Ardón documented *susto* in a 1991 ethnographic study and showed that the condition's prevalence and mortality outcomes were measurable. *Susto* does not appear in populations that do not share the cultural framework that recognizes it.

*Hwa-byung* (Korean, *fire illness*) appears predominantly in middle-aged Korean women. It is conceptualized as the build-up of long-suppressed anger and resentment, manifesting as chest pressure, palpitations, hot flashes, and depression. The condition appears in Korean diasporic populations as well as in Korea.

*Ataque de nervios* appears in Caribbean and Latin American populations. Acute attacks of crying, screaming, trembling, palpitations, and loss of consciousness, typically precipitated by family conflict or grief. The condition has been formally entered into the DSM-5 as a cultural concept of distress.

The most important Western culture-bound syndrome is **anorexia nervosa**, as Caroline Giles Banks argued in 1992. Anorexia appears predominantly in cultures that value extreme thinness in women (the U.S., Western Europe, increasingly East Asian cities under exposure to Western media). It is rare or absent in populations that have not absorbed those beauty standards. Anorexia is not therefore *less real* than non-Western culture-bound syndromes; it is real, often fatal, and biomedically treatable. It is also culturally produced. *The distinction between universal and culture-bound disorders is itself culturally produced.*

The DSM-5 renamed the category from *culture-bound syndromes* to *cultural concepts of distress* partly to avoid the implication that biomedical diagnostic categories were themselves not culture-bound. The implicit message of the rename: biomedicine's depression, biomedicine's PTSD, biomedicine's borderline personality disorder are *also* cultural concepts of distress, particular to the contemporary Western diagnostic tradition. The rename has been received with mixed enthusiasm. The conceptual point — that no diagnostic category is fully culture-free — is the medical-anthropology contribution to psychiatry.

### Kleinman's explanatory model

Arthur Kleinman, the Harvard psychiatrist-anthropologist, proposed in 1978 a clinical method for working with the cultural construction of illness. The **explanatory model** is the patient's own understanding of what is happening to them — what caused the illness, what its consequences will be, how it should be treated, who should treat it. Kleinman recommended that clinicians ask eight questions of the patient at the start of any consultation:

1. What do you call this problem?
2. What do you believe is the cause of this problem?
3. What course do you expect it to take?
4. How serious is it?
5. What kind of treatment do you think the patient should receive?
6. What are the most important results you hope to receive from this treatment?
7. What are the chief problems the sickness has caused?
8. What do you fear most about the sickness?

The questions are not therapeutic theater. They are clinical instruments. They surface the patient's framework so the clinician can map their own framework onto it, identify the gaps, and either translate or negotiate. *Lia Lee's doctors did not ask these questions.* If they had, they would have learned that the Lees believed Lia's seizures had spiritual causes, that the family wanted her treated with both biomedicine and *txiv neeb* ritual, that the family was deeply suspicious of phenobarbital because it caused side effects they understood as harmful, and that they would coordinate care if they were treated as adult partners rather than as obstacles. Whether different conduct would have changed the outcome is impossible to know. The framework would have been visible, which would have changed what each side could ask of the other.

The explanatory model is now standard in medical-school curricula. It is taught alongside Kleinman's other major contribution — the *illness narrative* interview, in which the patient's experience of the disease is recorded in their own words rather than reduced to symptom checklists. Both methods come from medical anthropology and have shaped contemporary clinical training.

## 5. Tool #17 — Culture-bound syndrome / medical pluralism analyzer

The seventeenth tool in your AI-Augmented Anthropology Toolkit takes a presented health complaint and helps you analyze it across the four-frame disease/illness/sickness/malady distinction, identify the ethnomedical systems likely to be relevant, and surface the patient's explanatory model. The tool is for the medical-anthropology student, the public-health worker, the medical educator, or the clinician approaching a culturally complex case.

**Refined prompt template:**

> You are a medical anthropologist applying the conceptual apparatus of the field to a presented case. The user will paste a description of a health complaint, a clinical encounter, or a public-health situation.
>
> Walk through the analysis in five steps.
>
> **Step 1 — Disease, illness, sickness, malady.** Pull apart the four. What is the *disease* (the biomedical-pathology layer, where one is identifiable)? What is the *illness* (the patient's experience, including pain, anxiety, social disruption)? What is the *sickness* (the social roles activated, the people in the patient's network responding to the perception of ill health)? Where the layers do not align, name the misalignment.
>
> **Step 2 — Ethnomedical systems.** Identify the ethnomedical frameworks likely to be in play. Biomedicine? Traditional Chinese medicine? Ayurveda? Curanderismo? Faith healing? Ethnomedicine specific to the patient's heritage? Multiple simultaneously? Note where the patient and the provider may operate inside different frameworks.
>
> **Step 3 — Cultural concepts of distress.** Could the presentation be a culture-bound syndrome or cultural concept of distress (susto, hwa-byung, ataque de nervios, anorexia nervosa, etc.)? Or is it more readily classified within standard biomedical categories? Or both?
>
> **Step 4 — Structural and political-economic factors.** Apply the critical medical anthropology lens. What structural conditions (class, race, immigration status, geography, insurance status) shape the case? Is there evidence of structural violence? Could this be a syndemic — multiple conditions amplifying each other under social conditions?
>
> **Step 5 — Explanatory-model questions.** Generate three to five Kleinman-style explanatory-model questions specific to this case, designed to surface the patient's framework and identify the gaps with the provider's framework.
>
> Be specific. Use the chapter's named scholars (Boas, Farmer, Singer, Kleinman, Banks, Rubel, Fadiman) where their work is genuinely relevant.

**Test case 1 — A 78-year-old Vietnamese-American man brought to the ER by his daughter for "fatigue and confusion."** The man speaks limited English; the daughter translates. He has been diagnosed with type 2 diabetes for fifteen years; a recent A1c reading is 11.2 (very poorly controlled). The daughter says her father has been refusing to take metformin because his Vietnamese herbalist has prescribed a regimen of bitter melon, cinnamon, and a daily acupuncture session.

The tool should surface: disease (poorly controlled diabetes); illness (fatigue and confusion as the patient experiences them, possibly with hyperglycemia-induced cognitive symptoms); sickness (the daughter as caregiver; the clinic as the consulted authority); ethnomedical pluralism (biomedicine and Vietnamese traditional medicine operating in parallel); the structural factor of language barriers; the explanatory-model questions that would let the ER physician understand what the patient thinks his diabetes is and how the herbal regimen fits.

**Test case 2 — A 34-year-old Black woman presenting to a Boston hospital with chest pain.** She reports her pain at 8/10. The triage nurse rates her pain as 4/10 and assigns a non-urgent classification. After three hours of waiting, she is seen by a physician who orders an EKG that turns out to be abnormal. The patient is admitted for cardiac workup.

The tool should surface: disease (possible cardiac event); illness (the woman's pain and fear as she experiences them); sickness (the partial activation of the sick role — she is allowed to wait, but not granted urgent attention); the structural-violence layer (multiple studies have documented systematic underrating of pain in Black patients, particularly Black women, by triage staff); the explanatory-model questions a culturally competent provider would ask.

**Test case 3 — A 19-year-old Latina college student presenting to the campus health center with weight loss, fatigue, and a sense that "something is missing."** The intake form notes she has been struggling academically and recently lost a grandmother to whom she was close. She mentions in passing that her mother and aunt have suggested she has *susto*.

The tool should surface: disease (possible depression, possible thyroid dysfunction, possible malnutrition); illness (the patient's experience of grief and anomie); sickness (the family's recognition of the condition through the *susto* framework); the cultural-concept-of-distress angle; the medical-pluralism question (the family's framework alongside the campus health center's); the explanatory-model questions that a sensitive provider would ask.

**Failure modes (three documented).**

**Failure mode 1 — Romanticizing ethnomedicine.** The tool sometimes drifts toward presenting non-biomedical traditions as universally beneficial alternatives to biomedicine. Many ethnomedical traditions have been deeply harmful in specific applications (the use of bloodletting in early modern European medicine; the use of mercury and lead in classical Ayurvedic preparations; specific TCM ingredients sourced from endangered species). The tool needs explicit instruction to evaluate ethnomedical traditions on their actual empirical record, not as romantic alternatives.

**Failure mode 2 — Dismissing biomedical concerns to make space for ethnomedicine.** The diabetes case can produce a response that downplays the medical urgency of the A1c of 11.2 (which is genuinely dangerous — a blood-sugar level that elevated produces real organ damage and elevated mortality risk). The tool should treat biomedical realities as serious empirical claims while still surfacing the patient's framework. Cultural sensitivity does not mean clinical incompetence.

**Failure mode 3 — Hallucinating scholar attributions.** The tool sometimes attributes specific quotations or case studies to Kleinman, Farmer, or Singer that those scholars did not actually publish. Always require citations to specific publications and flag uncertain attributions with `[verify]`.

The tool joins the running project — your second-term capstone — alongside the previous sixteen.

## 6. Integration — Reading three cases

The chapter's apparatus on three contemporary cases.

**Lia Lee revisited.** The Hmong family in Merced. The disease was epilepsy. The illness was *qaug dab peg*. The sickness was a contested social role — the family treated Lia as a marked future shaman; the medical system treated her as a dangerously non-compliant patient, then briefly as a foster-care case. The ethnomedical pluralism was real; the U.S. medical system did not recognize it. The structural factors — Hmong refugee status, language barriers, insurance limits, the specific power dynamics of a small California county hospital — produced the worst possible outcome. Kleinman's explanatory-model questions would not have guaranteed a different outcome. They would have guaranteed a better-informed encounter, in which both sides could see what the other side was holding.

**The COVID-19 disparities, 2020–2022.** Black, Latino, Indigenous, and Pacific Islander populations in the United States died from COVID-19 at rates 1.5 to 3 times the white-population rate, depending on the period and the specific demographic. The disparities were not biological; they tracked exposure (essential-worker concentration in the affected communities), access to care (insurance status, hospital availability), pre-existing comorbidities (which themselves track structural factors), and trust in the medical system (the Tuskegee legacy and ongoing medical racism). The CMA framework reads the COVID disparities as a syndemic — the virus interacting with prior chronic conditions and structural arrangements to produce concentrated mortality. The framework also predicts that the pandemic-response disparities would deepen the trust gap, which they have.

**Long COVID and the chronic-disease frame.** Some 5–10% of COVID patients have developed long-term conditions involving fatigue, cognitive impairment, autonomic dysfunction, and varied other symptoms. The biomedical category of *long COVID* is being constructed in real time. Patients describe their illness experience richly; the disease-side biology is not yet well-understood. Many long-COVID patients describe being told their symptoms are not real because the testing is unrevealing — the same gap between disease and illness that medical anthropology has been describing for forty years. The discipline's apparatus reads long COVID as a disease in slow construction (biology being mapped), an illness already present (patient experience clear), a sickness still ambiguous (the social-role acceptance varies by employer, family, and physician).

The chapter's apparatus reads all three. The framework holds across very different kinds of cases.

## 8. Summary and connections

Medical anthropology is the application of anthropological methods to the human body in health and illness. The discipline's first move is the careful pulling-apart of *disease* (biology), *illness* (experience), and *sickness* (social role). The second is the recognition that biomedicine is itself an ethnomedical system, with extraordinary empirical strength at its core and culturally specific blind spots in its delivery. The third is the analysis of medical pluralism — the rule, not the exception — and the strategic ways patients navigate multiple ethnomedical systems simultaneously. The fourth is the critical-medical-anthropology framework that reads health inequalities as products of structural violence and syndemic interaction. The fifth is the recognition that culture-bound syndromes and cultural concepts of distress are not exceptions to biomedicine's universal categories; biomedicine's categories are also culturally produced.

Lia Lee, the Hmong-American girl whose family and doctors loved her and could not save her, is the chapter's anchor case because her story shows the apparatus working — and shows the apparatus failing when not applied. *Both sides loved Lia. Both sides held frameworks the other could not see.* The discipline exists to make the frameworks visible.

**Connections forward.** Chapter 18 (Human-Animal Relationship) takes the body in a different direction — the boundary between human and animal, with health, zoonotic disease, and the One Health framework crossing the boundary repeatedly. Chapter 19 (Indigenous Anthropology) returns to the medical sovereignty question raised here — whose ethnomedical framework counts, who decides, who pays. Chapter 20 (Anthropology on the Ground) closes the book with the applied work that this chapter has been gesturing toward throughout — Paul Farmer's Partners in Health, Dána-Ain Davis's reproductive justice work, Angela Garcia's addiction-and-dispossession ethnography, all of which use the apparatus this chapter has assembled.
