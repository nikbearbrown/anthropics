# Chapter 13 — Religion and Culture

## Suggested titles

## TL;DR

Religion is universal across human cultures and one of the discipline's most analytically dense subjects. This chapter walks through religion as a *system of beliefs and practices around the sacred*; the symbolic and ritual structures (Eliade on sacred place, van Gennep on rites of passage); the structuralist analysis of myth (Lévi-Strauss); and the contemporary forms — including the *nones*, secular religion, and contested boundaries between religion and not-religion.

## 1. Casting the circle

A clearing in a forest in upstate New York, around 9 p.m. on the autumn equinox. A small group — eight people — has gathered. One of them, the *high priestess* of the coven, is preparing to cast a circle. She holds a ritual knife, the *athame*. She walks the perimeter of an imagined circle on the ground, perhaps 12 feet across, tracing it with the knife extended. As she walks, she calls the four directional guardians — *guardians of the east, of the air; of the south, of the fire; of the west, of the water; of the north, of the earth*. She marks the perimeter with salt water (earth and water) and incense (fire and air). She calls the spirit above. She calls the spirit below. The circle is cast.

For the next two hours, this 12-foot ring of forest floor is sacred space. Inside the ring, ordinary rules of behavior are suspended; ritual rules apply. The participants will speak in formalized language. They will perform specific symbolic actions. They will invite communication with the deities the coven recognizes — the Goddess and the God, or specific named deities depending on the working. When the ritual ends, the priestess will dismiss the directional guardians, reverse the circle, and return the ground to its ordinary state. The 12-foot ring will become forest floor again.

This is a Wiccan ritual. Wicca is a 20th-century religious movement, drawing on older pagan European traditions and synthesized in the 1940s and 1950s, primarily in England. It has perhaps a million adherents globally as of 2024 — a tiny fraction of the world's religious population, but a useful case study because the procedure of casting a circle is so explicit. Most religious practices have built their sacred spaces over centuries; Wiccans build theirs each time, in front of you, with visible mechanics.

The mechanics will look familiar to anyone who has read Mircea Eliade's *The Sacred and the Profane* (1957). Eliade argued that *every sacred place* in *every religion* has three structural features. *A threshold* separating the sacred from the profane. *An axis mundi* — a point of contact with the divine, where something sacred has happened or is happening. *An imago mundi* — a microcosm of the world as the religion understands it. The Wiccan circle has all three: the perimeter is the threshold, the center is the axis where the deities are invoked, the four directions and above/below produce the cosmological microcosm. Same structure as a Catholic cathedral. Same structure as a Sikh gurdwara. Same structure as a Yoruba *Ile Òrìṣà*. Same structure as a Mongolian *ovoo*. Same structure as the Adae ceremony at the Asante stool house (Chapter 8).

This chapter walks through religion as anthropology has come to understand it. The argument is not that all religions are *the same* — they are not. The argument is that religion across cultures has a small number of recurring structural features: *symbols* that carry condensed meaning; *rituals* that arrange action over time; *sacred places* that anchor encounter with the divine; *myths* that organize the cosmology; and *communities* that hold the whole thing together. Spotting the recurring features is what the discipline does. Understanding what each specific tradition does *with* the features is the work.

## 2. Concept 1 — Defining religion, and what it includes

### Two definitions, two angles

The discipline uses two definitions of religion that are often cited together. They emphasize different things.

**Émile Durkheim** (1858–1917), in *The Elementary Forms of the Religious Life* (1912), defined religion as *a unified system of beliefs and practices relative to sacred things — that is, things set apart and forbidden — beliefs and practices which unite into one single moral community called a Church, all those who adhere to them*. Durkheim's definition has three parts: (1) a system of beliefs and practices, (2) about *sacred things* (set apart from the ordinary), (3) shared by a community. Notice what is not in the definition: a deity is not required. A creator god is not required. Durkheim was working from his fieldwork on Australian Aboriginal totemism, where the sacred was often a particular animal or plant species rather than an anthropomorphic god. His definition extends to traditions (Theravada Buddhism, certain forms of Confucian practice) that lack a creator-god while remaining recognizably religious.

**Clifford Geertz** (1926–2006), in *The Interpretation of Cultures* (1973), defined religion as *(1) a system of symbols which acts to (2) establish powerful, pervasive, and long-lasting moods and motivations in men by (3) formulating conceptions of a general order of existence and (4) clothing these conceptions with such an aura of factuality that (5) the moods and motivations seem uniquely realistic*. Geertz's definition foregrounds *symbols*, *moods*, and *the way the symbolic system produces a sense of reality*. Geertz's religion is closer to a worldview that *feels* true — a system that makes the world coherent in a way that seems factual rather than constructed.

The two definitions are complementary. Durkheim catches the social-structural side of religion (what people do, with whom, around what). Geertz catches the experiential and meaning side (what religion feels like and why it persists in a person's mind). Most contemporary anthropology of religion uses both.

A working definition for the chapter: *religion is a shared system of beliefs and practices regarding the interaction of natural and supernatural phenomena*. The definition is approximate and admits exceptions; the analytical work follows.

### Distinguishing religion, spirituality, worldview

Three related terms, often blurred.

**Religion** — institutional. A tradition with shared texts, formal practices, ritual specialists, named denominations.

**Spirituality** — personal. The individual's sense of connection to something larger than the ordinary. A spiritual practice may or may not be embedded in a religious institution. Pew Research Center surveys have tracked a substantial U.S. population identifying as *spiritual but not religious* — 27% of Americans in 2017, up from 19% in 2012.

**Worldview** — broader still. The overall outlook through which a person makes sense of the world. Worldviews can be religious or secular. A committed materialist atheist holds a worldview. A practitioner of *qigong* who does not affiliate with any institution holds a spirituality and a worldview. A devout Catholic holds a religion, a spirituality, and a worldview, in tightly aligned form.

**The nones.** A growing demographic in the contemporary U.S., people with no religious affiliation. Pew found 16% of Americans were nones in 2007; 22.8% by 2014; 29% by 2021. Among millennials, the number was 35% by 2014. Nones are not the same as atheists or agnostics — many nones hold spiritual or religious beliefs but reject institutional affiliation. The trend is one of the most significant religious changes in the contemporary U.S., and the chapter will return to it.

### Witchcraft, sorcery, magic

The early-20th-century discipline drew distinctions among *religion*, *magic*, and related terms. James Frazer in *The Golden Bough* (1890) treated magic as primitive proto-science, religion as more developed proto-science, and science as the apex. The framing has not aged well — the evolutionary ladder treats Western traditions as the destination, exactly the move Boas dismantled. The contemporary discipline treats magic, witchcraft, and sorcery as forms of religious practice with their own internal logics.

**Witchcraft** is, broadly, the use of intangible means (spells, incantations, blessings, curses) to cause changes in circumstances. It is associated with formulaic language and ritual gestures. Most cultures with witchcraft traditions distinguish *good* witchcraft (healing, protection, blessing) from *bad* witchcraft (harm, illness, curse). The Beng of Côte d'Ivoire, as documented by Alma Gottlieb, requires that their kings be witches — the king's witchcraft protects the people, and a non-witch could not effectively rule. *Witchcraft is power; power can be used either way.*

**Sorcery** is similar to witchcraft but uses material elements — magical bundles, charged objects, a target's hair or nail clippings — rather than purely intangible means. The distinction between witchcraft and sorcery varies by tradition; the two often blur.

**Magic** in the broader sense is the manipulation of supernatural forces to produce desired outcomes. *Sympathetic magic* (Frazer's term) operates by similarity — a doll resembling a person can be used to affect that person. *Contagious magic* operates by past contact — a target's hair, having been in contact with the target, can be used to act on the target. Frazer's typology survives as descriptive vocabulary even after his evolutionary ladder was abandoned.

### Beings: deities, spirits, ancestors

Most religious traditions populate the world with non-human beings of various kinds.

**Deities** — gods and goddesses, typically anthropomorphic, often with personalities and biographies. Polytheistic traditions (Hindu, ancient Egyptian, Yoruba, Greek/Roman) have many. Monotheistic traditions (Judaism, Christianity, Islam, Sikhism) have one (with various intermediate figures depending on the tradition).

**Spirits** — non-human beings without the full personhood of deities. Tree spirits, river spirits, ancestor spirits, household spirits. *Animism* is the worldview that spirits inhabit natural features (rocks, rivers, trees, animals). E. B. Tylor coined the term *animism* in 1871; the term has had a contested career but is still used. Animist traditions have been documented across most of the world's pre-monotheistic societies and remain widespread in many contemporary contexts.

**Ancestors** — deceased relatives who maintain ongoing relationships with the living. Most West African religious traditions, much of East Asia (especially Confucian-influenced cultures), many Native American traditions, ancient Mediterranean religions all foreground ancestor veneration. The Adae ceremony of the Asante (Chapter 8) is an ancestor ritual.

**Shamanism** is the practice of specialists (*shamans*) who enter altered states to communicate with spirits on behalf of their communities. The term derives from the Tungusic languages of Siberia (*šamán*) but the practice is documented across the circumpolar north, the Americas, parts of Africa, and elsewhere. Shamans typically heal, divine, mediate disputes, escort the souls of the dead.

A note: monotheistic state religions have often suppressed animist and shamanistic traditions, sometimes violently. The contemporary distribution of religion is partly a record of which traditions held political power when. Variations that survive at the periphery (Indigenous traditions in the Amazon, the circumpolar north, parts of Southeast Asia) are valuable not because they are *primitive* but because they preserve cultural patterns the suppressors aimed to eliminate.

## 3. Concept 2 — Symbols, sacred places, and rituals

### Symbols, multivocality

Chapter 3 already introduced symbols. In religious context, symbols carry particularly dense meaning.

A **symbol** is an object, image, gesture, sound, or event that has been culturally invested with meaning beyond itself. Two main subtypes:

**Metaphor** — the symbol has no natural connection to its referent. The Christian cross, the Islamic crescent and star, the Wiccan pentagram — none has any natural connection to the religious tradition it represents; the connection is conventional and culturally produced.

**Metonym** — the symbol is a part standing for the whole. A church wafer that *is* the body of Christ in Catholic theology; a relic of a saint that *is* the saint's continued presence; ancestor altars containing pieces of the ancestor's clothing or hair that *are* the ancestor's continued presence. The metonym is more than representation — it is participation in the thing represented.

**Multivocal** symbols carry multiple meanings simultaneously. The U.S. flag carries different meanings draped over a soldier's casket, waved at a political rally, burned in protest, hung at a Fourth of July picnic. The same physical object signals different things to different people in different contexts. Religious symbols are particularly multivocal because their meaning has accumulated across centuries and across diverse practitioners.

### Sacred place

Mircea Eliade's *The Sacred and the Profane* (1957) provides one of the most-cited frameworks for thinking about religious place. Eliade argued that every sacred place — from the smallest household shrine to the largest cathedral — has three structural features.

**Threshold.** The boundary that separates the sacred from the profane. A doorway, a gate, an altar rail, the perimeter of a Wiccan circle, the courtyard wall of a mosque. The threshold is not just architectural — it is the boundary between two states of being. Crossing it requires preparation: ablution (Muslim, Jewish, Hindu); the removal of shoes (Hindu temple, mosque, many Buddhist contexts); the lighting of incense; a prescribed greeting. *The threshold is often the most carefully observed feature of any sacred place.*

**Axis mundi** — a point of contact with the divine. A specific spot within the sacred place where the divine has manifested or continues to manifest. The Holy of Holies in the Jewish Temple. The altar at the center of the Catholic mass. The Kaaba in Mecca. The bodhi tree in Buddhist tradition. The sacred fire at the heart of a Zoroastrian fire temple. Eliade argued that the *axis mundi* is the religion's vertical link — the point at which the human world and the divine world touch.

**Imago mundi** — the sacred place as microcosm of the cosmos. Cathedrals oriented east toward Jerusalem. Hindu temples mapping the universe through their architecture. Bali traditional houses oriented to the volcano in the north. The Wiccan circle's four directional guardians. A sacred place is not just a place — it is a model of the universe as the religion understands it. To be in the sacred place is to be at the center of the world.

The framework does not mean every religion's sacred place looks the same. The framework means each tradition implements the three features differently, and recognizing the features lets you read any tradition's sacred geography.

### Rituals and rites of passage

A **ritual** is a patterned, repeated, conventional sequence of actions that combines symbols, roles, and meanings to accomplish something the participants consider important. Chapter 3 introduced ritual broadly. This section develops the religious specifics.

The French anthropologist **Arnold van Gennep**, in *The Rites of Passage* (1909), identified a particular subclass of rituals — those that move a person from one social status to another. Rites of passage have three phases.

**Separation.** The person is removed from their ordinary social position. Often this is physical (the bride leaves her parents' house; the initiate goes to a separate camp; the deceased's body is taken from the home). Sometimes it is symbolic (the new soldier's hair is shaved; the bride wears a veil; the candidate puts on special robes).

**Transition (liminality).** The person exists in an in-between state, neither the old status nor the new. The British anthropologist **Victor Turner**, building on van Gennep, called this state *liminal* — *betwixt and between*. Liminal phases are often marked by inversions of normal social rules: hierarchy is suspended, ordinary clothing is replaced, ritual specialists impose unusual demands. Among initiates in many African societies, the liminal phase produces what Turner called **communitas** — an intense egalitarian solidarity among people sharing the experience. Turner argued that *communitas* is the existential opposite of *structure* (the ordinary social hierarchy); rituals produce it deliberately because it provides experiences ordinary structured social life cannot.

**Incorporation.** The person is reintegrated into society in the new status. Public announcement. New clothing. New name. New rights and obligations. The wedding reception; the graduation ceremony's hat-tossing; the baby's naming ceremony.

The three-phase pattern is recognizable across an enormous range of rituals: weddings, funerals, initiations, ordinations, graduations, naturalizations, retirements. The pattern doesn't always operate cleanly — modern industrial-society life cycles often shorten or skip phases — but the underlying structure is durable.

### Other rite categories

Anthropologists distinguish three main types of ritual.

**Rites of passage** (above) — individual or group transitions of status.

**Rites of intensification** — periodic rituals that affirm and reinforce community bonds. The annual harvest festival; the weekly Friday prayer; the monthly Buddhist *uposatha*; the quarterly Akan Adae. These rituals do not change anyone's status; they renew the community's commitment to its shared frame.

**Rites of affliction** — rituals performed in response to specific problems (illness, drought, conflict). These rituals often involve divination (identifying the cause), healing (specific ritual treatment), and resolution (restoration of order). Many shamanistic practices fall here. The Beng witch-king's curative rites, the Navajo *blessing way* and *enemy way* ceremonies, the Yoruba *ifá* divination rituals. Rites of affliction are typically led by ritual specialists (shamans, diviners, priests) rather than by the afflicted themselves.

## 4. Concept 3 — Myth, secular religion, and contemporary contests

### Myth as cultural infrastructure

A **myth** is a foundational narrative that organizes a culture's understanding of its origin, purpose, and place in the cosmos. Anthropologists do not use *myth* in the colloquial sense of *false story*. Myths are stories that *do work* — they explain, they justify, they bind communities together. Whether they are historically accurate is usually beside the point; whether they are *culturally consequential* is the analytical question.

Most cultures have *creation myths*, *origin myths*, *culture-hero myths*, *trickster myths*. The structures recur. **Earth-diver myths**, in which a creator deity sends an animal to bring up mud from beneath primordial waters to make land, are documented across northern Asia, Native North America, and parts of Southeast Asia. The pattern is so widespread that some scholars have proposed it tracks an ancient circumpolar population dispersal — myth as evidence for migration.

**Claude Lévi-Strauss**, in his four-volume *Mythologiques* (1964–1971) and earlier essays, developed *structural analysis of myth*. Lévi-Strauss argued that myths are organized through *binary oppositions* — pairs of categories the myth holds in tension and resolves through narrative. *Raw / cooked*, *nature / culture*, *life / death*, *kin / stranger*, *good / evil*. The myth is the public arena where these oppositions get processed. *Mythemes* — the minimum repeating units of the myth — can be extracted and compared across versions of the same myth and across different myths.

The classical case: Mary Douglas's analysis of taboos in *Purity and Danger* (1966), which Chapter 3 introduced. The pangolin among the Lele violates the categories of the animal classification system (mammal but scaled, etc.), so it becomes ritually significant — both repellent and sacred. The structural pattern illuminates the cultural logic.

Lévi-Strauss's structuralism has been criticized — it can be applied so universally that it loses its specificity, and some critics have argued that it imposes a Western analytical category on non-Western narratives. The framework is most useful as a tool, not as a totalizing theory.

### Communitas, liminality, and pilgrimage

Victor Turner's elaboration of van Gennep produced one of the discipline's richer concepts: **communitas**.

Communitas is the experience of intense egalitarian solidarity among people sharing a liminal phase. Initiation candidates in an African boys' camp; soldiers in basic training; pilgrims walking the Camino de Santiago together; participants in a Burning Man encampment; passengers stuck on an airplane during a long delay. Communitas dissolves the ordinary hierarchies of class, age, gender, professional status. The participants experience each other as fundamentally equal in a way that ordinary social life rarely permits.

Turner argued that communitas is the *existential opposite* of structure. Structure is the differentiated, role-based, hierarchical order of ordinary social life. Communitas is the undifferentiated, anti-structural experience that arises in liminal phases. The two are complementary: societies need structure to function and communitas to renew. Rituals deliberately produce communitas because the experience is socially regenerative.

**Pilgrimage** is one of the most-studied cases. Catholic pilgrimage to Santiago de Compostela; Hindu pilgrimage to the Kumbh Mela; Muslim hajj to Mecca; Buddhist pilgrimage to Bodh Gaya. Pilgrimage typically involves separation (leaving home), liminality (the journey itself, often involving hardship and the dissolution of ordinary social distinctions), and incorporation (return home transformed). Turner studied Marian pilgrimage in Mexico and Catholic pilgrimage to Lourdes in France. Pilgrimages produce communitas at scale. The hajj brings together approximately two million Muslims annually in conditions designed to maximize the dissolution of social distinction — all pilgrims wear identical white *ihram* garments; all walk the same routes; all perform the same actions. The communitas of the hajj is one of the strongest cohesive forces in global Islam.

### Secular religion

Émile Durkheim's framework opens an unexpected analytical door. If religion is *a unified system of beliefs and practices relative to sacred things, shared by a community*, then a number of contemporary institutions look religious by definition.

**Sport.** A community of fans, ritualized weekly observances, sacred symbols (team colors, jerseys, championship trophies), pilgrimage to championship games, taboos, beliefs about the proper order of things, ecstatic communitas after a championship win. The sociologist Émile Durkheim's framework, applied to contemporary U.S. football culture, would call it religious. The anthropologist of ritual Catherine Bell argued that sport's similarity to religion is structural rather than coincidental — both manage anxieties about competition and chance through stylized performance.

**Brand cults.** Apple, Tesla, Harley-Davidson, Lego — the more committed end of brand affiliation looks religious in Durkheim's frame. Sacred objects (the new iPhone), shared practices (annual product launches), specialized vocabulary, communal identity. The anthropologist Russell Belk documented brand devotion as *secular religion* in the 1980s and 1990s; the analysis has become more central to consumer-culture research since.

**National holidays.** July 4 in the U.S., Bastille Day in France, Republic Day in India. Each involves rites of intensification — periodic public performance of national identity, ritual specialists (politicians, military), sacred symbols (flags, anthems), pilgrimage (Washington D.C. visits during Independence Day; Tiananmen Square during October 1). Benedict Anderson's *Imagined Communities* (Chapter 8) made this argument explicit: nations are imagined into existence partly through their secular-religious rituals.

**Politics as religion.** When a political movement adopts a charismatic leader, sacred texts, ritualized performance of belonging, and intolerance of heretics, it begins to look religious in Durkheim's frame. The 20th-century totalitarianisms produced clear cases. Some recent populist movements have been read in similar terms. The framework is uncomfortable for political analysts who want to keep religion and politics separate, but the analytical tool is real.

The lesson: *religion is not a bounded category that ends at the church door*. The structures the discipline identifies in religion — sacred / profane distinction, ritualized repetition, multivocal symbols, communitas, mythic narrative — appear in many places that are not labeled religious. The analytical apparatus travels.

## 5. Integration — Three contemporary religious cases

Three cases that exercise the chapter's full apparatus.

**A Catholic Easter Vigil in São Paulo.** The mass takes place at midnight on the eve of Easter Sunday. The cathedral is dark. A new fire is lit outside, the *paschal candle* is blessed and processed in. Specific liturgical readings rehearse the salvation history from Genesis through the New Testament. Catechumens (adult converts) are baptized at the front of the church. The community renews its baptismal vows. The Easter proclamation is sung. The mass breaks into ordinary Sunday liturgy, but the frame is now *resurrection*. The whole sequence is a *rite of intensification* (annual renewal of the community) that contains a *rite of passage* (the catechumens' baptism). The cathedral itself is sacred place by Eliade's three criteria — threshold (narthex), axis mundi (altar), imago mundi (the church's east-facing orientation, the cosmological architecture). The communitas of the gathered congregation at the moment of the resurrection proclamation is intense. Brazil's particular Catholic tradition adds local elements — the Portuguese-language liturgy, the Brazilian choral repertoire, the syncretic incorporation of African-derived practices in some communities. The chapter's apparatus reads all of it.

**A Wiccan Beltane circle in Massachusetts.** May 1 evening. A coven of perhaps twelve gathers in a private wood. The high priestess casts the circle as in §1's cold open. A ritual of fertility, growth, and the integration of god and goddess. Specific symbolic actions (the Great Rite, the leaping of the Beltane fire, the gathering of green branches). The ritual is a rite of intensification — an annual renewal of the coven's relationship with the deities and the cycle of seasons. The temporary sacred place exhibits Eliade's three criteria as constructed in real time. The communitas of the gathered coven during the chant of *the goddess goes among her people* is the experience the ritual was designed to produce.

**Aṣẹ at a Yoruba festival in Lagos.** A weeklong celebration honoring Ifá, the deity of divination. Ritual specialists (*babalawo*) chant the *Odu Ifá* corpus — 256 named sacred texts that organize Yoruba cosmology. Initiates undergo rites of passage, including elaborate divination consultations. Daily processions through the city involve specific costumes, drumming, dance. Offerings (often symbolic foods) are placed at sacred shrines. The festival exemplifies all three rite categories simultaneously: rites of passage for new initiates, rites of intensification for the broader community, and rites of affliction for those seeking specific healing or guidance. The contemporary Yoruba tradition exists alongside Christian (35–40% of Nigeria) and Muslim (45–50%) majority practices; Ifá adherents are a minority within Nigeria but maintain a globally distributed diaspora practice. The chapter's apparatus reads the local tradition; the migration apparatus from Chapter 10 reads the diaspora dimension.

The framework holds across the three cases. The local content is vastly different. *Religion is the architecture; specific traditions are the buildings inside.*

## 7. Chapter summary — what you can now do

You can define religion using both Durkheim's social-structural and Geertz's symbolic-experiential definitions, and use both as needed.

You can distinguish *religion*, *spirituality*, and *worldview*; recognize the *nones* trend in contemporary religious affiliation; identify *animism*, *polytheism*, *monotheism*, and *atheism* as distinct positions.

You can identify *symbols* in religious context, distinguish *metaphor* from *metonym*, and recognize *multivocal* symbols.

You can apply Eliade's three characteristics of sacred place (threshold, axis mundi, imago mundi) to any religious site.

You can apply van Gennep's three phases (separation, transition, incorporation) and Turner's *liminality* and *communitas* to specific rituals.

You can distinguish *rites of passage*, *rites of intensification*, and *rites of affliction*.

You can apply Lévi-Strauss's structural analysis to a myth — extract mythemes, identify binary oppositions, ask what work the myth is doing.

You can recognize *secular religion* in non-religious institutions (sport, brand cults, national rituals, political movements) using the chapter's analytical apparatus.

The single idea that matters most: *religion is one of the most analytically rich domains in human life. The structural features — sacred / profane, ritual phases, multivocal symbols, mythic narratives, communitas — are recurring patterns across an enormous diversity of traditions. Once you see the patterns, you see them everywhere, including in places that do not call themselves religious.*

The common mistake to watch for: *treating religion as belief alone, ignoring practice and institution*. Religion is what people believe, but it is also what they do, where they gather, what symbols they share, and how they reproduce their community across time. Belief is one component, not the whole.

If you can take any cultural practice — a religious service, a national holiday, a sporting event, a graduation, a family Thanksgiving — and identify (a) the rite type, (b) the van Gennep phases, (c) the sacred place features, (d) the multivocal symbols, (e) the communitas, (f) the social function, you have absorbed the chapter's apparatus.

## What would change my mind

If a major body of cross-cultural cognitive-science research showed that the structural features the chapter foregrounds (sacred/profane distinction, three-phase rites, multivocal symbols) are artifacts of Western analytical projection rather than features of religious traditions themselves, the chapter's strong claims about cross-cultural recurring patterns would need revision. The current evidence supports the recurring-pattern reading; the chapter holds the position cautiously rather than absolutely.

## Still puzzling

The boundary between religion and not-religion in the contemporary period. The chapter argued that secular religion (sport, brand cults, national rituals, political movements) shares structural features with religion. Whether this means the categories are continuous (religion is a wider phenomenon than the institutional traditions usually labeled religious) or discontinuous (the resemblance is structural but the underlying experience is different) is contested. I do not have a confident view.
