## TL;DR

Language is what makes human culture possible. This chapter walks through how language evolved (FOXP2, mirror neurons, the hyoid bone), how it shapes thought (Sapir-Whorf, color terms, metaphor), and how it does power — through speech communities, code-switching, performative utterances, and language ideologies that decide whose talk counts as right.

## 1. A microphone, a proverb, an embarrassed speaker

A family compound in Ghana. The occasion is an *outdooring* — a public ceremony to formally introduce a newborn child to extended family and community. The afternoon has settled into the comfortable rhythm of the gathering when a middle-aged man stands up, takes the microphone, and prepares to pour libation — the ritual offering of drink to the ancestors that opens nearly every formal Akan event.

He surveys the audience. Then, mid-motion, he stops. Embarrassment crosses his face. He looks down at his feet and says:

*"When the tongue is present, the teeth do not make noise."*

The audience laughs. The speaker sets down the cup, steps aside. An older man — a brother he had assumed was not present — slowly rises from a table at the edge of the gathering and walks to the microphone. By the rules of seniority in this culture, the elder brother should pour the libation. The younger man had been about to commit a public breach. The proverb saved him.

The anthropologist Jennifer Hasty, who was conducting fieldwork at this gathering, had heard the proverb before. She had not heard it used quite this way. It is a proverb about voice — about who gets to speak and when. The tongue is mobile, central, productive of speech. The teeth are fixed, supportive; alone, they only clash meaninglessly. The younger man, by speaking the proverb, was casting himself as the teeth and his elder brother as the tongue. The proverb does the social work the speaker could not do directly. It both apologizes for the near-mistake and properly restores the order of seniority — all in twelve words.

This is what language does. It is not just a vehicle for information. It is a tool for managing relationships, asserting and yielding status, creating events (a libation, a marriage, a name), and — because of this — a primary site of power. *Linguistic anthropology* studies language as one of the things culture is made of, alongside food and kinship and ritual. Language is the air culture breathes.

This chapter has three jobs. First, walk back through the evolutionary and biological ground that made language possible — the throat, the brain, the genes. Second, ask what language *does to thought* — the long-running argument over linguistic relativity (the *Sapir-Whorf hypothesis*) and linguistic universals, which turn out to be both real. Third, look at language as social action — speech communities, code-switching, performative utterances, gender and race in language, and the *language ideologies* that organize all of this.

## 2. Concept 1 — What language is, and how it emerged

### Language is not all communication

All animals communicate. Honeybees do the famous waggle dance to indicate the direction and distance of nectar sources. Octopuses change skin color and texture to signal hunting, courtship, and threat. Songbirds compose dawn-chorus territorial songs. Vervet monkeys produce different alarm calls for leopards, eagles, and snakes — and other vervets respond appropriately to each.

This is communication. Most of it is not language.

**Language**, as anthropologists and linguists use the term, is a specific kind of communication: vocal or gestural units (words, signs) that can be combined and recombined according to grammatical rules to convey an *infinite* range of meanings. The word *infinite* is doing real work. The waggle dance is rule-governed and remarkable, but a bee cannot use it to discuss the weather or comment on the laziness of the queen. Vervet alarm calls cannot describe a leopard that *might come tomorrow*. Human language, in contrast, can produce sentences that have never been uttered before, that describe events that have not yet happened, that refer to entities that do not exist (unicorns, justice, last Thursday). The technical term is *productivity* or *generativity*. It is the property that distinguishes language from every other communication system on the planet.

Studies that have taught great apes to use sign language (Koko the gorilla, Washoe the chimpanzee, Kanzi the bonobo) show that great apes can master a vocabulary of perhaps a few hundred signs and combine them in simple ways. Whether this is *protolanguage* — a precursor — or simply highly trained behavior is contested. What is not contested: in the wild, no nonhuman primate produces anything that meets the productivity test for full language.

### The biological prerequisites

Several biological features had to be in place before *Homo sapiens* could speak.

**The vocal tract.** Modern humans have an unusual descended larynx (voice box) and a large rounded tongue, which together produce a wide range of vowels and consonants. The descended larynx is also why humans can choke on food: our throat is configured for sound production, with a side cost of food going down the wrong tube. The hyoid bone — a small U-shaped bone in the throat that anchors tongue muscles — is informative about when this configuration evolved. The few preserved hyoids in the fossil record (including Neanderthal specimens) suggest that the modern vocal tract was in place by roughly 500,000 years ago. This implies Neanderthals had the vocal apparatus for speech — though whether they used it for full language is, as Chapter 5 noted, contested.

**Brain regions.** Two named regions matter most. **Broca's area** in the left frontal lobe is associated with the production of speech. **Wernicke's area** in the left temporal lobe is associated with comprehension. Damage to Broca's area produces *expressive aphasia* — the patient can understand language but cannot fluently produce it. Damage to Wernicke's area produces *receptive aphasia* — fluent but meaningless speech, with comprehension impaired. The functional split between the two regions was discovered in the 19th century by Paul Broca and Carl Wernicke from clinical cases of patients with localized brain damage, and the discovery is one of neuroscience's foundational findings.

**Mirror neurons.** A class of neurons that fire both when an animal performs an action and when it observes another animal performing the same action. First discovered in macaque monkeys in the 1990s, mirror neurons may be the substrate for *imitation* — the foundation of cultural transmission. The neuroscientist Michael Arbib has argued that mirror neurons enabled hominins to pass tool-making skills across generations through observation, and that the same neural machinery later supported gestural communication and ultimately spoken language.

**FOXP2.** A gene on chromosome 7 sometimes called *the language gene*, though that name oversells it. FOXP2 was identified in the 1990s when researchers traced a familial speech disorder (the *KE family* in West London) to a mutation in this single gene. Members of the family with the mutation showed difficulties with articulation, grammar, and language comprehension. FOXP2 turned out to be present in many vertebrates — mice, bats, fish, songbirds — but the human version differs from the chimpanzee version by two amino acid substitutions. Some researchers suggest these mutations occurred around 260,000 years ago and contributed to the emergence of full language. The cautious version of the claim: *FOXP2 is one of many genetic changes contributing to language; calling it "the language gene" is misleading*.

### The tool-language connection

Chapter 5 walked through the tool industries — Oldowan (2.5 million years ago), Acheulean (1.6 million years ago), Mousterian (300,000 years ago) — as cognitive evidence. Most contemporary linguistic anthropologists think tools and language co-evolved.

Two reasons. First, tool-making requires *sequential planning* — selecting the right core, executing a series of strikes in a specific order, producing the intended shape. The same cognitive machinery may underlie the sequential planning required to produce sentences. Second, *transmission of tool-making skill* across generations would benefit enormously from gestural or vocal communication; learners can imitate, but precise instruction (*hold it this way; strike here, not there*) requires something closer to language. Arbib's model: simple imitation around 2.5 million years ago supported Oldowan tool-making; more elaborate imitation around 1.6 million years ago supported Acheulean hand axes and the emergence of *protolanguage*; the explosion of cognitive innovations in the past 100,000 years coincided with the emergence of full language.

The dating is uncertain. Estimates for the emergence of full human language range from 50,000 to 200,000 years ago. The window is wide because the evidence is indirect — language leaves no fossils.

## 3. Concept 2 — What language does to thought: relativity, universals, and metaphor

### The Sapir-Whorf hypothesis, two versions

In the early 20th century, the German-American anthropologist Franz Boas (you have met him three times now) trained a student named Edward Sapir, who in turn trained a student named Benjamin Whorf. Sapir and Whorf, working in the 1920s and 1930s, developed what came to be called the **Sapir-Whorf hypothesis**: the proposition that the language a person speaks shapes how they think.

The hypothesis comes in two strengths.

**Strong Sapir-Whorf** (linguistic determinism): the language you speak determines what you can think. If a language lacks a word for a concept, speakers of that language cannot think the concept.

**Weak Sapir-Whorf** (linguistic relativity): the language you speak influences what comes easily to you, what you notice, what habits of attention you develop — but does not strictly limit your thought.

The strong version does not survive. Whorf's claim about Hopi time perception — that Hopi has no grammatical tense and that Hopi speakers therefore experience time as a continuous flow rather than as countable units — was overstated; the linguist Ekkehart Malotki documented in the 1980s that Hopi has elaborate ways of marking time, including spatial metaphors very similar to those of English. The famous claim that *Eskimos have 400 words for snow* is also wrong on the facts (the anthropologist Laura Martin documented in 1986 that Inuit and Aleutian languages have roughly two root words for snow, used much like English uses *snow*). And speakers of any language can readily learn new concepts they previously lacked words for.

The weak version, however, has substantial support. Steven Levinson's research on the Australian Indigenous language *Guugu Yimithirr* found that speakers of Guugu Yimithirr describe the location of objects using cardinal directions (*north of your foot*, *south of the cup*) rather than relative directions (*to your left*, *behind you*). Speakers of such languages develop a far better intuitive sense of cardinal direction than speakers of relative-direction languages — they navigate more accurately, gesture in cardinal directions even in conversation, and orient themselves continuously. The language has trained an attentional habit. Different language, different habit.

The work on color terms is similar. Brent Berlin and Paul Kay's 1969 cross-cultural study of color vocabulary in 98 languages found that color terms across languages follow a regular pattern: a language with two color terms always has *black/dark* and *white/light*; a language with three adds *red*; languages add green and yellow next, then blue, then brown, then purple, pink, orange, and gray. The pattern reflects what the human visual system most easily distinguishes, regardless of culture — a *linguistic universal*. *And* — Kay's later work with Willet Kempton showed — speakers of languages that distinguish blue from green draw a sharper perceptual boundary between the two colors than speakers of languages that have only one term for the *blue/green* range. *Both* universalism and relativity are operating, on the same data.

The contemporary view: linguistic relativity and linguistic universals are both real, in different domains and at different scales. Strong determinism does not survive; mild influence does.

### Metaphor as a tool of thought

The cognitive linguists George Lakoff and Mark Johnson argued in *Metaphors We Live By* (1980) that metaphor is not just a literary device. It is the primary mechanism by which abstract concepts are built up out of concrete bodily experience.

Consider mood. *Up* and *down* are concrete spatial concepts derived from bodily experience — gravity, posture, falling, rising. We use these spatial concepts to think about mood: *up* (happy, on top of the world) versus *down* (sad, in the dumps, depressed — a word that literally means *pressed down*). The metaphor *MOOD IS VERTICAL ORIENTATION* is so deeply built into English that we do not notice it as metaphor. We feel that *down* is what sadness *actually is*.

Lakoff and Johnson cataloged dozens of these structural metaphors that organize abstract domains:

- LIFE IS A JOURNEY (*the right path*, *moving forward*, *crossroads*, *arriving at*).
- LOVE IS A JOURNEY (*we've come a long way*, *our relationship is at a dead end*, *we're going separate ways*).
- LOVE IS SWEET (*sweetheart*, *honeymoon*, *sugar*).
- ARGUMENT IS COMBAT (*indefensible*, *attack*, *armed with facts*, *defeat*, *retreat*).
- TIME IS MONEY (*spend time*, *waste time*, *invest time*, *cost me an hour*).

The TIME IS MONEY metaphor is particularly revealing about cultural specificity. English-speakers in industrialized capitalist societies use it constantly. It is not present in all languages, and where it is present, it is partly a marker of the spread of capitalist labor relations. *Time is treated as money in this society, so time is talked about as money.* Whorf, looking at Hopi, did not find an equivalent metaphor.

Some metaphors track human biology and are nearly universal (UP IS GOOD, KNOWING IS SEEING, ARGUMENT IS COMBAT, IDEAS ARE OBJECTS). Others are culturally specific. Esther Afreh's 2018 study of speeches by the Asantehene (the king of Asante in Ghana) found that the king routinely uses metaphors familiar in English (*life is a journey*, *life is a battle*, *ideas are food*, *knowing is seeing*, *death is sleep*) — and that these same metaphors exist in Akan proverbs. Some metaphors travel; others stay home.

## 4. Concept 3 — Language as social action: communities, code-switching, and power

### Speech communities and code-switching

A 10-year-old uses the word *derpy* to describe her stuffed animal. Her mother does not know the word. The mother asks for an explanation. The daughter cannot give one — *Mom, I just can't explain it to you. You just have to know.*

Every language is spoken by a hypothetical *language community* (everyone who speaks English, say). Inside that community are smaller **speech communities** — groups whose members share particular vocabulary, grammatical forms, intonation patterns, and understandings about how language is used in different situations. The 10-year-old's *derpy* belongs to a speech community her mother is not part of.

Speech communities can be regional (a *dialect* — Philadelphia's *woohder* for *water*, Pittsburgh's *yinz* for *you all*), social (a *vernacular* — African American Vernacular English, Chicano English, Native American English), professional (the dense legalese of first-year law students, the acronym-laden talk of military service members), or generational (the slang vocabulary that 50-year-olds cannot keep up with).

Most people belong to multiple speech communities. The practice of moving between them — speaking AAVE at home, *Standard English* in a job interview; speaking a regional dialect in conversation with old friends and a more standard variety with new colleagues — is called **code-switching**. Code-switching is not confused or sloppy speech. It is sophisticated linguistic adaptation: matching the speech style to the audience and the social context.

The linguistic anthropologist Elizabeth Mertz, in her 2007 book *The Language of Law School*, traced how first-year law students learn to *think like a lawyer* — which turns out to be substantially a matter of learning a particular speech community's register. Through Socratic-method classroom interactions, students are trained to set aside the moral and emotional content of legal cases and to treat them as texts to be analyzed for legal-doctrinal moves. The vocabulary, the cadence, the distancing from emotion: all are linguistic markers of professional membership. By the end of the first year, students who have not learned to talk this way are filtered out. The result, Mertz argues, is an American legal profession dominated by people trained to set aside humanistic concerns in favor of textual authority — a consequence of how the speech community is gatekept.

### Vernaculars and stigma

A *vernacular* is a non-regional dialect associated with a specific social group — typically defined by ethnicity, age, gender, or class. African American Vernacular English (AAVE) is the most-studied vernacular in U.S. linguistics. AAVE is a fully grammatical system with regular rules — it is not *broken English* or *bad grammar*. Sentences like *He be working* and *She runnin'* are grammatically systematic; the *be* in *He be working* is the *habitual be*, marking an ongoing or repeated action (*he works regularly*, distinct from *He working* which means *he is working right now*).

The linguist William Labov documented this systematic structure in the 1960s. Labov's work, and the work of many AAVE researchers since, has established beyond serious doubt that AAVE is a valid linguistic system. *And yet* — AAVE remains stigmatized in formal U.S. settings (classrooms, courtrooms, job interviews). Speakers are routinely judged as less intelligent, less competent, less serious for using forms that are, linguistically, just as rule-governed as Standard English. The stigma is a social fact about the dominant culture's *language ideology*, not a linguistic fact about AAVE.

### Performative utterances — when saying *is* doing

Some sentences describe the world (*The cat is on the mat*). Others *do something* (*I now pronounce you husband and wife*). The British philosopher J. L. Austin, in *How to Do Things with Words* (1962), called the second kind **performative utterances**. Performatives include:

- *I now pronounce you husband and wife.*
- *I sentence you to ten years.*
- *You're fired.*
- *I name this ship the Queen Elizabeth.*
- *I apologize.*
- *I promise I'll be there.*

Each of these is not a description but an *action*. Austin's deeper insight was that *most speech is performative in some sense* — even ordinary statements aim to do something (warn, persuade, request, inform). Pure description is the exception, not the rule.

Ritual language is the densest case. The Polish-British anthropologist Bronisław Malinowski (you met him in Chapter 1 with the kula ring) argued in the 1930s that the Trobriand peoples used magical language to compel agricultural growth — yams, taro, palms. The spells were not just expressions of hope; for the Trobrianders, the speech *did something to the world*. A linguistic anthropologist working in the same tradition today might study a healing chant, a divorce decree, a presidential oath of office — all places where speech is the action.

A particularly rich case: *naming ceremonies*. In many West African societies, a child is not considered a full person until they have been publicly named in a formal ceremony. The naming itself, performed by an elder or religious official, is what makes the child a member of the community. Linguistic anthropologist Nicholas Sweet, working in southeastern Senegal, documented a Pular naming ceremony where the imam recited a formal Arabic-language blessing and announced the child's name *Aissatou*. The naming was performative. The child became Aissatou by being named Aissatou.

Sweet also recorded what happened next. Auntie Aissatou, the child's namesake great-aunt, had been delayed and missed the ceremony. Arriving afterward, she wrapped a scarf around her head in imitation of the imam, processed into the compound, and performed her own parodic naming: *The name of the child has come here. It is Buubu Nooge — Trash Owl.* The audience laughed and protested. Auntie Aissatou's parody was *also* performative — but at a different register. She was using the form of formal language to express her displeasure at being excluded. Performative speech can be serious or ironic, official or rebellious. *Whose performative speech counts?* is always a political question.

### Language ideologies and language as power

Every culture has theories about how language works — who speaks correctly, what counts as proper, what kinds of speech are valued, what kinds are stigmatized. These theories are **language ideologies**. They are mostly invisible to the people who hold them; they feel like common sense.

Examples of language ideologies in contemporary U.S. culture:

- *Standard English is correct; AAVE is incorrect.* (False as a linguistic claim; powerful as a social fact.)
- *Men and women speak differently.* (Mostly false; women and men speak more similarly than most ideologies suggest. Janet Hyde's 2005 meta-analysis of hundreds of studies found small or zero effects on most linguistic variables; most claimed differences are cultural performances rather than biological facts.)
- *Children have a "window" for learning language; missing it leaves them permanently impaired.* (Partly true; the developmental window is real but more flexible than the popular version claims.)
- *People who use slang are intellectually lazy.* (False; slang is generative and rule-governed, often more cognitively demanding than the standard variety.)
- *English is becoming more sloppy; kids today don't know how to write.* (Every generation since at least the 18th century has said this; the evidence does not support it.)

Language ideologies do real work. The ideology that AAVE is incorrect produces classroom and courtroom outcomes for AAVE speakers. The ideology that women's speech is overly emotional produces hiring decisions. The ideology that a particular national variety is the *real* language of a country (Castilian Spanish in Spain, Mandarin in China, Standard French in France) produces educational and political policy that suppresses regional variants.

The term *mansplaining* — which entered Merriam-Webster in 2018 — is itself a piece of language ideology, naming a pattern of male-condescending-explanation. The pattern is real (Rebecca Solnit's 2008 essay *Men Explain Things to Me* documented it widely); whether *all* men *always* speak this way is the kind of generalization the language ideology smooths over. The term is performative — naming the pattern lets people call it out.

### Race and language

Marcyliena Morgan's 2009 ethnography *The Real Hiphop* (mentioned in Chapter 1) traced how Black Los Angeles emcees craft linguistic codes that index gentrification, gang violence, and racialized policing. The point is not just that Black speech communities have their own vernaculars (which they do). It is that *racial categories themselves are produced and contested through linguistic practice*. To speak in certain ways is to be marked. Marking can be claimed (in-group solidarity) or imposed (out-group stigma).

Anthropologist Summerson Carr's 2011 *Scripting Addiction* (also mentioned in Chapter 1) studied an addiction-treatment program for homeless women and showed how counselors trained their clients to perform a specific kind of *healthy talk* — particular ways of saying *I am an addict, I am in recovery, I take responsibility* — that demonstrated progress to the institution. Whether the women *believed* what they were saying mattered less than whether they could *say it correctly*. The institution evaluated through linguistic performance.

These are not exotic cases. They are the rule. *Whoever controls the official language of an institution controls who counts as a competent member of it.*

## 5. Integration — Reading language for what it does

The three concepts of this chapter are not separate. They overlap.

The biology that made language possible (Concept 1) is not a separate fact from what language does to thought (Concept 2). Mirror neurons may be the substrate for both imitation and metaphor. The descended larynx made vocal performatives possible. Brain regions specialized for speech production may have co-evolved with the cognitive habits that produced metaphor.

The cognitive effects of language (Concept 2) cannot be separated from its social effects (Concept 3). The ARGUMENT IS COMBAT metaphor is not just a way English speakers organize abstract thought — it is also what makes American political discourse so often hostile, what shapes courtroom adversarial procedure, what models a particular relationship between disagreement and violence. *How language makes us think* and *how language makes us behave* are the same thing under two descriptions.

And the social effects of language (Concept 3) feed back into the biology. Languages that go unspoken die. The ones that survive reshape the brains of their speakers across generations. Native bilinguals show different patterns of cortical activation than monolinguals. The cognitive reserve of speaking multiple languages may delay the onset of dementia by several years (the evidence is mixed but suggestive). Language is a biological substrate that shapes the biology that produces it.

### A worked integration — language revitalization

Hundreds of languages are dying. UNESCO estimates that of roughly 7,000 languages spoken today, half will be effectively extinct within a century. Most of these are Indigenous languages displaced by colonial languages — English, Spanish, Mandarin, French, Russian, Portuguese, Arabic.

**Language revitalization** is the deliberate effort to keep a language alive or bring it back from dormancy. Welsh, Hebrew, Hawaiian, and several Indigenous North American languages (Cherokee, Navajo, Mohawk, Chinuk Wawa) have notable revitalization programs. The methods vary: *immersion schools* where young children learn the language as their first or second language; *master-apprentice* programs pairing fluent elders with motivated learners; recordings and transcriptions of the last fluent speakers; integration into local media and signage.

Revitalization brings together every theme of this chapter. *Biological*: children's developmental window must be exploited for native fluency. *Cognitive*: the categories the language uses are part of the worldview being preserved. *Social*: a community that speaks its language has a foothold in claims to land, sovereignty, and cultural identity that a community that has lost its language has more difficulty making.

Henry Zenk's work with the Confederated Tribes of Grand Ronde on Chinuk Wawa (mentioned in Chapter 2) is one example of a successful revitalization program. Zenk has worked with the tribe for 50 years; the language now has fluent young speakers in the community for the first time in two generations.

The point: *language is not a coat you wear over thought*. It is part of the thought, part of the community, part of the body, part of the future the community can imagine. Losing a language is losing a way of being in the world.

## 7. Chapter summary — what you can now do

You can define language by the productivity test, distinguishing it from other communication systems.

You can name the biological prerequisites of language and resist the seduction of single-cause stories — *FOXP2 is one of many genetic changes; mirror neurons are necessary but not sufficient; the descended larynx is a precondition for sound, not for thought*.

You can hold linguistic relativity and linguistic universals as both real, in different domains, and you can reject the strong determinism version of Sapir-Whorf without rejecting the weaker influence version.

You can identify metaphors in everyday speech and trace them to their source domains. *MOOD IS UP/DOWN, ARGUMENT IS COMBAT, TIME IS MONEY* are not literary devices; they are the cognitive scaffolding of abstract thought.

You can name speech communities, dialects, vernaculars, and code-switching, and recognize them in your own life.

You can identify performative utterances and the felicity conditions they require to do their work.

You can recognize language ideologies — the invisible assumptions about how language works that organize who speaks correctly, who is right, and whose talk counts.

The single idea that matters most: *language is not a passive tool we pick up to convey thoughts. It is part of the cognitive apparatus, part of the social structure, and part of the contest for power. Most of what we mean by culture is happening through language.*

The common mistake to watch for: *treating one's own language ideologies as common sense*. Every speaker holds them. The work is to notice them, not to pretend you don't have any.

If you can listen to a five-minute argument and identify (a) which speech community each speaker belongs to, (b) which performatives they are using, (c) which metaphors structure their thinking, and (d) which language ideology each is relying on, you have absorbed the chapter's analytical apparatus.

## What would change my mind

If a coordinated body of new evidence — better methods for testing strong-version Sapir-Whorf claims, ancient DNA showing language-relevant genes far older than current dating suggests, primate field studies showing wild non-trained primate communication that meets the productivity test — overturned the current consensus on any of the chapter's three main claims, the chapter would need substantial revision. The chapter holds the cautious version of each claim because the cautious version is what the evidence supports.

## Still puzzling

The relationship between *FOXP2* and the other genetic changes that contributed to human language. FOXP2 was the first to be identified with confidence, and it has dominated popular accounts. The technical literature now treats FOXP2 as one node in a polygenic story, but the popular discourse has not caught up. The contemporary picture is messier and more honest than the *language gene* shorthand suggests. I do not have a clean account of which other genes mattered most, and the answers are still coming in.
