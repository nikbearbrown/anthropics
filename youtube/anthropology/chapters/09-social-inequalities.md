## 1. Three rooms in three cities

A delivery rider in Lagos. He is 23, has a smartphone, has a motorbike on credit, and has a fare in his app. The customer is 12 minutes away. The traffic is terrible. The sun is hot. The bike's brakes squeak. If he is late, he loses a star. If his rating drops below 4.7, he loses access to the app. If he loses access to the app, he cannot pay the bike loan. He pulls into traffic.

A bank associate in São Paulo. She is 28, has a master's degree from a public university, works 70 hours a week at an investment bank, and is the only Black woman in her cohort of 30 analysts. The senior partner has just made a comment in a meeting that everyone laughs at except her. She does not laugh. She looks down at her notes. She does not say what she would say if she were the one being talked about, because she has learned that the cost of saying it is too high. She does say it, later, to her friend on the phone. Her friend tells her something she already knows: *don't make yourself a target*. It is good advice. It is also a small daily defeat.

A nurse in a rural Indian district hospital. She is 45, has a daughter in nursing school, and is the only one in her family to have completed college. Her caste — a Dalit caste, the descendants of the people once called *untouchables* — was officially abolished as a legal category 75 years ago. Her colleagues are mostly from higher-caste backgrounds. They speak to her courteously. Her name is on the duty roster. She is not invited to the doctors' tea breaks. She has not been invited to the doctors' tea breaks in 15 years. The exclusion is not violent. It is the kind of soft, durable exclusion that does not require any active malice to keep working.

Three rooms in three cities. Three people whose access to power, dignity, and material resources is shaped by axes — class, race, gender, caste — that the people in the rooms did not choose. Each of these inequalities operates partly through interpersonal interactions, partly through institutional rules, partly through structural patterns built up over centuries, and partly through what people in the room *do not say* — the hegemonic common-sense that lets the room work the way it does without anyone having to enforce it.

This chapter is about how anthropology analyzes these inequalities. It does so by working at four levels (interpersonal, institutional, structural, systemic), with five theoretical traditions (Marxist, Boasian, Du Boisian, Foucauldian, intersectional), across four major axes (race, class, gender, intersecting combinations of these). The end goal is not just an inventory. The end goal is a working analytical tool: when you encounter an inequality in your own life — at school, at work, in your community — the chapter's apparatus lets you ask better questions about it than *who is to blame* or *whose fault is this*.

## 2. Concept 1 — Levels of inequality and the theoretical history

### Inequality vs. inequity

**Inequality** is the unequal distribution of resources — money, education, healthcare, political voice. **Inequity** is unequal distribution that is *unjust* because it results from systematic exclusion or oppression. Some inequality is functional (a brain surgeon should make more than a barista); some inequality is the residue of historical injustice (descendants of enslaved people own a fraction of the household wealth of descendants of enslavers — that is inequity, not inequality).

The two concepts are easy to slide between. The slide is politically useful for those defending the status quo (calling all inequality *natural*) and for those attacking it (calling all inequality *unjust*). Anthropologists try to be precise: *which* inequalities trace to systematic oppression, and *which* don't.

### Four levels

A useful framework for thinking about how inequality operates: it works at four levels, each sustaining the others.

**Interpersonal inequality** is power imbalance rooted in personal interaction. The senior partner who interrupts the junior associate. The teacher who calls on boys more often than girls. The shopkeeper who follows certain customers more closely than others. Interpersonal inequality is visible in the moment.

**Institutional inequality** is encoded in the policies and practices of organizations — schools, employers, governments. The school district that funds white-majority schools at higher rates than majority-Black schools. The mortgage policy that uses ZIP code as a proxy for race. The hiring algorithm that screens out résumés with names common in stigmatized groups. Institutional inequality is mostly invisible to those it does not harm.

**Structural inequality** is the accumulated effect of institutional decisions across society and history. The U.S. racial wealth gap — descendants of enslaved people own a fraction of the wealth of descendants of slaveowners — is structural; it traces back through 400 years of slavery, Jim Crow, redlining, GI Bill exclusions, and ongoing housing and labor discrimination. Structural inequality is a property of the whole system, not any specific institution. It is also self-reinforcing: each generation's structural disadvantage becomes the next generation's interpersonal and institutional disadvantage.

**Systemic inequality** is the confluence of all three. The "isms" — racism, sexism, classism, ableism, homophobia, ageism — operate at the systemic level when they have become so embedded in interpersonal habits, institutional rules, and structural patterns that they reproduce themselves without anyone visibly enforcing them.

A useful linguistic distinction here: *systematic* (of a system, methodical) and *systemic* (pervading a whole). *Systematic oppression* is intentional and organized — Jim Crow laws were systematic. *Systemic oppression* is normalized and reproduced without any explicit policy — contemporary U.S. housing inequality is systemic. The two often coexist; what was once systematic becomes systemic when the explicit policy is removed but the pattern persists.

### A short tour of theoretical traditions

Five frameworks have shaped how anthropology analyzes inequality.

**Social Darwinism / Unilineal Cultural Evolution.** Discredited but historically important. The English social theorist Herbert Spencer (in *Principles of Biology*, 1864–67) repurposed Darwin's biological evolution into a justification for social hierarchy: those who succeed deserve to succeed; those who fail deserve to fail; competition naturally produces the best outcomes. The American anthropologist Lewis Henry Morgan and the British anthropologist Edward Tylor's unilineal-cultural-evolution scheme — *savagery → barbarism → civilization* — provided the matching anthropological framework: European industrial societies were the most advanced; non-Western societies were earlier-stage versions. Both frameworks served as ideological cover for colonialism and racial hierarchy. Both were dismantled by Franz Boas (Chapter 1) and the discipline that grew up around him. *Social Darwinism is not science; it is politics dressed as science.* Knowing the framework matters because it has not gone away — it shows up in contemporary arguments about meritocracy, intelligence testing, and welfare policy.

**Conflict theory (Marx).** The German political philosopher Karl Marx, writing in the mid-19th century, argued that hierarchy is the central engine of social conflict, not its solution. Marx focused on capitalism and identified two main classes: the **bourgeoisie** (those who own the means of production — factories, land, capital) and the **proletariat** (those who sell their labor for wages). The conflict between owners and workers, Marx argued, is the underlying dynamic of capitalist societies. Most subsequent class analysis builds on or argues with Marx.

**Du Boisian race-and-class analysis.** The American sociologist W. E. B. Du Bois, working in the early 20th century, brought race into the conflict-theory frame. Du Bois argued that the labor of Black Americans was systematically devalued — that race was a primary mechanism through which class inequality operated, not an addition to it. Du Bois's *The Philadelphia Negro* (1899) is one of the earliest empirical ethnographies of Black American life. His later work (*Black Reconstruction in America*, 1935) argued that the failure of post-Civil-War Reconstruction in the U.S. South was a primary missed opportunity to build interracial working-class solidarity, and that the racial divisions that emerged afterward kept both Black and white workers from realizing their economic interests. Du Bois's influence on Franz Boas (Chapter 1) and the subsequent dismantling of biological-race theory in anthropology was substantial.

**Functionalism.** The French sociologist Émile Durkheim and the British anthropologists Bronisław Malinowski and Alfred Radcliffe-Brown (Chapter 3) argued that social institutions — including hierarchies — function to maintain social order. From a functionalist view, inequality serves a purpose: it motivates effort, it organizes division of labor, it produces leadership. Functionalism has been criticized — it treats existing arrangements as natural, and it ignores the colonial and historical contexts that produced specific inequalities. But the functionalist insight that institutions persist because they do *something* for *somebody* is durable; the question is just *who*.

**Critical Race Theory (CRT).** Developed by U.S. legal scholars in the 1980s (Derrick Bell, Kimberlé Crenshaw, Patricia Williams, Richard Delgado), CRT begins from the observation that racism is *endemic* — built into the laws, policies, and institutions of the United States — rather than being a personal failing of individual racists. CRT challenges *color blindness* (the claim that ignoring race makes racism go away) and *meritocracy* (the claim that everyone has equal opportunity to succeed) as ideological covers that hide structural racism. CRT has become politically contested in the past decade. The contestation is itself instructive — it tracks closely with how the framework cuts at the foundations of comfortable national narratives.

## 3. Concept 2 — Power, agency, and the major axes

### Power, hegemony, and the state apparatus

Chapter 8 introduced power, authority, and biopower. This chapter extends the toolkit.

**Hegemony** (Antonio Gramsci, writing from a fascist Italian prison in the 1930s) is power that operates through *consent* rather than coercion. A regime has hegemony when most of its subjects accept its legitimacy as common sense. The U.S. belief that *anyone can succeed if they work hard enough* — the meritocracy myth — is hegemonic; it explains existing inequality without requiring the inequality to change. Hegemony is the most efficient form of power because it does not need to be enforced — it is reproduced by the people it constrains.

**Ideological state apparatus** (Louis Althusser, French Marxist philosopher, 1970s). Althusser distinguished two clusters of state institutions. *Repressive state apparatus* — police, military, courts, prisons — works by force. *Ideological state apparatus* — schools, religious institutions, mass media, family, sports, arts — works by belief. The ideological apparatus reproduces the conditions that allow the repressive apparatus to be needed only occasionally. A society with strong ideological state apparatus rarely needs to use the repressive apparatus; a society with weak ideological apparatus has to use the repressive apparatus constantly.

**Biopolitics** and **necropolitics**. Chapter 8 introduced Foucault's *biopower* — the state's capacity to make live or let die through population-level regulation. The Cameroonian philosopher Achille Mbembe extended Foucault's framework with **necropolitics** (Mbembe, 2003): the *power of death*, the state's capacity to decide *which lives matter* and *which deaths are acceptable*. Necropolitics operates explicitly in some contexts — the death penalty, military operations, immigration enforcement that treats border deaths as collateral. It operates implicitly in others — the unequal allocation of healthcare, the differential police violence experienced by different populations, the "afterlife of slavery" (Saidiya Hartman's term) by which Black Americans face skewed life chances, premature death, and incarceration centuries after slavery's formal end.

**Habitus** (Pierre Bourdieu, French sociologist). Habitus is the set of *embodied dispositions* — habits of speech, posture, taste, response — that people acquire through their position in society and that they then carry without reflection. A working-class child grows up with one habitus; a middle-class child grows up with another. The differences are in everything: how to hold a fork at a formal dinner, how to address a stranger, what books to read, what humor is funny, what counts as appropriate ambition. *Habitus is how class reproduces itself across generations without anyone having to teach it explicitly.* The middle-class child arrives at the prestigious university already fluent in the conventions of prestige; the first-generation college student has to learn the conventions, and the learning is visible. Bourdieu's framework explains how class persists even when explicit class barriers are removed.

### Agency

The concept of **agency** — the capacity to act and make decisions — runs through this chapter. The British sociologist Anthony Giddens, in his theory of *structuration* (1984), argued that agency and structure are mutually constitutive: people act inside structures, and their actions either reproduce or modify those structures. Bourdieu made a parallel argument with habitus.

Agency is not the same as *free will*. Agency is *constrained capacity*. A delivery rider in Lagos has agency — he chooses which fares to take, which routes to ride. He has less agency than a senior bank executive, who chooses what business strategy to pursue, what risks to take, what people to hire. Both have agency; one has more. Agency is unequally distributed in roughly the same patterns as resources, voice, and dignity.

### Race and racism

**Race**, as Chapter 1 established, is biologically not a clean category — most genetic variation is *within* groups, not between them, and any way of drawing racial lines is statistically arbitrary. But race is *socially* very real. It is one of the most consequential axes of inequality in the contemporary world.

**Racism** is power intertwined with racial prejudice. Racial prejudice without power (a racially-biased thought in someone with no institutional or structural means to act on it) does damage but limited damage. Racism — racial prejudice combined with institutional, structural, or systemic power — does enormous damage. Anthropologist Alan Goodman's working definition: *racism is the use of race to establish and justify a social hierarchy and system of power that privileges and advances certain individuals or groups, usually at the expense of others*.

The racial categories in widespread use today owe much to the late-1700s work of the German naturalist Johann Friedrich Blumenbach, whose five-fold scheme — *Caucasian*, *Mongolian*, *Malayan*, *Ethiopian*, *American* — explicitly placed *Caucasian* (white Europeans) at the top of a hierarchy. The categories have been challenged repeatedly. *Caucasian* persists in scientific and popular usage as a euphemism for *white*, carrying with it (though most users do not know this) the residue of Blumenbach's racial hierarchy.

**Whiteness studies.** A body of scholarship — Nell Painter's *The History of White People* (2010), Karen Brodkin's *How Jews Became White Folks* (1998), Matthew Frye Jacobson's *Whiteness of a Different Color* (1998) — has shown that the racial category *white* is itself socially constructed and historically contingent. Irish, Italian, Jewish, and Slavic immigrants to the early-20th-century U.S. were not initially considered *white* and only became so over decades. The construction of whiteness was tied to access to political and economic power; whiteness expanded to include groups whose inclusion served existing power structures.

**White privilege** — the ways that white people in racially unequal societies receive advantages and avoid obstacles that nonwhite people face — was named in Peggy McIntosh's 1989 essay *White Privilege: Unpacking the Invisible Knapsack*. McIntosh's list of 50+ invisible advantages — including not having to think about one's race most of the time, knowing one will be widely represented in popular media, not being followed in stores — has become foundational to the discourse.

**White supremacy** is the broader ideology that white people are superior and should dominate. White supremacy operates *overtly* (the KKK, neo-Nazi movements, explicit racial-hierarchy political projects) and *covertly* (the school-to-prison pipeline, mass incarceration, redlining, the *welfare queen* trope deployed against Black women though white women receive more government benefits).

**Microaggressions** — everyday slights, insults, and exclusions directed at members of stigmatized groups — were named by psychologist Chester Pierce in 1970 and elaborated by Derald Wing Sue in the 2000s. Microaggressions can be verbal (*Where are you really from?*), nonverbal (the woman clutching her purse on the elevator with a Black man), or environmental (the workplace whose imagery represents only one racial group). They are *micro* in the sense of *small at a single event*; their cumulative effect over years is large.

**Racial refusal** (Dána-Ain Davis, in *Reproductive Injustice: Racism, Pregnancy, and Premature Birth*, 2019) is the silent form of racism: the refusal to acknowledge race as relevant to a clinical, educational, or institutional outcome that demonstrably tracks race. Davis's research with NICU staff caring for Black women's premature infants found that medical staff routinely framed premature-birth disparities as class problems — even when the Black women were college-educated professionals. The refusal to name race was itself a way of allowing racial disparity to persist.

**Racial capitalism** (Cedric Robinson, in *Black Marxism: The Making of the Black Radical Tradition*, 1983) is the framework arguing that capitalism and racial hierarchy emerged together and remain entangled. Robinson rejected the assumption (common in earlier Marxist analysis) that race was a *distraction* from class; he argued that racial categorization was *part of how capitalism worked*, organizing labor markets, justifying slavery, structuring colonial extraction. Racial capitalism is increasingly central to contemporary anthropology of inequality.

### Class

**Class**, in the most basic sense, is a group of people with similar socioeconomic status and similar proximity to power. In capitalist societies, class is largely organized around wealth (Marx's bourgeoisie / proletariat), but Bourdieu's expansion has shown that class also operates through *cultural*, *social*, and *symbolic* forms of capital alongside the *economic* form.

**Bourdieu's four capitals.**
- **Economic capital**: money and assets convertible to money.
- **Cultural capital**: educational credentials, taste, fluency in elite cultural references.
- **Social capital**: useful relationships, network access.
- **Symbolic capital**: prestige, recognition, honor.

The four are interconvertible, with friction. Economic capital can be converted into cultural capital (paying for elite schooling). Cultural capital can be converted into economic capital (an elite degree gets a high-paying job). Social capital connects to both (the right connections open doors). Symbolic capital is the most fluid — a celebrity athlete can convert symbolic capital into economic (endorsements), social (access to elite networks), and even cultural capital (university honorary degrees). Capital can also be lost — a scandal converts symbolic capital into stigma, with knock-on effects.

The sociologist Lauren Rivera's *Pedigree: How Elite Students Get Elite Jobs* (2016) used participant observation in the hiring processes of top investment banks, consulting firms, and law firms. Rivera found that hiring decisions were heavily based on *cultural fit* (a euphemism for shared cultural capital — same schools, same hobbies, same vacation patterns, same ways of dressing) rather than narrowly on competence. *Cultural capital reproduces class by sorting workers into elite jobs partly by their already-acquired cultural fluency*. Bourdieu's framework predicts the result.

**Social mobility** — the capacity to move between classes — is a foundational claim of capitalist societies (the *American Dream*) and is, empirically, much rarer than the claim suggests. The sociologist Katherine Newman's research on *downward mobility* documents how easily middle-class status is lost (job loss, divorce, illness) and how hard it is to recover. The 2008 financial crisis and the COVID-19 economic disruption both produced waves of downward mobility from which many households have not recovered.

**Racial capitalism** (above) and **class** intersect tightly. Black Americans have, on average, far less accumulated wealth than white Americans of equivalent income — a structural pattern that traces directly to the post-Civil-War failure of land redistribution, the GI Bill's exclusions of Black veterans, the redlining of Black neighborhoods, and ongoing housing and labor discrimination. *Class is not just economic; class is racial; race is not just symbolic; race is economic.*

### Gender and patriarchy

**Patriarchy** is a system of social inequality based on gender, in which power is concentrated in the hands of men and characteristics associated with femininity are devalued. Most contemporary societies are patriarchal to some degree, though the form varies dramatically.

Patriarchy operates through similar levels as racism: *interpersonally* (the senior partner's comment that goes unchallenged); *institutionally* (the parental-leave policy that treats women as the default caregiver); *structurally* (the gender wage gap that persists across cohorts); *systemically* (the cultural expectation that women will absorb emotional labor at home and at work).

The anthropologist Sharon Hicks-Bartlett (2000), working in *Meadow View* (a poor, mostly Black midwestern suburb), documented how women living in poverty bore disproportionate responsibility for keeping families together — managing low-wage part-time work in environments where public services were minimal, often without bus access. The intersection of gender, class, and race here matters: the burdens were not just gendered, not just classed, not just racialized — they compounded.

The sociologist Signithia Fordham (2013) studied Black teenage girls in a predominantly white high school over two years. Fordham found that the girls' academic performance was shaped by an internal logic she called *competing to lose* — strategically downplaying achievement to maintain peer-group belonging in environments where high achievement was associated with separation from one's community. *Patriarchy and racism interact with peer dynamics in ways that produce specific patterns of self-suppression that individual choices alone cannot explain*.

## 4. Concept 3 — Intersectionality and contemporary cases

### Intersectionality

The term *intersectionality* was coined by U.S. legal scholar **Kimberlé Crenshaw** in a 1989 paper analyzing legal cases where Black women had been excluded from anti-discrimination protection because their experience did not fit either the *race* category (defined by Black men's experience) or the *gender* category (defined by white women's experience). Crenshaw's argument: identities do not stack; they intersect. The experience of being a Black woman is not *the experience of being Black* + *the experience of being a woman*. It is its own thing, with its own dynamics, that neither single-axis analysis can capture.

The intuition behind intersectionality predates Crenshaw's term. **Sojourner Truth's 1851 *Ain't I a Woman* speech** at the Ohio Women's Convention pointed out that the women's-rights movement of the time was implicitly a white-women's-rights movement, and that Black women's experience was being erased from the *woman* category. Black feminist scholars (the Combahee River Collective in 1977, bell hooks throughout the 1980s, Patricia Hill Collins's *Black Feminist Thought* in 1990) developed the analytical machinery that Crenshaw's term named.

**Anthropological extensions.**

The American anthropologist **Faye Harrison** has argued that *race is always lived in class- and gender-specific ways* (Harrison 1995). The lived experience of a working-class Black woman differs from that of a wealthy Black woman, and both differ from that of a working-class white woman; the differences are not additive but interactional.

The anthropologist **Leith Mullings** developed the concept of **Sojourner syndrome** in the context of the Harlem Birthright Project (a CDC-funded study of racial disparities in maternal and infant health). Mullings found that Black women in Harlem faced compounded structural inequalities — racism in healthcare, gendered caregiving expectations, class-based resource constraints — that produced higher stress levels, poorer health outcomes, and shorter life expectancy than either Black men or white women in similar conditions. *Sojourner syndrome* names the embodied effect of intersecting oppressions on Black women's bodies.

**Misogynoir** (a term coined by queer Black feminist Moya Bailey in the late 2000s) names the specific intersection of misogyny and anti-Black racism that Black women experience. Misogynoir operates in popular culture (the sexualized stereotyping of Black women), in political discourse (the *welfare queen* trope), in everyday interpersonal interactions, and increasingly in algorithmic systems (AI-generated images that exoticize or hypersexualize Black women's bodies; content-moderation systems that disproportionately flag Black women's posts).

### Caste

The chapter has used race and class as primary axes, but **caste** is a parallel system in many parts of the world, especially South Asia. Caste is a system of inherited social position — assigned at birth, mostly unchangeable through individual effort — that organizes occupation, marriage, residential location, ritual purity, and political voice.

The Indian caste system formally operates through *varna* (the four-fold classical hierarchy: Brahmins, Kshatriyas, Vaishyas, Shudras, with Dalits / *untouchables* outside the *varna* system) and *jati* (thousands of more specific birth-defined groups within and across the *varnas*). The Indian Constitution of 1950 abolished caste discrimination as a legal matter; caste-based discrimination remains pervasive in practice. The introductory cold open with the rural Dalit nurse is one face of this — the courteous exclusion that does not need violence to keep working.

The American anthropologists **Allison Davis, Burleigh Gardner, and Mary Gardner**, in *Deep South* (1941), argued that the U.S. South operated a *racial caste system* — not legally identical to the Indian system but functionally similar in its inheritability, its intermarriage taboos, and its enforcement through both formal law (Jim Crow) and informal norms. The *racial caste* analysis was controversial and remains contested; some scholars find it illuminating, others find it overstated (Indian caste has its own deep specificity that does not transfer). Isabel Wilkerson's 2020 book *Caste: The Origins of Our Discontents* revived the analogy. The argument is worth taking seriously and worth holding lightly — the analogy illuminates some patterns and obscures others.

### Resistance

Power produces resistance. Most of the inequalities the chapter has surveyed have been contested, often successfully, by the people they harm.

**Palestinian resistance to Israeli occupation** is the case the source material foregrounds. The 1948 establishment of the Israeli state displaced 700,000+ Palestinians from territory now in Israel proper — an event Palestinians call the **Nakba** (Arabic: *catastrophe*). The Occupied Palestinian Territory (West Bank, East Jerusalem, Gaza) has been under Israeli military occupation since 1967. Resistance takes multiple forms: armed insurgency, peaceful protest, international advocacy, cultural production, scholarship. The **West-Eastern Divan Orchestra** — co-founded in 1999 by Israeli pianist Daniel Barenboim and Palestinian-American scholar Edward Said — brings Israeli, Palestinian, and other Arab musicians together for joint performance. Said and Barenboim were explicit that the orchestra was not a peace project; it was a project to create *the conditions for peace* by demonstrating that mutual respect across the conflict was possible. The orchestra continues to perform internationally.

**Black Lives Matter**, founded in 2013 after the acquittal of George Zimmerman in the killing of Trayvon Martin, has grown into a global movement contesting necropolitics — the disproportionate readiness of state apparatus to allow Black death, especially through police violence. The movement combines Crenshaw-style intersectional analysis (Black women, Black trans people, and Black LGBTQ+ people are foregrounded), Mbembe-style necropolitical critique, and direct-action tactics drawn from the U.S. civil-rights tradition. Outcomes are mixed; structural change has been incremental; the movement has reshaped the discursive terrain in ways that durably matter.

**The #MeToo movement** (2006 origin with Tarana Burke; viral 2017 expansion) contests sexual harassment as a structural feature of male-dominated workplaces. It has brought down individual perpetrators (Harvey Weinstein, Bill Cosby, R. Kelly) and shifted institutional norms in many sectors. Its limits have also been visible — the movement has had less reach into industries where workers are non-unionized and easily replaceable, and the intersectional question (whose harassment counts? whose accusations are believed?) has produced internal contestation that mirrors Crenshaw's original critique.

## 5. Integration — Reading inequality through multiple lenses

The chapter's analytical apparatus, applied to one case, produces the kind of integrated reading the discipline aims for. Take the Black maternal mortality crisis in the U.S., a case Mullings, Davis, and others have worked on extensively.

The numbers: Black women in the U.S. die in childbirth at three to four times the rate of white women. The disparity holds across income, education, and access to prenatal care.

A single-axis analysis would not get this result. *Race alone* is not enough — there are racial disparities in many health outcomes, but the maternal mortality gap is unusually steep. *Class alone* is not enough — the gap persists for college-educated, professionally employed Black women. *Gender alone* is not enough — pregnancy is a near-universal female experience, but mortality clusters racially. *Healthcare access alone* is not enough — even Black women with insurance and prenatal care show the disparity.

Intersectional analysis (Crenshaw, Hill Collins, Mullings's *Sojourner syndrome*) gets further. The compounded stress of navigating race, class, and gender simultaneously produces measurable physiological effects (elevated cortisol, weathering, allostatic load) that increase pregnancy risk. *And* — Dána-Ain Davis's *racial refusal* — medical staff routinely fail to treat Black women's pain reports as credible. *And* — historical structural racism (housing patterns, environmental exposures, intergenerational poverty) produces underlying health conditions that interact with pregnancy. *And* — the medical institution itself was built around white women's bodies as the implicit norm; Black women's bodies are read as deviations.

The intersectional reading does not just add factors. It changes the kind of intervention that would work. Adding more prenatal care visits — the standard policy response — does not fix the problem because the problem is not access; it is the way care is delivered when access exists. Training medical staff to recognize their own biases helps but does not fix structural racism or systemic stress. Increasing Black representation in the medical profession helps both directly (Black patients fare better with Black doctors) and indirectly. The most effective interventions operate at multiple levels simultaneously and address the intersection of axes rather than each axis alone.

This is what the chapter's apparatus is *for*. Inequality is not a single thing to be measured. It is multiple intersecting axes operating at multiple levels through multiple mechanisms. The analytical work is to map the specific inequality, identify the axes and levels in play, and design interventions that engage all of them.

## 7. Chapter summary — what you can now do

You can distinguish *inequality* from *inequity* and identify the four levels (interpersonal, institutional, structural, systemic) at which inequality operates.

You can place the major theoretical traditions for analyzing inequality and recognize each one's distinctive contribution: Marx on class conflict, Du Bois on race-and-class, Gramsci on hegemony, Bourdieu on habitus and capitals, Foucault and Mbembe on biopower and necropolitics, Crenshaw on intersectionality, Robinson on racial capitalism.

You can identify *Whiteness*, *White privilege*, *White supremacy*, *microaggressions*, *racial refusal*, and *racial capitalism* as distinct concepts that name distinct phenomena and apply them to specific cases.

You can apply Bourdieu's four capitals (economic, cultural, social, symbolic) to a specific institution and trace how the capitals are accumulated, converted, and lost.

You can apply *intersectionality* to a contemporary inequality — not as a slogan but as analytical discipline that examines how multiple axes compound in specific ways.

The single idea that matters most: *inequality runs on multiple axes that intersect rather than stack, operating at multiple levels that reinforce each other through both hegemonic consent and overt force*. Single-axis analysis and single-level intervention both routinely miss what is happening. The analytical work is to hold multiple axes and multiple levels in view at once.

The common mistake to watch for: *treating inequality as a problem of individual bad actors rather than a property of systems*. Bad actors exist; they are not what makes inequality systemic. The systemic properties — hegemony, structural patterns, habitus — operate without requiring bad actors at all. Reform programs that focus only on bad actors leave the systemic mechanisms intact.

If you can take a contemporary news story about an unequal outcome and (a) name the axes operating, (b) name the levels at which the inequality operates, (c) identify which scholars' frameworks help explain it, and (d) propose interventions that engage the intersections, you have absorbed the chapter's analytical apparatus.

## What would change my mind

If a major body of cross-cultural empirical research demonstrated that intersectional analysis systematically *over-fits* — finding compounded effects that more rigorous single-axis or controlled-variable analysis cannot replicate — the chapter's strong claim that intersection is more than the sum of axes would need revision. The contemporary empirical record on Sojourner syndrome, maternal mortality disparities, and similar cases supports the intersectional reading; that support could be re-examined as methods improve.

## Still puzzling

The relationship between *racial capitalism* and *capitalism in non-Western societies* (China, India, parts of Africa) where the racial categorization that organized European and U.S. capitalism does not have the same structure. Cedric Robinson's framework was developed primarily in the Atlantic context. Whether and how it extends to capitalism elsewhere is contested. I do not have a clean view of the answer.
