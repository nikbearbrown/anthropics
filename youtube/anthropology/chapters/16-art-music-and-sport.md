# Chapter 16 — Art, Music, and Sport

## Suggested titles

- *Trobriand Cricket and the Logic of Cultural Performance*
- *What Art, Music, and Sport Have in Common*

## TL;DR

Art, music, and sport are forms of cultural performance — embodied, public, shared, and shaped by the cultures that produce them. This chapter walks through art as iconography and material culture, music as social practice and political tool, and sport as performance. Throughout: the analytical question of *cultural appropriation*, when cultural forms travel across communities under unequal power relations.

## 1. Trobriand cricket

The Trobriand Islands, off the eastern coast of Papua New Guinea, sometime in the late 19th century. British missionaries arrived with cricket. The aim was clear: replace the Trobriand practice of inter-village ritualized warfare with a *civilized* British sport. The missionaries brought bats, balls, wickets, and the *Marylebone Cricket Club* rule book. They taught the game.

What happened next was not the cricket they expected.

The Trobrianders kept the bats and balls and wickets. They threw out most of the rules. They made each team massive — sometimes 50 players — to mirror the size of the inter-village rivalries the cricket was supposed to replace. They added ritualized entry dances at the start of each match, with full ceremonial dress, body paint, and rhythmic chanting. They added victory dances after each successful action. They introduced *kayasa* — the village leader's coordinated war chants delivered from the sideline. They followed an iron rule that *the home team always wins*. The match was not about winning; the match was about hosting. Defeating a guest team would have been a violation of hospitality.

The American anthropologist Jerry Leach documented Trobriand cricket in his 1976 ethnographic film *Trobriand Cricket: An Ingenious Response to Colonialism*. The film is a textbook of what *cultural appropriation in reverse* looks like — a colonized people taking the colonizer's cultural form and bending it until it serves their own purposes. Leach's deeper argument: this is what cultures do when given any cultural form. They do not adopt; they translate. The translation always carries the local logic.

Trobriand cricket is the chapter's anchor case for what art, music, and sport have in common. All three are *forms of cultural performance* — embodied, public, shared, shaped by the culture that produces them and shaping the culture in turn. The chapter walks through each domain in turn, then returns to the question that the Trobriand case foregrounds: *when cultural forms travel across communities, under what conditions is the travel celebration, and under what conditions is it appropriation?*

## 2. Concept 1 — Art and material culture

### The anthropological approach to art

The contemporary discipline takes a holistic approach to art. *Art* is not a category that pre-exists analysis; it is what cultures consider artistic. The category boundaries shift across cultures and time periods. The 18th-century European division between *fine art* and *craft* is not a universal distinction. Many cultures place ceremonial weaving, ceramic-making, and bodily decoration in the same domain as what Europeans would call painting and sculpture.

Three working questions anthropologists ask of any artistic practice.

**What does the art do?** Art communicates, decorates, commemorates, ritualizes, claims status, organizes labor, marks identity. *No single function defines art*; what art does varies with what the culture needs it to do.

**Who makes it for whom?** The artist's relationship to the audience, the patron, the broader community shapes what gets made. Patron-funded Roman portraiture differs structurally from the communal Aboriginal sand paintings that emerge from collective ceremonial practice.

**What does the art mean?** The iconographic content — the symbols, references, conventions — encodes meaning that requires cultural literacy to read. The Kangxi emperor's portrait carries meanings (imperial dignity, Confucian virtue, military authority) that Chinese viewers would read but European viewers would miss without instruction.

### Iconographic study

**Iconographic study** is the analytical practice of decoding the visual symbols, conventions, and references in artistic works. The discipline of art history developed iconography in the 19th and 20th centuries, particularly through Erwin Panofsky's mid-20th-century work. Anthropology has imported the framework and applied it cross-culturally.

A worked example. In Tibetan Buddhist iconography, the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara (in Tibetan, Chenrezig) is depicted with eleven heads and a thousand arms, each hand bearing an open eye in its palm. The eleven heads emerged from a story in which the bodhisattva attempted to liberate all beings, was overwhelmed by their suffering, and had his head split into eleven pieces — pieces that were reassembled by Amitabha Buddha so that the bodhisattva could continue the work. The thousand arms similarly emerged from need, with each eye watching for suffering and each hand offering aid. The iconography encodes the bodhisattva's defining quality: infinite compassion. *To read the image, you have to know the story; to know the story, you have to be inside the tradition.*

The same principle applies to non-Buddhist iconography. The eagle in U.S. visual culture means specific things (national strength, federal authority) that arose from specific historical conventions. The hammer-and-sickle means specific things (industrial worker / agricultural worker unity in Soviet ideology) within its convention. *Iconography is decoded through cultural knowledge, not deduced from form alone.*

### Prehistoric art

Some of the oldest preserved cultural production is *prehistoric art* — cave paintings, rock engravings, portable carvings. The Chauvet Cave paintings in France, dated to roughly 36,000 years before the present. The El Castillo Cave hand stencils in Spain, dated to at least 64,000 years before the present (predating *Homo sapiens* arrival in Western Europe and likely produced by Neanderthals — recall Chapter 5). The Maros-Pangkep limestone cave paintings in Indonesia, dated to around 45,500 years before the present. The Sahel rock art across Saharan Africa.

Anthropologists have offered multiple interpretations. The *hunting magic* hypothesis — paintings of game animals were intended to magically influence hunting success. The *shamanic vision* hypothesis (Lewis-Williams) — paintings depicted altered-state visions experienced by shamans during trance. The *information transmission* hypothesis — paintings preserved knowledge across generations. The *ritual record* hypothesis — paintings documented ceremonial events.

The hypotheses are not mutually exclusive. Most prehistoric art likely served multiple functions simultaneously. The most rigorous contemporary analysis combines iconographic features (which animals appear, in what configurations, with what additional symbols) with archaeological context (where the art appears, who could access it, what artifacts surround it) to constrain interpretation.

### Body art

A particularly cross-cultural form of artistic production: **body art** — markings, modifications, and decorations of the human body. Three major categories.

**Tattooing.** Documented across nearly every continent in pre-modern societies. Polynesian *tatau* (the source of the English word) was an elaborate practice combining genealogical signaling, religious initiation, and aesthetic display. Japanese *irezumi* developed as both criminal stigma and yakuza affiliation marker, with elaborate iconographic conventions. Maori *tā moko* uses chiseled rather than punctured marks and encodes lineage and status. Egyptian mummies show tattoos on women, typically associated with childbirth protection. The 5,300-year-old Iceman *Ötzi* found in the Alps had 61 tattoos, primarily over joints showing arthritis — possibly therapeutic. Contemporary tattoo culture in industrial societies is a modern revival drawing on multiple traditions.

**Scarification.** Found across sub-Saharan African societies, parts of Australia, parts of Melanesia. Practitioners cut or burn the skin in patterns; the resulting raised scars carry visual and tactile aesthetic significance. The Nuba people of Sudan, the Yoruba of Nigeria, the Karo of Ethiopia all have traditions. Scarification often marks rite-of-passage transitions (Chapter 13) — the scar is visible permanent evidence of having undergone the ritual.

**Body painting.** Less permanent than tattooing or scarification but no less culturally significant. Aboriginal Australian ceremonial body painting, with each clan's specific designs that correspond to ancestral *Dreaming* stories. Yanomami warrior face paint. Hindu festival color-throwing during *Holi*. Western theatrical makeup and the celebrity-culture cosmetic regimes that derive from it.

The anthropological point: *body art is not decorative addition to bodies; body art is the body's cultural inscription*. The body is itself an artistic medium across most cultures. The boundary between body and art is the cultural boundary the body produces by being inscribed.

## 3. Concept 2 — Music and ethnomusicology

### What music does

Music is found in every documented human culture. The earliest preserved musical instruments — flutes carved from vulture and bear bones, dated to roughly 40,000 years before the present in Europe — predate the cave paintings of the same era. Music is at least as old as visual art and, by some measures, older. Whatever evolutionary or cultural function music serves, it has been serving it for a long time.

What does music do? The anthropological inventory is rich.

**Communication.** Music carries information across distances and languages. The talking drums of West Africa transmit specific messages across miles using tonal patterns that encode spoken language. The yodels of Alpine herders carry across mountain valleys. The whistled languages of Gomera (Canary Islands), Kuşköy (Turkey), and Mazatec (Mexico) communicate full sentences over distances exceeding two miles.

**Ritual structure.** Most rituals (Chapter 13) involve music. Music marks the transitions between ritual phases, sustains the participants' attention through long sequences, and produces the *communitas* (Turner, Chapter 13) that ritual depends on. Hindu *kirtan*, Sufi *sema*, Catholic plainchant, Native American powwow drumming, hip-hop ciphers — all are music-as-ritual.

**Identity formation.** Music marks group membership. National anthems. Sports-team chants. Subcultural genres (punk, country, K-pop, EDM, narcocorridos). Religious hymnody. The sonic signal of *I belong with these people* is one of music's most consistent functions.

**Emotional regulation.** Music produces, intensifies, and modulates emotion. Mourning rituals across cultures use music to structure grief. Lullabies modulate infant arousal. Workplace music regulates labor pace. Therapeutic music interventions have measurable effects on dementia patients, premature infants, and people undergoing medical procedures.

**Resistance.** Music carries political content under conditions where other forms of expression are restricted. The chapter's middle section develops this thread.

### Ethnomusicology

**Ethnomusicology** is the cross-cultural study of music in social and cultural context. The field emerged in the late 19th and early 20th centuries (initially as *comparative musicology*) and was renamed in the 1950s. Contemporary ethnomusicology combines anthropological fieldwork, musicological analysis, and increasingly the digital archiving of endangered musical traditions.

**Frances Densmore** (1867–1957) is one of the foundational figures. Densmore worked for the Smithsonian Institution's Bureau of American Ethnology from 1907 to 1957 — a fifty-year career during which she recorded and transcribed the music of more than thirty Native American nations. The recordings are now an essential archival resource for Indigenous-led music revival movements. Densmore's work, like much salvage-era anthropology (Chapter 2), operated within colonial assumptions and methods. Contemporary Indigenous communities working with her recordings have had to negotiate that complicated legacy.

**Alan Lomax** (1915–2002) similarly recorded vast quantities of folk and traditional music across the U.S., the Caribbean, and Europe. His Library of Congress field recordings preserved blues, ballad, gospel, and folk traditions that would otherwise have been lost. The Lomax archive has been the basis for several generations of subsequent musical revival.

Contemporary ethnomusicology has shifted toward collaborative research models. The Indigenous community whose music is being recorded is increasingly co-author, co-owner, and co-distributor of the resulting recordings rather than passive subject. The shift mirrors the broader anthropological shift toward collaborative methods (Chapter 2).

### Music as resistance

Music has been a vehicle of political resistance across the historical record. Two cases illustrate.

**The African American spiritual tradition.** Spirituals — religious songs sung by enslaved Africans and their descendants in the U.S. South — operated on multiple levels. The surface level was Christian biblical narrative. The deeper level was coded coordination for resistance. *Go Down, Moses* called on the Israelites' liberation as a model for Black liberation. *Wade in the Water* may have functioned as instruction for fugitive slaves traveling the Underground Railroad — water masked scent trails from tracking dogs. *Steal Away* signaled secret meeting times. *Swing Low, Sweet Chariot* may have communicated about specific Underground Railroad routes. The historian Frederick Douglass wrote in his 1845 *Narrative* about how spirituals carried meaning that white slaveholders missed; the songs sustained an interior cultural world that the brutal external system could not penetrate. Harriet Tubman, the Underground Railroad conductor, used spirituals as practical signals during escape operations.

The spiritual tradition continued into the 20th-century civil rights movement. *We Shall Overcome*, adapted from a 19th-century Charles Tindley hymn, became the movement's anthem. Aretha Franklin's *Respect* (1967), originally an Otis Redding song reworked into a feminist demand. Sam Cooke's *A Change Is Gonna Come* (1964), inspired by Dylan's *Blowin' in the Wind* and the experience of being denied a hotel room because of his race. Nina Simone's *Mississippi Goddam* (1964), written in response to the Birmingham church bombing. The civil rights movement was sung as much as spoken.

**The protest music of the 1960s globally.** Bob Dylan's *The Times They Are a-Changin'* (1964) and *Masters of War* (1963) exemplify the U.S. anti-war and civil rights protest tradition. Comparable movements existed elsewhere — Mercedes Sosa in Latin America (the *nueva canción* movement); Fela Kuti in Nigeria (Afrobeat as political critique of Nigerian military dictatorship); Mikis Theodorakis in Greece (whose music was banned during the 1967–74 military junta); Joan Baez and Pete Seeger in the U.S. The pattern: under conditions of political repression, music carries the political content that more explicit forms of expression cannot.

Contemporary protest music continues. Russian punk band Pussy Riot's 2012 protest performance in a Moscow cathedral. Hong Kong protesters' adoption of *Glory to Hong Kong* (2019) as movement anthem. Iranian protest music since 2022 (Shervin Hajipour's *Baraye*, which won the 2023 Grammy for Best Song for Social Change). The pattern continues.

### Cultural appropriation in music

Music is also the domain where the contested question of *cultural appropriation* most regularly surfaces. Where does cultural exchange end and appropriation begin?

A working framework. **Cultural appropriation** is the use of cultural elements (musical traditions, sacred symbols, dress, language) by members of a dominant culture from a marginalized culture, in conditions where the borrowing reproduces structural inequality — economically (the dominant-culture borrower profits while the source community does not), reputationally (the borrower receives recognition the source community is denied), or symbolically (the borrowing strips meaning from elements that were sacred or significant in the source culture).

Contrast with **cultural exchange** — the reciprocal flow of cultural elements between communities under conditions where the borrowing is consensual, attributed, and beneficial to both sides.

The distinction is hard to operationalize cleanly. Some specific cases are widely agreed:

- *Elvis Presley's appropriation of Black blues and R&B* in the 1950s. Presley performed material derived from Black artists (Big Mama Thornton's *Hound Dog*, Arthur Crudup's *That's All Right*, etc.), achieved enormous commercial success, while the Black artists who originated the material received little of the resulting wealth or recognition. Most contemporary commentators classify this as appropriation.

- *Black hip-hop sampling old soul and jazz records*. The samples typically attribute and license the source; the hip-hop artists are themselves usually members of communities adjacent to the source communities. Most contemporary commentators classify this as exchange.

Other cases are contested:

- *White artists performing reggae*. Bob Marley's collaborations with white producers and the spread of reggae across racial lines after his death. Some classify this as exchange; some as appropriation.
- *Non-Indigenous performers using Indigenous musical materials*. The Lakota traditional songs sampled in non-Indigenous popular music. Most Indigenous musicians and communities classify this as appropriation when done without consent.

The framework cannot resolve every case. The framework can sharpen the analysis.

## 4. Concept 3 — Sport as cultural performance

### Anthropology of sport

Sport is one of the most-contested cultural domains. Most contemporary readers grow up inside one or two sports cultures and assume the patterns they know are the natural form of sport. They are not. Sport varies enormously across cultures in what counts as sport, who plays, who watches, what the games mean, and what relation sport has to other cultural domains.

The **anthropology of sport** treats sport as a *form of cultural performance* — embodied, public, structured, and culturally specific. The analytical apparatus from Chapter 13 (ritual; communitas; rites of intensification) extends substantially to sport. Most major sports operate as *secular religion* (Chapter 13) — with sacred space (the stadium), ritual repetition (the season cycle; weekly matches), multivocal symbols (team colors, jerseys, championship trophies), priestly class (commentators, coaches), mythic narrative (origin stories of the franchise), pilgrimage (championship games), and intense communitas during peak moments.

Three contemporary cases illustrate.

**Trobriand cricket** (the cold open). Christian missionaries introduced British cricket to replace ritualized warfare. Trobrianders kept the materials and rebuilt the rules until cricket served the same social function the warfare had served. The case is emblematic: *cultural forms travel; cultures translate.*

**Reservation ball (rezball)**. An aggressive style of basketball played in Native American reservation communities, particularly across the Southwest U.S. and the Great Plains. Rezball features fast tempo, high scoring, and intense crowd participation. The Netflix docuseries *Basketball or Nothing* (2019) and the documentary *Rez Ball* (2024) document the tradition. Rezball functions as community pride, athlete development pathway (some rezball stars have moved to college and professional basketball), youth-recreation alternative, and continuing cultural practice in conditions where many other Indigenous activities have been suppressed. The Lakota anthropologist Beatrice Medicine and others have written about rezball as an Indigenous cultural form rather than a derivative of mainstream basketball.

**Brazilian futebol**. Football (soccer) in Brazil is an institution adjacent to the Catholic Church in cultural significance. The 1950 World Cup loss to Uruguay (the *Maracanazo*) is treated as a national trauma still discussed three generations later. Pelé's status as national hero. The current Brazilian national team's cultural weight. Anthropologist Roberto DaMatta has written extensively on how Brazilian futebol carries the country's racial, class, and regional tensions in ways that other domains cannot. Brazilian futebol is sport as national myth, performed weekly.

### Sport and gender

Sport is one of the densest sites of gender ideology (Chapter 12). Most contemporary professional sports were until recently restricted to men or sex-segregated. Title IX in the U.S. (1972) opened school sports to girls; the WNBA started in 1996; women's professional soccer emerged in the 2000s and became a major commercial force in the 2010s and 2020s. The U.S. women's national soccer team's 2019 lawsuit for equal pay with the men's team became a major test case for sport-related gender inequality. The 2023 Australia and New Zealand Women's World Cup drew unprecedented audiences.

The contested edge is *trans inclusion in women's sports*. The biological-essentialist position argues that some athletic advantages from male-typical hormonal exposure remain after transition and create unfair competition. The trans-rights position argues that the testosterone-suppression standards already in place address most of the concern and that exclusion produces greater harm than inclusion. Major sport governing bodies (World Athletics, FINA/World Aquatics, the IOC) have set varying standards; the standards have been repeatedly contested. The chapter does not resolve the question; the chapter places it in its framework.

### Sport and inequality

Sport intersects with inequality in multiple ways. Access to sport — coaching, equipment, time, venues — is unequally distributed by class, race, geography, and ability. The pipeline from youth sports to elite sport is heavily filtered by family resources. The professional success that follows for elite athletes is concentrated in a tiny fraction of those who pursue it. The communities that produce elite athletes often see little reinvestment from the resulting wealth.

The Brazilian anthropologist Damo, the U.S. anthropologist Susan Brownell on Chinese sport, and many others have documented how sport produces, reflects, and sometimes contests structural inequality. The same sport that opens a path out of poverty for a few star athletes generates exploitative labor conditions for thousands of minor-league players whose careers do not pan out. The same sport that builds national pride at the World Cup operates a global trafficking system in young African players moved to European academies that mostly do not produce careers.

### Cultural appropriation in sport

The mascot question. Many U.S. professional and college sports teams have used Indigenous-themed names, mascots, and imagery. The Cleveland Indians (renamed Guardians in 2022). The Washington Redskins (renamed Commanders in 2022 after decades of advocacy by Indigenous activists). The Atlanta Braves and Kansas City Chiefs (still in use as of 2024). The college mascots that have been retired (the Stanford Indians; the Dartmouth Indians) and those still in use (the Florida State Seminoles, with formal Seminole Tribe of Florida endorsement).

The Indigenous-led Change the Mascot campaign and similar efforts have argued that Indigenous-themed mascots are a clear case of cultural appropriation — non-Indigenous communities profiting from caricatures of Indigenous identity, with the source communities receiving neither attribution nor benefit and often experiencing direct psychological harm from the caricatures. Studies (Stephanie Fryberg and colleagues) have documented measurable negative psychological effects on Indigenous youth exposed to mascot imagery. The mascot question is one of the most-active ongoing debates over appropriation in sport.

## 5. Integration — Reading three performance cases

Three contemporary cases that exercise the chapter's apparatus.

**Maori *kapa haka* at the 2023 New Zealand–Australia Women's World Cup opening.** The opening ceremony featured a Maori cultural performance — *haka* dance, *waiata* singing, formal *whakatau* welcome by Maori elders. The performance was choreographed by Maori cultural specialists. The Maori community had genuine input into the framing. The performance carried meaning the international audience could not fully read, but the performance was itself an act of cultural sovereignty — Maori people performing Maori culture for a global audience on their own terms. The chapter's apparatus reads it as *postcolonial self-representation* (Chapter 15's returned gaze) and as *iconographic performance* operating in the space where art, music, sport, and ritual meet.

**A 2024 K-pop performance in Madison Square Garden.** A major Korean group performs to a sold-out crowd in New York. The performance integrates choreography, fashion, music, video, and audience interaction in a tightly produced spectacle. The K-pop industry has been studied extensively as a contemporary cultural-export phenomenon (Hallyu); the industry combines elements of Western popular music, Korean traditional aesthetics, and a distinctive fan-engagement model. The performance carries the *technophilia* (Chapter 15) of the contemporary mass-media spectacle, the *secular-religion* features of devoted fan culture, and the *cultural-translation* logic of a non-Western cultural form's global travel. Whether the form's spread constitutes cultural exchange or appropriation depends on which dimension you weigh.

**A Lakota powwow in South Dakota in summer 2024.** The annual gathering features competitive dance, drum group performance, traditional regalia, food, vendors, and family reunion across the diaspora. The powwow is one of the most-vital Indigenous cultural events in contemporary North America, despite (or because of) more than a century of suppression by federal and state authorities. The chapter's apparatus reads it as art (regalia and dance), music (drum-group performance), sport (competitive dance), and ritual (rite of intensification, callback to Chapter 13) all at once. Non-Indigenous attendance is welcome under specific protocols. Photography and recording without consent are explicitly forbidden. The powwow is sovereign space; the visitors are guests.

The chapter's apparatus reads all three. Each is a different performance in a different cultural context. The framework holds across the variation.

## 7. Chapter summary — what you can now do

You can analyze a work of art using *iconographic study* — decoding symbols, conventions, and references through cultural literacy.

You can identify *body art* (tattooing, scarification, body painting) cross-culturally and read each as a cultural inscription rather than as decorative addition.

You can apply *ethnomusicology* — the study of music in social and cultural context — to specific musical traditions and understand music as ritual structure, identity formation, emotional regulation, and political resistance.

You can recognize *sport as cultural performance* and identify the secular-religion features (sacred place, ritual repetition, multivocal symbols, communitas, mythic narrative).

You can distinguish *cultural appropriation* from *cultural exchange* using the analytical framework — economic benefit distribution, reputational benefit, consultation, structural context.

The single idea that matters most: *art, music, and sport are not separate domains; they are forms of cultural performance that share underlying logic. The Trobrianders' transformed cricket, the Lakota powwow, the K-pop spectacle, the Maori haka — all are cultural performances doing similar kinds of work in different contexts.*

The common mistake to watch for: *treating cultural forms as universal in their meaning rather than in their function*. The same cricket bat means different things in a missionary's hands and a Trobriand chief's hands. The same musical instrument means different things in different traditions. The same body of athletic technique means different things at the Olympics and on the reservation. *Form travels; meaning is local.*

If you can take any cultural performance — a piece of art, a musical work, a sport, a ritual — and identify (a) the cultural function, (b) the iconographic content, (c) the production-and-audience structure, (d) the appropriation-or-exchange status, you have absorbed the chapter's apparatus.

## What would change my mind

If a major body of evidence demonstrated that the cross-cultural patterns this chapter foregrounds (cultural appropriation as a meaningful analytical category; sport-as-performance shared across societies; body art as universally significant) were artifacts of specific Western analytical projection rather than features of the cultural traditions themselves, the chapter's strong claims about cross-cultural patterns would need revision. The current evidence supports the recurring patterns; the chapter holds the position cautiously.

## Still puzzling

The relationship between *cultural appropriation as moral concept* and *cultural appropriation as structural-analytic concept*. The two are not the same. The moral concept asks *is this wrong?*; the structural-analytic concept asks *what are the power relations producing this exchange?*. They overlap but do not reduce to each other. Working out exactly how they relate is a continuing puzzle.
