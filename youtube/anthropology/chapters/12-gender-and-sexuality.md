# Chapter 12 — Gender and Sexuality

## Suggested titles

- *Hijra at the Door — and the Limits of the Gender Binary*
- *Sex, Gender, Sexuality: Three Concepts, Many Cultures*
- *What "Boy or Girl?" Misses*

## TL;DR

Sex, gender, and sexuality are three distinct concepts, often conflated. This chapter walks through their variation across cultures — multiple-gender systems (hijra, two-spirit, fa'afafine), the biological reality of intersex bodies (1.7% of births), gender as performance (Butler), patriarchy as structure, and the cross-cultural prevalence of same-sex relationships in societies that deny they exist.

## 1. Hijra at the door

A house in Mumbai. A baby has been born — a boy, the family announces. Within days, sometimes hours, a small group of *hijra* arrives at the door. They are dressed in saris. They bring drums. They sing. They demand to see the baby.

The *hijra* are India's third-gender community — historically about half a million people across the subcontinent, now closer to several million, with smaller communities throughout the Indian diaspora. Most *hijra* were assigned male at birth and have transitioned, often through *nirvana* (a ritual castration), to a feminine identity that is not exactly *woman* in either the Hindu or Muslim cosmologies. The *hijra* identity is its own thing — recognized in classical Hindu texts, formally acknowledged by the Indian Supreme Court in 2014 as a third gender for legal purposes, and hereditarily organized into *gharanas* (lineage households) led by senior *gurus*.

The baby's family welcomes the *hijra* visit, or pretends to welcome it. The *hijra* sing blessings over the child — fertility, prosperity, long life. The blessings are believed to carry weight; *hijra* are thought to have special powers, residue of their liminal position between male and female, between the human and the sacred. The family pays them — money, sweets, sometimes cloth. If the family refuses to pay or pays too little, the blessing can be withdrawn or replaced with a curse — a particular kind of curse that involves the *hijra* lifting their saris in a public gesture of shame to the family. *Pay the hijra* is widely understood Indian household wisdom.

The *hijra* leave, ululating. They have done their work. The baby has been welcomed into the world by people whose existence the rigid male-female binary cannot account for.

This is one of the chapter's central facts: *gender systems with three or more categories are not exotic outliers. They are common.* The *hijra* of India and Pakistan; the *kathoey* of Thailand; the *fa'afafine* of Samoa; the *muxe* of Zapotec Mexico; the *bissu* of the Bugis of Indonesia; the *machi* of the Mapuche of Chile; the *travesti* of Brazil and Argentina; the *waria* of Indonesia. Native American *two-spirit* traditions across dozens of distinct nations. Variant-gender categories were widespread across pre-colonial Polynesia. The contemporary U.S. recognition of *non-binary* and *transgender* identities is, on a global-historical timescale, a return to a more typical pattern.

The chapter that follows walks through gender as anthropology has come to understand it. By the end, you should be able to distinguish *sex*, *gender*, and *sexuality* analytically; to recognize multiple-gender systems and read their cultural logic; to identify *patriarchy* as structure rather than as a moral failing of individual men; and to use *intersectionality* (Chapter 9) to see how gender operates differently across class, race, and citizenship.

## 2. Concept 1 — Sex, gender, sexuality, and the intersex evidence

### Three distinct concepts

The discipline distinguishes three terms that everyday English often conflates.

**Sex** refers to biological categories — male, female, and the documented variations beyond the binary. Sex is determined by a constellation of features: visible genitalia, internal reproductive organs, secondary sex characteristics (breasts, body hair distribution), chromosomes (XX, XY, and atypical patterns like XXY, XO), reproductive capacities, hormonal profiles. Most humans cluster around two clusters of these features. A meaningful minority do not.

**Gender** refers to cultural roles, identities, and expectations associated with the sex categories. Gender is what culture does with sex. The biological capacity to gestate a child does not, by itself, dictate that the gestating parent will be socially defined as the primary caregiver, will be expected to wear particular clothing, will have particular employment opportunities, or will speak in a particular way. Those expectations are gendered — culturally produced and culturally variable.

**Sexuality** refers to erotic thoughts, desires, practices, and the social identities organized around them. Sexuality intersects with gender (most cultures organize sexuality around gendered categories) but is not reducible to it. The same sexual practice can carry very different cultural meanings depending on the sexuality framework around it.

Conflation of the three is the source of most public confusion about gender. *Sex* and *gender* are not the same. *Gender* and *sexuality* are not the same. *Sexual orientation* and *gender identity* are not the same. The chapter's analytical work is partly the work of holding the three separate so that the relationships among them become visible.

### Intersex bodies and what they do to the binary

The biologist Anne Fausto-Sterling, in her 2000 book *Sexing the Body* and subsequent work, estimated that approximately 1.7% of human births involve some form of *intersex* condition — a constellation of biological features that does not fit either of the standard male/female clusters. The number depends on how strictly *intersex* is defined; Fausto-Sterling's 1.7% includes a wide range of conditions (genital ambiguity at birth, atypical chromosome patterns like XXY or XO, hormone insensitivity syndromes, etc.). Stricter definitions yield smaller numbers; broader ones yield larger.

Even at 1.7%, the implication is large. Applied to global births of roughly 140 million per year, the figure suggests something like 2.4 million intersex babies born annually. In a city of 300,000 people, the figure suggests roughly 5,000 intersex residents.

Most contemporary medical practice, particularly in the U.S. and Europe through the 1990s, treated intersex as a *problem* to be *solved* through surgical and hormonal intervention assigning the child to one binary category. The interventions were usually performed in infancy, before the child could give consent or articulate a gender identity. The result was a generation of intersex adults — many of them now activists — who have argued that the interventions were a form of mutilation, that the assignments were often wrong (the surgeon's guess about which gender the child would identify with proved incorrect in a substantial fraction of cases), and that informed consent should be the standard before any irreversible intervention.

The intersex movement, organized since the 1990s through groups like InterACT and the Intersex Society of North America, has succeeded in slowing the medical default to early surgery in many jurisdictions. Several countries (Malta in 2015; Germany in 2021) have legally restricted non-emergency intersex surgery on minors. The medical norm is shifting toward delayed intervention and informed consent.

The analytical implication is the foundational claim of this section: *the male/female binary is a cultural overlay on a biological reality that is more continuous than the binary acknowledges*. The 1.7% figure is the data that breaks the binary. Once you accept that 1.7% of bodies do not fit the binary, you have to decide whether the binary describes biology or describes a cultural decision to fit biology into two boxes. The discipline's working answer: it describes the cultural decision.

### Primate evidence and what it does (and does not) say

Anthropology has often turned to primate studies to ask whether human gender patterns have biological roots. The evidence is more variable than popular accounts suggest.

In the 1950s, primatologists working largely with male-dominant species (savanna baboons; rhesus macaques) produced an account of *primate male dominance* that was extrapolated to claims about human gender. Females were described as marginal; males as the central political actors. This account fit the dominant U.S. gender ideology of the period.

By the 1980s, fieldwork by women primatologists (Jane Goodall on chimpanzees, Sarah Hrdy on langurs, Frans de Waal on bonobos, Adrienne Zihlman on multiple species) had complicated the picture substantially. Many primate species are organized through *female kinship cores* — related females who form the social backbone, with males moving between groups. Females are sexually assertive, politically strategic, and often centrally important to group survival. Male dominance, where it exists, is far less rigid than 1950s accounts suggested.

Compare bonobos and chimpanzees — humans' two closest evolutionary relatives, sharing 99% of our DNA. *Bonobos* are female-dominant; sexual contact is frequent and varied (across sex/gender combinations); group conflicts are typically resolved through sexual contact rather than aggression. *Chimpanzees* are male-dominant; sexual contact is more limited; group conflicts are frequently violent. Both species are equally close to humans evolutionarily. *The same DNA produces dramatically different gender patterns.* No single primate model predicts what is *natural* for humans; the variability is the data.

A note on the *Man the Hunter* hypothesis. In the 1960s, anthropologists Sherwood Washburn and Chet Lancaster argued that hunting by males was the central evolutionary engine of human social organization — that male hunting cooperation produced the cognitive, linguistic, and social capacities that made humans human. The hypothesis was widely accepted and widely taught. It was also wrong on the empirics. Subsequent research (Adrienne Zihlman, Frances Dahlberg's *Woman the Gatherer*, Sally Slocum's reanalysis) showed that gathering — typically performed by women in the gathering-hunting societies studied — provided the majority of calories, that women's labor was central rather than supplementary, and that the hunting-as-driver story overstated males' contribution to subsistence and selectively read the evidence. The Wilamaya Patjxa burial that opened Chapter 1 — a 9,000-year-old Andean female hunter — is part of the archaeological record that has continued to push back on the man-the-hunter model.

## 3. Concept 2 — Multiple-gender systems and gender as performance

### What multiple-gender systems look like

A *multiple-gender system* recognizes more than two gender categories. The categories are typically more than the additive sum of male and female; they are their own things, with their own social roles, their own ritual significance, their own rights and obligations.

**Hijra** in India and Pakistan. Recognized as a third gender under Indian Supreme Court ruling in *NALSA v. Union of India* (2014), formally enumerated in the Pakistani national census since 2017. Most hijra were assigned male at birth and have transitioned through some combination of social practice, hormonal treatment, and (historically and in some cases still) the *nirvana* castration ritual. Hijra households (*gharanas*) are organized under senior *gurus*, with hereditary teaching of ritual roles. Hijra perform blessings at births and weddings, the income from which has been the community's primary economic basis. Contemporary hijra face substantial stigma and economic exclusion alongside the formal legal recognition; the gap between the categories' historical ritual prestige and the contemporary lived reality is large.

**Two-spirit** identities across Native North American nations. Approximately 130 Native nations in pre-colonial North America had documented variant-gender categories — *winkte* among the Lakota, *nadleeh* among the Navajo (the same Navajo whose matrilineal kinship Chapter 11 covered), *bote* among the Crow, *lhamana* among the Zuni, *miati* among the Hidatsa. The pan-Indian English term *two-spirit* was coined in 1990 at an Indigenous LGBT conference in Winnipeg as a replacement for the older European-imposed term *berdache* (which had derogatory associations). Each Native nation had its own specifics; what they shared was recognition of categories beyond the male-female binary, often associated with spiritual roles, ritual responsibilities, and specific economic-productive roles. European colonization aggressively suppressed two-spirit traditions through the residential-school system and missionary pressure; the contemporary two-spirit movement is a revival, both spiritual and political.

**Fa'afafine** in Samoa. Translated approximately as *in the manner of a woman*, *fa'afafine* are people assigned male at birth who live as women within Samoan society. Estimates suggest 1–5% of the Samoan population. Fa'afafine roles include caregiving (often within their own families of origin, where they are valued as caregivers for elderly parents), arts and entertainment, increasingly entrepreneurship and politics. Samoa has a sister category *fa'afatama* (in the manner of a man) for those assigned female at birth who live as men, though this category is less prominent in popular accounts. Both categories are widely recognized in Samoan society without the stigma faced by analogous categories in many other societies.

**Bissu** among the Bugis of Sulawesi, Indonesia. The Bugis recognize five distinct genders: *makkunrai* (cisgender female), *oroané* (cisgender male), *calabai* (those assigned male who live in feminine roles), *calalai* (those assigned female who live in masculine roles), and *bissu* (a fifth category transcending the others, with priestly and ritual roles). The Bugis system is one of the most fully elaborated multiple-gender systems documented; the bissu specifically have served traditional ritual functions tied to royal courts and pre-Islamic Bugis cosmology.

**Muxe** among the Zapotec of southern Mexico, particularly in Juchitán de Zaragoza, Oaxaca. *Muxe* (pronounced moo-shay) are people assigned male at birth who take on feminine social roles. The category is widely recognized; muxe play prominent roles in religious festivals, family caregiving, and community life. Juchitán's annual *Vela de las Intrépidas* festival celebrates the muxe community.

The list could continue. The analytical claim it supports: *multiple-gender systems are common, ancient, and culturally elaborate. The contemporary Euro-American gender binary is one variation, not the universal default.*

### Gender as performance

The philosopher **Judith Butler** in *Gender Trouble* (1990) argued that gender is not an *expression* of an inner essence (whether biological or cultural) but a *performance* — a set of repeated bodily, linguistic, and social actions that produce the appearance of an underlying gender identity. The performance is not optional or insincere; it is the only way gender exists. *We do not have genders that we then express; we perform genders that, through repetition, come to seem like things we have.*

Butler's framework was philosophically dense and politically consequential. The framework gave queer and transgender activists a vocabulary for arguing that *gender presentations* — clothing, hairstyle, voice, gesture — are not symptoms of an underlying biological reality but constituents of a gender identity that is just as real as any other identity is. The framework also generated substantial pushback: critics argued that *performance* implied insincerity, that Butler's framework dissolved the material reality of gendered oppression, that biology mattered more than the framework allowed.

Butler clarified in subsequent work that *performance* in her sense is not the same as *theatrical pretense*. Performance is the constitutive activity that produces the gendered identity. Without the performance, the identity does not exist as a social fact. *Without the wedding ceremony, the marriage does not exist as a social fact* — Butler's analogue is John Austin's *performative utterance* (Chapter 6's *I now pronounce you...*). The performance does not make the identity fake; it makes it real.

The framework has been productive across the discipline. The Turkish anthropologist Salih Can Açıksöz's research on Turkish disabled veterans (in Ozyegin's *Gender and Sexuality in Muslim Cultures*, 2015) traces how government-funded assisted reproductive technology programs reconstruct masculinity for veterans whose injuries threaten the heterosexual-fatherhood model of masculine identity. The Egyptian anthropologist Maria Frederika Malmstroöm's work on Cairene women shows how cooking, skin care, and (in some cases) circumcision are performed as aspects of femininity. *Gender is not had; gender is done.* The work is daily, embodied, and political.

### Heteronormativity and patriarchy

Two structural concepts that recur across gender anthropology.

**Heteronormativity** is the cultural assumption that heterosexuality is the natural and default form of sexuality, and that all other forms are deviations from the norm. Heteronormativity operates through media (the default romantic plot), through institutions (marriage, parenting, religious community membership), through language (the assumed *partner* across gender lines), and through everyday interactions (the question *do you have a boyfriend?* asked of a young woman). Heteronormativity does not require explicit homophobia; it operates by treating heterosexuality as the unmarked category against which everything else is marked.

**Patriarchy** is the structural arrangement in which men hold disproportionate political, economic, religious, and family authority. Chapter 9 already covered patriarchy as one axis of social inequality. From the gender-anthropology side, patriarchy is the dominant gender ideology of most contemporary societies — implemented through specific institutional structures (male-headed households, gendered division of labor, restricted female access to property and political voice) that vary in their severity but are widely distributed.

Notice that *patriarchy* is not the same as *individual sexism*. Sexism is the personal attitude. Patriarchy is the structure. A society can have low individual sexism and high patriarchy if its institutions reproduce male advantage even without explicit individual misogyny. A society can have high individual sexism and reduced patriarchy if its institutions prevent the individual attitudes from converting into structural advantage. The distinction matters for analysis and for political strategy.

A note on **matriarchy**. Matriarchy in the strict sense — women holding political, economic, and ritual authority across the society — is documented in essentially zero contemporary societies and very few historical ones. The Iroquois Confederacy and the Mosuo (Chapter 11) come closest: matrilineal descent, matrilocal residence, female political voice. But even there, men hold formal political offices (male sachems among the Iroquois, brothers as household heads among the Mosuo) alongside the matrilineal family structure. The widespread popular use of *matriarchy* to describe what are technically matrilineal or matrilocal societies obscures the analytical fact that *true matriarchy is rare to non-existent*. Why this is the case is one of the discipline's open questions.

## 4. Concept 3 — Sexuality across cultures and the politics of gender today

### Same-sex relationships in heteronormative societies

One of the more counterintuitive findings of cross-cultural sex/gender research: *societies that publicly disavow same-sex sexuality often have substantial same-sex sexual practice in widely-known, semi-tolerated forms*. The disavowal is one cultural fact; the practice is another. Both are real.

Three patterns recur.

**Age-graded same-sex sexuality** — the older partner is presumed to *give* and the younger to *receive*. Patterns documented in Classical Greece (the *erastes/eromenos* pedagogical relationship), among the Azande of Sudan (the *boy-wife* relationship described by Evans-Pritchard, 1970), among the *Sambia* of New Guinea (the ritualized same-sex initiation described by Gilbert Herdt, 1981 and 1984), in some classical Persian and Ottoman contexts. The pattern is age-graded, often time-limited (the younger partner ages out, marries a woman, and may eventually take a younger partner of his own), and embedded in broader social-reproduction structures. The pattern is *not* what most contemporary readers would call *gay* or *queer* — it is its own thing.

**Gender-egalitarian same-sex sexuality** — partners of similar age and social standing engage in mutual same-sex relationships. The *supi* relationships in West African boarding schools (Ghanaian girls; documented by Serena Owusua Dankwa, 2009; M.E. Kheswa Gyasi-Gyamera and L. Søgaard, 2020). The Zande women's same-sex relationships described by Evans-Pritchard alongside the male-male age-graded pattern. The pattern is more recognizable to contemporary Western readers as *something like gay or lesbian relationships*, though local frameworks may not use those categories.

**Ritualized same-sex sexuality** — same-sex contact within ritual contexts, embedded in religious or initiation practices. The Sambia case Herdt described falls here as well as in the age-graded category. Some hijra contexts include ritualized same-sex contact with patrons. *Bissu* roles among the Bugis include same-sex sexual practices in ritual contexts.

The cross-cultural pattern: *same-sex sexual practice is widespread across human societies*, including in many that publicly reject it. Whether such practices constitute *homosexuality* in the contemporary Western sense is contested; the contemporary Western category itself is a 19th-century invention (the term *homosexual* was coined in 1869 by the Hungarian writer Karl Maria Kertbeny). Older societies had practices but typically not the identity-category *homosexual* organized around exclusive same-sex orientation.

### Contemporary terminology

The vocabulary of contemporary gender and sexuality has expanded substantially in recent decades. A working set of definitions, with the awareness that the vocabulary will continue to evolve:

- **Cisgender** — describes a person whose gender identity matches the sex assigned at birth.
- **Transgender** — describes a person whose gender identity does not match the sex assigned at birth. Includes binary trans (trans man, trans woman) and non-binary identifications.
- **Non-binary** — describes a person whose gender identity is not exclusively *male* or *female*.
- **Gender non-conforming** — describes a person whose gender presentation does not match the cultural expectations of their assigned gender, regardless of identity.
- **Queer** — originally a slur in English, reclaimed by activists since the 1990s as an umbrella term for non-normative gender and sexuality identities. The term carries a more political and fluid sense than specific identity labels.
- **Heterosexual / straight** — sexual orientation primarily toward people of a different gender from one's own.
- **Gay / lesbian** — sexual orientation primarily toward people of the same gender from one's own.
- **Bisexual** — sexual orientation toward more than one gender.
- **Pansexual** — sexual orientation that is not organized by the gender of partners.
- **Asexual** — describes a person who experiences little or no sexual attraction.
- **Two-spirit** — pan-Indian English term for Indigenous variant-gender identities. Should be used by Indigenous people themselves; non-Indigenous people typically use it only with care.

The terminology is contested. New terms are coined; older terms shift meaning; specific communities adopt and discard labels at different paces. The chapter's claim is not that the terminology is settled but that the analytical move — distinguishing sex, gender, and sexuality; recognizing gender variation cross-culturally — survives the terminological churn.

### #MeToo, gender violence, and the contemporary politics

Chapter 9 covered the #MeToo movement (origin 2006, viral 2017) as a contemporary social movement. From the gender-anthropology side, #MeToo is a case study in how *gender ideology* (Chapter 9) gets contested. The movement's central analytical move — making sexual harassment visible as structural rather than individual — is a feminist anthropology move applied politically.

The anthropologist Peggy Reeves Sanday's 1990 *Fraternity Gang Rape* documented patterns of sexual violence in U.S. fraternity culture decades before #MeToo. Sanday's argument: fraternity culture is structured to produce specific gendered violence through *the normalization of violation as bonding*, *the deployment of alcohol to compromise consent*, *the protection of perpetrators by group loyalty*, and *the silencing of victims by social pressure*. The argument was widely contested when Sanday published it. By 2017, the analytical structure of #MeToo cases recognized in major news media largely matched Sanday's account.

Anthropology has also documented the substantial cross-cultural variation in gender-based violence. Some societies (Chambri in Mead's account; Mosuo as documented by Cai Hua) have low rates of gender violence. Some (where strong patriarchy intersects with weak rule of law) have very high rates. The variation is the data — gender violence is not a constant of the human condition but a structurally produced phenomenon.

## 5. Integration — Reading three contemporary cases

Three contemporary cases that exercise the chapter's apparatus.

**A trans woman in São Paulo.** Born and assigned male; transitioned in her late twenties through hormone therapy and gender-affirming surgery; works as a software engineer; is in a long-term relationship with a cis woman; identifies as a queer trans woman. The chapter's vocabulary: she is *transgender* (gender identity differs from sex assigned at birth); her relationship is structurally similar to many lesbian relationships, though the heteronormativity of her local culture may not classify it that way. Brazil has a legally recognized gender-recognition process (revised 2018) that allows her to update identification documents without surgical requirement. Her social experience reflects the heightened violence Brazil has against trans women — Brazil has one of the world's highest documented rates of trans-related murder — and the simultaneous cultural visibility of trans icons in Brazilian media.

**A hijra elder in Mumbai.** Sixty years old; transitioned in her late teens through *nirvana*; serves as a *guru* to a household of nine younger hijra; performs blessings at births and weddings. Following the 2014 *NALSA* ruling, she has government documents listing her as third gender. She receives some elder-care benefits under expanded gender-inclusion programs. She also lives in poverty by middle-class standards and has experienced regular street harassment despite the legal recognition. Her position is *both* legally protected *and* socially marginalized — the legal change has not produced the social change.

**A two-spirit person in a contemporary Lakota community.** Late twenties; raised on a reservation; came out as two-spirit in their early twenties through participation in the National American Indian and Alaska Native Two-Spirit Society's gatherings. They are involved in *LandBack* organizing alongside two-spirit cultural revival. Their gender identity is connected to Lakota *winkte* tradition specifically, not to the broader pan-Indian *two-spirit* category alone. They speak Lakota (which has gendered grammatical forms different from English) and English. Their community recognition is contested — some elders embrace the revival; some, raised in Christian missionary contexts, reject it.

Three lives, three frameworks, three different ways the chapter's analytical apparatus illuminates what is happening. None of the three fits cleanly into a binary gender system. Each operates in its own cultural-political context. Each would be misread without the chapter's vocabulary.

## 7. Chapter summary — what you can now do

You can distinguish *sex* (biology), *gender* (culture), and *sexuality* (erotic practice and identity) and avoid the conflations that produce most public confusion about these terms.

You can identify *intersex* (1.7% of births) and use it to recognize that the male/female binary is a cultural overlay on a biological reality that is more continuous than the binary acknowledges.

You can identify *multiple-gender systems* and place specific cases (hijra, two-spirit, fa'afafine, bissu, muxe) within them.

You can apply Judith Butler's *gender as performance* to a specific cultural setting and understand how gender is *done* through repeated practice rather than *had* as an inner essence.

You can identify *patriarchy* as structure rather than as moral failing and distinguish it from individual sexism.

You can recognize the cross-cultural prevalence of same-sex sexual practice — including in societies that publicly disavow it — and place it in age-graded, gender-egalitarian, or ritualized patterns.

You can use the contemporary terminology (cisgender, transgender, non-binary, gender non-conforming, queer, gay, lesbian, bisexual, pansexual, asexual, two-spirit) with analytical precision rather than as slogans.

The single idea that matters most: *gender is plural across cultures and across history, and the contemporary U.S. binary is a recent and contested cultural arrangement, not a universal default.* Recognizing your own gender system as one among many is the precondition for reading other systems analytically.

The common mistake to watch for: *projecting Western LGBTQ+ identity categories onto non-Western variant-gender systems*. Hijra are not Indian trans women. Two-spirit is not Indigenous queer. Fa'afafine is not Samoan gay. Each is its own cultural category with its own history and its own internal logic.

If you can take a description of any society's gender system and identify (a) the number of recognized categories, (b) the cultural roles of each, (c) the implications for sexuality and kinship, (d) the contemporary status, you have absorbed the chapter's apparatus.

## What would change my mind

If a major body of cross-cultural neurobiological research demonstrated that gender identities are largely fixed by prenatal hormonal exposure — that gender is determined biologically with cultural overlays as relatively minor adjustments — the chapter's strong commitment to gender as cultural construction would need revision. The current evidence does not support this strong-biological reading; the chapter holds the cultural-construction position cautiously rather than absolutely.

## Still puzzling

The relationship between *contemporary trans visibility in the U.S./Europe* and *traditional multiple-gender systems in non-Western societies*. Some scholars argue these are continuous (trans recognition in the contemporary West is a return to a more typical pattern). Some argue they are discontinuous (Western trans identities arise from a specific medicalized framework that has no parallel in traditional multiple-gender systems). The discipline is divided, and I do not have a confident view on which framing is more accurate.
