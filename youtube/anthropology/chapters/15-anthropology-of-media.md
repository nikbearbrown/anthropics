# Chapter 15 — Anthropology of Media

## Suggested titles

- *The Phone with the Name — and Other Lessons of Media Anthropology*
- *Looking at Looking: The Gaze, the Screen, and the Self*

## TL;DR

Media — from cave paintings to smartphones — are tools for storing and sharing information across space and time. This chapter walks through media as cultural infrastructure, the *gaze* (imperial, male, voyeuristic, returned) as the central analytical move in visual anthropology, and the digital media that have reshaped contemporary sociality, including its illicit shadowlands.

## 1. The phone with a name

A modest kitchen in Dublin. An older woman, sixty-three, is holding her phone. She has named it. She talks to it the way one talks to a small dog — affectionately, with mild scolding when it misbehaves. She tells the anthropologist Daniel Miller, who is sitting across from her at the kitchen table, that the phone is good company. Her husband died four years ago. The phone, she says, is the closest thing she has to a daily relationship with another being.

The anthropologist is part of the *Why We Post* project — a multi-year ethnographic study Miller led across nine countries in the early 2010s, asking how smartphones and social media were being used in different cultural contexts. The team produced eleven books between 2016 and 2018, each focused on a specific country site (rural Ireland, urban China, Brazil, Cameroon, Italy, India, Trinidad, Turkey, Chile). The headline finding was a cliché only because it turned out to be true: *the same device produced very different cultural objects in different settings*.

The Irish phone became a companion. The Cameroonian phone became a money-routing infrastructure (mobile money for household budgeting; the project documented how mobile-money flows had reshaped infant mortality in rural communities, a finding Chapter 1 already mentioned). The Brazilian phone became a tool for managing extensive familial networks across the diaspora. The Chinese phone became a vehicle for the *internet identity* of a generation that had never had any other kind of public identity. The Italian phone, in some communities, became the visible marker of generational digital divide. *Same hardware, different cultural practice.*

Miller's project is a foundational case for what *media anthropology* tries to do. It refuses two seductive simplifications. The first: *media are technology and technology is universal* — meaning the same device produces the same effects everywhere. The second: *media are cultural and culture is local* — meaning the technology is incidental to the cultural use. Miller's project rejected both. The technology and the culture *make each other* in the specific sites where they meet. The anthropology of media is the work of tracking that making.

This chapter walks through media as anthropology has come to study it. By the end you should be able to read any media artifact — a *National Geographic* photo spread, a Bollywood film, a TikTok feed, a propaganda poster, a documentary photograph from a colonial mission archive — using the chapter's apparatus.

## 2. Concept 1 — Basic media, mass media, and the technophilia of the present

### What media are

The simplest definition: **media** are tools for storing and sharing information across space and time. The definition is broad. It includes cave paintings, cuneiform tablets, illuminated manuscripts, printed books, photographs, telephones, films, radio broadcasts, television, the internet, social media, podcasts, augmented reality. The definition does not require digital technology. The definition does not even require *recording* in the modern sense — an oral epic transmitted across generations is media in the broad sense, as the structure that allows information to outlive its individual carriers.

Anthropology distinguishes *basic media* from *mass media* by *reproduction at scale*. Basic media operate one-to-one or one-to-few — a letter, a face-to-face conversation, a manuscript copied by hand. Mass media operate one-to-many through mechanical or electronic reproduction — the printed book, the broadcast radio program, the newspaper, the film, the television show, the website.

The shift to mass media has occurred in waves.

**Print** (1040s in China; 1440 in Europe). The Chinese artisan Bi Sheng created the first known movable-type printing system around 1040 CE, using baked-clay character blocks. Four centuries later, the German entrepreneur Johannes Gutenberg independently invented a metal-movable-type system, beginning his commercial operation around 1440 with calendars, indulgences, and the famous 180-copy Bible. The European printing revolution that followed reshaped Europe's religious geography (Reformation), political organization (rise of nation-states using vernacular print to consolidate identity — the *imagined community* of Chapter 8), and intellectual life (the Scientific Revolution depended on the rapid circulation of scientific findings through printed journals).

**Photography** (1820s onward). The first photographic image, by Joseph Nicéphore Niépce, dates to roughly 1826. By the 1850s photography had become commercial; by the 1870s a mass medium. Photography arrived just in time to document the high-colonial period (1870s–1920s), with consequences this chapter's Concept 2 unpacks.

**Broadcast (radio, television)** (1920s onward). Mass-distribution one-to-many media that did not require literacy. By the 1950s, broadcast television was the dominant mass medium in many wealthy countries. State broadcasters (BBC, NHK, Doordarshan) and commercial networks competed for shares of national attention. The *imagined community* of the nation got its most efficient cultivation tool here.

**Internet** (1990s onward). The fourth wave. Many-to-many rather than one-to-many. The internet has been less transformative in the same direction as the previous waves than it has been *qualitatively different* — it dissolves the distinction between sender and receiver, recipient and producer. The implications are still unfolding.

### Technophilia

The anthropologist of technology Genevieve Bell has used the term **technophilia** for the cultural condition of embracing new technologies and integrating them into everyday life. The contemporary period is technophilic in a pronounced way. New devices arrive on annual cycles; the latest generation is presumed superior to the previous; the absence of a particular device (a smartphone, a streaming subscription) reads as deprivation rather than choice.

Technophilia is not universal. The same technology can be embraced enthusiastically in one society and resisted in another. The Amish in the U.S. have famously selective relationships with technology (some technologies adopted, others not, by community deliberation). Some Indigenous Australian communities have adopted mobile phones rapidly while resisting other digital technologies. The variation is the data. Technophilia, like other cultural orientations, is locally produced.

### The Why We Post project — same device, different culture

Daniel Miller's *Why We Post* project (2012–2017) is the contemporary anchor case for media anthropology. The project deployed nine ethnographers, each spending 15 months in one site, studying how smartphones and social media were used in their context. The project published *How the World Changed Social Media* (2016), an open-access summary, alongside the eleven country-specific monographs.

A few findings stand out.

**Posting practices vary.** What Brazilians post is not what Italians post is not what rural Chinese post. The same Facebook platform produces different cultural objects depending on what is locally considered postable.

**Privacy works differently.** In some sites, social media is *more* private than face-to-face conversation (intimate revelations made on Facebook to selected friends would not be made aloud at a family dinner). In other sites, social media is *less* private (anything posted is assumed to be visible to neighbors and elders).

**Generational dynamics shift.** In rural India, the ethnographer found that mothers were teaching their daughters how to use Facebook to maintain inter-generational kin contact across migration distances. In Italy, the same platform was generationally divisive — teens used it; their parents did not.

**Mobile money matters.** The Cameroon site documented how mobile-money infrastructure had reshaped household economics, infant mortality (the case from Chapter 1), and inter-village exchange in ways that no part of the technology's design had anticipated.

The project's deeper claim, which carries through this chapter: *the digital is not separate from the cultural. The digital is one of the tools through which the cultural now operates.*

## 3. Concept 2 — The gaze: imperial, male, voyeuristic, returned

### What the gaze is, analytically

The concept of *the gaze* developed in 1970s film theory and has been one of the most-imported analytical frameworks in media anthropology. **The gaze** is the structured way an audience looks at people in visual media (and the way the people in the media look back, or fail to). The gaze is not just the act of looking. The gaze carries a *power relation* — the gazer scrutinizes, interprets, judges; the gazed-upon is positioned as object rather than subject.

Two features of the gaze are particularly important.

**The gazed-upon is typically unaware of the gaze.** In photography and film, the subjects do not know how the image will be used, who will see it, or what stories will be told around it. This produces what film theorists call *the voyeuristic structure* — the audience is in the position of an unseen watcher.

**The gaze carries power.** The watcher has interpretive control. The watcher decides what the image means, what context to read it in, what stories to attach to it. The watched cannot push back through the image itself.

The British film theorist **Laura Mulvey**, in her 1975 essay *Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema*, named the **male gaze** as a central feature of mainstream cinema. The camera looks at women in particular ways — lingering, fragmenting (close-ups of body parts), positioning women as objects of desire rather than subjects of action. The male gaze, Mulvey argued, structures the experience of cinema for all viewers regardless of their own gender; the female viewer either identifies with the male gaze (being placed in the position of looking at women) or with the female object of the gaze (being placed in the position of being looked at). Mulvey's framing has been challenged, qualified, and extended over the past 50 years; the basic insight has survived.

The **imperial gaze** extends the framework to colonial and post-colonial visual representation. European photography of colonial subjects in the late 19th and early 20th centuries operated by specific conventions — the subject posed in *traditional* dress, against *typical* backdrop, with no individual name or biography. The image was made for European audiences, who consumed it as evidence of *what those people are like*. The imperial gaze produces *the colonial other* as an object of European knowledge and rule. The gaze does political work; the colonial system depended on producing a population of subjects whose *otherness* justified the rule over them.

### *National Geographic* — Lutz and Collins

The cultural anthropologist **Catherine Lutz** and the sociologist **Jane Collins** conducted a systematic content analysis of *National Geographic* magazine for the 1950–1986 period, published as *Reading National Geographic* (1993). They examined 600 photographs depicting non-Western peoples. The findings are damning in a particular way.

The photographs *did not* depict obvious colonial exploitation or contemporary inequality. They depicted *exotic, idealized, traditional* lifeways. People in *traditional* dress, in *traditional* settings, performing *traditional* activities. The colonial and postcolonial histories that had produced contemporary conditions in the photographed locations were systematically absent. The political-economic forces that had brought *National Geographic* photographers to those locations in the first place were systematically absent. The photographed subjects were positioned as *outside history* — as if they existed in a timeless ethnographic present.

The effect: *National Geographic* readers (largely middle-class American children and their parents) absorbed an image of the non-Western world as *other* in a particular way. Not poor, not exploited, not on the receiving end of the very systems that had produced the magazine's affluent readership. *Different*. *Picturesque*. *Less developed*. The imperial gaze, three generations after political decolonization, was still doing its work in the school library.

The pattern Lutz and Collins documented is not specific to *National Geographic*. The same critique applies to mainstream National-Geographic-like media (the BBC's wildlife documentaries, the Discovery Channel's anthropology programming), to coffee-table books on world cultures, to a substantial fraction of contemporary travel journalism. *Once you have the gaze concept, you see the imperial gaze everywhere.* The point is not to refuse the genre; the point is to read it with the apparatus the gaze concept provides.

### Basel Mission Society photographs

The Swiss historian **Paul Jenkins**, working from the Basel Mission Society archive, traced how 19th-century missionary photography in Ghana operated by the imperial gaze. The case is specific. Christian Hornberger, a Basel Mission Society missionary in southeastern Ghana in the 1860s, was instructed to take photographs that would be sold to European Christians who donated to the African mission. The photographs needed to *raise money*. The photographs that raised money were photographs that depicted Africa as *needing missionary intervention*.

The early Hornberger photographs emphasized strangeness, exoticism, the *darkness* of the people (in both literal and metaphorical senses, with all the racial baggage of the period). The later photographs softened — Africans depicted in postures Europeans recognized: families eating dinner together, women grinding corn, craftspeople at work. The shift mirrored European donors' shifting tastes.

The most striking subset: photographs of African children in European clothing. Jenkins traced these to the BMS *child redemption* program, in which European Christians were invited to *purchase* the freedom of an enslaved or pawned African child, give the child a Christian name, and pay for their upkeep at the mission compound. The program was widely advertised and well-funded. It was also, in the children's own experience as later interviewed, a new form of bondage — they were unhappy on the mission compound, separated from their families, forced into uncomfortable European dress, and many ran away. The BMS abandoned the program by 1868.

The photographs survive. They depict, by their own internal logic, *redeemed African children*. They depict, with the Lutz-Collins critical apparatus, *the imperial gaze caught in the act of producing the very subjects it claimed to be saving*.

### Postcolonial self-representation — Buckley

The case turns when post-colonial subjects produce their own images. The anthropologist **Liam Buckley**, working in The Gambia in the 1990s and 2000s, documented studio photography in Banjul. The photographs were taken by Gambian photographers, for Gambian customers, for Gambian distribution.

The aesthetic choices were specific. Photographs were staged against painted backdrops depicting *modernity* — staircases ascending into airplanes, lavishly stocked open refrigerators, computer-equipped offices, foreign cityscapes. The customers wore imported clothing, held props (cell phones, briefcases, sunglasses), often arranged with multiple family members in coordinated outfits. The Gambian word *juuntuwaay* described the props and imported goods staged in the photographs.

What is happening here? Buckley argues that the photographs are *not* attempts to deceive — viewers know the staircase is painted, the office is fake, the cell phone may be a prop. The photographs are *aspirational performance* — claiming a mode of life that the photographed subjects either inhabit, are working toward, or simply enjoy claiming. The photographs are *Gambians' self-representation in the visual register of modernity*. The imperial gaze is replaced — or at least joined — by a *returned gaze* in which the photographed subjects are now the subjects of their own image-making, on their own aesthetic terms.

The same pattern operates in many post-colonial contexts. Ghanaian video film (Concept 1's Dig Deeper) is the cinematic version. Bollywood is the industrial-scale version. K-pop is the global-distribution version. *Postcolonial subjects looking at themselves and producing their own images for their own audiences is a major feature of contemporary global media.*

## 4. Concept 3 — Sociality, media ideologies, and the digital shadowlands

### Sociality as the broader frame

The cultural anthropologist Marilyn Strathern, in her work on Melanesian kinship, used the term **sociality** to describe how people construct and maintain their relationships — personal and group — through repeated practice. Media-anthropology has imported the term broadly. *Sociality* names the work that media does in constituting and maintaining the relationships of contemporary life. Texts to a friend; group chats with extended family; comments on a sibling's social-media post; a video call with a parent on a different continent. *These are not just communications about a relationship; they are the relationship's substance.*

Sociality through media is not new (letters from 19th-century European immigrants to their families in the old country were the same kind of relationship-substance), but its scale has expanded dramatically. Most people in most contemporary societies maintain a substantial fraction of their meaningful relationships through digital media at this point. Pew estimated in 2022 that the average U.S. teen spends 4–8 hours daily on smartphones, with a majority of that time in social-media interaction.

### Media ideologies — Gershon

The anthropologist **Ilana Gershon**, in *The Breakup 2.0* (2010), introduced the concept of **media ideologies** — the cultural assumptions about what particular media are *appropriate for*. Gershon's research started with a classroom question to her undergraduate students: *what counts as a bad breakup?* She expected stories about lying, cheating, infidelity. She got, instead, stories about *being broken up with by text* or *seeing your relationship status change on Facebook before you'd been told*. The students' anger was about the medium choice, not the content.

The deeper finding: Americans hold *media ideologies* — sometimes implicit, sometimes explicit — about which forms of communication are appropriate for which kinds of social action. A breakup, in the dominant U.S. media ideology, is a *consensual dialogic act* requiring face-to-face conversation. A breakup conducted through text message *unilateralizes* the speech act and removes the breaker-upper from witnessing the consequences. The media ideology says this is wrong. The breaker-upper, holding a different media ideology, sees text-breakup as an efficient way to avoid an emotionally difficult scene.

The framework generalizes. Posting your engagement on Instagram *before* telling your parents is a media-ideology violation in many U.S. families. Sharing a partner's pregnancy news on social media before telling close friends is another. The ideologies are real and contested. They are also enormously consequential for the relationships they govern.

### Public-sphere media: bloggers, activists, the digital commons

Digital media's most generative use, in many contexts, has been the construction of *public spheres* outside state and corporate control. Iranian women's blogs (Concept 2's Dig Deeper). Egyptian Facebook groups during the 2011 revolution. Black feminist Twitter (now Black feminist BlueSky and Threads in shifting platform contexts). Afro-Cuban activist blogs (Sandra Abd'Allah-Alvarez Ramírez and others). Brazilian Black-women coding initiatives (Silvana Bahia and Olabi). Hong Kong protest organization through encrypted messaging.

The pattern: where mainstream media is captured by state or corporate interests, digital media has provided *alternative public spheres* in which marginalized groups can speak. The patterns are uneven — the same digital platforms that empower these spheres also enable surveillance, harassment, and disinformation campaigns directed against them. Twitter (now X) has been particularly studied as a site where Black voices have built durable critical communities that have been simultaneously celebrated, monetized, harassed, and platform-engineered against.

### The digital shadowlands — sakawa

Digital media also enable *illicit* economies. Piracy, scams, human trafficking, illegal pornography, ransomware. The shadowlands are real and consequential.

A specific case: **sakawa** in Ghana. A Hausa term meaning *putting inside*, sakawa refers to magically-enhanced internet fraud, primarily targeting wealthy foreigners. The phenomenon emerged in Ghana around 2010 in the context of an oil boom whose wealth concentrated among older elites, leaving substantial populations of unemployed young men with limited economic prospects.

The mechanics of sakawa combine technical skill (creating convincing online personas, manipulating dating-site interactions, executing payment schemes) with traditional spiritual practice (ritual apprenticeship to a *malam* or spiritual leader who guarantees *blessings* for fraud success in exchange for prescribed rituals — sometimes minor, sometimes elaborate and disturbing). The successful sakawa operator displays sudden wealth — luxury cars, designer clothing, large parties — that becomes part of a recognizable cultural type in Ghanaian urban life.

The Ghanaian filmmaker Ben Asamoah's 2018 documentary *Sakawa* documents the practice. Sakawa is illegal under Ghanaian and international law. It is also a recognizable youth subculture with its own aesthetic, music genres (*sakawa music* is its own subset of contemporary Ghanaian hiplife), and moral economy. The framework it operates within is not simply *crime*; it is *redress*. From the operators' perspective, sakawa redirects wealth from the global rich to the global poor, using spiritual technology to balance a moral ledger that the legitimate economy has refused to balance. This is not a defense of sakawa; it is an account of how the practitioners themselves understand what they are doing.

The pattern recurs globally. Russian and East European cybercrime networks. Nigerian *419* email scams. Chinese romance-scam call centers. Each operates within a moral economy where the practitioners construct narratives that make the practice intelligible — sometimes righteous — within their context. The narratives are not the same as legal or ethical justifications. They are also not nothing.

## 5. Integration — Reading three media artifacts

Three contemporary media artifacts read through the chapter's full apparatus.

**A *National Geographic* spread on a remote Indigenous group, 2024.** Glossy photography, evocative text, beautiful production values. The Lutz-Collins critique applies: even contemporary *National Geographic* coverage tends to position non-Western peoples as exotic, idealized, traditional, and outside contemporary history. The imperial gaze persists. *And yet* the magazine has, since the 2010s, increasingly published Indigenous-authored writing and photography alongside the staff coverage; the trajectory is mixed rather than uniformly retrograde.

**A 60-second TikTok from a Brazilian Afro-Brazilian feminist creator.** The creator addresses the camera directly. She wears specific clothing and uses specific cosmetic choices that signal her cultural positioning. She speaks Portuguese, with English subtitles auto-generated. She names a specific issue (police violence against Black women in Rio). She uses the platform's editing tools to layer in archival footage and music. She tags specific hashtags. The video reaches roughly 200,000 viewers across continents within 48 hours.

The artifact carries traces of all the chapter's apparatus. It is *postcolonial self-representation* (Gambian-style returned gaze). It operates within Black feminist *digital sociality* (the platforms-and-network of contemporary activism). It deploys the platform's own *media ideology* about how political content should be delivered (face to camera; specific tone; specific tags). It crosses Appadurai's *mediascape* (Chapter 10) at speed. Whether it reaches durable political outcomes depends on factors the video itself cannot control.

**A WhatsApp group chat among five members of an extended Indian family scattered across Mumbai, Dubai, London, and New Jersey.** Messages flow daily — voice notes, photos, links, video calls. The chat is the family's *primary site of relationship maintenance*; in-person reunions happen once a year, but the chat happens every day. The chat is heavily gendered — the older women are the most active participants; the men post less. The chat carries food photos (a Sunday meal in Mumbai shared with the daughter in London who cooked the same dish two hours later); ritual observances (Ganesh Chaturthi celebrated simultaneously across the four cities); financial coordination (mobile-money transfers for extended family obligations); minor disputes and reconciliations. *The family is constituted, in part, through the chat.* This is sociality as Strathern's framework would have it.

The chapter's apparatus reads all three. Each is a different piece of contemporary media practice. The framework holds across the variation; the variation is the data.

## 7. Chapter summary — what you can now do

You can distinguish *basic* from *mass* media and place the four major waves of mass-media development.

You can apply the concept of *the gaze* in its three classic subtypes (imperial, male, voyeuristic) to specific visual artifacts, and you can recognize the *returned gaze* in postcolonial self-representation.

You can read Lutz and Collins's *National Geographic* analysis as a model for analyzing similar media in your own context.

You can identify *media ideologies* in your own and others' practice — the implicit assumptions about which media are appropriate for which kinds of social action.

You can analyze *digital sociality* — the work that media does in constituting and maintaining contemporary relationships.

You can recognize *illicit media* (sakawa, scams, piracy) without collapsing them to *crime* or excusing them as *redress*; you can hold the technical mechanics, the moral economy, the harm to victims, and the political-economic context simultaneously.

The single idea that matters most: *media is one of the primary mechanisms through which contemporary culture operates. Reading media well is a prerequisite for reading contemporary cultural life at all.*

The common mistake to watch for: *treating media as transparent vehicle for content*. Media is never neutral. The medium shapes the message; the production context shapes the medium; the political economy shapes the production context. *What you see is mediated all the way down.*

If you can take any media artifact you encounter — an Instagram post, a documentary, a news photograph, a music video, a TikTok — and identify the gaze, the production context, the assumed audience, and the cultural-political work it is doing, you have absorbed the chapter's apparatus.

## What would change my mind

If a major body of evidence demonstrated that contemporary mainstream Western media has substantially corrected for the imperial gaze rather than merely producing more diverse versions of the same gaze, the chapter's somewhat skeptical position on the trajectory of correction would need revision. The current evidence suggests partial correction in some outlets and persistent imperial-gaze patterns in many others; the chapter holds that mixed view.

## Still puzzling

The relationship between *digital media as alternative public sphere* and *digital media as surveillance infrastructure*. The same platforms that empower marginalized communities also enable state and corporate surveillance against those communities. Whether the empowerment outweighs the surveillance, in any specific case or aggregated across cases, is contested. I do not have a confident view.
