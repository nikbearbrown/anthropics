# Chapter 4 — Biological Evolution and Early Human Evidence

## TL;DR

Human biology is the product of evolutionary processes that work the same way across all life — mutation, natural selection, gene flow, drift, speciation. This chapter walks through how we know what a species is (four definitions, none perfect), how Darwin and Mendel together explain inheritance and change, and what the fossil record from the Miocene through the Pliocene tells us about when our ancestors stood up and walked.

## 1. Three sets of footprints in wet ash

A volcano in northern Tanzania erupts. The year is roughly 3.5 million years before the present. A layer of fine, slightly damp ash settles across a stretch of savanna. Then it rains. Then the rain stops, and across the wet ash come three creatures, walking. They cross about seventy-five feet of the ash bed, leaving prints. Then the sun comes out, the ash hardens, and another eruption seals the prints under a second layer. They lie there for three and a half million years.

In 1976, the British paleoanthropologist Mary Leakey is working a site in this region called Laetoli when one of her assistants, a man named Andrew Hill, dives to the ground to dodge an elephant-dung projectile that another team member has thrown at him in jest. Lying on his back in the dust, Hill notices an indentation in the rock. The indentation has a heel, a ball, and toes.

The Laetoli footprints — a continuous trail of prints from at least three individuals, walking in roughly the same direction — are the earliest unambiguous physical evidence of upright bipedal walking in the hominin lineage. The prints' shape shows a striding gait, an arched foot, a big toe aligned with the other toes (not opposable), and a heel-strike pattern recognizable to any podiatrist. The walkers were, almost certainly, members of *Australopithecus afarensis* — the species to which the famous skeleton known as *Lucy*, found three years earlier in the Afar region of Ethiopia, also belongs.

Three sets of footprints, half a million years before any stone tool. Already walking. Already upright. Already, by the most basic physical definition, on the human side of the family tree.

How did we get there? That is the question this chapter is asking. The answer requires three distinct kinds of biology. The first is *species* itself — what makes one population a different species from another, and why the answer turns out to be more complicated than a single definition. The second is *evolutionary mechanism* — what Darwin and Mendel between them figured out about how species change and where the change comes from. The third is *the primate fossil record* — what the bones we have actually tell us, where the gaps are, and how a creature that once swung in trees ended up walking across wet volcanic ash leaving prints we can read three and a half million years later.

## 2. Concept 1 — What is a species, and why is the answer hard?

### The Linnaean machine, and what it gets right

In 1735 a Swedish botanist named Carl Linnaeus published a slim book called *Systema Naturae* in which he proposed a system for naming every living thing. The system was simple. Two Latin names per organism. The first — capitalized, italicized — was the *genus*: a group of closely related species. The second — lowercased, italicized — was the *species*: a particular kind within that genus. The house cat is *Felis catus*. The modern human is *Homo sapiens*. The bonobo is *Pan paniscus*. The chimpanzee is *Pan troglodytes*.

This is **binomial nomenclature**. It has held up for nearly three hundred years because it does two jobs cleanly. First, it gives science a shared vocabulary that crosses languages — a *Felis catus* in a Japanese journal is the same animal as a *Felis catus* in a Brazilian journal, no translation required. Second, the genus name signals close relatedness. If two organisms share a genus, they are evolutionary siblings. If they share only a family or order, they are cousins or distant cousins.

Linnaeus arranged organisms hierarchically. From most general to most specific: *kingdom*, *phylum*, *class*, *order*, *family*, *genus*, *species*. (Subdivisions like *subclass* and *superorder* and *tribe* came later.) Humans, in this hierarchy: kingdom *Animalia*, phylum *Chordata*, class *Mammalia*, order *Primates*, family *Hominidae*, genus *Homo*, species *sapiens*.

The hierarchy works for everything that is unambiguously alive and clearly different from everything else. Where it breaks is at the *species* level, where the careful work of distinguishing one species from a closely related one runs into the question that turns out to be much harder than it looks: *what makes one species different from another?*

### Four answers, none complete

Biology has not settled on a single definition of *species*. The discipline routinely uses four, sometimes interchangeably, and the fact that they sometimes disagree is itself informative.

**The biological species definition.** A species is a group of organisms that can interbreed and produce fertile offspring with each other but cannot do so with members of other groups. The key concept is *reproductive isolation*. A gorilla and a bonobo cannot interbreed, so they are different species. *Felis catus* (house cat) and *Felis silvestris* (European wildcat) can interbreed, but the offspring of such matings are usually less viable than the parents — different species, on this definition, but on the boundary.

The biological definition is intuitive. It also has limits. It cannot apply to *fossils*, because we cannot test fossil interbreeding. It cannot apply easily to *asexual reproduction*, where the concept of mating partners is moot. And it falls apart in *hybrid zones* where populations regularly interbreed but maintain distinct characteristics. On the island of Sulawesi, the moor macaque (*Macaca maura*) and the Tonkean macaque (*Macaca tonkeana*) have been interbreeding successfully for at least 150 years. They produce fertile offspring. Yet most primatologists treat them as separate species because the two populations remain morphologically and ecologically distinct.

**The ecological species definition.** A species is a population maintained by natural selection in a particular ecological niche. Gene flow is neither necessary nor sufficient; what matters is that selection pressures keep the populations distinct. The Sulawesi macaques fit better here. The two populations occupy different elevations, different food sources, different behavioral patterns. Selection maintains the distinction even when the gene flow does not.

**The phylogenetic species definition.** A species is a group of organisms that share a unique derived characteristic — a feature inherited from a common ancestor that no other species has. This is the workhorse definition for paleoanthropology, where you cannot test interbreeding and you usually have to work from morphology alone. If you find a population of fossil leg bones with a distinctive trait that does not appear in any other known population, you have, on this definition, a species.

The catch: how distinctive does a trait have to be? *Australopithecus africanus* and *Australopithecus afarensis* differ in skull shape, dental morphology, and pelvic geometry. Are those differences derived characteristics that mark separate species, or are they intraspecific variation within a single species? Paleoanthropologists argue about exactly these calls.

**The mate recognition species definition.** A species is a set of organisms that recognize each other as potential mates. American crickets are the canonical example: in any single habitat there can be more than thirty species of cricket, and the females of each species will only mate with males that produce that species' specific call. The mate-recognition system is the species barrier. This works beautifully for organisms with elaborate mating signals; it works less well for organisms (like most mammals) where the signals are subtler.

### Why the disagreement matters

A naive reader might conclude that biology should just pick one definition and stick with it. But each definition is doing different work for different questions. *Reproductive isolation* answers the question of how species stay separate. *Ecological niche* answers the question of why selection maintains the separation. *Derived characteristics* answer the question of how to identify species in the fossil record. *Mate recognition* answers the question of how species boundaries are enforced behaviorally.

The four definitions agree on the easy cases — humans and chimpanzees are clearly different species by all four. They diverge on the hard cases — extinct hominins, hybridizing populations, ecologically distinct but interbreeding groups — and the disagreement is the data. *When the four definitions converge on a verdict, the verdict holds. When they diverge, you have not yet finished thinking.*

This is the species concept doing the same kind of work as the wink/blink test from Chapter 3: a tool for sorting the world into useful categories, with the boundaries of the tool naming exactly the cases that need more work.

## 3. Concept 2 — How evolution works: Darwin + Mendel, woven together

### Two missing pieces, finally fitting

Charles Darwin published *On the Origin of Species* in 1859. The book proposed a mechanism — *natural selection* — by which species change over time. Darwin's mechanism was elegant and powerful. It was also, as he himself recognized, missing a piece. He could explain how *some* variants survived and reproduced more than others. He could not explain *where the variations came from* or *how they were inherited*. Inheritance, in 1859, was a black box.

In 1866, an Augustinian friar named Gregor Mendel — working in a monastery garden in what is now the Czech Republic — published the results of an eight-year experiment with pea plants. He had identified two laws of inheritance. The world ignored him for thirty-five years. When his work was rediscovered around 1900, it filled exactly the gap Darwin's mechanism had left open.

The two together — Darwin's natural selection plus Mendel's inheritance — produced what biologists call the *modern synthesis*: a unified theory of how species change and how the change gets passed on.

### The Mendelian machinery

Mendel did not have the word *gene*. He used the word *factor*. He bred pea plants for eight years, tracking traits that came in two distinct phenotypes — yellow vs. green pods, round vs. wrinkled seeds, tall vs. short plants — and asked how those traits passed from parent to offspring.

Two laws emerged.

**The law of segregation.** Each parent contributes one of two *factors* (we now call them *alleles*) for each trait. The two alleles separate (segregate) during the formation of sex cells. At fertilization, each offspring gets one allele from each parent. If the alleles are different — say, yellow from one parent and green from the other — one will typically be *dominant* and one *recessive*. The offspring will display the dominant trait, but the recessive allele is still there in their genome and can re-emerge in the next generation.

**The law of independent assortment.** Different traits are inherited independently of each other. The allele for pea color sorts separately from the allele for seed shape. Yellow-and-round, yellow-and-wrinkled, green-and-round, and green-and-wrinkled offspring appear in the predicted ratios, regardless of which combinations were in the parents.

The terminology that grew up around this work: a *gene* is a unit of heredity, a stretch of DNA that codes for a trait. An *allele* is a variant of a gene. Your *genotype* is your full set of alleles. Your *phenotype* is what those alleles produce — your eye color, your blood type, your height.

Most human traits are not Mendelian in the strict pea-plant sense. Most are *polygenic* — controlled by many genes acting together. Height, weight, skin color, intelligence — none follows Mendel's tidy 3:1 dominant-recessive ratios. Some traits *are* Mendelian: blood type (A, B, O alleles, with O recessive to both A and B), Huntington's disease, cystic fibrosis, sickle cell anemia, Tay-Sachs disease. The Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man database catalogs nearly 5,000 such single-gene traits in humans.

### Darwin's natural selection, unpacked

Darwin's mechanism has five components, all of which must hold for natural selection to operate.

1. *All organisms can produce more offspring than the food supply will sustain.* Populations would grow exponentially if nothing checked them.
2. *All organisms show variation.* No two individuals are identical; even siblings differ.
3. *There is competition for limited resources.* Not all variants survive long enough to reproduce.
4. *Variations are heritable.* The traits that helped one individual survive are passed to its offspring.
5. *Small inherited changes accumulate over many generations into large differences.*

The phrase *survival of the fittest* — coined not by Darwin but by the philosopher Herbert Spencer — is widely misunderstood. *Fittest* in evolutionary terms does not mean strongest, fastest, or smartest. It means *best matched to the current environment*. A peppered moth in a soot-darkened English city in 1850 was *fitter* if dark, because dark moths were less visible to predators against soot-stained tree bark. The same dark coloration in a clean rural environment fifty years earlier would have been *less* fit — easier to spot against pale bark. Fitness is contextual, not absolute.

Spencer's misuse of Darwin's mechanism produced *social Darwinism* — the idea that *biological* evolutionary fitness translates into *social* fitness, which translated, in late-nineteenth-century European thought, into the rationalization of colonialism, racial hierarchy, and class inequality. Chapter 1 already noted this; the discipline has been clear about it for over a century. Darwin's biology does not say what social Darwinism claimed. The two should not be conflated.

### The four mechanisms of evolutionary change

Natural selection is one of four mechanisms by which the genetic composition of a population changes across generations.

**Mutation** is the only mechanism that *creates* genuinely new genetic material. A mutation is a random change in a gene's DNA sequence — sometimes a single base substitution, sometimes a larger structural change. Mutations are mostly neutral (no effect), sometimes harmful, occasionally beneficial. The probability that any specific gene mutates in any specific individual is roughly one in 10,000 to one in 100,000. The probability that *some* gene in *some* individual mutates is much higher — mutations are rare per locus per individual, but common per genome per generation.

Mutations only matter evolutionarily if they occur in *sex cells* (sperm and ova), because only those mutations are heritable. A mutation in your liver cells affects you but does not pass to your children.

**Natural selection** is what Darwin described. It does not create new traits; it selects from among the variants mutation has already produced. The mosquitoes carrying the genetic variant that confers insecticide resistance did not mutate *because* of the insecticide. The variant existed at low frequency in the population before the spraying began. Spraying killed the susceptible variants and left the resistant ones to reproduce. Within a few generations, the resistant variant dominated the population — *not because the spraying caused the mutation*, but because the spraying caused the selection.

**Gene flow** is the movement of alleles between populations through migration and interbreeding. When a hominin population in East Africa mixed with a hominin population in West Asia 60,000 years ago, alleles flowed in both directions. Modern non-African human genomes carry roughly 1–4% Neanderthal DNA, the residue of such gene flow during the late Pleistocene.

**Genetic drift** is random change in allele frequencies, especially powerful in small populations. If a small group of ten individuals migrates from a larger population to colonize a new region, the genetic composition of those ten is unlikely to perfectly mirror the source population — by chance alone, some alleles will be over-represented and others lost. The smaller the population, the larger the drift effect. Early hominin populations were small. Drift mattered a lot.

### Speciation — when populations diverge

When the four mechanisms operate on populations that have become reproductively isolated from each other, the populations diverge. Eventually they may become different species.

The most common pattern is **allopatric speciation** — *allo-* (other) + *patric* (place). Two populations of the same species are separated by a geographic barrier (a river, a mountain range, an ocean, a canyon). Each population accumulates its own mutations, experiences its own selection pressures, drifts in its own direction. Given enough time, the two populations diverge enough that even if the geographic barrier is removed, they no longer interbreed. Speciation has occurred. The squirrels on the two rims of the Grand Canyon — *Sciurus aberti* on the south, *Sciurus kaibabensis* on the north — are a textbook case.

Other patterns exist. **Sympatric speciation** is divergence without geographic separation, often driven by ecological or behavioral isolation within the same range. The cichlid fish of Lake Tanganyika in East Africa — over 250 species, all derived from a relatively small number of ancestral species in less than 10 million years — have undergone an *adaptive radiation* in which sympatric speciation, driven partly by female mate selection on male coloration, played a major role.

### Gradual or punctuated?

For a century after Darwin, the dominant assumption was that evolution proceeded *gradually* — small changes accumulating slowly over many generations. The fossil record was expected to show smooth transitions. It mostly did not. Most fossil sequences show long stretches where species are morphologically stable, punctuated by relatively brief periods of rapid change.

In 1972 Niles Eldredge and Stephen Jay Gould proposed that this pattern — long stasis interrupted by short bursts of change — was the *real* signal, not an artifact of an incomplete fossil record. They called it **punctuated equilibrium**. The model is not anti-Darwinian. It is a claim about the *tempo* of natural selection: long periods when stabilizing selection holds populations roughly constant, punctuated by environmental changes or geographic separations that drive rapid divergence.

The contemporary view: both gradualism and punctuated equilibrium operate, in different cases and at different scales. One published estimate suggests roughly 30–35% of speciation events show clear punctuated-equilibrium patterns; the rest show gradual change. The argument over which is more *common* is less productive than the argument over which model fits a *specific* case.

## 4. Concept 3 — Primates, hominins, and the long walk

### What makes a primate

Around 65 million years ago, in the early Cenozoic era, a small mammal we have come to call *Purgatorius* was scrambling through the forests of what is now Montana. *Purgatorius* had two features that, together, suggest it was on its way to becoming the kind of animal we would later call a primate: an elongated last lower molar and an enlarged upper central incisor. The teeth are not enough to make it definitely a primate, but they are enough to make it the best candidate we have for an ancestral form.

Modern primates — the order *Primates* — share a cluster of characteristics that distinguish them from other mammals.

- *Opposable thumbs* (and, in non-human primates, opposable big toes), giving precise grip.
- *Five digits* on each appendage, with *flat nails* instead of curved claws.
- *Pads at the fingertips*, dense with nerves and fat, providing fine tactile sensitivity.
- *Reduced reliance on smell*; relatively short snouts.
- *Forward-facing eyes* with overlapping visual fields, producing *binocular* vision and *depth perception*.
- *Postorbital bars* — bony rings that fully surround the eye sockets — which appear in early primates and stabilize the eye for fine vision.
- *Relatively large brains* in proportion to body size.
- *Slow reproductive rates*; few offspring at a time, with long juvenile dependency.

Two big ideas about why primates evolved this cluster of traits.

**Arboreal theory** argues that life in the trees selected for grasping hands and depth perception. A primate that can grip a branch and accurately judge the distance to the next branch survives; one that cannot, falls.

**Visual predation hypothesis** (proposed by Matt Cartmill) notes that forward-facing eyes are characteristic of *predators* — owls, cats, eagles — not of generic tree-dwellers. The grasping hands and forward vision may have evolved for stalking and seizing small prey on shrubs and low canopy, rather than for arboreal locomotion as such.

**Angiosperm theory** notes that the rise of flowering plants — *angiosperms* — coincided roughly with primate origins, and the primate cluster of traits is well suited to manipulating fruits, seeds, and small flowers. Primates may have co-evolved with the flowering plants whose fruits they consume.

These hypotheses are not mutually exclusive. Different primates may have started for different reasons, and the trait cluster may have multiple origins.

### The two suborders

The primate order divides into two suborders.

**Strepsirrhini** — lemurs, lorises, galagos. These are sometimes called *prosimians*. They have a *toothcomb* (lower incisors angled forward and pressed close together, used for grooming), a *grooming claw* on the second toe, and rhinariums (wet noses, like a dog's). Most are nocturnal. All living strepsirrhines are found in the Old World (Africa, Asia, Madagascar).

**Haplorrhini** — tarsiers, monkeys, apes, humans. These have *dry noses*, generally larger brains than strepsirrhines, and are mostly diurnal. The haplorrhines further split into two infraorders: *Tarsiiformes* (tarsiers, an oddball group with prosimian-like traits but molecular evidence placing them with the haplorrhines) and *Simiiformes*, which includes everything else.

Within Simiiformes, two main groups: **Platyrrhini** (the New World monkeys — Central and South America — recognizable by flat-facing, sideways-opening nostrils, and often by prehensile tails) and **Catarrhini** (Old World monkeys, apes, and humans — narrow downward-opening nostrils, no prehensile tails). Catarrhini divides further into the Old World monkeys (*Cercopithecoidea*) and the apes (*Hominoidea*) — gibbons, orangutans, gorillas, chimpanzees, bonobos, humans, and the extinct ape lineages.

The naming is dense, but the underlying logic is clean: each level of the hierarchy reflects a divergence in the evolutionary tree, and the deeper the divergence, the higher the taxonomic level at which the groups separate.

### From hominoid to hominid to hominin

The terminology around human evolution has been revised in the past few decades, and it can confuse readers of older sources. The current consensus:

**Hominoidea** is the superfamily — all apes plus humans, modern and extinct.
**Hominidae** (hominids) is the family — great apes and humans, modern and extinct: orangutans, gorillas, chimpanzees, bonobos, humans, and their close fossil ancestors.
**Hominini** (hominins) is the tribe — humans, modern and extinct, plus extinct ancestors *after* the divergence from the chimpanzee lineage. This is the lineage that includes *Homo*, *Australopithecus*, *Paranthropus*, and *Ardipithecus*.

So: every hominin is a hominid, every hominid is a hominoid, but not vice versa. The chimpanzee is a hominoid and a hominid but not a hominin.

### Bipedalism — the trait that defines hominins

What distinguishes the hominin lineage from the rest of the great apes is *habitual upright bipedal locomotion*. We walk on two legs, not as an occasional posture but as our primary mode of moving across the landscape.

Bipedalism is hard. The body has to balance on one leg for most of each stride. The skeleton has to redirect weight from a horizontal to a vertical configuration. Several anatomical changes were required.

- The **foramen magnum** — the opening at the base of the skull where the spinal column enters — moved from the back of the skull (as in quadrupeds) to the bottom (so the head sits balanced on top of the spine). Paleoanthropologists use foramen magnum position as a diagnostic for bipedalism in fossil specimens.
- The **femur** (thigh bone) developed a *valgus angle*, angling inward toward the knee so that the knees and feet pass under the body's center of mass during walking.
- The **pelvis** became shorter and broader, redirecting forces from the spine through the hip joint efficiently.
- The **spine** developed S-shaped curves that absorb shock and balance upper body weight.
- The **foot** developed an arch that springs back during the step phase, and the *big toe* realigned parallel to the other toes (rather than being opposable as in apes).

Why did bipedalism evolve? The hypotheses are many — to free hands for carrying tools or food, to expose less body to direct sun (thermoregulation), to see over tall grass, to cover ground efficiently. None is fully testable; multiple causes likely operated together. What we can observe in the fossil record is that bipedalism appears early in the hominin lineage — well before brain expansion, before stone tools, before nearly every other trait we associate with humans.

### The early hominin record

The fossil hominin record is sparse — maybe 6,000 primate species ever existed and we have specimens from perhaps 3% of them. What we do have, between 7 million years ago and 2 million years ago, sketches a story.

***Sahelanthropus tchadensis*** — found in Chad in 2002, dated roughly 7 million years ago. A nearly-complete cranium with a chimpanzee-sized brain (about 350 cubic centimeters) and a foramen magnum position that is *intermediate* between quadrupeds and bipeds. Whether *Sahelanthropus* was bipedal is contested. Whether it is a hominin or a chimp-line ancestor is contested. What it represents is the lineage at or near the human/chimpanzee divergence point — which genetic evidence places between 5 and 7 million years ago.

***Orrorin tugenensis*** — found in Kenya in 2001, dated to about 6 million years ago. The specimen includes a femur whose *femoral head* (the ball at the top of the thigh bone) is much larger than in quadrupeds, suggesting it bore the weight of upright locomotion. Probably bipedal. Probably hominin.

***Ardipithecus ramidus*** — found in Ethiopia, described by Tim White's team in 1994, dated to about 4.4 million years ago. Bipedal on the ground (forward-positioned foramen magnum), but with an opposable big toe, suggesting it was also still climbing in trees. The transitional nature of *Ardipithecus* — neither a chimp nor a modern human, but visibly part of the journey — is what makes it valuable.

***Australopithecus afarensis*** — Lucy's species. The Laetoli footprints. Found across East Africa. Dated 3.9 to 2.9 million years ago. Cranial capacity around 400 cc (chimp-sized brain). Long arms, curved finger and toe bones (still capable of arboreal life). But the pelvis and lower limbs are unmistakably bipedal. *A. afarensis* did both — climbed trees, walked the ground.

***Australopithecus africanus*** — first described by Raymond Dart in 1924 from the *Taung skull*, a juvenile cranium recovered from a lime quarry in South Africa. Bipedal. Brain about 450 cc. Slightly more modern facial features than *afarensis*.

***Paranthropus*** — a robust line that diverged from the *Australopithecus* gracile line around 2.5 million years ago. *P. robustus* in South Africa, *P. boisei* in East Africa, *P. aethiopicus* (the "black skull") between them. Massive jaws. *Sagittal crests* on the skull (ridges that anchored huge chewing muscles). A diet of tough plant material, possibly nuts and tubers. The *Paranthropus* lineage went extinct roughly 1 million years ago without descendants.

***Australopithecus garhi*** — Ethiopia, about 2.5 million years ago. Found near butchered animal bones. The earliest fossil hominin associated with stone tool use, though the tools themselves are not yet attributed to a specific maker.

***Australopithecus sediba*** — South Africa, about 2 million years ago. Discovered in 2008 by a team that included nine-year-old Matthew Berger, the son of the lead paleontologist Lee Berger. A confusing mosaic of features — some australopithecine, some pointing toward early *Homo*. Possibly transitional.

The picture is messy. There were probably several hominin species coexisting on the African landscape between 4 and 2 million years ago, and the lineage that produced modern humans is one branch among several. *Paranthropus* went extinct. *A. sediba* may or may not be on the line to *Homo*. The next chapter — *The Genus Homo* — picks up the story where this one ends.

## 5. Integration — Reading the deep past with care

The chapter has covered three strata of biological reasoning. Pull them together.

A *species* is a population that an evolutionary process has stabilized as a distinct unit. Four definitions agree on the easy cases and disagree on the hard ones. The disagreements are not failures — they show where the world is genuinely complicated.

*Evolution* is a small set of mechanisms — mutation, selection, drift, gene flow, speciation — operating on populations across generations. Darwin gave us the selection mechanism; Mendel gave us the inheritance mechanism. Together they produce a working theory.

The *primate fossil record* is sparse and getting denser. We can place living primates in a clean taxonomy. We can identify early hominins by characteristic traits — bipedalism above all. We can trace a story that begins at least 7 million years ago with the divergence from the chimpanzee lineage and runs through *Ardipithecus*, *Australopithecus*, and *Paranthropus* to the threshold of *Homo*.

What the chapter has not done — what no chapter could honestly do at this length — is settle every open question. The phylogenetic relationships among the early hominins are contested. Whether *Australopithecus sediba* is on the line to *Homo* or a side branch is contested. Whether *Sahelanthropus* was bipedal is contested. The discipline lives with these contests; it does not resolve them by appealing to authority. Each new fossil discovery — Lee Berger's team's recent finds at Rising Star Cave, the *Homo naledi* discovery in 2013, the work being done at Olduvai and at Hadar — adds evidence that either confirms, complicates, or overturns existing claims.

This is what the chapter is asking you to absorb: *the discipline's confidence in any specific claim about deep human origins should be calibrated to the density of the evidence and the convergence of independent methods*. Where multiple lines of evidence — fossils, genetics, dating, comparative anatomy — point the same direction, the claim holds up. Where the evidence is thin or conflicting, the claim is provisional. *Strong claim, strong evidence; weak evidence, hedged claim.* That is how the field works at its best.

You can read a phylogenetic tree as a working diagram of evolutionary divergence, and you can hold the *gradualism / punctuated equilibrium* tension as a productive empirical question rather than a settled doctrine.

The single idea that matters most: *the body you live in is the product of three and a half billion years of selection on inherited variation*. Chapter 5 picks up the last two million years of that long story; this chapter set up everything that came before.

The common mistake to watch for: *treating evolutionary change as directional, progressive, or aimed at producing humans*. It is none of those things. The lineage that led to us is one branch among many. The other branches — *Paranthropus*, the Neanderthals (Chapter 5), *Homo erectus* — were not "less successful" prototypes. They were their own species, doing their own evolutionary work, in their own ecological niches, for their own time on the planet.

If you can pick up a museum label that says *Sagittal crest, robust mandible, dated to 1.8 million years ago, found in East Africa* and tell yourself *this is probably Paranthropus boisei, hyper-robust line, gone extinct around a million years ago, not on the line to us*, you have absorbed the chapter's diagnostic apparatus.

## What would change my mind

If a single fossil discovery in the next decade clearly placed bipedalism in a non-hominin lineage (say, a chimpanzee-line ancestor with a pelvis indistinguishable from *Australopithecus*), the chapter's strong claim that bipedalism is the diagnostic trait of the hominin tribe would need revision. The trait would still be diagnostic of *something*, but no longer specifically of the human side of the divergence.

## Still puzzling

The exact relationship between *Australopithecus sediba* and the genus *Homo*. *Sediba* shows a mosaic of features that complicates the simple story of *afarensis → africanus → habilis → erectus*. Lee Berger's team has argued *sediba* is on the line to *Homo*; others have argued it is a side branch. The dispute is not yet settled, and I do not have a confident view about which way it will resolve.
