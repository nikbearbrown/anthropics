## Candidate 01 — Build the Proposal Problem Statement Validator with Claude (Ch 8)

- Source: writing-guide/chapters/08-proposal-writing-about-problems-and-solutions.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: Most proposals fail before the solution — they fail at the problem definition. The problem is too big, the problem is fake (no evidence it affects anyone specific), or the problem is already solved. A validator catches all three before you draft 1,500 words.
- The artifact: A CLI Python script (~30 lines) that takes a one-sentence problem statement as input and runs it through a 3-failure-mode audit: (1) too big — does the problem have more than one solution and more than one audience? (2) fake — does the statement include specific who, what consequence, and what cause, or does it gesture at evidence? (3) already solved — does Claude know of existing major interventions addressing this exact problem? Outputs a PASS/WARN/FAIL per failure mode with the diagnostic sentence from ch08: "Right now, [specific group] is experiencing [specific consequence] because of [specific cause]."
- Prompt seed: `claude "Audit the following research proposal problem statement for the three classic failure modes: (1) TOO BIG: does this problem have a single identifiable audience and a plausible single-intervention solution, or does it sprawl across multiple audiences and solutions? (2) FAKE: does the statement name a specific group, specific consequence, and specific cause — or does it use vague gestures like 'many people', 'experts agree', 'it is well known'? (3) ALREADY SOLVED: describe any major existing interventions addressing this exact problem (government programs, established nonprofits, legislation). For each failure mode, rate: PASS/WARN/FAIL with one sentence explanation. Also rewrite the problem statement into the form: 'Right now, [specific group] is experiencing [specific consequence] because of [specific cause].' Problem statement: [INPUT]"`
- Read / check: Verify the three failure modes match ch08 exactly (too big, fake problem, already solved — these are the chapter's own categories); confirm the diagnostic sentence rewrite produces a concrete, bracket-filled result (not just the template with brackets still in it); check that the "already solved" check is flagged as requiring human verification (Claude may not know recent local programs).
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Knowledge of recent local or disciplinary-specific programs — Claude's knowledge of existing interventions is broad but may miss local government programs or recent legislation. Human must verify the "already solved" check against current sources. For the video, use a synthetic problem statement with one of each failure mode to demonstrate all three.
- Output medium: screen-recording mp4 (screen-rec of terminal: problem statement input, then 3-mode audit output printing — FAIL highlighted red for the failure mode being demonstrated, PASS in green for the others, then the rewritten diagnostic sentence appearing)
- The change: After the audit, feed the failing problem statement to a second prompt: "Given this problem statement just failed [MODE], narrow it until it passes. Propose 3 narrower versions of the problem that are proposal-scale." Show the narrowing process on screen.
- Teardown angle: The proposal that begins with climate change as its problem has nowhere to land. Narrowness is not a failure of ambition — it is the condition that makes a proposal possible. The validator catches the sprawl before it becomes 1,500 words of promising nothing.
- Exclusions: Full proposal structure (solution section, feasibility analysis, obstacles); grant writing conventions; RFP-specific requirements; the Pronovost central-line example beyond problem definition.
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 02 — Build the Rhetorical Analysis Appeal Scanner with Claude (Ch 11)

- Source: writing-guide/chapters/11-rhetorical-analysis-interpreting-the-art-of-rhetoric.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: Aristotle named three modes of persuasive appeal 2,400 years ago and they still map something real about how persuasion works. Ethos: the projected credibility. Logos: the logical reasoning with evidence. Pathos: the emotional register. But most students can name the appeals without seeing them in actual text. A scanner makes them visible.
- The artifact: A CLI Python script (~30 lines) that takes a short persuasive text (op-ed, editorial, speech excerpt — 200-500 words) and produces a 4-appeal annotation: for each sentence or passage, Claude tags it as primarily ETHOS / LOGOS / PATHOS / KAIROS (the fourth appeal — timeliness), with a brief explanation of the move. Output: an annotated version of the text with color-coded tags.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Analyze the following persuasive text for rhetorical appeals. For each substantive sentence or passage: (1) identify the primary appeal being made: ETHOS (speaker credibility/character), LOGOS (logic/evidence/statistics), PATHOS (emotion/values/identity), or KAIROS (appeal to timeliness/urgency/this-specific-moment); (2) explain in one phrase what specific move is being made (e.g., 'establishing expertise through named authority', 'using statistics to imply scale', 'evoking shared loss'). Return JSON: [{text: string (the passage), appeal: string, move: string}]. Do not evaluate whether the argument is correct — analyze how it works. Text: [INPUT]"`
- Read / check: Verify the JSON output is well-formed; confirm the appeal tags are consistent with ch11's definitions (ethos = projected character, not actual credentials; logos = reasoning with evidence, not just statistics; pathos = emotional register, not just emotional words); check that KAIROS appears when the text explicitly invokes timeliness (ch11 identifies this as a fourth appeal beyond Aristotle's three).
- Human supplies (Claude can't): A short persuasive text to analyze — the human must supply a real op-ed or editorial. For the video, Jamil Smith's "The Revolutionary Power of Black Panther" (referenced in ch11) is the ideal choice; the first 3 paragraphs demonstrate all 4 appeals. If copyright is a concern, a public-domain speech excerpt works.
- Output medium: Manim (animated text annotation: the passage appears, then each sentence gets tagged with its appeal label in color — ETHOS=blue, LOGOS=green, PATHOS=red, KAIROS=gold — tags appear one at a time with the move explanation)
- The change: After the appeal scan, ask Claude to identify: where is the argument's weakest appeal (the one that is least well-developed or most overused)? And what would a single additional move look like to strengthen it? This produces the analysis → prescription transition.
- Teardown angle: Reading a text not for what it says but for how it was made to work — that is the discipline the genre requires. The appeal scanner makes the seams visible. A reader who agrees with the argument and one who disagrees should produce substantially the same annotation.
- Exclusions: Full Aristotelian rhetoric history; Roman rhetoric (Cicero, Quintilian); contemporary critical discourse analysis; multimodal rhetoric (images, video); the genre conventions of specific persuasive forms (political speech vs. op-ed vs. advertisement).
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 03 — Build the Annotated Bibliography Generator with Claude (Ch 16)

- Source: writing-guide/chapters/16-annotated-bibliography-gathering-evaluating-and-documenting-sources.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: Citation 9 goes in because the title sounds right and there's a PDF on the desktop. The annotated bibliography requires you to prove — annotation by annotation — that you actually read what you cited. And the act of writing the annotations often changes the argument. Claude can help draft the summary, but the evaluation and the relevance judgment are yours.
- The artifact: A CLI Python script (~25 lines) that takes a list of source URLs or citation info (title, author, year, a pasted abstract) and generates a 3-part annotation stub for each: (1) a 1-sentence summary of what the source argues (Claude drafts from abstract), (2) a credibility evaluation template (flagging publication type, author credentials, peer-review status — with blanks for the human to complete), (3) a relevance connection prompt asking the human: "How does this source connect to your specific thesis?" Output: a structured annotation scaffold.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Generate an annotated bibliography entry scaffold for the following source. The annotation must have three parts: (1) SUMMARY: one to two sentences describing specifically what this source argues — not the topic, the actual argument or finding. Draft from the abstract provided. (2) EVALUATION: assess the source's credibility using these categories: publication_type (peer-reviewed/research-org/journalism/other), author_credentials (named expert/journalist/anonymous), methodology_visible (yes/implied/absent), publication_date relevance. Flag any credibility concern. (3) RELEVANCE: [LEAVE BLANK — human must complete] 'This source connects to my argument because ___.' Output as formatted text, not JSON. Source: [CITATION INFO + ABSTRACT]"`
- Read / check: Verify the summary is 1-2 sentences and captures the source's actual argument (not just its topic); confirm the credibility evaluation uses the ch16 framework (summary + evaluation + relevance — the three required components); check that the RELEVANCE section is genuinely left blank with the completion prompt — Claude should not invent relevance claims that only the human can make.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): The actual source to read — Claude generates the scaffold from the abstract but the human must read the full source to complete the evaluation and relevance sections. This is the point: the annotation scaffold reduces overhead but preserves the judgment. The human cannot delegate the reading.
- Output medium: screen-recording mp4 (screen-rec of terminal: citation + abstract pasted in, annotation scaffold printing — SUMMARY filled by Claude, EVALUATION with flags noted, RELEVANCE section blank with the completion prompt — the blank is the pedagogical moment)
- The change: Feed 5 sources through the scaffold generator simultaneously, then ask Claude to identify: do these 5 sources all agree with each other, or do any of them complicate the same claim? This produces the "bibliography that never changes its mind" audit — flagging if all sources are too aligned.
- Teardown angle: The annotation does three things, not one — summary, evaluation, relevance. The student who writes the annotations often ends up with a better thesis than the one they started with. This is the assignment working exactly as intended. Claude can scaffold the summary; only you can complete the evaluation.
- Exclusions: Citation format management (APA, MLA, Chicago — handled by Zotero etc.); database access for full-text retrieval; disciplinary-specific annotation conventions (law review, medicine).
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 04 — Build the Argumentative Research Thesis Sharpener with Claude (Ch 14)

- Source: writing-guide/chapters/14-argumentative-research-enhancing-the-art-of-rhetoric-with-evidence.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: The thesis for argumentative research has one additional requirement beyond the position argument: there must be contested ground around it in the existing literature. If everyone already agrees with your thesis, you are restating a consensus, not advancing an argument. The sharpener checks for contested ground and tests whether the claim commits to anything falsifiable.
- The artifact: A CLI Python script (~30 lines) that takes a draft thesis statement and runs a 3-step evaluation: (1) consensus check — does Claude know whether this is already a settled position in the relevant scholarly conversation, or does active debate exist around it? (2) falsifiability check — is the claim specific enough that evidence could prove it wrong? (3) specificity check — does the thesis commit to a specific claim about a specific evidence base, or is it a floating claim that sounds like it engages the literature but doesn't? Output: a 3-criterion scorecard with a recommended revision.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Evaluate the following thesis statement for argumentative research writing on three criteria: (1) CONTESTED GROUND: is this a position that experts in the relevant field actively debate, or is it already a consensus finding? If consensus, flag as 'RESTATES CONSENSUS'. If contested, name the main scholarly positions that disagree with or complicate this thesis. (2) FALSIFIABILITY: what specific evidence would, if found, prove this thesis wrong? If you cannot name specific falsifying evidence, flag as 'TOO VAGUE TO FALSIFY'. (3) EVIDENCE COMMITMENT: does the thesis name a specific evidence base (specific dataset, study type, period, population) or does it float without evidence commitment? Recommend one revision that improves the weakest criterion. Thesis: [INPUT]"`
- Read / check: Verify the consensus check is disciplinarily sensitive (what is contested in food systems policy may be consensus in nutritional epidemiology — Claude should name the relevant field); confirm the falsifiability check asks for specific falsifying evidence (not just "evidence that contradicts it" — that's too easy); check that the recommended revision actually improves the weakest criterion rather than replacing the thesis entirely.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Knowledge of the specific scholarly conversation — Claude's sense of "contested vs. consensus" depends on its training data and may be incomplete for narrow disciplinary debates or very recent findings. Human must verify the contested-ground assessment against the actual literature. For the video, use the EAT-Lancet Commission thesis from ch14 as the worked example.
- Output medium: screen-recording mp4 (screen-rec of terminal: thesis input, then 3-criterion scorecard printing — contested ground assessment, falsifiability check, evidence commitment assessment — then the recommended revision appearing last)
- The change: Take the recommended revised thesis and ask Claude to produce a 3-bullet "scholarly conversation map": who are the 3 most important voices in the debate on this thesis, and what position does each hold? This connects the thesis directly to the ch14 "joining a conversation" framework.
- Teardown angle: If everyone in the relevant scholarly conversation already agrees on your thesis, you are restating a consensus, not advancing an argument. The thesis must occupy contested ground. The sharpener makes the ground visible before you draft 3,000 words defending it.
- Exclusions: Full literature review methodology; the difference between argumentative research and empirical research design; citation management; disciplinary argument conventions (STEM vs. humanities).
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 05 — Build the Personal Narrative Hinge-Scene Finder with Claude (Ch 6)

- Source: writing-guide/chapters/06-memoir-or-personal-narrative-learning-lessons-from-the-personal.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: Every personal narrative needs a hinge — the single moment when something changed. Most students write around the hinge, circling the experience without landing on the specific moment that transforms everything. Claude can ask the questions that find it.
- The artifact: A CLI Python interactive session (~20 lines) where Claude asks 5 targeted questions one at a time, pushing the writer to make fragmentary memories more specific: "What did you see?", "What did someone say?", "What did you do next that you wouldn't have done the week before?" After 5 answers, Claude identifies which specific moment has the strongest claim to being the hinge — the single moment when something changed — with a 2-sentence explanation. Claude does not write any prose; it finds the moment.
- Prompt seed: `claude "I am writing a personal narrative for a college composition class. I am NOT asking you to write the narrative — I am asking you to help me find the hinge moment (the single moment when something changed). Here is my topic: [TOPIC]. Here are 5 fragmentary memories: [LIST]. Ask me one question at a time (5 total) that pushes me to make these memories more specific — more concrete detail, more sensory, more precise about what changed. After my 5 answers, identify which specific moment has the strongest claim to being the hinge and explain in 2-3 sentences why that moment, not the others. Do not write any prose for the essay. Your job is to help me see my own material."`
- Read / check: Verify Claude asks questions one at a time (not all 5 at once — confirm the interactive structure); check that the questions push for specificity (sensory detail, what changed, who was present) not for more narrative summary; confirm the hinge identification is 2-3 sentences explaining WHY this moment, not just naming it.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): The actual memories — the human must bring real material. For the video, use a concrete topic (learning to drive, first day at a new school, a competition that went wrong) with 5 deliberately vague memory fragments to demonstrate how Claude's questions make them specific.
- Output medium: screen-recording mp4 (screen-rec of interactive terminal session: topic and memories input, then Claude's 5 questions appearing one at a time with the human's typed responses, then the hinge identification appearing at the end — a genuine interactive session recorded)
- The change: After the hinge is identified, ask Claude to name 3 things the writer should include in the scene to make the hinge moment concrete for a reader who wasn't there — not what to write, but what material to gather (sensory details, dialogue, a specific object). The difference between "ask questions" and "find material" is the creative discipline.
- Teardown angle: The hinge is not the story's climax. It is the moment of transformation — before which the writer was one person, after which a different person. Claude cannot find it by reading around the topic. It finds it by asking the questions that make the writer see the specific moment they were circling.
- Exclusions: Full memoir/personal narrative structure beyond the hinge; creative nonfiction craft (scene vs. summary, interiority, narrative distance); the literacy narrative subgenre (Ch 4); publication conventions for personal essays.
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 06 — Build the Source Credibility Hierarchy Ranker with Claude (Ch 15)

- Source: writing-guide/chapters/15-research-process-accessing-and-recording-information.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: A citation that does not change what the paragraph is arguing is decorative. Decorative citations are one of the clearest signals that a student hasn't understood the genre. The source hierarchy makes the difference between a source that does work and one that is furniture.
- The artifact: A CLI Python script (~25 lines) that takes a list of citations from a research paper and ranks them by the 4-tier hierarchy: (1) peer-reviewed journal article (strongest for empirical claims), (2) named research organization report (strong secondary), (3) major journalism with named authors (context and framing), (4) Wikipedia / anonymous / LLM output (not a source — flag as lead requiring verification). For each citation: tier assignment, one-sentence explanation, and a flag if it is the only source supporting a key claim (single-source dependency risk).
- Prompt seed: `claude "Rank the following citations by their credibility tier for use in argumentative research writing. Tiers: TIER 1 = peer-reviewed journal articles in field-appropriate journals; TIER 2 = named research organization reports (National Academies, RAND, Pew, government agencies) with named contributors; TIER 3 = major journalism (NYT, Atlantic, Guardian, WSJ) with named authors; TIER 0 = Wikipedia, anonymous sources, LLM outputs — not a source, treat as a lead requiring verification. For each citation: {citation, tier, reason (one sentence), single_source_risk: true if this is the only citation supporting a named key claim}. Citations and claimed key points: [LIST]"`
- Read / check: Verify the tier assignments are consistent with ch15's hierarchy (peer-reviewed > research-org > journalism > anonymous — this matches exactly); confirm the single_source_risk flag is meaningful (if the same key claim is supported by 3 TIER 1 sources, risk = false); check that Wikipedia is correctly flagged as TIER 0 with the explanation "treat as a lead, not a source."
- Human supplies (Claude can't): A real citation list from a paper in progress — human must supply. For the video, a synthetic list of 8 citations including 1 Wikipedia, 2 peer-reviewed, 2 journalism, 1 government report, 1 blog post, and 1 LLM-generated "citation" demonstrates the full range.
- Output medium: screen-recording mp4 (screen-rec of terminal: citation list input, then tier-ranked output printing — TIER 1 in blue, TIER 3 in yellow, TIER 0 in red with the "not a source" flag — the single-source risk flags appearing in orange)
- The change: After ranking, ask Claude to suggest TIER 1 replacements for any TIER 3 or TIER 0 sources that carry key claims — showing the upgrade workflow from "this sounds right" to "this is checkable."
- Teardown angle: The mix matters. Eight peer-reviewed articles with no journalism reads as disconnected from current discourse. Eight magazine articles with no peer-reviewed work reads as unsubstantial. The ranking is not an argument for academic snobbery — it is a map of which sources do which kind of work in an argument.
- Exclusions: Citation format enforcement (APA/MLA); database navigation (JSTOR, PubMed, Scopus); interlibrary loan workflows; disciplinary-specific source hierarchies (legal citation, clinical evidence).
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 07 — Research the EAT-Lancet Scholarly Conversation with Claude (Ch 14)

- Source: writing-guide/chapters/14-argumentative-research-enhancing-the-art-of-rhetoric-with-evidence.md
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: The EAT-Lancet Commission didn't discover that food production has environmental consequences. What it did was synthesize the existing conversation — decades of dispersed evidence into a single coherent argument. But the scholarly conversation the Commission joined is itself a multi-decade argument among people who don't agree. How do you map a conversation that is genuinely open?
- The artifact: A sourced scholarly conversation map for the food-systems sustainability debate: (1) the major disciplinary nodes (agronomists, climate scientists, nutritionists, economists, ethicists, anthropologists), (2) the 3 most important disagreements in the conversation, (3) where the EAT-Lancet Commission sits relative to those disagreements, (4) what an "entry-level" student paper thesis could argue that adds something not already covered. Formatted as a Manim web diagram with animated discipline-nodes and connecting disagreement arrows.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Map the scholarly conversation on food-systems sustainability for a student writer joining it for the first time. (1) Name 6 disciplinary nodes in the conversation and for each: a representative researcher or institution and one sentence on their main concern. (2) Identify the 3 most significant disagreements in the current conversation — where do disciplinary perspectives clash most directly? (3) Place the EAT-Lancet Commission (Willett et al. 2019, The Lancet) in the conversation: which disciplinary nodes does it engage, which does it underweight? (4) Propose one specific sub-conversation a student writer could enter: a thesis statement that occupies contested ground and is supportable with 8-10 sources. ~600 words with named sources."`
- Read / check: Verify that named researchers are real and appropriately affiliated (Wageningen University agronomists, IPCC climate scientists, WHO nutritionists); confirm the EAT-Lancet placement is accurate — it engages climate + nutrition + economics but underweights cultural anthropology (this is a documented criticism); check that the proposed thesis occupies genuinely contested ground.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Access to the EAT-Lancet Commission report for direct quotation — Claude can paraphrase accurately but the human should verify specific statistics against the actual 2019 Lancet publication before citing them.
- Output medium: Manim (web diagram animation: 6 discipline nodes appear as labeled circles, then connecting lines draw between nodes that disagree, EAT-Lancet appears as a central node with arrows showing which nodes it engages and which it misses, then the student's entry point highlighted as a gap)
- The change: Ask Claude to name 2 researchers who have published responses or critiques of the EAT-Lancet report specifically — showing that the conversation continued after the report and giving the student a "reply" thread to join.
- Teardown angle: To enter a scholarly conversation, you must find the specific sub-conversation that addresses your question — not the whole literature. You must read enough to see the picture undistorted. And you must position yourself honestly: as a new participant whose contribution will be modest. The map makes those moves possible.
- Exclusions: Full EAT-Lancet methodology (planetary boundary modeling); the rebound effect debate (too narrow for a mapping exercise); LCA (life cycle assessment) methodology for food systems; the politics of dietary guidelines.
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 08 — Build the Objective Stance Translator with Claude (Ch 8)

- Source: writing-guide/chapters/08-proposal-writing-about-problems-and-solutions.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: "This policy is destroying lives" is emotional. "This policy increased the rate of [specific consequence] by 23 percent in the affected population" does the same work with more persuasive force for a skeptical reader. The objective stance is not the absence of voice — it is a specific kind of voice calibrated for the unconvinced.
- The artifact: A CLI Python script (~20 lines) that takes 5 emotional/subjective sentences from a draft proposal and translates each to the evidence-anchored equivalent: the emotional language identified, the specific evidence it was reaching for named (even if placeholder), and the rewrite in objective stance. Output: a side-by-side before/after table.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Translate the following emotionally-framed sentences from a research proposal into objective-stance equivalents. For each sentence: (1) identify the emotional move being made; (2) name the specific evidence that would do the same rhetorical work — even as a placeholder (e.g., 'a peer-reviewed study showing X%' or 'government data from [agency]'); (3) rewrite the sentence in objective stance — measured, evidence-anchored, naming the specific actor who should do what. Do not erase the writer's conviction — translate it into persuasive force that works on a skeptical reader. Output as a table: Original | Emotional move | Evidence needed | Rewrite. Sentences: [LIST]"`
- Read / check: Verify the rewrites stay true to the original claim's direction (do not weaken the proposal's position — strengthen the argument's form); confirm the "evidence needed" column is specific (not "some evidence" but "a peer-reviewed study in [field] showing [specific type of finding]"); check that the rewrites use active voice directed at a specific decision-maker audience.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): A real draft proposal with emotionally-charged language — human must supply 5 sentences from their actual draft. For the video, use the ch08 worked examples: "This policy is destroying lives" → "This policy increased X by 23% (citation)" as the demonstration sentence.
- Output medium: Manim (side-by-side table animating: original sentence appears in red, then emotional move labeled, then evidence needed labeled in yellow, then rewrite appears in green — the translation visible as a color transformation)
- The change: After the translation, ask Claude to identify: which of the 5 rewrites still lacks the specific evidence it needs (evidence_needed is a placeholder, not a real citation)? Flag those as the human's next research tasks — making the translation into a to-do list.
- Teardown angle: The objective stance is not the absence of conviction. It is conviction expressed in the form that works on a skeptical reader. The proposal that names the strongest objection and addresses it earns trust. The proposal that only preaches to the converted has failed.
- Exclusions: Full proposal structure; grant writing conventions; counterargument strategy; the specific audience conventions of academic vs. policy vs. business proposals.
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 09 — Research the Pronovost Checklist: Why Structure Saves Lives with Claude (Ch 8)

What does the research say about when checklists work and when they don't?

Formatted as a Manim 3-panel slate.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Research the Pronovost central-line checklist study and the broader literature on checklist effectiveness in high-stakes professional work. (1) Describe the Pronovost study: publication (NEJM), methodology (ICU protocol, nurse authority to stop), key findings (35% infection reduction), and why 5 items was the right scope. (2) Research the boundaries of checklist effectiveness: where does the literature show checklists work consistently (aviation, surgery, anesthesia)? Where do they fail or backfire (creative tasks, complex diagnosis, non-routine situations)? Cite specific studies. - Read / check: Verify the Pronovost study publication is correctly identified (the original was in "Critical Care Medicine" 2006, not NEJM — Gawande's essay in the New Yorker popularized it, and "The Checklist Manifesto" is the 2009 book; the ch08 footnote identifies the book and New Yorker piece); confirm the aviation/surgery examples are accurate (WHO Surgical Safety Checklist, airline crew resource management); check that the "where checklists fail" literature is real (Carayon et al. on checklist over-reliance in healthcare).
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Access to the original Pronovost study — the New Yorker article and "The Checklist Manifesto" are the popularizations. For a research paper context, the human should trace the claim to the original publication (Critical Care Medicine, or the Michigan Keystone Project data). Acceptable to cite Gawande for a writing class.
- Output medium: Manim (3-panel: panel 1 = Pronovost findings as animated bar chart (before/after infection rates); panel 2 = "checklist works/fails" two-column table building row by row; panel 3 = writing workflow with checklist icons on the rote steps and judge icons on the judgment steps)
- The change: Ask Claude to design a 5-item "proposal submission checklist" equivalent to Pronovost's central-line checklist — specifically the steps that are consistently missed under deadline pressure (not "have a clear thesis" — that requires judgment; more like "verify all citations are in the specified format" — that is a checklist item).
- Teardown angle: The problem was not knowledge. The problem was that the application of knowledge fails predictably under pressure. The checklist is not bureaucracy — it is the structural solution to a specific cognitive failure mode. Writing has the same failure mode; the discipline is recognizing which steps belong on the list.
- Exclusions: Full aviation crew resource management history; hospital infection control policy; the WHO Surgical Safety Checklist implementation; Atul Gawande's broader work beyond the central-line example.
- Score: 7/10

## Candidate 10 — Build the Profile Interview Question Generator with Claude (Ch 7)

- Source: writing-guide/chapters/07-profile-telling-a-rich-and-compelling-story.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: The profile is the genre where you get one person to tell you something they have never said out loud — the thing they have been carrying. Most interview questions get surface answers. The question that breaks the surface is the one the subject didn't expect. Claude can generate 20 questions, but only the writer knows which one to use.
- The artifact: A CLI Python script (~20 lines) that takes a profile subject description (name, occupation, what makes them interesting, one known fact about them) and generates 3 tiers of interview questions: (1) 5 surface questions (warm-up, fact-gathering), (2) 5 opening questions (inviting reflection), (3) 5 breakthrough questions (the ones designed to surface what the subject has never said). The script flags which tier each question is in and reminds the writer: "You cannot predict which question will break the surface. Bring all 15."
- Prompt seed: `claude "Generate 15 interview questions for a profile subject, organized in three tiers. Subject: [DESCRIPTION]. Tier 1 (SURFACE — fact-gathering, 5 questions): chronological, career-focused, answerable with facts. Tier 2 (OPENING — reflection, 5 questions): invite the subject to evaluate, compare, or explain a choice. Tier 3 (BREAKTHROUGH — 5 questions): designed to surface what the subject has never said out loud — the moment of transformation, the cost of a decision, the thing they regret or are proud of in private. For each question, label its tier and the specific move it is making (e.g., 'Asks subject to name the turning point', 'Invites contradiction between public and private view'). End with: 'You cannot predict which question will break the surface. Bring all 15.'"`
- Read / check: Verify the breakthrough questions are genuinely unexpected (not just "tell me more about X" but questions that reframe the subject's self-narrative — "If you could un-make one decision from this period, what would it be and why?" is a breakthrough question); confirm the surface questions are actually surface (fact-gathering, not analytical); check that the tier labels are consistent.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Knowledge of the specific subject — Claude generates generic questions given the description; the writer must know enough about the subject to adapt the breakthrough questions to their specific vulnerability.
