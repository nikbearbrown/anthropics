*The price of one currency in terms of another — what determines it, what it does, and the policy choices that constrain it.*

In 2014, one euro bought about $1.40. By 2017, one euro bought about $1.05. By 2024, one euro bought about $1.10. Over the same decade, the British pound fell from about $1.55 to under $1.30 against the dollar. The Japanese yen depreciated from about ¥80 per dollar to ¥150 per dollar — meaning a U.S. visitor's dollar bought nearly twice as many yen as it had a decade before.

These movements are exchange-rate changes, and they have huge effects on who wins and who loses in the global economy. A weakening dollar makes U.S. exports cheaper for foreigners (helping U.S. exporters) but makes imports more expensive for Americans (hurting U.S. consumers). A strengthening dollar does the opposite. The same change that helps Boeing sell jets to European airlines hurts the American family on a European vacation.

Behind the visible currency movements is a market — the **foreign exchange (FX) market** — that's the largest financial market in the world by trading volume. Roughly $7 trillion changes hands daily, dwarfing any stock or bond market. The FX market is where currencies are exchanged for one another by importers, exporters, investors, central banks, hedge funds, and tourists.

This chapter is about how exchange rates work. By the end you should understand what moves them, what their effects are on the macroeconomy, and the choice among exchange-rate regimes — from completely floating rates (like the U.S. dollar) to fixed pegs (like the Saudi riyal pegged to the dollar) to currency unions (like the euro).

**Learning objectives.** By the end you should be able to: (1) define exchange rate, appreciation, and depreciation; (2) describe the foreign exchange market and identify its main participants; (3) explain how exchange rates are determined by supply and demand for currencies; (4) describe purchasing power parity and use it to compare currencies; (5) compare floating exchange rate, soft peg, hard peg, and currency union arrangements and identify trade-offs.

**Prerequisites.** Chapter 23 (international trade and capital flows). Chapter 28 (monetary policy).

## What an exchange rate is

An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another.

When we say "the dollar-euro rate is 1.10," we mean one dollar buys 1.10 euros, or equivalently, one euro buys $0.91. The exchange rate is bidirectional — you can quote it either way.

**Appreciation** — a currency rises in value against another. The dollar appreciates when it buys more euros than before.

**Depreciation** — a currency falls in value against another. The dollar depreciates when it buys fewer euros than before.

A currency can appreciate against one currency and depreciate against another at the same time, depending on the underlying economic dynamics. A more useful aggregate measure is the **trade-weighted exchange rate** — an index of a currency's value against a basket of trading partners' currencies, weighted by trade volume.

## The foreign exchange market

The FX market exists to convert one currency to another. The participants:

**Importers and exporters.** A U.S. company importing German cars needs euros to pay the manufacturer. A German company selling cars in the U.S. needs to convert the dollar revenue back into euros for its books. Trade flows generate much of the underlying transaction demand.

**Investors.** A U.S. investor buying European stocks needs to convert dollars to euros. A European pension fund buying U.S. Treasuries needs to convert euros to dollars. Cross-border portfolio flows generate enormous FX volume.

**Central banks.** When central banks intervene in currency markets to influence exchange rates, they buy or sell their currency in large quantities.

**Speculators and hedge funds.** Trading on expected currency movements. The largest single category by volume.

**Tourists and individuals.** Small in aggregate but visible.

The FX market is largely electronic, decentralized, and operates 24 hours a day across global financial centers (London, New York, Tokyo, Singapore, Hong Kong, Frankfurt). Currencies trade in pairs — EUR/USD, USD/JPY, GBP/USD, etc. The U.S. dollar is the most-traded currency by far; about 90 percent of FX transactions involve the dollar on one side.

## What determines the exchange rate

Exchange rates are determined by supply and demand in the FX market.

**Demand for a currency** comes from:
- Foreign buyers wanting goods/services from the country (exports).
- Foreign investors wanting assets in the country (inward capital flows).
- Speculators expecting the currency to appreciate.

**Supply of a currency** comes from:
- Domestic buyers wanting goods/services from abroad (imports).
- Domestic investors wanting foreign assets (outward capital flows).
- Speculators expecting the currency to depreciate.

When demand exceeds supply at the current exchange rate, the currency appreciates. When supply exceeds demand, it depreciates. The same supply-and-demand framework from Chapter 3, applied to currencies as a special good.

Several specific factors shift these curves:

**Interest rate differentials.** When U.S. interest rates rise relative to other countries', global investors want to park their money in U.S. assets. They buy dollars to do so, raising demand for the dollar. The dollar appreciates. (This is what happened during the Fed's 2022–2023 rate hikes — the dollar appreciated substantially against most major currencies.)

**Growth differentials.** Countries with stronger expected growth attract investment, supporting the currency.

**Inflation differentials.** Countries with persistently higher inflation tend to have depreciating currencies over time (purchasing power parity, below).

**Confidence and political stability.** Countries with stable institutions and reliable rule of law attract more investment and tend to have stronger or steadier currencies.

**Trade balances.** Persistent surplus countries see their currencies appreciate over time as demand for their exports requires currency conversion.

**Speculation.** Short-run movements often reflect speculators' positions ahead of expected events (Fed announcements, election outcomes, war).

## Purchasing power parity

In the long run, exchange rates should adjust so that a basket of goods costs the same in different countries (after currency conversion). This is **purchasing power parity (PPP)**.

If a Big Mac costs $5 in the U.S. and €4 in France, PPP would predict an exchange rate of $1.25 per euro ($5 / €4). If the actual rate is $1.10 per euro, the dollar is overvalued relative to PPP — a Big Mac costs $4.40 in dollar terms in France ($1.10 × 4 = $4.40), cheaper than $5 in the U.S.

PPP holds well in the long run for tradable goods (where arbitrage forces prices toward parity). It holds poorly for non-tradable goods (housing, services) and in the short run (where currencies fluctuate based on capital flows and speculation).

The Big Mac Index — published by The Economist for several decades — uses Big Mac prices to compare PPP across countries. As of recent updates, the dollar appears overvalued against the euro and the yen and roughly fair against the British pound. The index is approximate but informative about long-run currency mispricings.

## Exchange-rate effects on the macroeconomy

A weaker domestic currency:
- Makes exports cheaper to foreign buyers → exports rise.
- Makes imports more expensive to domestic buyers → imports fall.
- Net exports rise → AD shifts right → expansionary effect.
- Imported goods cost more → inflation rises.

A stronger domestic currency:
- Makes exports more expensive → exports fall.
- Makes imports cheaper → imports rise.
- Net exports fall → AD shifts left → contractionary effect.
- Imported goods cost less → inflation moderates.

Central banks pay attention to exchange rates partly for these AD effects. A central bank fighting inflation may welcome a stronger currency (helps disinflate). A central bank fighting recession may welcome a weaker currency (boosts exports). The opposite policies cause grief in trading partners — a weaker dollar hurts foreign exporters; a stronger dollar makes U.S. exports less competitive.

## Exchange-rate regimes

Countries face a fundamental policy choice about how their currency operates internationally.

**Floating exchange rate.** Let the FX market set the rate. Central bank does not actively manage it. The U.S., U.K., Japan, Canada, Australia, the eurozone all have floating rates. Most major economies do.

Pros: monetary policy independence (the central bank can set rates based on domestic conditions); automatic adjustment to external shocks (if exports decline, the currency depreciates, restoring competitiveness).

Cons: exchange-rate volatility creates uncertainty for trade and investment; speculators can drive large short-run swings.

**Soft peg.** Central bank actively intervenes to keep the currency within a range against another currency or basket. China for many years (against the dollar, with a slow controlled appreciation). Some emerging markets.

Pros: more predictability than pure float; can prevent excessive volatility.

Cons: requires substantial reserves to defend the peg; vulnerable to speculative attack if the peg becomes inconsistent with fundamentals.

**Hard peg.** Central bank commits to a fixed rate without bands, often through institutional structures like a currency board (Hong Kong's peg to the dollar). The central bank is committed to defending the peg with whatever reserves are needed.

Pros: maximum predictability; eliminates exchange-rate risk for trade and investment with the anchor country.

Cons: complete loss of monetary-policy independence (the central bank is constrained to follow whatever interest rates the anchor country sets); breaking the peg can be catastrophic when it eventually happens.

**Currency union.** Countries share a currency. The euro is the largest example (20 member countries as of 2024). Earlier examples: the gold standard (multiple countries with gold-backed currencies effectively pegged to each other and to gold).

Pros: maximum integration with members; eliminates exchange-rate uncertainty for intra-union trade; lower transaction costs.

Cons: complete loss of independent monetary policy. The eurozone runs into this when member countries face very different economic conditions (Greek depression while German economy is strong) — the single ECB monetary policy can't be right for both.

The trilemma in international finance: a country can have at most two of (1) free capital movement, (2) fixed exchange rate, (3) independent monetary policy. Choose any two; the third becomes infeasible. Floating-rate countries (U.S., U.K.) get capital mobility and monetary independence by giving up the fixed rate. Hard-peg countries (Hong Kong) get capital mobility and fixed rate by giving up monetary independence. China for many years tried to control its capital account, which let it have both a (managed) exchange rate and some monetary independence at the cost of restricting capital flows.

## Synthesis

Pull back. Exchange rates are prices set in the world's largest financial market by the same supply-and-demand mechanics as any other price. Demand and supply respond to interest rates, growth, inflation, confidence, trade, and speculation. In the long run, purchasing power parity provides an anchor; in the short run, capital flows and expectations dominate.

Exchange-rate movements have substantial effects on the macroeconomy through net exports and import prices. A weaker currency boosts exports and inflation; a stronger currency does the opposite.

Countries choose among exchange-rate regimes — floating, soft peg, hard peg, currency union — based on a trade-off between exchange-rate stability and monetary-policy independence. The trilemma constrains all combinations. Most large advanced economies have chosen floating rates (with capital mobility and monetary independence). Some smaller economies and trading-bloc participants have chosen pegs or unions.

The Federal Reserve, like other major central banks, doesn't directly manage the dollar's exchange rate but is keenly aware of exchange-rate effects on the U.S. economy. The dollar's strength in 2022–23, partly a consequence of the Fed's rate hikes, helped slow U.S. inflation by making imports cheaper while contributing to weaker dollar-denominated commodity prices. The same strength hurt U.S. exporters and contributed to financial stress in some emerging markets that owed dollar-denominated debt. International monetary policy has international consequences, by design and by accident.
