*How political decisions get made — and why the rules of politics produce predictable distortions even when everyone is acting reasonably.*

In September 2009, the U.S. government imposed a tariff on tires imported from China. The tariff started at 35 percent, fell to 30 percent in year two, and 25 percent in year three. The stated goal: protect American tire manufacturing jobs from cheap Chinese imports.

The tariff worked, in a narrow sense. Some U.S. tire manufacturing jobs were preserved. Approximately 1,200 of them, by most estimates.

The tariff also cost American consumers — through higher tire prices — about $900,000 per job preserved, per year.

Twelve hundred jobs preserved. About a billion dollars in total cost. A cost-per-job that, if the jobs paid $50,000, exceeded the wage by a factor of 18. The tariff was a transfer from consumers (millions, paying a few dollars more per tire) to a small number of workers and tire-company shareholders (concentrated, with a clear interest in lobbying).

The structure of this transaction is the topic of this chapter.

The market models from Chapters 3 through 17 assume policy outcomes match what's economically efficient — or at least that we can identify the optimal policy and pursue it. Real political systems don't reliably produce optimal policies. They produce *political* outcomes, and the structural features of how political decisions get made — voting rules, information costs, lobbying, special interests — predict systematic departures from textbook efficiency.

This is the field of **public choice economics**, founded primarily by James Buchanan and Gordon Tullock in the 1960s. Its core insight is that the same self-interest assumptions economists apply to consumers and firms also apply to voters, politicians, and bureaucrats. Once you take that seriously, you can predict why policies that nobody would design from scratch — like the Chinese tire tariff — get enacted anyway.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to identify the political structures that produce predictable inefficiencies, evaluate proposals to address them, and read political news with the eyes of someone who knows the structural logic.

**Learning objectives.** By the end you should be able to: (1) define rational ignorance and explain why voters know less than they could; (2) explain how special-interest politics produces "concentrated benefits, dispersed costs"; (3) describe pork-barrel spending and logrolling; (4) state the median voter theorem and identify when it does and doesn't predict policy outcomes; (5) describe the voting paradox (Arrow's impossibility result, in introductory form).

**Prerequisites.** Chapter 13 (collective action problem). Chapter 11 (regulatory capture).

## Rational ignorance — why voters don't study

Start with a fact most people find uncomfortable. The average voter, in the average election, knows surprisingly little about the candidates, the issues, or the policies on the ballot.

This isn't a moral failing. It's the predicted outcome of how political information works.

A consumer deciding what car to buy gets enormous benefit from being well-informed — the right car will serve her for years; the wrong one will frustrate her. The cost of becoming informed (research, test drives, reading reviews) is moderate. The benefit-to-cost ratio of being informed is high. So consumers are usually reasonably informed.

A voter deciding how to vote in a presidential election gets a tiny share of any benefit from being well-informed. Her vote almost certainly will not change the outcome — for her individual vote to matter, the election would have to come down to a single ballot, which has a probability so small as to be effectively zero in any large election. The personal benefit of being well-informed is therefore tiny. The cost — researching all the candidates, understanding all the policies, reading the actual budget documents — is substantial.

Rational benefit-cost analysis says: don't bother. Vote based on rough impressions, party identification, the candidate's appearance on TV, what your friends say. **Rational ignorance** is the formal name for this — voters are *rationally* uninformed because the cost of becoming informed exceeds the personal benefit.

This isn't a theory voters embrace, but it's a structural feature of large-electorate politics that has predictable consequences. Voters know less than ideal democratic theory assumes. Politicians who can present a simple, emotionally resonant message often beat politicians with better but more complex policies. Media coverage that's entertaining beats media coverage that's informative. Single-issue voters (who know a lot about one issue) often dominate over breadth-of-issue voters (who know a little about many).

Rational ignorance also explains why a few highly motivated, well-organized interest groups can dominate the political process even when a large majority of citizens disagree.

## Concentrated benefits, dispersed costs

The Chinese tire tariff illustrates the core dynamic. The tariff produces concentrated benefits — about 1,200 jobs in a small number of plants — and dispersed costs — millions of consumers each paying a small amount more for tires.

The 1,200 workers and the few tire-company shareholders have intense interest in the tariff. Each worker keeps a job. Each shareholder gets a more profitable company. They organize, lobby, contribute to political campaigns. They're a coherent political force.

The 200 million American tire-buying consumers each pay a few extra dollars per year. None of them organize. None of them lobby. None of them threaten politicians with electoral defeat. The dollar amount per consumer is too small to motivate action, even though the aggregate cost across all consumers is enormous.

The result: the policy that benefits the small organized group passes, even though it makes society as a whole worse off. The arithmetic of *concentrated benefits, dispersed costs* favors the organized few over the unorganized many.

This pattern recurs across thousands of policies. Sugar import quotas raise the price of sugar for every American food consumer to benefit a few thousand sugar producers. Specific industry subsidies, occupational licensing requirements, tax loopholes for narrow categories of taxpayers — each follows the same structure. The total deadweight cost across all such policies is significant, even though no single one individually exceeds the political will to oppose it.

## Pork-barrel spending and logrolling

Two related dynamics deepen the pattern.

**Pork-barrel spending** is government spending designed to benefit a small geographic area, paid for from general tax revenue. A bridge that benefits one congressional district, paid for by all U.S. taxpayers. A research facility in one senator's state. A military base that doesn't fit the Pentagon's actual needs but provides jobs in a particular community. The senator who gets the project gets the political credit; the cost is spread across 330 million Americans.

**Logrolling** is the practice of legislators trading votes — "I'll vote for your project if you vote for mine." Through logrolling, projects that would never pass on their merits can pass as part of larger packages. A senator from Texas votes for a bridge in Maine in exchange for the Maine senator's vote on a Texas dam. Each project, taken alone, is wasteful. Together, they assemble a majority.

Pork-barrel spending and logrolling are not technically illegal. They are features of how legislative politics works. The institutions designed to constrain them (line-item vetoes, balanced-budget amendments, rules limiting earmarks) have had limited and uneven success. The structural incentive — politicians benefit personally from delivering benefits to their constituency, even at the cost of the larger system — keeps reproducing the behavior.

## The median voter theorem

Now flip to the other side: when does democracy actually produce policies the majority wants?

The **median voter theorem** says that in a single-issue, two-candidate election, both candidates will converge on the position preferred by the median voter — the voter exactly in the middle of the distribution.

The logic is straightforward. Suppose Candidate A is to the left of the median voter; Candidate B is to the right. Voters to A's left will vote for A; voters to B's right will vote for B. The voters in the middle (between A and B) will split. Whoever is closer to the median wins. So both candidates have an incentive to move toward the median. In equilibrium, both candidates announce policies very close to what the median voter wants.

This is a useful baseline. It explains why two-party democracies tend to converge on similar positions on most issues — both parties chase the same median voter. It also explains why sharp ideological differences between parties usually produce electoral defeat for the extreme party.

The theorem has limits. It assumes a single issue, two candidates, full voter participation, and complete information. Real elections involve many issues simultaneously, multiple candidates, low and uneven turnout, and rationally ignorant voters. With multiple issues, the median voter on one issue may differ from the median voter on another. Politicians can win with a coalition of voters who each agree with them on one or two issues even if no candidate matches the average voter on any single issue.

In multi-candidate primary elections, the median voter is the median *primary voter*, who is typically more ideologically extreme than the median general-election voter. This is why presidential primaries push candidates toward the wings while the general election pulls them back toward the center. The shift is not strategic insincerity; it's responding to two different median voters at two different stages.

## Voting paradoxes — when no winner exists

Sometimes no policy can command a majority. The simplest example is the **voting paradox**, identified by Marquis de Condorcet in 1785.

Three voters, three options (call them A, B, C):

- Voter 1 prefers A > B > C.
- Voter 2 prefers B > C > A.
- Voter 3 prefers C > A > B.

In pairwise comparisons:
- A vs. B: voters 1 and 3 prefer A; voter 2 prefers B. A wins.
- B vs. C: voters 1 and 2 prefer B; voter 3 prefers C. B wins.
- C vs. A: voters 2 and 3 prefer C; voter 1 prefers A. C wins.

So A beats B, B beats C, and C beats A. No option commands a majority. The "winning" policy depends entirely on the *order* in which votes are taken — whoever controls the agenda controls the outcome.

This generalizes to **Arrow's impossibility theorem** (Kenneth Arrow, 1951, eventual Nobel): no voting system can simultaneously satisfy a small set of intuitive fairness criteria when there are three or more options. Any voting system will violate at least one fairness condition somewhere.

The implication is not that democracy is impossible — democracy works in practice — but that no voting system perfectly aggregates preferences. The choice of voting rule (plurality, ranked choice, runoff, proportional representation) systematically advantages some outcomes over others. There is no neutral aggregation mechanism. The agenda-setter, the rule-maker, and the procedural designer all have power that doesn't show up in any individual ballot.

## Why politics looks the way it does

Pull the threads together. Public choice theory predicts:

- Voters will be rationally ignorant on most policy details.
- Concentrated-benefit / dispersed-cost policies will dominate dispersed-benefit / concentrated-cost policies.
- Pork-barrel spending will persist.
- Logrolling will produce coalitions for individually-bad bills.
- Median voter logic will pull two-party democracies toward the center on average.
- Voting paradoxes will appear in close-three-way contests.
- Procedural rules will matter as much as voter preferences.

These predictions are not normative claims. They don't say democracy is bad. They say the structural features of large-electorate politics produce predictable patterns. Once you see the patterns, the politics gets less surprising.

The corrective implications differ by case. Some cases call for changing the procedural rules — campaign-finance reform, term limits, ranked-choice voting. Some call for changing the institutional structures — independent regulatory bodies, central bank independence, sunset clauses on tax breaks. Some call for accepting that the patterns are durable and adapting — assume tariffs will appear when concentrated industries lobby, design trade agreements to limit them in advance, build coalitions of dispersed losers ahead of the next round.

The reformer's hardest lesson: *neither side of "the market is bad and government should fix it" or "government is bad and the market should fix it" is structurally complete*. Markets fail in well-documented ways (Chapters 9–16). Government fails in well-documented ways (this chapter). The empirical question is which failures are larger, in which contexts, with which available remedies. The honest economist's answer is: it varies, and reasonable people will disagree. The dishonest economist's answer is to pretend the trade-off doesn't exist.

## Synthesis

Pull back. Public choice economics applies the standard rationality assumptions to political behavior. The result is a framework for predicting which kinds of policies will be over-supplied (concentrated-benefit / dispersed-cost) and which under-supplied (the reverse), why information in democracy is structurally weaker than in markets, why two-party systems converge on the median, and why no voting system perfectly aggregates preferences.

The Chinese tire tariff is the case study that ties it together. A small organized group benefits enormously. A large dispersed group pays a tiny amount each. The political structure favors the organized group. The textbook welfare analysis says the tariff is wasteful. The political-economy analysis says the tariff was predictable.

The lesson isn't that politics produces only bad outcomes — democracies have produced enormous economic growth and wide expansion of human welfare. The lesson is that the political process has a structure, and the structure systematically distorts in identifiable directions. Designing policies that work in spite of those distortions is the institutional-design problem that occupies much of applied public economics.

This concludes the micro half of the book. From Chapter 19 onward, we shift to the macro perspective — the economy as a whole, growth, business cycles, money, inflation, and the macroeconomic policy tools that try to steer it.

**Exercise 2 — Stress-test the median voter theorem.** Ask an LLM under what conditions the median voter theorem fails to predict observed political outcomes. The exercise tests the LLM's grasp of the theorem's assumptions: single-issue, two candidates, full participation, complete information. Ask it to explain why U.S. presidential primaries appear to produce more extreme candidates than the general electorate would prefer.

**Exercise 3 — Design a voting paradox.** Ask an LLM to construct a three-voter, three-option example demonstrating Condorcet's voting paradox. The LLM should produce preferences such that A beats B, B beats C, and C beats A in pairwise majority votes. Verify the math. Then ask: what real-world policy choice has this structure?

**Exercise 4 — Evaluate a campaign-finance reform.** Tell an LLM you're considering proposing a campaign-finance rule (e.g., a public-funding-only system, a strict $50 individual contribution cap, mandatory disclosure of all donations above $200). Ask it to use public-choice logic to predict the effects on (a) policy outcomes, (b) candidate selection, (c) voter information. Push for specific predictions, not generic arguments.

Evaluate the LLM's classifications.

Chapters 20 through 32 work through growth, unemployment, inflation, money and banking, and the fiscal and monetary policy tools macro economists deploy. The micro half taught us how individual markets work, how they fail, and what policy can and can't fix. The macro half asks the same questions about the economy as a whole.

**What would change my mind.** The public-choice framework treats voters and politicians as straightforwardly self-interested. The empirical record shows that *some* political behavior is consistent with this assumption and some is not — voters often behave altruistically, politicians sometimes resist their own narrow interests, bureaucrats often work harder than their incentives strictly demand. The framework predicts the systematic patterns; it doesn't capture the individual variation. Whether the systematic patterns dominate the variation depends on the specific institutional context. In some democracies, the patterns dominate; in others, civic norms and institutional culture moderate them substantially.

**Still puzzling.** I do not have a clean account of why some democracies seem more capable of resisting concentrated-benefit / dispersed-cost dynamics than others — Northern Europe consistently produces less of this pattern than the U.S., despite having no fundamentally different voting structures. The institutional details (proportional representation, multi-party coalitions, civic culture, media structure) seem to matter, but the predictive accuracy of any single feature is weak.
