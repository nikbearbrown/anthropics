*How the U.S. government raises and spends money — and how those decisions affect the macroeconomy.*

In October 2013, the U.S. federal government partially shut down for 16 days. National parks closed. Some federal employees were furloughed. Government services slowed or stopped. The cause: Congress could not agree on a continuing resolution to keep the government funded.

This was not unique. The U.S. government has shut down or partially shut down at least four times in the past three decades. Each time the immediate trigger was a political dispute. The deeper cause was a structural mismatch between what the government spends and what it collects in taxes — a chronic federal budget deficit that requires either continual borrowing or political compromise on competing priorities.

The federal budget is not a household budget. It's bigger (about $7 trillion in spending and $4.4 trillion in revenue in 2024), more complex (thousands of programs), and operates under different constraints (it can borrow at very low rates and is the issuer of its own currency). The arguments about whether and how the federal budget should be "balanced" — and the political fights that periodically shut down the government — sit at the intersection of macroeconomics, politics, and political economy.

This chapter is about U.S. fiscal policy. By the end you should understand the structure of federal/state/local spending and revenue, how taxes are classified, the mechanics of expansionary and contractionary fiscal policy, the lags that complicate fiscal stabilization, and the long-running debate over balanced budgets.

**Learning objectives.** By the end you should be able to: (1) identify major spending categories and revenue sources for U.S. federal, state, and local governments; (2) classify taxes as regressive, proportional, or progressive; (3) distinguish annual deficit from accumulated debt; (4) explain expansionary and contractionary fiscal policy and trace each through AD-AS; (5) identify the three lags that complicate fiscal policy; (6) evaluate the major arguments for and against a balanced-budget requirement.

## How the U.S. government spends

The U.S. federal government spent about $7 trillion in fiscal year 2024 (the year ending September 30, 2024). The major categories:

**Mandatory spending** (~60% of the budget). Programs whose spending is determined by formulas in law, not by annual appropriations. Major items:
- **Social Security** (~$1.5T): retirement, disability, survivor benefits.
- **Medicare** (~$0.9T): health insurance for those 65+.
- **Medicaid** (~$0.6T): health insurance for low-income individuals (joint federal-state).
- **Other mandatory** (~$0.6T): SNAP, federal employee retirement, veterans' benefits, EITC.

**Discretionary spending** (~30% of the budget). Annually appropriated by Congress. Two broad categories:
- **Defense** (~$0.9T): military, foreign aid for security, Veterans Affairs operations.
- **Non-defense discretionary** (~$0.9T): education, transportation, housing, science, environment, federal courts, executive branch.

**Net interest** (~10% of the budget, growing rapidly). Interest payments on the national debt. About $0.7T in 2024 and rising as both the debt and interest rates grow.

State and local governments together spend about $4 trillion more. Their major spending: education (~30%), Medicaid match (~15%), public safety, transportation infrastructure, prisons, public employee benefits. State and local revenues come primarily from income taxes (states), sales taxes (states), and property taxes (mostly local).

## How the U.S. government raises money

Federal revenue in 2024 was about $4.4 trillion. Major sources:

- **Individual income tax** (~$2T, ~50% of revenue).
- **Payroll taxes** (~$1.6T, ~37%) for Social Security and Medicare.
- **Corporate income tax** (~$0.4T, ~10%).
- **Excise taxes, customs duties, estate taxes, miscellaneous** (~$0.4T, ~3%).

The U.S. federal tax structure is **progressive** — higher earners pay a higher fraction of their income in tax. Tax brackets, deductions, and credits implement progressivity. The top marginal rate (for the highest-income earners) is currently 37 percent.

State income taxes range from zero (Texas, Florida, Washington) to 13.3 percent (California). State sales taxes typically range from 4 to 9 percent. Property taxes vary enormously by locality.

## How taxes are classified

Three categories based on how the tax burden falls relative to income:

**Progressive tax.** Higher-income people pay a higher fraction of their income. Federal income tax is progressive (with bracket-creep, deductions, and credits as nuances). Estate tax is progressive.

**Proportional tax (flat tax).** Everyone pays the same percentage. Some state income taxes are flat. The Medicare payroll tax is proportional (1.45 percent of all wages, plus 0.9 percent surcharge above a threshold).

**Regressive tax.** Lower-income people pay a higher fraction of their income. Sales taxes are regressive in their general operation — lower-income households spend a higher fraction of their income, so a sales tax falls more heavily on them as a percentage of income. The Social Security payroll tax has a regressive feature — it's only collected on income up to a cap (about $168,000 in 2024), so high earners pay a smaller fraction of total income.

The progressivity of the overall U.S. federal tax system is a product of all the components together. Taking everything into account (including refundable credits like the EITC), the U.S. federal tax system is moderately progressive, though substantially less so than in many other developed countries. State and local taxes, in aggregate, are typically regressive.

## Annual deficit vs. accumulated debt

Two distinct concepts often confused.

**Annual budget deficit** (or surplus) — the gap between revenue and spending in a single year. The U.S. ran a budget deficit of about $1.8 trillion in 2024 (spent $7T, collected $4.4T, plus some other adjustments). A surplus would mean the government collected more than it spent.

**National debt** (or accumulated debt) — the cumulative sum of all past deficits, minus surpluses. As of 2024, the U.S. national debt is about $34 trillion (with adjustments for intra-government holdings, "debt held by the public" is about $28 trillion). This represents the total value of all the bonds the U.S. Treasury has issued and not yet repaid.

The deficit adds to the debt each year. A persistent deficit grows the debt. The debt-to-GDP ratio is the common measure of the relative size — about 100 percent of GDP for the U.S., depending on how it's measured.

The U.S. has had budget deficits in nearly every year for decades, with rare exceptions (briefly in the late 1990s). Debt-to-GDP has risen from about 30 percent in 1980 to over 100 percent today, with sharp jumps during the 2008 crisis and the COVID pandemic.

## Expansionary vs. contractionary fiscal policy

Fiscal policy is the government's use of spending and tax decisions to influence the macroeconomy.

**Expansionary fiscal policy** — increase spending, cut taxes, or both. This shifts AD right. In a recession (Keynesian zone), this raises real GDP and employment. The 2009 American Recovery and Reinvestment Act (~$800B) and the 2020 CARES Act (~$2T) were the largest discretionary expansionary packages in U.S. history.

**Contractionary fiscal policy** — decrease spending, raise taxes, or both. This shifts AD left. In an overheating economy, this cools inflation. Examples are rare in U.S. history; political resistance to tax increases and spending cuts is substantial.

Fiscal policy works through the multiplier (Chapter 25). The multiplier varies with the type of spending or tax change, the state of the economy, and the monetary policy stance. Government infrastructure spending typically has a higher multiplier than transfers; both are higher when interest rates are at the zero lower bound.

The U.S. also has substantial **automatic stabilizers** — programs whose spending automatically rises in recessions (unemployment insurance, SNAP, Medicaid) and whose revenue automatically falls (income tax). These provide significant counter-cyclical support without requiring new legislation.

## The three lags

Discretionary fiscal policy is hard because of timing. Three lags complicate it:

**Recognition lag.** It takes months to know a recession has started. The NBER doesn't officially declare a recession until well after it began. By the time policymakers are sure, several quarters may have passed.

**Decision lag.** Congress must enact legislation. This typically takes months to year-plus, depending on political alignment. Major bills can take several years.

**Implementation lag.** Once enacted, spending or tax changes take time to affect the economy. New infrastructure programs may take years to design and start work. Tax changes take effect within months but their economic effect comes through subsequent decisions.

The combined lags can mean fiscal policy enacted in response to a recession arrives during the recovery, when its effect is mostly inflationary rather than stimulative. This is one reason most macroeconomists prefer monetary policy as the primary stabilization tool — the Fed can move within weeks, much faster than Congress.

Automatic stabilizers don't have these lags. They operate immediately, without legislation. This is one reason they are sometimes preferred over discretionary action.

## The balanced-budget debate

Should the federal government be required by law to balance its budget every year?

**Arguments for a balanced-budget amendment.** Discipline politicians from running chronic deficits. Prevent intergenerational transfer (current beneficiaries paid by future taxpayers). Prevent debt accumulation that limits future fiscal flexibility. Make policy more sustainable.

**Arguments against.** Strips the government's ability to respond to recessions (Keynesian fiscal stabilization becomes impossible). Forces pro-cyclical policy (in recessions, when revenue falls, the government would have to cut spending or raise taxes — making the recession worse). Forces difficult choices between essential spending (defense, Social Security) and other priorities every year. Different from household budgets; the federal government has unique borrowing capacity.

Most economists oppose a strict balanced-budget rule. The mainstream view is that some flexibility is necessary, that automatic stabilizers should operate freely in recessions, and that long-run budget sustainability is a separate question from year-to-year balance. Variations like a "cyclically adjusted" balanced budget (balance over the cycle, not every year) might combine some discipline with the flexibility needed for stabilization.

The political argument for balanced budgets often masks deeper questions: which programs should be reduced, which taxes should be raised, who should bear the costs of fiscal restraint. Without resolving these underlying questions, "balance the budget" is a slogan rather than a policy.

## Synthesis

Pull back. The U.S. federal government spends about 25 percent of GDP — much of it on Social Security, Medicare, defense, Medicaid, and net interest on the debt. Revenue comes mainly from individual income taxes and payroll taxes. The U.S. tax system is progressive overall (federal) and slightly regressive at the state-local level.

The U.S. has run persistent annual deficits for decades, accumulating a national debt now equal to roughly 100 percent of GDP. Whether this is sustainable is one of the central macroeconomic questions of our time.

Fiscal policy — discretionary spending and tax changes plus automatic stabilizers — is the second of the two main macroeconomic stabilization tools (monetary policy being the first). Discretionary fiscal policy is hampered by recognition, decision, and implementation lags; automatic stabilizers operate without these.

The balanced-budget debate sits at the intersection of macroeconomic theory, political constraints, and intergenerational equity. Strict balance would prevent counter-cyclical policy; complete flexibility allows accumulation of debt that may eventually constrain future generations. Most economists favor flexibility for stabilization while urging long-run sustainability — a balance that is difficult to enact politically.

Spending categories (Social Security, Medicare, defense, etc.) and revenue sources (income tax, payroll tax, etc.). Compute the deficit. Verify that it matches recent CBO estimates.

**Exercise 2 — Classify a specific tax.** Tell an LLM about a specific U.S. tax (federal income tax, sales tax, gasoline tax, payroll tax, property tax). Ask it to classify the tax as regressive, proportional, or progressive, and to explain the reasoning. Push for specifics about which features make the tax fall in that category.

**Exercise 3 — Apply expansionary fiscal policy.** Tell an LLM the U.S. is in a recession with high unemployment. Ask: design a $500B fiscal stimulus package. What spending categories would have the highest multiplier? What tax changes would? Push for evidence-based reasoning.

**Exercise 4 — Trace the three lags.** Walk an LLM through a hypothetical: a recession begins in March 2026. Ask it to estimate when (a) the recession is officially recognized, (b) Congress passes legislation, (c) the spending actually starts. Compare to typical historical lags.

**Exercise 5 — Balanced-budget thought experiment.** Tell an LLM that a balanced-budget amendment has been ratified, requiring federal spending to equal revenue every year. Ask it to predict the consequences for: (a) recession response, (b) discretionary spending stability, (c) tax policy, (d) intergenerational equity. Push the LLM to be specific.
