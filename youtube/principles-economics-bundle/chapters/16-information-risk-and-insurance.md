# Chapter 16 — Information, Risk, and Insurance

*The third major source of market failure — when one side knows what the other doesn't.*

In 1970, an economist named George Akerlof published a short paper titled "The Market for 'Lemons.'" It was rejected by three top journals before it found a home, and a few decades later it won him the Nobel Prize.

The paper made one observation. Used cars come in two qualities — good ones and "lemons" (cars with hidden problems the buyer won't discover for months). The seller knows which is which. The buyer doesn't.

Now think through what happens. The buyer, unable to tell good from bad, will only pay an average price — say, halfway between what a good car is worth and what a lemon is worth. At that average price, no one with a *good* car wants to sell — they would lose money. So the good cars stay off the market. Only the lemons are offered for sale. The buyer, observing this, lowers her offer further. Even more good cars exit. In the limit, the market unravels — only lemons trade, and a lot of mutually beneficial transactions never happen.

This is **asymmetric information** — when one side of a transaction knows materially more than the other — and it produces a market failure that doesn't fit any of the three categories we've covered (monopoly, externality, public good). It's a fourth category, and once you see it, you see it everywhere. Health insurance. Job markets. Lending. Insurance of any kind. Any market where the seller knows something about the product that the buyer can't easily verify.

This chapter is about the diagnosis and the institutional responses. By the end you should be able to identify a market suffering from information problems, explain whether it's an *adverse selection* problem (sorting issue at the contracting stage) or a *moral hazard* problem (behavior issue after the contract is signed), and evaluate the standard responses — signaling, screening, mandatory pooling, regulation.

**Learning objectives.** By the end you should be able to: (1) define asymmetric information and explain why it can cause markets to break down; (2) distinguish adverse selection from moral hazard with examples of each; (3) explain how insurance pools risk and why the basic insurance market is vulnerable to adverse selection; (4) describe the major institutional responses to information problems (warranties, signals, screening, mandates); (5) explain the structural logic of the ACA's individual mandate and similar policies.

**Prerequisites.** Chapter 3 (supply and demand). Chapter 6 (consumer choice). Chapter 8 (efficiency).

## Asymmetric information — the lemons problem

Most of the markets we've studied so far assumed that buyers and sellers have roughly equivalent information about the product being traded. Sometimes they do. Often they don't.

Examples beyond used cars:

- **Health insurance.** The applicant knows her health risks — family history, chronic conditions, lifestyle — better than the insurer.
- **Auto insurance.** The driver knows her driving habits better than the insurer.
- **Job markets.** The job applicant knows her work ethic, abilities, and personal circumstances better than the employer.
- **Lending.** The borrower knows her ability and intent to repay better than the lender.
- **Restaurants.** The chef knows the freshness of the seafood better than the diner.
- **Investment advice.** The advisor knows whether their recommendation serves your interest or theirs better than you do.

The pattern is consistent. The party with more information has incentives that don't always align with the uninformed party's interests. Markets respond by developing institutions to bridge the information gap, but these institutions have their own costs and their own failures.

Two kinds of information problem matter most.

## Adverse selection — sorting at the contracting stage

**Adverse selection** is the problem Akerlof described. Before the contract is signed, the informed party has private information that determines whether the contract is favorable or unfavorable to them. Since only those who find the contract favorable accept, the contracting party ends up with a non-random selection of the population.

In the lemons market, the informed party is the seller, and the contract favors lemon-sellers (who get more than their car is worth) over good-car sellers (who would get less than their car is worth). Lemons are over-represented among cars actually sold.

In the health-insurance market, the informed party is the buyer, and the contract favors high-risk buyers (who expect more in benefits than they pay in premiums) over low-risk buyers (who expect less in benefits than they pay). High-risk individuals are over-represented among insurance buyers. Insurers raise premiums to cover the higher expected costs. Some low-risk individuals drop out. Premiums rise again. In the worst case, the market collapses.

The institutional responses to adverse selection are several:

**Signaling.** The informed party takes an action that demonstrates their type. A college diploma signals high productivity (a Michael Spence model). A long warranty signals product quality. A large equity stake signals a founder's confidence in the venture.

**Screening.** The uninformed party offers a menu of contracts that induces the informed party to reveal their type by which contract they choose. Auto insurers offer high-deductible/low-premium contracts (which low-risk drivers prefer) and low-deductible/high-premium contracts (which high-risk drivers prefer). The choice itself reveals information.

**Mandatory pooling.** The state requires everyone to participate, eliminating the selection problem. Social insurance (Social Security) works this way. The ACA's individual mandate (in its original form) was an attempt to do this for private health insurance.

**Reputation and repeat play.** When a seller stays in business and customers can rate her, the reputation effect partially substitutes for direct verification of quality. Yelp, Amazon reviews, and rating systems generally are partial responses to information asymmetry.

**Verification by third parties.** Inspectors, certifiers, accreditors. The Underwriters Laboratories (UL) certification on electrical equipment, the FDA approval on drugs, professional licensure for doctors and lawyers. These institutions exist partly to bridge information gaps that markets can't bridge directly.

## Moral hazard — behavior after the contract

**Moral hazard** is a different information problem. After the contract is signed, the informed party has incentives to behave differently than they would have without the contract — and the uninformed party can't fully observe the behavior.

Examples:

- A car-insurance policyholder drives more recklessly because the insurance covers most of any accident damage.
- A health-insurance policyholder uses more medical services because the insurance covers most of the cost.
- A bank knows depositors' funds are insured by the FDIC, so it makes riskier loans than it otherwise would.
- An employee with permanent tenure works less hard than they did before getting tenure.

The pattern: the contract changes the incentives. The informed party adjusts their behavior. The uninformed party (the insurer, the regulator, the employer) bears the cost. The behavior change is rational from the informed party's perspective; the cost is externalized.

Institutional responses to moral hazard:

**Deductibles and copays.** Insurance contracts typically require the insured to pay a portion of any claim. This restores some skin in the game and reduces the moral-hazard problem.

**Monitoring.** Telematics in auto insurance (a device records driving behavior). Drug-testing in employment. Bank stress tests by regulators. The cost of monitoring is real, but it can substantially reduce the moral hazard.

**Performance-based contracts.** Pay tied to outcomes rather than effort. CEO compensation tied to stock performance. Salesperson commissions on sales. The contract structure itself addresses the incentive problem.

**Capital requirements (in finance).** Banks must hold a fraction of their loans as their own capital, ensuring they bear losses if the loans default — which reduces the moral hazard from deposit insurance.

## Insurance — pooling risk

Insurance is the textbook example of an institution that exists primarily to address risk and information problems.

The basic mechanism: many people each face a small probability of a large loss (a house fire, a car accident, a serious illness). Each individual's loss is uncertain. The aggregate across many individuals is statistically predictable. By pooling premiums into a fund and paying out claims from the fund, insurers convert *individual risk* (very unequal across people in any given year) into *predictable cost* (averaged across many people).

This mechanism works only if a few conditions hold:

- The losses are *independent* across people (one person's house fire doesn't make another's more likely).
- The probability of loss is *roughly known* (so the actuary can price the premium).
- The insured *can't fully control* the loss (otherwise moral hazard dominates).
- The insured pool is *roughly random* (otherwise adverse selection dominates).

Insurance markets exist when these conditions are roughly met. They struggle or collapse when one or more fail. Earthquake insurance struggles because the losses are correlated across many policyholders. Health insurance for some chronic conditions struggles because the probability of loss is too well-known to one side. Long-term care insurance has been a disastrous market for many private insurers because of moral hazard and adverse selection together.

### Government and social insurance

The classic response to insurance-market failures is government provision. Social Security insures against the risk of outliving your savings. Medicare insures against medical costs in old age. Unemployment insurance covers temporary job loss. The Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation insures private pensions. Federal flood insurance covers risks the private market won't.

The justification for government provision in each case is some combination of: the private market fails because of adverse selection, the private market fails because of correlated risks, the externalities from individual catastrophe spill onto society, or the program serves a redistributive purpose alongside its insurance function.

The major U.S. programs combine insurance and redistribution in ways that make them politically durable but economically complex. Medicare's premium structure is partly insurance, partly transfer. Social Security's benefit formula is partly insurance, partly transfer. The mixing makes the programs broadly supported but harder to evaluate as pure insurance products.

### The ACA mandate logic

The Affordable Care Act of 2010 included an "individual mandate" — a requirement that everyone obtain health insurance or pay a tax penalty. The penalty was eliminated for federal taxes in 2017, though some states retain mandates of their own.

The mandate's structural logic was an adverse-selection response. Without a mandate, the healthy can drop out, premiums rise, more healthy drop out, and the insurance market collapses (the "death spiral"). With a mandate, the healthy stay in the pool. Premiums reflect the average risk of the whole population, which is much lower than the average risk of those who would voluntarily insure.

The mandate was politically controversial in ways the structural logic was not. It paired the insurance reform with a coercive element. Some states with low mandate enforcement and other ACA states without active mandates have experienced rising premiums and reduced participation in ACA marketplaces, broadly consistent with the adverse-selection prediction. The empirical magnitude is debated.

## Synthesis

Pull back. Information asymmetry is a third major source of market failure, distinct from monopoly (Chapter 9–11) and externalities (Chapter 12–13). Adverse selection sorts the wrong people into contracts. Moral hazard changes behavior after contracts are signed. Both produce inefficient outcomes. Both are often addressed through institutional responses — signaling, screening, mandates, monitoring — that have their own costs.

The most important practical applications are in insurance markets. The basic insurance contract is a remarkable institutional invention: it converts individual risk into predictable group cost, making possible projects, lifestyles, and freedoms that wouldn't otherwise exist. The insurance contract is also vulnerable to both information problems in ways that limit which risks can be insured and at what cost. Government-provided social insurance fills some of the gaps the private market can't.

The U.S. healthcare system is the working example where most of these problems are visible at once. Adverse selection (insurers screen for pre-existing conditions or charge more). Moral hazard (people use more care when insured). Information asymmetry (patients don't know what care is necessary). Network effects (the value of a health plan depends on which doctors are in network). Geographic monopsony in physician markets. The result is a system that produces substantial waste alongside substantial gains. The chapter we just finished doesn't tell you whether the U.S. healthcare system is good or bad. It tells you why none of the simple market models work for it.

The next chapter looks at financial markets — another domain where information problems are central, and where the institutional responses (banks, regulators, capital requirements) are themselves a major source of complexity.

## LLM Exercises

**Exercise 1 — Diagnose the type.** Give an LLM this list and ask it to classify each as primarily an adverse-selection problem, a moral-hazard problem, or both:
- A driver buys auto insurance, then drives more aggressively.
- A person with cancer buys life insurance without disclosing the diagnosis.
- A student gets a job at a software company that promises six months of severance, then puts in less effort.
- A homeowner installs a fire-suppression system after buying fire insurance.
- A bank knows its deposits are FDIC-insured, then makes riskier loans.

**Exercise 2 — Design a screening contract.** Tell an LLM you run an auto insurance company. You suspect your customers fall into two groups — careful drivers (low accident probability) and reckless drivers (high probability) — but you can't tell them apart. Ask the LLM to design two insurance contracts that, taken together, will get the careful drivers to choose one and the reckless drivers to choose the other. The exercise teaches the screening logic concretely.

**Exercise 3 — Predict the death spiral.** Tell an LLM a hypothetical: a state outlaws insurer screening for pre-existing conditions but does *not* require people to buy insurance. Ask it to walk through what should happen to the insurance market over the next several years using adverse-selection logic. Push: what additional policy would be needed to prevent collapse?

**Exercise 4 — Argue for and against the individual mandate.** Ask an LLM to argue both sides of the ACA individual mandate as a structural response to adverse selection. The pro side should focus on adverse-selection logic and pool stability. The anti side should focus on personal liberty and the costs of coercion. Evaluate which case is stronger and what evidence would settle the empirical disagreement.

**Exercise 5 — Identify a moral-hazard problem you face.** Tell an LLM about a contract or institutional arrangement in your own life (employment, insurance, banking, relationships) that you suspect produces moral-hazard effects. Ask the LLM to identify the specific mechanism and to suggest institutional changes that would reduce it. Evaluate whether the suggestion is structural or just exhortational.

**What would change my mind.** The case that government provision of social insurance is necessary depends on the specific failures of private insurance markets. If, in some specific market, the private adverse-selection problem could be solved through better information technology or better screening contracts, the case for government provision weakens. The current U.S. health-insurance market may or may not be one of those cases. The empirical question is open.

**Still puzzling.** I do not have a clean account of why some moral-hazard problems are large in some empirical settings and small in others. Auto insurance moral hazard appears to be modest; health insurance moral hazard varies enormously by category of care. The factors that make moral hazard large or small in a given market — visibility of behavior, marginal cost of the behavior, individual taste for the activity — are well-documented but predictively imprecise.
