# Chapter 9 — Monopoly

*The market with one seller — and what it costs everyone else.*

In May of 1773, the British Parliament passed an obscure piece of corporate-rescue legislation. The East India Company — a London-based trading conglomerate that had effectively run a third of the world for a century, and which we would today recognize as the original "too big to fail" — was struggling financially. Parliament's solution was to grant the company a monopoly on tea sales to the American colonies, with a continued tax that the colonies were already protesting.

By November, the colonists had had enough. On a December evening, men dressed as Mohawk warriors boarded three East India Company ships in Boston Harbor and dumped 342 chests of tea — about 92,000 pounds of it — into the water. The Boston Tea Party.

Two and a half years later, the colonies declared independence. The proximate cause wasn't the tax — taxes had been the issue for years. The proximate cause was the *monopoly grant*, which made it impossible for colonial merchants to compete in the tea trade and turned the colonies into a captive market for an English company they had no political voice in. A monopoly imposed by political power, used to extract value from people who had no exit option, became the spark for one of history's larger political revolutions.

Monopolies are not just an economic curiosity. They are an institutional choice with consequences large enough to be the subject of revolutions, antitrust suits, and recurring political fights. This chapter is about why.

We're going to do three things. First, look at *why* monopolies exist — the barriers to entry that protect a firm from competition. Second, work through the monopoly's pricing and output decision and see how it differs from the competitive firm we met in Chapter 8. Third, calculate what the monopoly costs society — the deadweight loss — and see how it compares with the perfect-competition benchmark.

By the end you should be able to look at a market dominated by one or a few firms and locate the source of the monopoly power, predict roughly what the firm is doing on its cost curves, and explain what's lost when the market doesn't get competed.

**Learning objectives.** By the end you should be able to: (1) define monopoly and identify the major sources of monopoly power; (2) distinguish natural monopolies from legal monopolies; (3) explain why a monopoly faces a downward-sloping demand curve and why marginal revenue is therefore less than price; (4) find a monopoly's profit-maximizing price and quantity using MR = MC; (5) compare a monopoly's outcome to the perfect-competition outcome and identify the deadweight loss.

**Prerequisites.** Chapter 7 (cost curves). Chapter 8 (perfect competition, P = MC, productive and allocative efficiency).

## Why monopolies exist

A monopoly is a market with one seller and no close substitutes. The interesting question is *why*. In Chapter 8 we saw that positive economic profit attracts entry, and entry competes profit down to zero. For a monopoly to persist with positive profit, *something must prevent entry*. The thing that prevents entry is what economists call a **barrier to entry**.

Barriers come in several flavors.

**Natural monopoly.** Some industries have such large economies of scale that one firm can serve the entire market at lower average cost than two firms could. Water utilities are the classic example: the cost of laying pipe is enormous and almost entirely fixed; the marginal cost of delivering an additional gallon is tiny. Two competing water companies, each with their own pipe network, would each have higher average cost than a single firm covering the whole city. Same for natural gas distribution, electricity transmission, the original AT&T telephone network. When a single firm has structurally lower cost than any pair of firms, "natural monopoly" emerges from the cost curves themselves.

**Legal monopoly.** A government grants exclusive rights. Patents (an inventor gets twenty years of monopoly on a drug or technology), copyrights (an author or musician gets decades of monopoly on a creative work), trademarks (a company gets exclusive use of a brand name), licenses (a city grants one taxi medallion at a time), franchises (the post office had a legal monopoly on first-class mail for over a century). The justification for legal monopolies is usually that they create incentives — to invent, to write, to invest in a brand. The cost is the period of higher prices and lower output.

**Control of a key resource.** If one firm owns essentially all of a critical input, no one else can compete. Alcoa controlled most of the world's bauxite (aluminum ore) for decades. De Beers controlled most of the world's diamond supply for the better part of the twentieth century — using the control of supply to maintain artificial scarcity and thus high prices. These cases are rare but enormously consequential when they exist.

**Network effects.** A product becomes more valuable as more people use it. The first telephone was useless. The hundredth made the network valuable. The millionth made it indispensable. Modern social networks (Facebook, X, LinkedIn), payment networks (Visa, Mastercard), platforms (App Store, Google Play) all exhibit strong network effects. A new entrant has to overcome not just the cost of building a competing product but the chicken-and-egg problem that no one wants the new product until other people are on it. Network effects can produce monopoly-like dominance even without legal protection or extreme economies of scale.

**Predatory pricing.** A dominant firm temporarily prices below cost to drive competitors out of the market, then raises prices once it's the only seller. This is illegal in most jurisdictions, but it's hard to prove — courts have to distinguish "aggressive but legitimate competition" from "below-cost pricing intended to monopolize."

In real markets, more than one barrier often operates at once. Apple has scale economies *and* network effects (the App Store) *and* IP protection (patents and copyrights) *and* significant brand power. Disentangling them is part of what antitrust authorities try to do. We'll come back to that in Chapter 11.

## How a monopoly decides

The competitive firm in Chapter 8 was a *price taker*. It faced a horizontal demand curve at the market price; its marginal revenue was always equal to that price; it produced where P = MC.

The monopoly is a *price maker*. It is the entire market, so its demand curve is the *market* demand curve — downward-sloping. To sell more, it has to lower the price.

Here's where things get subtle. When the monopoly sells one more unit, it has to lower the price not just on that unit, but on all the units it would have sold at the previous price too. So the *marginal revenue* from selling an additional unit is the new (lower) price *minus* the price reduction times all the units it was already selling.

This means **marginal revenue is below price for a monopoly**, and the marginal revenue curve lies below the demand curve.

A worked example. Suppose a monopolist faces a demand curve: at $10 the firm can sell 1 unit; at $9, 2 units; at $8, 3 units; at $7, 4 units. Total revenue at each level: 1×$10 = $10; 2×$9 = $18; 3×$8 = $24; 4×$7 = $28. Marginal revenue (the change in total revenue from each additional unit sold): $10, $8, $6, $4.

Notice the gap. To sell the 4th unit, the firm collects $7 from the buyer. But the marginal revenue is only $4, because the firm also had to lower the price from $8 to $7 on the 3 units it would have sold anyway. The price-cut-on-existing-units takes a bite out of the additional-unit-revenue.

The monopoly's profit-maximizing rule is the same as any firm's: produce up to the quantity where MR = MC. But because MR < P, this quantity is *lower* than the quantity where P = MC. The monopoly then *charges the price the demand curve will bear at that quantity* — which is read off the demand curve, not off the MR curve.

Two consequences. The monopoly produces less than a competitive industry would. The monopoly charges more than a competitive industry would.

## What it costs the rest of us

Now compare the monopoly outcome to the perfect-competition benchmark.

A perfectly competitive industry would produce up to the point where P = MC — the quantity at which the demand curve crosses the supply (= MC) curve. At that point, the marginal value of the good to consumers exactly equals its marginal cost of production. All the gains from trade are captured. Total surplus is maximized.

A monopoly produces only up to MR = MC, then charges the price the demand curve allows at that lower quantity. That price is *higher* than the marginal cost of producing the last unit. The gap (P − MC) is the slice of social value the monopoly is leaving on the table — units that would be worth producing (because consumers value them at more than MC) but the monopoly chooses not to produce because MR is too low.

This is the **deadweight loss** of monopoly. It's a triangle on the diagram, bounded by the demand curve, the marginal-cost curve, and the vertical line at the monopoly's quantity. The area of that triangle is the social value destroyed by monopoly pricing.

The monopoly also captures economic profit — the rectangle whose height is the gap between the monopoly price and the average cost at the monopoly quantity, and whose width is the quantity. This profit is not destroyed; it's *transferred* from consumers to the monopoly. Economists distinguish two kinds of welfare losses from monopoly: the *transfer* (a distributional issue, but no aggregate efficiency loss) and the *deadweight loss* (a real reduction in total surplus).

For a sense of scale: estimates of the total deadweight loss from market power in the U.S. economy run from very small (less than 1 percent of GDP, in some classical estimates) to several percent (in more recent estimates that try to account for all the ways modern firms exercise market power). The estimates vary because measuring counterfactual efficiency is hard. The point is that monopoly is not free, even when the monopoly is "earning" its profits through legitimate means.

A monopoly can also be productively *inefficient*. Without competitive pressure, the firm has weaker incentives to control costs. The X-inefficiency literature — going back to Harvey Leibenstein in the 1960s — argues that firms shielded from competition often operate above the bottom of their cost curves. A competitive firm has to be efficient to survive. A monopoly can be sloppy and still earn profit.

## A useful contrast — and a complication

A natural monopoly produces a particular regulatory dilemma worth flagging.

If you let a natural monopoly set its own price, it produces less than is socially efficient and charges more. But if you force it to price at marginal cost (the textbook efficiency condition), the price will be below average cost — because in a natural monopoly, average cost is still falling at the relevant output level, so MC is below ATC. The firm loses money on every unit. It exits the market or requires a government subsidy.

Real-world solutions usually compromise. Set the price at *average* cost — the firm breaks even, neither earning monopoly profits nor losing money. Allow some markup over marginal cost but constrain it through regulation. Or have the government own the asset directly and operate it on a cost-recovery basis. Each of these has trade-offs in terms of incentives, efficiency, and political accountability. Public utility regulation is a sprawling subfield built around these compromises. We'll touch on it in Chapter 11.

## Synthesis

Pull back. A monopoly is what happens when the conditions of perfect competition fail — when entry is blocked, when one firm has structural advantages no one can match, when network effects pull users into a single platform, when patents exist for good reasons but produce real costs. The model of monopoly is the same MR = MC machinery as any firm — but because MR < P for a monopoly, the result is lower output, higher price, deadweight loss to consumers, and economic profit transferred to the monopolist.

The deeper lesson: the case for competitive markets is not the case for laissez-faire. It's the case for *competition*, which sometimes requires institutional intervention — antitrust, patent expiration, regulation, occasionally public ownership — to prevent any single firm from becoming the entire market. The Boston Tea Party was the political response to a monopoly enforced by an empire. Modern antitrust is the institutional response to monopolies that arise from technology, network effects, and acquisition. Both share the conviction that one seller is too few, even when the seller is well-run.

The next chapter looks at the in-between cases — markets with several firms that aren't perfectly competitive but aren't monopolies either. That's where most of the interesting industrial economics lives.

## LLM Exercises

**Exercise 1 — Identify the barrier.** Pick three companies that look monopolistic in some product market — Google search, the only hospital in a small town, a regional electric utility, the U.S. Postal Service for first-class mail, your pick. Ask an LLM to identify the *primary* barrier to entry for each, naming it from the list (natural monopoly, legal, resource control, network effects, predatory pricing, brand). Then ask: what would have to change for a competitor to break in?

**Exercise 2 — Build the marginal revenue table.** Tell an LLM: "A monopoly faces this demand: at $20, 1 unit; $18, 2 units; $16, 3 units; $14, 4 units; $12, 5 units; $10, 6 units." Ask it to compute total revenue and marginal revenue at each quantity. Verify by hand. Then ask: at which quantity is marginal revenue zero, and what does that tell you about the price elasticity at that point?

**Exercise 3 — Find the deadweight loss.** Tell an LLM that a monopoly produces 4 units at a price of $14, while a perfectly competitive industry would produce 6 units at a price of $10 (with the same cost structure). Ask the LLM to identify (a) the consumer surplus transferred to the monopoly as profit, and (b) the deadweight loss to society. A correct answer distinguishes the two clearly.

**Exercise 4 — Argue both sides of a patent.** Ask an LLM to argue both sides: why a 20-year drug patent is justified, and why it's harmful. Evaluate which argument is stronger, and which costs and benefits the LLM tends to underweight. The exercise teaches that legal monopolies create real value and real costs at the same time.

**Exercise 5 — Diagnose a network-effect market.** Pick a platform with strong network effects — Uber, X, Facebook, Visa, the App Store. Ask an LLM to explain why competitors have failed to displace it, using the network-effects barrier specifically. Then ask: what *kind* of intervention (regulation, mandated interoperability, breakup, subsidized competitor) would a textbook economist recommend, and what's the strongest argument against each? Push the LLM if it gives a one-sided answer.

## What comes next

Chapter 10 — Monopolistic Competition and Oligopoly fills in the territory between perfect competition and monopoly: many firms with differentiated products (your local restaurants, your shampoo brands), and few firms with strategic interaction (the U.S. car industry, U.S. airlines, the global smartphone OS market). Chapter 11 — Antitrust Policy looks at how governments respond to firms that grow too dominant, with case studies including Standard Oil, AT&T, and Microsoft.

**What would change my mind.** The deadweight-loss argument against monopoly assumes a static comparison — what monopoly output is now versus what competitive output would be now. The argument weakens (and sometimes flips) when the monopoly profit funds R&D that wouldn't otherwise happen, as in many pharmaceutical cases. A drug that exists only because patent protection made it profitable to develop produces *value* — even at monopoly prices — that wouldn't exist without the monopoly. The static welfare loss has to be weighed against the dynamic gain. Whether the trade-off is worth making depends on the industry, and is a real source of disagreement among economists.

**Still puzzling.** I do not have a clean way to predict when a tech-platform monopoly will be dislodged. Yahoo, MySpace, and Nokia were all near-dominant before being displaced. Apple, Google, and Facebook have held on much longer. The mix of technological inertia, network effects, regulatory environment, and acquisition strategy that determines durability is something the textbook treatment skates past, and the empirical literature is still developing.
