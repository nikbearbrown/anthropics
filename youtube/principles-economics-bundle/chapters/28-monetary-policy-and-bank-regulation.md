# Chapter 28 — Monetary Policy and Bank Regulation

*The Federal Reserve — what it does, how it does it, and what it can't do.*

In December 2008, the Federal Reserve cut its benchmark interest rate to a target range of 0 to 0.25 percent — effectively zero. The U.S. economy was in the deepest recession since the Great Depression. Standard monetary policy says: in a recession, cut interest rates to encourage borrowing, investment, and spending. But the rate was already at zero. The Fed had run out of conventional ammunition.

What it did next was unprecedented. The Fed launched **quantitative easing (QE)** — buying massive quantities of long-term Treasury bonds and mortgage-backed securities directly. Over the next several years, the Fed's balance sheet expanded from roughly $900 billion to about $4.5 trillion. The goals: push down long-term interest rates, support asset prices, signal a sustained commitment to economic recovery.

The intervention was controversial — it was new, it was huge, and it raised novel questions about the boundary between monetary and fiscal policy. It also seems to have worked, in the limited sense that the U.S. recovery, while slow, was substantially better than what would have happened without intervention. The 2020 COVID shock prompted an even larger Fed expansion, peaking near $9 trillion.

This chapter is about the Federal Reserve and what it does. By the end you should understand how the Fed influences interest rates and the money supply, what its main tools are (including QE and the post-2008 framework), what bank supervision and regulation it does, and the major policy debates around central bank independence and effectiveness.

**Learning objectives.** By the end you should be able to: (1) describe the structure and governance of the Federal Reserve System; (2) explain the major monetary-policy tools (open market operations, discount rate, reserve requirements, IOR); (3) distinguish expansionary from contractionary monetary policy and trace each through the AD-AS framework; (4) explain quantitative easing and the zero-lower-bound problem; (5) describe the bank-supervision and lender-of-last-resort roles of the Fed.

**Prerequisites.** Chapter 24 (AD-AS). Chapter 25 (Keynesian framework). Chapter 27 (money and banks).

## How the Fed is structured

The **Federal Reserve System** was created by Congress in 1913, in response to a series of banking panics in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Its purpose: provide a stable currency, supervise banks, serve as lender of last resort, and (later) conduct monetary policy.

The Fed has three main parts:

**The Board of Governors.** Seven members appointed by the U.S. President and confirmed by the Senate to 14-year terms. The Chair (a 4-year renewable term) is appointed from the Board. Located in Washington, D.C.

**Twelve regional Federal Reserve Banks.** Each district covers a major U.S. region. They handle banking-system services, supervision of banks in their district, and economic research. The presidents of these banks serve in monetary-policy decisions.

**The Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC).** This is the body that makes the most important monetary-policy decisions. It consists of the seven Board governors plus five regional Fed bank presidents (rotating, except for the New York Fed president, who's permanent). It meets eight times a year to set interest rates and decide on other monetary actions.

The Fed's **independence** from the executive branch is unusual among U.S. agencies. Long terms (14 years), staggered appointments, and a structure that prevents any single president from controlling the institution were designed to insulate monetary policy from short-run political pressure. The independence is not absolute — Congress can change the Fed's mandate, and the President appoints governors — but in practice, monetary decisions are made on technical grounds with relatively little direct political interference.

The Fed has a **dual mandate** from Congress: maximum employment and stable prices. Most other major central banks (European Central Bank, Bank of England) have inflation control as their primary mandate; the U.S. is unusual in formally requiring the Fed to balance both.

## The traditional toolkit

Before 2008, the Fed used three main tools to influence the money supply and interest rates:

**Open Market Operations (OMO).** The Fed buys or sells U.S. Treasury securities in the open market.
- *Buying* securities pumps reserves into the banking system → banks have more reserves → lower interest rates → more lending → expansionary.
- *Selling* securities drains reserves → tighter banking → higher interest rates → contractionary.

OMO has been the Fed's primary tool. Day-to-day adjustments to bank reserves are made through OMO, conducted by the New York Fed at the direction of the FOMC.

**Discount rate.** The interest rate the Fed charges banks that borrow directly from it (the "discount window"). Lowering the discount rate makes it cheaper for banks to borrow, encouraging more lending. Raising it discourages borrowing.

In practice, the discount rate has been less central than OMO, because banks borrow from each other (the federal funds market) rather than from the Fed except in emergencies.

**Reserve requirements.** The fraction of deposits banks must hold as reserves. The Fed can lower the requirement (encouraging more lending) or raise it (discouraging it). The Fed eliminated formal reserve requirements in March 2020 as part of its post-2008 operating framework changes.

## Expansionary vs. contractionary policy

**Expansionary monetary policy** — increasing the money supply, lowering interest rates — is used during recessions or when inflation is below target. The mechanism:

1. Fed buys securities (OMO).
2. Banks have more reserves; interest rates fall.
3. Households and firms borrow more.
4. Investment and consumption rise.
5. Aggregate demand shifts right.
6. Real GDP rises; unemployment falls; some inflation pressure.

**Contractionary monetary policy** — decreasing the money supply, raising interest rates — is used to fight inflation. Mechanism is the reverse:

1. Fed sells securities (OMO).
2. Bank reserves fall; interest rates rise.
3. Borrowing slows.
4. Investment and consumption fall.
5. Aggregate demand shifts left.
6. Inflation pressure declines; real GDP slows.

Both directions work through interest rates, which work through investment decisions and credit-sensitive spending (mortgages, cars, durable goods).

## The zero lower bound problem

Standard monetary policy assumed interest rates are positive — the Fed can always cut them to fight a recession. Then in 2008, the Fed cut rates to essentially zero and the recession kept getting worse. The Fed had hit the **zero lower bound (ZLB)**.

Why is zero a lower bound? In theory, central banks could push rates negative — and some have (the European Central Bank, the Bank of Japan, the Swiss National Bank). In practice, sustained negative rates are limited because depositors can convert to physical cash to avoid the negative rate, which puts a floor on how negative rates can go.

When the policy rate hits the ZLB, the Fed can't use the standard tool. It has to find unconventional approaches.

**Quantitative easing (QE).** Buy long-term assets directly, pushing down long-term interest rates (which the federal funds rate doesn't directly affect when the policy rate is at zero). The Fed's QE programs from 2008 to 2014 bought about $3.5 trillion of long-term Treasuries and mortgage-backed securities. The 2020–2022 QE was even larger.

**Forward guidance.** Announce explicitly how long policy rates will remain low. If markets believe the announcement, long-term rates fall (because long rates depend on expected future short rates). The Fed has used forward guidance extensively since 2009.

**Operation Twist** (2011–2012). Sell short-term securities and buy long-term ones, flattening the yield curve without expanding the balance sheet.

QE and the unconventional toolkit have been controversial. The arguments for: the Fed had to do something, and these tools, while novel, had measurable effects on long-term rates and asset prices. The arguments against: blurring the line between monetary and fiscal policy, distorting asset markets, generating financial-stability risks. The empirical evidence has been mixed; most macroeconomists now accept QE as a legitimate tool but with appropriate skepticism about its long-run effects.

## Bank supervision and regulation

Beyond monetary policy, the Fed is one of several U.S. agencies that supervises banks. Major activities:

**Capital requirements.** Banks must hold a minimum amount of equity capital relative to their assets. Higher capital means more loss-absorption capacity. Post-2008 reforms (Basel III, Dodd-Frank) substantially raised capital requirements for U.S. banks.

**Liquidity requirements.** Banks must hold enough liquid assets to cover potential outflows during a stress period.

**Stress tests.** Annual exercises requiring large banks to model their performance under hypothetical economic shocks. Failure can trigger regulatory action.

**Resolution authority.** Mechanisms for orderly resolution of failing banks, designed to avoid the disorderly bankruptcy that contributed to the 2008 crisis (e.g., Lehman Brothers).

**Lender of last resort.** In a panic, the Fed will lend to solvent but illiquid banks, providing emergency liquidity. The principle dates to Walter Bagehot in 1873: lend freely, at high rates, against good collateral. The Fed's emergency lending in 2008 followed this principle.

## Velocity of money

A useful conceptual tool. The **equation of exchange** states:

> M × V = P × Q

Where M is money supply, V is velocity (the average number of times each dollar is spent per year), P is the price level, and Q is real output (GDP).

Rearranging: nominal GDP (P × Q) equals the money supply times its velocity. If velocity is constant, increases in M produce proportional increases in P × Q. This is the basis of the **quantity theory of money** — the long-run claim that excessive money creation produces inflation.

In the short run, V is not constant — it fluctuates with the business cycle, financial conditions, and monetary policy. The Fed's massive monetary expansion 2008–2014 didn't produce massive inflation because V fell sharply at the same time, leaving M × V (and therefore nominal GDP) growing only modestly.

In the long run, V is more stable, and the link between M and P (money and inflation) reasserts itself. This is one reason the Fed's monetary expansion 2020–2022, when combined with already-rising V and a supply-constrained economy, did produce a substantial inflation episode.

## Synthesis

Pull back. The Federal Reserve is the institutional center of U.S. monetary policy. Its structure (Board, regional banks, FOMC) is designed for a balance of central direction and regional input, with substantial independence from short-run political pressure. Its dual mandate is maximum employment and stable prices.

Its main pre-2008 tools were OMO (buying/selling Treasuries to influence reserves and rates), the discount rate, and reserve requirements. Post-2008, with the policy rate at zero and reserves abundant, the toolkit has changed: QE for direct long-term-rate intervention, forward guidance for managing expectations, and a new operating framework that uses interest on reserves to set the policy rate.

The Fed also supervises banks, sets capital requirements, runs stress tests, and serves as lender of last resort. The bank-regulation and monetary-policy roles overlap because both depend on a healthy banking system — monetary transmission requires banks to be willing and able to lend.

Independence of the central bank is institutional armor that lets monetary policy work over multiple political cycles. The Fed's credibility, built up over decades, is what makes its forward guidance and inflation-fighting commitments effective. Loss of credibility would substantially impair the framework. Maintaining it is one of the Fed's central institutional responsibilities.
