# Chapter 11 — Monopoly and Antitrust Policy

*The legal apparatus that tries to keep markets from collapsing into one seller — and why it sometimes fails in both directions.*

In 1911, the U.S. Supreme Court ordered the breakup of Standard Oil, which by then controlled roughly 90 percent of American oil refining. The company was split into 34 separate firms — including the predecessors of what would later become Exxon, Mobil, Chevron, and Amoco. The decision was the largest application yet of a 1890 law called the **Sherman Antitrust Act**, and it set the precedent for federal action against firms that had become "too large" — though what "too large" actually means has been the subject of continuous legal and economic argument ever since.

In 1982, after a thirty-year process, the Department of Justice broke up AT&T — the company that had operated essentially every telephone in the United States for most of the twentieth century. The breakup created seven regional "Baby Bell" companies and freed long-distance service for competitors. Within fifteen years, the long-distance industry had been transformed and the price of a long-distance call had fallen by more than 90 percent.

In 2024, the Department of Justice and several state attorneys general were prosecuting Google for monopolizing internet search and online advertising. As I write this, the case is still working through the courts. Google argues that its dominance is the product of a better product. The government argues that contracts paying device manufacturers and browsers to set Google as the default search engine — and other practices — have insulated Google from competition. Whatever the verdict, the structural question is the same one Standard Oil's lawyers and AT&T's lawyers faced: when does dominance cross from "earned by being good" to "maintained by tactics that block competitors"?

This chapter is about that legal apparatus. The model from Chapters 8 and 9 told us *what* monopoly costs society. The model from Chapter 10 told us *how* a few firms can produce monopoly-like outcomes through coordination. This chapter is about the institutional response: the laws, the agencies, the regulatory frameworks that exist to prevent or correct excessive market power. By the end you should be able to read the news about a major antitrust case and locate it in the framework — what kind of market power is being alleged, what remedy is being sought, what the costs and benefits of intervention look like.

**Learning objectives.** By the end you should be able to: (1) identify the major U.S. antitrust laws and their core prohibitions; (2) calculate the four-firm concentration ratio and the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index from market-share data; (3) evaluate the standard antitrust remedies — blocked merger, behavioral relief, structural breakup; (4) explain the regulatory dilemma for natural monopolies and contrast cost-plus with price-cap regulation; (5) define regulatory capture and identify when it's likely.

**Prerequisites.** Chapter 9 (monopoly, deadweight loss). Chapter 10 (oligopoly, cartels).

## The legal toolkit

Three U.S. laws form the core of American antitrust.

**The Sherman Act (1890).** Prohibits "every contract, combination... or conspiracy in restraint of trade or commerce" (Section 1) and prohibits monopolization or attempts to monopolize (Section 2). Broad and old. Most of the heavy enforcement work since has used or extended this statute.

**The Clayton Act (1914).** Targets specific anticompetitive practices the Sherman Act handled awkwardly — price discrimination, exclusive-dealing contracts that lock customers in, and (importantly) mergers that "may substantially lessen competition." This is the statute that controls modern merger review.

**The Federal Trade Commission Act (1914).** Created the FTC and prohibited "unfair methods of competition" — a deliberately flexible standard that lets enforcement evolve as new business practices appear.

Two agencies enforce these laws: the **Federal Trade Commission (FTC)** and the **Antitrust Division of the U.S. Department of Justice (DOJ)**. They split jurisdiction informally; in practice, they coordinate to avoid stepping on each other's cases.

The economic content of antitrust enforcement focuses on three things: mergers (block or modify), restrictive practices (sue or fine), and outright monopolization (sue or break up).

## Measuring concentration

Before regulators can decide whether a market is too concentrated, they need a way to measure concentration. Two metrics are standard.

**Four-firm concentration ratio (CR4).** The combined market share of the four largest firms in the industry. CR4 = 50 percent means the top four firms together hold half the market. CR4 above 80 percent is generally considered highly concentrated; CR4 below 40 percent is generally considered competitive.

**Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI).** Sum of the squared market shares of all firms in the industry. If one firm holds 100 percent, HHI = 100² = 10,000 (the maximum). If the market has ten firms each with 10 percent, HHI = 10 × 100 = 1,000. The squaring matters: it gives proportionally more weight to large firms. A market with one firm at 50 percent and ten firms at 5 percent each has HHI = 2,500 + 250 = 2,750 — much higher than ten firms with 10 percent each.

DOJ's standard thresholds (as of recent guidelines): HHI below 1,500 is unconcentrated; 1,500–2,500 is moderately concentrated; above 2,500 is highly concentrated. A merger that would push HHI above the moderate threshold *and* increase HHI by more than 200 points typically triggers detailed review.

Numbers like these are tools, not verdicts. A market with HHI of 4,000 in a small town might still be competitive (because new entry is easy); a market with HHI of 1,800 might be effectively monopolized (because barriers are high). The regulators look at the numbers, then look at the structure.

## Three categories of enforcement

### Mergers

Most antitrust action is on mergers. The standard process: large companies planning to combine notify the FTC and DOJ. The agencies review whether the combined firm would substantially reduce competition. Most mergers go through unchallenged. Some are approved with conditions (the merging firms must divest specific assets to maintain competition). Some are blocked — though "blocked" usually means the agencies signal they will sue, and the firms abandon the deal rather than fight in court.

Famous merger blocks: AT&T's proposed acquisition of T-Mobile in 2011 (blocked by DOJ; would have left three major U.S. carriers). Staples' proposed merger with Office Depot in 2015 (blocked by FTC; would have created near-duopoly in office supplies). Penguin Random House's proposed acquisition of Simon & Schuster in 2022 (blocked by DOJ on grounds that it would harm competition for author advances).

The merger review framework is, on the whole, well-developed. The agencies have decades of practice. The error margin in either direction — letting harmful mergers through, blocking benign ones — is real but relatively narrow.

### Restrictive practices

These are tactics that don't change the structure of the industry but distort competition within it. Examples:

**Price-fixing collusion.** Two or more firms agree to set a common price. The detergent cartel from Chapter 10. This is per se illegal — no need to prove anticompetitive effects; the agreement itself is the violation.

**Bid-rigging.** Two or more firms in a competitive bid agree on which will "win" and at what price. Common in industries with repeat procurement (construction, defense contracts, vitamin supply).

**Tying and bundling.** Selling product A only to customers who also buy product B. The Microsoft case in the 1990s (must take Internet Explorer to get Windows) was a famous example. The practice can be efficient (better integration) or anticompetitive (leveraging a monopoly in one product to dominate another); the agencies look at intent and effects.

**Predatory pricing.** Discussed in Chapter 9. Pricing below cost to drive competitors out, then raising prices later. Hard to prove because courts have to distinguish "aggressive but lawful competition" from "below-cost pricing meant to monopolize."

**Exclusive dealing and exclusive territories.** Contracts that prevent retailers from selling competitors' products, or carve up territories among distributors. Sometimes legal, sometimes not — depends on whether the practice forecloses substantial competition.

Restrictive-practices cases often turn on what economists call the *rule of reason*: do the practices, on balance, harm or help competition? Courts weigh evidence of efficiency benefits against evidence of anticompetitive effects. The analysis is rarely clean.

### Monopolization

The hardest cases. A firm has dominant market share. Did it get there by being good (legal) or by tactics that exclude competitors (illegal)? The line is fuzzy.

Standard Oil (1911), AT&T (1982), Microsoft (the 1990s case ended in a behavioral remedy rather than a breakup), Google (ongoing in 2024) — all of these are or were monopolization cases. The remedies range from behavioral (the firm must change specific practices) to structural (the firm must be split into smaller pieces).

Structural breakups are dramatic but rare. Behavioral remedies are common but harder to enforce, because the firm has every incentive to find new ways to do what the old way is now prohibited from doing. Microsoft's behavioral remedy from the early 2000s is widely regarded as having had limited effect on the underlying market structure. AT&T's structural breakup in 1982 reshaped a whole industry. The trade-off between dramatic and durable cuts both ways.

## The natural-monopoly dilemma — and how to regulate

We met natural monopoly in Chapter 9. The cost structure makes one firm cheaper than two. Splitting up a natural monopoly raises average costs and consumer prices. Letting it operate unregulated produces monopoly pricing. So most natural monopolies are regulated.

The two main regulatory frameworks are **cost-plus** and **price-cap**.

**Cost-plus regulation.** The regulator sets the price at "the firm's costs plus a fair rate of return on capital." Sounds simple. The problem is that the firm now has every incentive to *inflate* its costs — gold-plate the equipment, hire excess staff, run a luxurious headquarters — because higher costs allow higher prices. The classic case study is U.S. utility regulation through much of the 20th century, where regulated utilities chronically over-invested in capacity and gold-plated their operations.

**Price-cap regulation.** The regulator sets a price (or a price ceiling) and then leaves the firm alone. The firm gets to keep the gains from any cost reductions. The incentive is now for efficiency. The problem is that the regulator has to set the cap right — too high and consumers overpay; too low and the firm exits or stops investing. The U.K. moved to price-cap regulation for many privatized utilities in the 1980s and 1990s, with mixed results.

Both frameworks rely on the regulator having good information about the firm's actual cost structure. The firm, of course, has every incentive to make this information hard to obtain. The asymmetry is a permanent feature of regulation, not a fixable bug.

## Regulatory capture

The deeper problem with regulating any concentrated industry is **regulatory capture** — the tendency for the regulators to gradually identify with the regulated industry's interests rather than with consumers'.

The mechanism is straightforward. Regulators spend their careers in one industry. They develop relationships with industry executives. They depend on the industry for the technical information needed to regulate it. They often hope to work in the industry after leaving government. The pressures all push in one direction — toward sympathy with the regulated firm.

George Stigler, a Chicago-school economist, formalized this in 1971: "Regulation is acquired by the industry and is designed and operated primarily for its benefit." This is a strong claim and not universally true, but the empirical record offers many cases that fit it. The original purpose of much U.S. transportation regulation (railroads, trucking, airlines) was to protect incumbent firms from competition; consumer protection was secondary. The deregulation of airlines, trucking, and other industries starting in the late 1970s was driven partly by recognition that the regulatory system had been captured.

Capture is also why some economists prefer market-based remedies (more competition, lower entry barriers) over direct regulation when the choice is available. A regulated monopoly with captured regulators may be worse than an unregulated monopoly under competitive pressure. Whether that's true in any specific case is empirical, not theoretical.

## Synthesis

Pull back. Antitrust policy is the institutional response to the market structures we examined in Chapters 9 and 10. Its tools: merger review, prohibition of restrictive practices, and (rarely) monopolization remedies including breakup. Its measures: concentration ratios and HHI. Its agencies: FTC and DOJ Antitrust Division. Its frameworks for natural monopoly: cost-plus or price-cap regulation, both of which are imperfect because the regulator can't fully see the firm's cost structure and is vulnerable to capture.

Two recurring lessons. First, *enforcement is itself imperfect*. Breakups are dramatic but rare. Behavioral remedies are common but often weak. Merger review catches some cases and misses others. Regulatory frameworks bend toward the regulated firms over time. Second, *the alternative to imperfect enforcement is not laissez-faire perfection*. A world without antitrust is a world where the temptations of Chapter 10 — coordinate to extract monopoly profits — go unchecked. The choice is not between perfect markets and government intervention. It is between two imperfect responses to the same underlying structural problem.

The Standard Oil breakup was a structural success. The AT&T breakup was a structural success that required decades of follow-on regulation. The Microsoft case is widely seen as having achieved less than was hoped. Google may be the next chapter; the verdict is not yet in. Each case is a calibration of the same trade-off: how much enforcement, applied how, to which dominant firms, with what remedies. The model gives you the framework for asking those questions. The answers depend on the specific case and the specific moment.

## LLM Exercises

**Exercise 1 — Compute concentration.** Tell an LLM: "Suppose an industry has firms with market shares 30%, 25%, 15%, 10%, 8%, 5%, 4%, 3%." Ask it to compute the four-firm concentration ratio and the HHI. Verify both by hand. Then ask: by current DOJ thresholds, how should this market be classified? What additional information would you need to evaluate whether intervention is warranted?

**Exercise 2 — Argue both sides of an antitrust case.** Pick a current or recent antitrust case (Google search, Apple App Store, Live Nation/Ticketmaster, your pick). Ask an LLM to argue both sides: that the firm's dominance is the result of a better product (no intervention warranted) and that the firm's dominance is maintained by anticompetitive tactics (intervention warranted). Evaluate which argument is stronger and what the determining evidence would be.

**Exercise 3 — Design a remedy.** Tell an LLM that a hypothetical case has been decided against a dominant firm and the court is now choosing a remedy. Ask the LLM to compare a structural remedy (breaking the firm into pieces) and a behavioral remedy (requiring the firm to change specific practices) — strengths and weaknesses of each. A good answer notes that breakups are durable but blunt, and behavioral remedies are flexible but easy to circumvent.

**Exercise 4 — Cost-plus vs. price-cap.** Tell an LLM you are the regulator for a natural-monopoly water utility in a mid-sized city. Ask it to lay out the trade-offs between cost-plus and price-cap regulation for this case. Push: which mode would create stronger incentives for the firm to invest in long-term infrastructure? Which would protect consumers more in the short run? The exercise tests whether the LLM names the gold-plating problem (cost-plus) and the under-investment / quality-cutting risk (price-cap).

**Exercise 5 — Spot regulatory capture.** Pick an industry that critics often accuse of having captured regulators (banking, defense, pharmaceuticals, energy, agriculture). Ask an LLM to identify three pieces of evidence that *could* indicate capture, and three pieces of evidence that *would* indicate the regulators are still serving the public interest. Then ask: what's the strongest single test you could run to distinguish between the two hypotheses?

Chapter 13 — Positive Externalities and Public Goods covers the mirror case (vaccines, basic research) where the market under-produces. Together, Chapters 12 and 13 expand the menu of "things markets can fail to do well" beyond the structural concentration we've been examining.

**What would change my mind.** The case for vigorous antitrust enforcement depends on the assumption that breaking up or constraining dominant firms produces better outcomes for consumers than leaving them alone. The empirical record is mixed. Some breakups (AT&T) clearly improved consumer welfare. Some interventions (the Microsoft behavioral remedy) appeared to do little. The blanket claim that "more antitrust = better outcomes" is too strong; "well-targeted antitrust = better outcomes" is more defensible but harder to operationalize.

**Still puzzling.** I do not have a clean way to predict when behavioral remedies stick and when they erode. The agencies sign consent decrees with specific compliance requirements; some of these are followed for decades, others are evaded within a few years. The factors that determine durability — the firm's culture, the agencies' monitoring resources, the visibility of compliance, the speed of technological change — are documented but not predictively crisp.
