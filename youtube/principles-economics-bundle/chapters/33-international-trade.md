# Chapter 33 — International Trade

*Why countries trade — and the math that shows trade can leave both sides better off, even when one side is "better at everything."*

Look at the iPhone in your pocket. The aluminum in its frame may have been mined in Australia, refined in China, and machined in Vietnam. The display is from South Korea. The chips are designed in California, fabricated in Taiwan, and assembled into the device in China. The battery comes from Japan or South Korea. The rare-earth elements in dozens of small components come from China and parts of Africa. The software was written in California with contributions from teams across the world.

When you bought the phone, the price you paid reflected the labor of probably hundreds of thousands of workers in dozens of countries, the design and engineering of thousands more, and the capital invested across decades by Apple and its suppliers. None of these flows would have happened in a closed economy. International trade is the institutional infrastructure that makes the global supply chain possible.

This chapter is about international trade — what it is, why countries do it, and the framework economists use to evaluate the gains. The next chapter (34) covers the political backlash against trade and the policy debates that surround it. Together, they're the closing of this book.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to apply absolute and comparative advantage with worked numerical examples, see why even a country that's "better at everything" benefits from trade, and understand why most modern trade is between similar countries trading similar goods (which doesn't fit the standard comparative-advantage story neatly).

**Learning objectives.** By the end you should be able to: (1) define absolute advantage and comparative advantage; (2) use a numerical example to demonstrate the gains from trade between two countries; (3) explain why a country can have absolute advantage in a good and still not have comparative advantage in it; (4) describe intra-industry trade and explain why it's so common; (5) identify the dynamic gains from trade beyond the static comparative-advantage story.

**Prerequisites.** Chapter 1 (specialization). Chapter 2 (PPF, opportunity cost, comparative advantage). Chapter 23 (current account).

## The gains from trade — a worked example

Two countries — call them U.S. and Brazil. Both produce wheat and sugar. U.S. workers are more productive at both. Specifically:

- One U.S. worker can produce 6 tons of wheat per year OR 4 tons of sugar per year.
- One Brazilian worker can produce 2 tons of wheat per year OR 3 tons of sugar per year.

The U.S. has **absolute advantage** in both — its workers produce more of either good than Brazilian workers do. Naively, you might conclude Brazil shouldn't bother trading with the U.S.

The economist's answer is different. Look at *opportunity costs*, not absolute productivity.

If a U.S. worker produces 6 wheat instead of 4 sugar, the cost of those 6 wheat is the 4 sugar foregone. Per ton of wheat, the cost is 4/6 = 0.67 sugar.

If a Brazilian worker produces 2 wheat instead of 3 sugar, the cost of those 2 wheat is the 3 sugar foregone. Per ton of wheat, the cost is 3/2 = 1.5 sugar.

**The U.S. has comparative advantage in wheat** because its opportunity cost of wheat is lower (0.67 vs. 1.5 sugar per wheat).

Conversely, the cost of one ton of sugar in the U.S. is 6/4 = 1.5 wheat. In Brazil, the cost is 2/3 = 0.67 wheat. **Brazil has comparative advantage in sugar** because its opportunity cost of sugar is lower.

Now suppose each country produces 100 worker-years of labor. Without trade:
- U.S. divides labor 50/50: produces 300 wheat + 200 sugar.
- Brazil divides labor 50/50: produces 100 wheat + 150 sugar.
- Total world output: 400 wheat + 350 sugar.

With trade, each country specializes in its comparative-advantage good:
- U.S. devotes all 100 worker-years to wheat: 600 wheat.
- Brazil devotes all 100 worker-years to sugar: 300 sugar.
- Total world output: 600 wheat + 300 sugar.

Compare. Total wheat rose from 400 to 600 (gain of 200). Total sugar fell from 350 to 300 (loss of 50). Specialization plus trade has raised total output. The countries can trade with each other and end up with combinations of wheat and sugar that neither could have produced alone.

For example, if they trade at a price of 1 wheat per 0.7 sugar, the U.S. could trade 200 wheat for 140 sugar. The U.S. ends up with 400 wheat + 140 sugar. Brazil ends up with 200 wheat + 160 sugar. Compared to no trade: U.S. has 100 more wheat and 60 fewer sugar; Brazil has 100 more wheat and 10 more sugar.

Both countries are better off. The U.S. could have made all 400 of its wheat alone but not while having 140 sugar (it would only have 140 sugar if it gave up 6/4 × 200 = 300 wheat — leaving only 300 wheat). Brazil could have made all 200 of its wheat alone but not while having 160 sugar (it would have 160 sugar only if it gave up 1.5 × 200 = 300 wheat — leaving only zero wheat). Trade lets both countries reach combinations of consumption that wouldn't be possible without trade.

This is the basic Ricardian comparative-advantage demonstration. The math is beautiful. The conclusion is robust: if countries differ in their relative productivities (their PPFs slope differently), specialization plus trade can raise total output and make both countries better off.

## Why "better at everything" doesn't kill the gains

The U.S. has absolute advantage in both goods. Yet trade with Brazil benefits both countries. This is the unintuitive part of the result, and it's worth pausing on.

The reason is opportunity cost. Each U.S. worker produces 6 wheat *or* 4 sugar. Choosing one means giving up the other. The U.S. has high productivity in both goods but a particular *trade-off* between them.

Brazil has lower productivity in both goods but a *different* trade-off. Per worker, Brazil sacrifices less wheat to make more sugar than the U.S. does. So Brazil should make sugar, even though it's worse at it absolutely than the U.S. is. The U.S. should make wheat, where its advantage is largest, even though it's *also* better than Brazil at sugar.

The real-world analog: a brain surgeon and her secretary. The brain surgeon could probably type faster than her secretary. But the surgeon should perform brain surgery (where her productivity advantage over the secretary is enormous) and let the secretary type (where the surgeon's advantage is small relative to the cost of her time). Both are better off. Same principle as the U.S. and Brazil.

## Modern trade — intra-industry and supply chains

The Ricardian framework predicts trade between countries that are very *different* (rich and poor, with very different relative productivities). It predicts much less trade between countries that are very *similar*.

The actual pattern of modern trade is the opposite. Most international trade happens between *similar* developed countries. The U.S. trades enormously with Canada, Mexico, the EU, Japan. Cars flow both ways across the Pacific. German cars sell in the U.S., U.S. cars sell in Germany. This is **intra-industry trade** — trade in similar goods between countries with similar productivity.

The standard Ricardian comparative-advantage story doesn't predict this well. The explanations:

**Product differentiation.** Cars are not identical. German Porsches and American Cadillacs are differentiated; consumers in both countries have heterogeneous preferences; both products find buyers in both countries. Each country has comparative advantage in its specific differentiated variety.

**Economies of scale.** Producing a particular car model at large scale is cheaper than producing many models at small scale. International trade lets each country specialize in particular varieties at scale, then trade for the others.

**Global supply chains.** Modern production is fragmented. Components, sub-assemblies, design, marketing all happen in different countries. Apple designs the iPhone in California; chips are made in Taiwan; assembly is in China. The "trade" we measure is gross flows, not net value-added in each country.

These features mean modern trade theory has moved beyond simple Ricardian comparative advantage to incorporate scale, differentiation, and supply-chain fragmentation. The basic insight that trade creates value remains; the specific mechanisms have multiplied.

## Dynamic gains beyond comparative advantage

Beyond the static gains from trade, there are dynamic gains that compound over time.

**Productivity transfer.** Firms that compete with foreign competitors face pressure to improve productivity. Closed-economy firms can rest on local-market dominance.

**Technology spillovers.** International trade brings exposure to new ideas, methods, and technologies. Engineers learn from competitors abroad. Workers absorb new skills.

**Specialization deepening.** Larger markets allow more specialization. The U.S. has hundreds of specialized law firms, medical practices, software firms, etc., that wouldn't exist in a smaller market.

**Investment incentives.** Larger markets justify larger investments. Pharmaceutical R&D is profitable partly because the global market for a successful drug is enormous.

These dynamic gains are harder to measure precisely than the static gains, but the empirical evidence broadly supports them. Countries that have opened to trade have generally seen productivity growth above what closed-economy peers experienced.

## Synthesis

Pull back. International trade exists because it can leave both sides better off. The Ricardian comparative-advantage demonstration shows mathematically how a country with worse absolute productivity in everything can still benefit from trade — by specializing in goods where its disadvantage is smallest and trading for the others. The mechanism is opportunity cost: it's the trade-offs that matter, not the absolute productivity levels.

Modern trade is more complex than Ricardian theory predicts. Most trade is intra-industry between similar developed economies, driven by product differentiation, economies of scale, and global supply chains. Apple's iPhone is the canonical case — components and labor from many countries combined into a single product whose value is shared across the global supply chain.

The dynamic gains from trade — productivity transfer, technology spillovers, specialization deepening, larger investment justifications — compound over time and may exceed the static gains the comparative-advantage framework captures. Countries that have opened to trade have generally outperformed closed-economy peers over decades.

The static and dynamic gains are real and substantial. The next chapter covers the costs that come with trade — the concentrated losses for workers in specific industries, the political backlash, the policy debates over protectionism and trade adjustment. Both the gains and the costs are real, and the policy challenge is how to maximize the former while addressing the latter.

## LLM Exercises

**Exercise 1 — Solve a comparative-advantage problem.** Tell an LLM: "Country A produces 10 units of wheat or 5 units of cloth per worker. Country B produces 4 units of wheat or 8 units of cloth per worker. Which country has comparative advantage in wheat? In cloth? At what price ratio could both gain from trade?" Verify the math.

**Exercise 2 — Trace a supply chain.** Pick a complex product (smartphone, car, laptop, designer handbag). Ask an LLM to trace the major components and their countries of origin. The exercise teaches that "trade" is much more than finished-goods exchange.

**Exercise 3 — Stress-test the gains.** Ask an LLM: "Under what conditions does the simple comparative-advantage gains-from-trade story fail or weaken?" Push for the conditions: monopoly behavior, zero domestic productivity, infant industries, externalities. Notice which conditions the LLM identifies.

**Exercise 4 — Diagnose intra-industry trade.** Ask an LLM why the U.S. and Germany trade enormous quantities of cars in both directions. The Ricardian framework doesn't predict this — what does? Push for product differentiation, economies of scale, supply chain explanations.

**Exercise 5 — Estimate the dynamic gains.** Ask an LLM to estimate how much of South Korea's economic transformation since 1960 was due to access to global markets vs. domestic policy choices. The exercise teaches the difficulty of separating dynamic gains from other growth factors.

The current evidence broadly supports trade as growth-enhancing, but specific country experiences vary.
