# Chapter 25 — The Keynesian Perspective

*The case for active stabilization — when the economy needs help, and what the help looks like.*

By 1933, four years into the Great Depression, the U.S. unemployment rate had reached 25 percent. About one in four working-age adults had no job. Industrial production had fallen by half. Banks were failing in waves. The mainstream economic view of the 1920s — that markets self-correct, that wages and prices would adjust to bring the economy back to full employment, that intervention was unnecessary and counterproductive — was being tested and was failing badly.

In 1936, John Maynard Keynes published *The General Theory of Employment, Interest, and Money*. The book argued that the self-correction story was wrong. In the short run, prices and wages don't adjust fast enough to clear markets. Demand-side recessions can persist for years without the supply side providing a fix. The cure has to come from outside — government spending and tax policy that raises aggregate demand directly.

The framework Keynes built became the basis for active macroeconomic stabilization policy in most of the developed world. Its successes (the postwar prosperity, the U.S. response to 2008–09, the COVID rescue) and its failures (the 1970s stagflation that undermined the framework's credibility) are the macroeconomic story of the past 90 years.

This chapter develops the Keynesian framework. By the end you should understand what makes the Keynesian view distinct, why it advocates for fiscal stimulus during recessions, how the multiplier works, and what the Phillips curve says about the trade-off between unemployment and inflation.

**Learning objectives.** By the end you should be able to: (1) explain why Keynesians believe wages and prices are sticky in the short run; (2) define and apply the expenditure multiplier; (3) describe the four main components of aggregate demand and how each can be influenced by policy; (4) explain the Phillips curve and the historical evidence for and against it; (5) identify the major Keynesian fiscal policy tools and their limitations.

**Prerequisites.** Chapter 21 (sticky wages). Chapter 24 (AD-AS model).

## Sticky prices, sluggish adjustment

The Keynesian framework starts with one observation: in the short run, prices and wages don't adjust fast enough to clear markets after a demand shock.

The mechanism. Demand for the firm's product falls. The firm has two ways to respond: cut the price (which keeps quantity sold high) or cut the quantity produced (which keeps the price up). In the simple competitive model, the firm should cut prices. In practice, firms often cut quantities first.

Why? Several reasons we touched on in Chapter 21:

- **Menu costs.** Changing prices is costly (printing, communication, customer relationships).
- **Coordination problems.** A firm that cuts prices first while competitors don't loses money but doesn't restore industry-wide demand.
- **Wage stickiness.** Cutting wages leads to morale problems, turnover, and adverse selection. Firms prefer layoffs to wage cuts.
- **Long-term contracts.** Many prices and wages are set for periods of months or years.
- **Money illusion.** Workers and firms react to nominal rather than real changes — they resist nominal wage cuts even when real wages would actually rise (because of falling prices).

The result of all these is that when AD shifts left, output and employment fall before prices and wages adjust. The economy enters a recession that the simple market-clearing model didn't predict.

This is the foundation of Keynesian policy advocacy. If the economy can't fix itself quickly, intervention is necessary. The waiting game costs millions of jobs.

## The expenditure multiplier

The Keynesian framework features a specific mechanism that amplifies the effects of policy: the **expenditure multiplier**.

Setup. Suppose the government spends an extra $100 billion. This spending becomes income for whoever the government paid (contractors, workers, suppliers). Those recipients spend some fraction of this income (say 70 percent — the **marginal propensity to consume**) and save the rest. So $70 billion gets spent again, becoming income for someone else. Those people spend $49 billion of it (70 percent of $70 billion). And so on.

The total increase in income is $100B + $70B + $49B + $34.3B + ... = $100B / (1 − 0.70) = $100B / 0.30 = $333B.

The multiplier is 1 / (1 − MPC) = 1 / 0.30 = 3.33. Every dollar of government spending generates $3.33 of total income.

This is the "Keynesian multiplier" in its simplest form. The actual U.S. multiplier is generally estimated to be substantially less than 3, partly because some of the spending leaks out (to imports, to taxes, to savings, to repayments of debt). Real-world multiplier estimates for U.S. fiscal stimulus during 2009 ranged from about 0.5 to 1.5, depending on the type of spending and the methodology.

The size of the multiplier matters because it determines how much fiscal stimulus is needed to fill an output gap. If the multiplier is 1.5, $200 billion of stimulus generates $300 billion of additional GDP. If the multiplier is 0.5, the same $200 billion generates only $100 billion. Estimates of the multiplier have been contested for decades, with no clean consensus.

The multiplier is generally larger when:
- The economy is in deep recession (fiscal slack absorbs the spending without causing inflation).
- Interest rates are at the zero lower bound (so the Fed isn't offsetting the stimulus).
- The marginal propensity to consume is high (lower-income recipients spend more).
- The spending is on goods and services, not transfers (transfers may be partly saved).

The multiplier is smaller when:
- The economy is at full employment (fiscal stimulus is mostly inflationary).
- Monetary policy is tightening to counter the stimulus.
- The MPC is low (recipients are wealthier or expectations are pessimistic).
- The spending leaks abroad (high import propensity).

## Fiscal stabilization tools

Keynesian fiscal policy uses two levers.

**Discretionary fiscal policy** — explicit decisions to spend more, tax less, or both during recessions; spend less, tax more, or both during booms. The 2009 American Recovery and Reinvestment Act (~$800B) was the largest U.S. discretionary stimulus since WWII. The 2020 CARES Act (~$2T) was even larger.

**Automatic stabilizers** — features of the tax-and-transfer system that automatically expand during recessions and contract during booms. Income tax revenue falls when incomes fall (because of progressive rates). Unemployment insurance pays out more in recessions. SNAP enrollment rises. Medicaid spending rises. These work without any new legislation.

Estimates suggest automatic stabilizers offset about 8–10 percent of any fiscal shock to GDP in the U.S. They're a significant counter-cyclical force even before any discretionary action.

Discretionary policy has well-known problems:
- **Recognition lag.** It takes months to know a recession has started.
- **Decision lag.** It takes months for Congress to enact legislation.
- **Implementation lag.** It takes months for the spending or tax changes to actually flow through.
- **Political constraints.** Stimulus is easier to enact than future restraint, leading to ratcheting deficits.

Automatic stabilizers avoid most of these but are limited in scale.

## The Phillips Curve

A specific implication of the sticky-price framework is the **Phillips curve** — an inverse relationship between unemployment and inflation. When unemployment is low, the labor market is tight, wages and prices rise faster, and inflation is high. When unemployment is high, the opposite.

The original Phillips curve (A.W. Phillips, 1958) was a stable empirical regularity in U.K. data from 1860 to 1957. Macroeconomists in the 1960s treated it as a stable trade-off — policymakers could choose more inflation in exchange for less unemployment, or vice versa.

This stability broke down in the 1970s. The U.S. experienced both high inflation *and* high unemployment simultaneously. Stagflation. The simple Phillips trade-off appeared to have died.

The Friedman-Phelps explanation: there's a *short-run* Phillips curve (which exhibits the trade-off) and a *long-run* Phillips curve (which is vertical at the natural rate of unemployment). The short-run curve shifts when expectations of inflation change. If the central bank tries to push unemployment below the natural rate, it generates inflation. People update their expectations. The Phillips curve shifts up. The same unemployment rate now corresponds to a higher inflation rate. Policymakers can't permanently buy lower unemployment with higher inflation; they only get a one-time gain followed by sustained higher inflation at the natural-rate unemployment.

This insight (and the data of the 1970s) substantially undermined the simple Keynesian policy framework. Modern macro accepts that:

- A Phillips-curve trade-off exists in the short run.
- It shifts when expectations change.
- The long-run Phillips curve is approximately vertical (no permanent unemployment-inflation trade-off).
- Monetary and fiscal policy should target inflation expectations as much as actual inflation.

## What survives of Keynes

The Keynesian framework, as Keynes wrote it in 1936, has been substantially revised. What survives, in modern macroeconomic thinking:

- **Sticky prices and wages produce non-trivial short-run unemployment.** Demand-side recessions are real.
- **Monetary and fiscal policy have real effects in the short run.** Stabilization is possible.
- **The multiplier is real but smaller than 1936-era estimates suggested.** Effective stimulus requires careful design.
- **Automatic stabilizers do useful work without new legislation.**
- **The Phillips curve operates short-run, not long-run.** Don't try to push unemployment below the natural rate.

What's been jettisoned:

- **Permanent unemployment-inflation trade-off.** Friedman and Phelps were right.
- **Crude multipliers above 3.** Real-world estimates much smaller.
- **Confidence that fiscal policy can fine-tune the cycle.** The lags are too long.
- **The view that monetary policy is ineffective.** Subsequent decades showed it has substantial effects.

The framework that emerges is closer to "neo-Keynesian" or "new Keynesian" than to Keynes himself: sticky prices generate short-run demand effects; monetary policy is the primary stabilization tool; fiscal policy is useful at the zero lower bound and for automatic stabilization; expectations matter enormously; the long run remains supply-side determined.

## Synthesis

Pull back. Keynesianism started with the observation that markets don't always self-correct quickly, especially in deep demand-side recessions. Sticky prices and wages mean that AD shocks cause real output and employment effects rather than just price-level adjustments. The expenditure multiplier amplifies the effects of policy interventions. Fiscal policy — discretionary spending and tax changes plus automatic stabilizers — is the Keynesian toolkit for stabilization.

The Phillips curve was once thought to give policymakers a stable menu of unemployment-inflation combinations. The 1970s stagflation showed it doesn't — the short-run curve shifts when expectations change, and long-run policy can't permanently push unemployment below the natural rate.

The Keynesian framework retains substantial influence on modern policy. The 2008–09 fiscal stimulus, the 2020 COVID stimulus, the standing role of automatic stabilizers, and the standard model of how recessions work are all built on Keynesian foundations. The framework has been substantially revised — multipliers smaller, expectations central, monetary policy primary — but its core insight that markets don't self-correct quickly remains the operating assumption of most macroeconomic policy.

The next chapter takes the opposite view. The neoclassical framework holds that markets do self-correct, that the long run is what matters, and that demand-side intervention is often counterproductive. Both perspectives capture parts of how the macroeconomy actually works; modern thinking lives between them.

## LLM Exercises

**Exercise 1 — Compute a multiplier.** Tell an LLM the marginal propensity to consume is 0.6. The marginal propensity to import is 0.1. The tax rate on additional income is 0.25. Ask it to compute the expenditure multiplier accounting for all these leakages. Verify by hand.

**Exercise 2 — Diagnose stickiness.** Pick a real industry or labor market you know. Ask an LLM to identify which sources of price/wage stickiness are most active there: menu costs, coordination problems, wage contracts, long-term agreements, money illusion. Push for specifics.

**Exercise 3 — Argue for vs. against fiscal stimulus.** Tell an LLM the U.S. has just entered a recession. Ask it to argue both sides on a $500B fiscal stimulus package: when would it work, when wouldn't it, what would determine the size of the multiplier? A good answer engages with both sides.

**Exercise 4 — Phillips curve diagnosis.** Ask an LLM to walk through the U.S. 1970s stagflation using the Friedman-Phelps short-run/long-run Phillips curve framework. Why did the simple trade-off break? What did it teach policymakers about inflation expectations?

**Exercise 5 — Identify automatic stabilizers.** Ask an LLM to list the major U.S. automatic stabilizers. Then ask: in the absence of any discretionary policy response, how much would the automatic stabilizers offset a 5 percentage point GDP shock? The answer should be in the 0.5–1 percentage point range, depending on assumptions.

**What would change my mind.** The case for active fiscal stabilization rests on the assumption that lags can be managed. If the recognition, decision, and implementation lags are systematically too long, fiscal stimulus typically arrives during the recovery phase, when it can be inflationary. The empirical record on whether fiscal stimulus has been well-timed during U.S. recessions is mixed — sometimes timely, sometimes late.

**Still puzzling.** I do not have a clean account of why some fiscal-stimulus packages produce visible economic effects (the 2009 ARRA appears to have raised GDP measurably) while others do not. The variation in apparent effectiveness across episodes and across types of spending is well-documented but not predictively understood.
