# Chapter 21 — Unemployment

*The most-watched single number in macroeconomics — what it counts, what it misses, and why it never quite reaches zero.*

In March 2020, the U.S. unemployment rate was 4.4 percent — close to its lowest level in fifty years. By April 2020, the rate was 14.8 percent. Twenty-three million people were out of work who had been working a month earlier. The COVID-19 pandemic and the lockdowns that followed produced the most abrupt change in unemployment since unemployment statistics began being collected systematically in the 1940s.

Then, more remarkably, the rate fell. By April 2022 it was back to 3.6 percent. The recovery was the fastest from any major recession in U.S. history. Some sectors — restaurants, travel, in-person services — took longer to recover than others. But in aggregate, the labor market had returned to its pre-pandemic state within two years.

The COVID episode is a useful frame for this chapter because it exposes things the standard story of unemployment usually hides. The rapid increase showed how quickly the labor market can lose jobs when something disrupts the normal economic flow. The rapid recovery showed how quickly jobs can come back when the disruption passes. And the messy specifics — workers leaving the labor force entirely, sectors recovering at very different rates, debates about labor shortages that may or may not have been real — showed how much the headline unemployment number conceals.

This chapter is about unemployment. By the end you should be able to define it precisely, distinguish three kinds (cyclical, frictional, structural), understand why the rate is never zero, and read employment statistics with appropriate skepticism.

**Learning objectives.** By the end you should be able to: (1) calculate the unemployment rate and the labor force participation rate from raw labor-market data; (2) distinguish unemployment from being out of the labor force, and explain why the distinction matters; (3) define and identify cyclical, frictional, and structural unemployment with examples; (4) explain why "sticky wages" can produce unemployment; (5) define the natural rate of unemployment and explain what it means.

**Prerequisites.** Chapter 4 (labor market). Chapter 14 (labor demand and supply). Chapter 19 (business cycle).

## What unemployment means — and what the rate counts

The Bureau of Labor Statistics defines the **unemployed** as people who:
- Don't have a job,
- Are available to work, and
- Have actively looked for work in the past four weeks.

The **employed** are people who have a job, even if they're working only an hour a week.

The **labor force** is the sum of the employed and the unemployed.

The **unemployment rate** = unemployed / labor force.

The **labor force participation rate** = labor force / working-age population.

These definitions matter. People who don't have a job and aren't looking aren't counted as unemployed — they're "out of the labor force." Stay-at-home parents, retirees, students, people with disabilities preventing work, people who have given up looking for jobs after long discouragement. None of them count as unemployed.

This produces a paradox that confused observers during COVID and confuses observers in many other periods. The U.S. unemployment rate during the deepest part of the 2009 Great Recession peaked at 10.0 percent. The Great Depression peak was around 25 percent. The COVID peak was 14.8 percent. These numbers are big — but they understate the labor-market disruption because *some people who lost their jobs simply stopped looking* and were counted as out of the labor force, not as unemployed.

The labor force participation rate — the share of working-age people who are in the labor force — captures part of what the unemployment rate misses. The U.S. labor force participation rate was around 67 percent in the late 1990s and is around 62 percent in the mid-2020s. That five-percentage-point decline represents about 13 million working-age people who are simply not in the official labor market. Some of them retired earlier than they would have. Some left the labor force during COVID and didn't return. Some are between formal jobs and informal work. The headline unemployment rate doesn't see them.

The BLS publishes a broader measure called **U-6** that includes people who want a job but have stopped looking, plus people working part-time who want full-time work. U-6 is typically several percentage points higher than the headline unemployment rate. In the 2009 recession peak, U-6 was about 17 percent against a headline of 10 percent.

## The historical pattern

Some patterns in U.S. unemployment over the past 70 years.

**Average around 5 to 6 percent.** Even in expansions, the rate doesn't usually fall much below 4 percent. Even in depressions short of the Great Depression, it doesn't usually exceed 10–11 percent.

**Recessions push the rate up sharply.** Each major recession since the 1950s has produced an unemployment-rate jump of 2 to 5 percentage points within 12–18 months.

**Recoveries are slower than collapses.** The unemployment rate typically takes 3 to 7 years to return to pre-recession lows after a recession ends. The COVID recovery is an exception — much faster, because the underlying disruption was specific and short-lived.

**Demographic patterns.** Younger workers, less-educated workers, and workers from racial minority groups consistently face higher unemployment rates than the population average. The gaps narrow during expansions and widen during recessions.

**Cross-country variation.** European unemployment rates have historically been higher than U.S. rates, though the gap has narrowed since the 2010s. Some countries (Spain, Greece, Italy) have had multi-year periods of double-digit unemployment.

## Three kinds of unemployment

To understand why the unemployment rate is what it is — and why it never reaches zero — economists distinguish three kinds.

**Cyclical unemployment** is the kind that rises and falls with the business cycle. When real GDP contracts in a recession, demand for goods and services drops, firms reduce production, and they lay off or fail to replace workers. This is the unemployment that rises in recessions and falls in expansions.

The mechanism is straightforward. Demand for the firm's product falls. The firm could lower the price of its product to maintain sales — but if it can't lower its costs (especially wages) at the same rate, profit margins collapse. So the firm keeps the price up and reduces the quantity, which means fewer workers needed. Cyclical unemployment is the labor-market consequence of demand-side macroeconomic shocks.

**Frictional unemployment** is the time it takes workers and jobs to find each other. Even in a roaring economy, some workers are between jobs. They've left the old job (voluntarily or involuntarily) and haven't yet started a new one. The new graduate looking for her first job. The laid-off engineer looking for the next opportunity. The worker quitting Uber to start a job in a few weeks. This is frictional unemployment, and it is, in some sense, healthy — it represents people moving through the labor market, not stuck.

The duration of frictional unemployment depends on how well-matched the job-seeker's skills are with available openings, how good the matching infrastructure is (job boards, recruiters, networks), and how willing the worker is to accept a job below her aspiration level. Even with perfect matching infrastructure, some frictional unemployment is unavoidable.

**Structural unemployment** is the kind that arises when the workers' skills don't match the available jobs. The 50-year-old steel-mill worker in Pennsylvania whose mill closed and whose skills aren't valued in any nearby growing industry. The 35-year-old truck driver who specialized in long-haul routes that are being automated. The Ph.D. in a field with no jobs.

Structural unemployment is the harder problem. Frictional unemployment resolves itself; the worker eventually finds a match. Structural unemployment requires the worker to retrain, relocate, or accept a different career — all of which are costly and don't always work.

## Sticky wages — why unemployment exists at all

In the simple supply-and-demand model from Chapter 3, any surplus (more workers willing to work at the going wage than firms want to hire) should drive the wage down until the market clears. Why doesn't this happen in practice?

Wages are **sticky** — they adjust slowly to changes in labor-market conditions, especially downward. Several reasons:

**Contracts.** Long-term employment contracts, union agreements, and even informal expectations create wages that can't be cut quickly even if demand for labor falls.

**Morale effects.** Cutting workers' wages produces resentment, reduced effort, and turnover. Many firms find it more efficient to lay off some workers than to cut wages for everyone.

**Efficiency wages.** Firms sometimes pay above the market-clearing wage to attract better workers, reduce turnover, and improve productivity. These wages stay above market clearing even when unemployment exists.

**Minimum-wage and other regulations.** Legal floors on wages prevent the market from clearing at a lower level.

**Adverse selection.** When wages are cut, the best workers are most likely to leave (because they have the best outside options). Firms that cut wages may end up with a worse workforce, not just a cheaper one.

The result of all these is that wages don't fall fast enough to clear the labor market in a recession. Some workers who would be willing to work at lower wages can't find jobs. Cyclical unemployment persists. The Keynesian framework we'll meet in Chapter 25 makes sticky wages central to its account of business cycles.

## The natural rate

Even in the best of times, U.S. unemployment doesn't fall to zero. It typically bottoms out around 3.5–4 percent in the deepest parts of expansions. This residual reflects frictional and structural unemployment that exists regardless of the business cycle.

This residual is the **natural rate of unemployment** — the unemployment rate that would prevail when cyclical unemployment is zero. The phrase "natural rate" is misleading; nothing about it is natural. It depends on labor-market institutions, education levels, the speed of technological change, demographic composition, and policy. The natural rate in the U.S. has varied from around 7 percent in the late 1970s to around 4 percent today, mainly because of changing demographics (an older, more-educated workforce) and improved matching technology (job-board efficiency, more flexible labor markets).

The natural rate matters because it's the floor on how low unemployment can sustainably go. If the unemployment rate falls *below* the natural rate, the labor market is unusually tight, wages start to rise rapidly, and inflation begins to pick up. The Federal Reserve treats the natural rate as a target — keeping unemployment near it, neither too high (recession) nor too low (inflation). The challenge is that the natural rate is not directly observable; it can only be estimated, and the estimates change over time.

## Synthesis

Pull back. Unemployment is not one thing. It has cyclical, frictional, and structural components, each with different causes and different policy implications. The headline unemployment rate captures only those who are actively looking; people who have given up appear in the labor-force-participation rate but not in the unemployment rate. Sticky wages — the inability of wages to fall fast enough — are the deep reason the labor market doesn't clear smoothly in recessions, producing cyclical unemployment that persists.

The natural rate — the floor of frictional and structural unemployment that exists even in good times — is the long-run target around which monetary and fiscal policy operate. Falls below it produce inflation; rises above it produce recession. The natural rate itself is shaped by institutions, demographics, and technology, all of which evolve.

The COVID episode showed something the textbook doesn't always emphasize: labor markets can adjust very fast when the underlying disruption is specific and short-lived. The 2008–2009 Great Recession produced unemployment that took years to recover; the COVID disruption took months to come back. The difference is partly about the underlying causes (financial system failure vs. health-related shutdown), partly about policy response (rapid fiscal support during COVID; slower during 2008), and partly about how much of the workforce was simply on temporary layoff vs. permanently unemployed. Each recession is a different story even as the broad framework — cyclical + structural + frictional + sticky wages — applies to all of them.

The next chapter takes up inflation, the other major macro indicator. Chapter 22 will show why some level of inflation is normal and expected, why high inflation matters for everyone, and how the relationship between unemployment and inflation (the Phillips curve, when it works) shapes monetary policy.

## LLM Exercises

**Exercise 1 — Compute the rates.** Tell an LLM: "An economy has 250 million working-age adults. 162 million have jobs. 6 million don't have a job but are looking. The rest are not in the labor force." Ask it to compute the labor force, the labor-force participation rate, and the unemployment rate. Verify by hand.

**Exercise 2 — Classify the unemployment.** Give an LLM scenarios and ask it to classify each as primarily cyclical, frictional, or structural:
- A college graduate looking for her first job in May.
- A coal miner laid off when his mine closed permanently and the area has no other coal mines.
- 23 million people losing their jobs during COVID lockdowns.
- A long-haul trucker whose company is replacing drivers with autonomous trucks.
- An auto worker laid off during a recession when auto sales fell sharply.

**Exercise 3 — Diagnose hidden unemployment.** Tell an LLM the official U.S. unemployment rate for a recent month and the labor-force participation rate. Ask: are the two consistent with the headline narrative? What would the U-6 measure show, roughly? Push for whether the labor market is as strong (or weak) as the headline suggests.

**Exercise 4 — Why don't wages just fall?** Ask an LLM to explain in plain language why wages don't fall to clear the labor market when unemployment rises. Push: which of the standard explanations (contracts, morale, efficiency wages, regulations) does the LLM weight most heavily? What evidence would change that weighting?

**Exercise 5 — Estimate the natural rate.** Ask an LLM to estimate the U.S. natural rate of unemployment over the past several decades. The estimate should be roughly: ~7% in the late 1970s, falling to ~5% in the late 1990s, to ~4% currently. Push for the explanation: which factors drove the decline? Demographics, education, matching technology, market institutions?

**What would change my mind.** The case that wages are reliably sticky downward depends on observation of pre-existing labor markets. If a country experimented with much more flexible wage-setting (no employment contracts, no minimum wage, weak unions), the cyclical unemployment problem might compress substantially. Whether the welfare gains would be worth the costs is contested; the empirical evidence is hard to find because no major economy has actually run the experiment.

**Still puzzling.** I do not have a clean account of why the U.S. labor force participation rate has not recovered to its pre-COVID level five years after the disruption. Multiple candidate explanations exist (early retirements, long COVID, child-care issues, choice of more leisure), but the cumulative evidence is inconclusive. The phenomenon matters because every percentage point of "missing" labor force represents about 2 million people who could be working but aren't.
