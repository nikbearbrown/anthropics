# Chapter 3 — Demand and Supply

*Where price comes from — and what happens when you try to override it.*

Pick up an organic Fuji apple at the supermarket. Look at the sticker. $2.75 a pound.

Now pick up the conventional Fuji apple right next to it. $1.72 a pound.

Same fruit, by genetics. Often grown in adjacent orchards. The organic one might even have traveled a shorter distance — small farms tend to be local. By the logic of "less travel, lower price," the organic apple should be the cheaper one. It is not. It costs sixty percent more.

Hold this puzzle. The answer turns out to be the entire content of this chapter, and the model that produces the answer is the most-used tool in economics. Once you have it, you can read prices the way a sailor reads weather — not perfectly, but well enough to know whether to put up the sails or take them down.

The model is called *demand and supply*. We're going to do three things with it. First, build the two curves and explain why they slope the way they do. Second, watch them meet — what economists call *equilibrium* — and learn the four-step analysis for what happens when something disturbs them. Third, see what happens when a government tries to override the price the curves produce, and use the diagram to make visible something most political arguments leave invisible: the *gains from trade* and who keeps them.

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this chapter you should be able to: (1) state the law of demand and the law of supply, and explain why each holds; (2) draw a demand curve and a supply curve, and identify what shifts each (vs. what causes a movement along it); (3) find the equilibrium price and quantity from a demand-and-supply diagram, and use the four-step method to predict the effect of a change; (4) define consumer surplus, producer surplus, and total surplus, and identify them on a diagram; (5) analyze price ceilings and price floors and predict their consequences, including the unintended ones.

**Prerequisites.** Chapter 1 (markets, scarcity). Chapter 2 (opportunity cost, marginal thinking). Comfort with two-axis graphs.

## The two curves

Start with the buyers.

**Demand** is the relationship between the price of a good and the quantity of that good buyers are willing and able to purchase. Two words are doing real work in that sentence. *Willing*: the buyer wants it. *Able*: the buyer can pay for it. Wanting without being able doesn't count. A homeless man wants a house. He has no demand for one in the economist's sense, because demand requires money behind the want.

The **law of demand** says: when price goes up, quantity demanded goes down. Other things equal — and that *other things equal* is doing important work, which we'll come back to. Why? Two reasons that compound. First, when something gets more expensive, some people switch to substitutes. Beef goes up; people eat more chicken. Second, even people who keep buying the thing buy less of it, because their fixed income now buys less. Both effects push in the same direction: higher price, lower quantity demanded.

Plot it. Vertical axis: price. Horizontal axis: quantity. A demand curve runs from upper-left to lower-right — the higher the price, the smaller the quantity people want.

Now the sellers.

**Supply** is the relationship between price and the quantity sellers are willing and able to produce. The **law of supply** says: when price goes up, quantity supplied goes up. Why? Higher prices mean more revenue per unit, which justifies pulling in higher-cost production. The marginal apple farmer — who can grow apples but only barely profits at $1 a pound — enters the market when the price hits $2. The farm that needs $5/pound to be worth bothering enters at $5. As price climbs, more producers find the math worth doing. Higher price, larger quantity supplied.

A supply curve runs from lower-left to upper-right.

Already we have the picture. Two curves, sloping opposite directions, drawn on the same axes. They cross at exactly one point. That crossing is **equilibrium** — the only price at which the quantity buyers want exactly equals the quantity sellers will produce. At any higher price, sellers want to sell more than buyers want to buy. The leftover sits on shelves; some seller drops their price; we slide back toward equilibrium. At any lower price, buyers want to buy more than sellers will produce; the queue grows; some buyer offers a higher price; we slide back up. The market is a corrective mechanism — a noisy, imperfect, often slow one — that pushes price toward the crossing.

This is the workhorse model of economics. Two curves, one crossing point. Almost everything we will do in microeconomics is some version of this.

## Movements along, shifts of

Here's a distinction that students stumble on every year. It is the difference between a *movement along* a curve and a *shift of* the curve, and getting it wrong makes the rest of the model unusable.

When the *price* of a good changes — and nothing else changes — buyers move along the demand curve. The curve doesn't move; the point on it does. Same for sellers: a price change moves them along the supply curve.

When *something other than the price* changes, the curve itself shifts. Consider demand. If consumer income rises, more people can afford the good at every price. The whole demand curve shifts to the right — at every price, more is demanded. If income falls, the curve shifts left.

The non-price things that shift the demand curve are what economists call **shift variables**. The big five are: income, taste, prices of related goods, expectations, and number of buyers.

*Income* — usually obvious. Get richer, demand more of most things. (A few goods run the other way — *inferior goods* like ramen noodles, where demand falls as income rises. We'll meet them properly in the consumer-choice chapter.)

*Taste and preference* — when fashion shifts, when health concerns spread, when an influencer's video changes what feels desirable. The 2010s vegetarian shift moved the demand curve for plant-based food right. Demand for fur coats has been moving left for forty years.

*Prices of related goods* — substitutes (chicken for beef) and complements (printer ink for printers). When the price of a substitute rises, demand for the original good shifts right — beef gets expensive, more people buy chicken. When the price of a complement rises, demand for the original good shifts left — gasoline gets expensive, demand for SUVs falls.

*Expectations* — if buyers think the price is about to rise, they buy now. Right shift in current demand. If they expect a price drop, they wait. Left shift.

*Number of buyers* — population growth, an aging cohort entering a market, immigration. More buyers, demand shifts right.

The supply curve has its own shift variables. Input costs (wages, materials, energy) — when they rise, supply shifts left because every unit costs more to make at every price. Technology improvements — usually shift supply right because the same inputs make more output. Natural conditions — drought shifts the wheat supply curve left; bumper crop shifts it right. Government policy — taxes shift supply left, subsidies shift it right. Number of sellers — more producers, supply shifts right.

A change in *price* never shifts either curve. A change in price moves you along an existing curve. This is the rule that students keep getting wrong, so I'm going to repeat it: *price changes are movements along the curves; everything else is a shift of a curve*.

## The four-step analysis

Now we can predict. Something happens in the world. How does it change the equilibrium price and quantity?

Four steps, every time.

**Step 1.** Draw the original demand-and-supply diagram with the equilibrium price and quantity labeled. Establish the baseline.

**Step 2.** Identify what changed. Was it something that affects buyers (a shift variable for demand)? Or something that affects sellers (a shift variable for supply)? Or both?

**Step 3.** Shift the relevant curve. Right or left? Use the rules in the previous section to decide. Be specific: "demand shifts right because consumer income rose" or "supply shifts left because the input price of corn rose."

**Step 4.** Find the new equilibrium. Compare it to the old one. The difference is your prediction.

Let's run one. In 2022, gasoline prices rose sharply across the United States. Demand for new SUVs softened. What does the four-step analysis say?

Step 1: Draw the SUV demand-and-supply diagram. Old equilibrium at price $P_0$, quantity $Q_0$.

Step 2: What changed? The price of a *complement* — gasoline — rose. SUVs and gasoline are consumed together; you need fuel to drive the car. Higher price of complement → demand for the original good shifts left.

Step 3: Shift the demand curve for SUVs to the left. The supply curve for SUVs has not changed (assume manufacturers' costs and technology are stable).

Step 4: Read the new equilibrium. Lower price, lower quantity. Prediction: SUV prices fall and the number sold falls.

This is the kind of prediction the model makes — and it is unusually robust within its domain. The model doesn't tell you *how much* the price falls or *how fast*. It tells you *direction* and *qualitative magnitude*. That's already enormously useful.

A small but important rule: when one curve shifts, use the four-step. When *both* curves shift at once — say, a hurricane wipes out the wheat crop (supply shifts left) at the same moment a new diet trend boosts wheat-pasta demand (demand shifts right) — you can predict the direction of one outcome cleanly (price will rise, both effects push that way) but not the other (quantity could rise, fall, or stay flat, depending on which shift is bigger). Use the diagram to see which combinations are pinned down by the model and which are not.

Now: what happens when government tries to override the crossing point?

## Price controls and what they hide

The most useful thing the demand-and-supply model does is make the consequences of price controls visible.

A **price ceiling** is a legal maximum price — *the price cannot go above this*. The classic example is rent control: a city declares that landlords cannot charge more than $1,500/month for a one-bedroom in some district. A price ceiling is *binding* if it sits below the market equilibrium; if it sits above, nothing changes.

When a binding price ceiling sits below equilibrium, two things happen at once. Quantity demanded rises (the lower price draws in more buyers). Quantity supplied falls (some sellers won't produce at the controlled price). The gap between them is a *shortage*. There is more demand for rent-controlled apartments than there are rent-controlled apartments to give. Always.

Politicians often pass price ceilings precisely because they don't want certain people priced out — renters, drug-buyers, gasoline-buyers. The ceiling solves the price problem for whoever still gets the good. It does not solve the underlying scarcity. The shortage gets allocated by something else: queues, lotteries, "who you know," landlord screening that disfavors riskier tenants, deferred maintenance, side payments under the table. The mechanism the diagram makes visible is that the price did not disappear; it transformed. Some of it became time. Some of it became favoritism. Some of it became corruption.

A **price floor** runs the other direction — a legal minimum price. *The price cannot go below this*. The classic example is the minimum wage. If the floor sits above equilibrium, quantity supplied (workers willing to work at that wage) exceeds quantity demanded (jobs offered at that wage). The gap is a *surplus*. In labor, we call it unemployment.

The standard textbook conclusion is that a binding minimum wage causes some unemployment. The standard *empirical* finding is messier. Real labor markets have features — labor-market monopsony, frictions, search costs — that the simple supply-and-demand picture doesn't include. In some studies, modest minimum-wage increases haven't produced measurable unemployment effects. In others, they have. The model is right *about the direction* if labor markets are competitive and the wage floor binds substantially. It is right *less robustly* than the rent-control case because the assumption of competitive labor markets is on shakier ground in many local labor markets than the assumption of competitive housing markets in cities.

Both controls share a structural feature: they make the *price* one variable, and let it take the political pressure, while the *quantity* variable adjusts in ways the model makes visible but the legislators often don't talk about. The diagram doesn't tell you whether price controls are good or bad. It tells you what the consequences are.

### Surplus — what the diagram secretly contains

There is one more thing buried in the demand-and-supply diagram, and it changes how you read every market debate. It is called **surplus**.

Look at a market in equilibrium. The equilibrium price is $80. Consumers who would have paid $300 for the thing get it for $80; their willingness-to-pay was much higher than what they paid. The difference, summed across all the buyers, is the **consumer surplus** — the gain consumers get from being able to buy at the market price rather than at their personal maximum. On the diagram it's the triangular area above the equilibrium price and below the demand curve.

Producers earn $80 per unit. Some of them would have produced for $20; their cost was much lower than what they got paid. The difference, summed across all sellers, is the **producer surplus**. On the diagram it's the triangular area below the equilibrium price and above the supply curve.

Add them up: **total surplus**, the size of the gains-from-trade pie. The free-market equilibrium maximizes this pie. That's not a moral claim about who deserves the pieces; it's a structural claim about the size of the pie itself.

Now apply this to price controls. A binding rent ceiling lowers the price, transferring some surplus from landlords to the tenants who still get apartments. But because the quantity transacted falls (fewer apartments built and rented), some surplus disappears entirely — not redistributed, evaporated. Economists call that the **deadweight loss**. The diagram shows it as a triangle that used to be filled and is now empty. A price floor produces a deadweight-loss triangle in the same way.

This is what the simple model tells you. It does not tell you the price ceiling is wrong. It tells you that any redistribution argument for the ceiling has to be measured against the deadweight loss it produces. The redistribution might be worth it. But the cost is real, and the diagram makes it visible.

## Synthesis

Pull back. The demand-and-supply diagram is one picture with three layers. The first layer is the two curves themselves — buyers' willingness, sellers' willingness — each driven by a price relationship and shifted by other forces. The second layer is the equilibrium where they meet, and the four-step prediction logic for what happens when a shift variable changes. The third layer is the surplus structure — the gains-from-trade pie, who gets which slice, how much shrinks when the government rearranges things.

Bring the apple back. Why does the organic Fuji cost $2.75 and the conventional one $1.72?

Use the model. Demand for organic apples has been shifting right for two decades — taste change, health concerns, income growth, more buyers entering. Supply for organic apples is constrained — organic certification is expensive, organic farming is labor-intensive, conversion from conventional takes years, the supply curve sits to the left of the conventional supply curve at every price. Two effects pushing the same way: a demand curve relatively far right, a supply curve relatively far left. The crossing is at a higher price.

The price isn't a moral statement about organic farming. It's a piece of evidence about how many buyers want it, how hard it is to produce, and how the two pressures balance. The price tag tells you both things at once, if you know how to read it.

That is what the demand-and-supply model does. It turns prices from random data into a compressed summary of what every buyer and seller in the market is doing. We will spend the next thirty chapters either using this model directly or examining the cases where it breaks. Both are useful. Knowing which is which is the skill.

## LLM Exercises

**Exercise 1 — The four-step in action.** Pick a current event that you suspect changed equilibrium price and quantity in some market — a hurricane, a tariff, a viral product launch, a tech layoff. Describe the event to an LLM and ask it to walk through the four-step analysis: original equilibrium, what changed, which curve shifted and which way, new equilibrium. Then push: ask the LLM to identify *which* of the demand or supply shift variables drove the change, by name. Evaluate whether it gets the named variable right or hand-waves.

**Exercise 2 — Movement along vs. shift of.** Give an LLM this list and ask it to classify each as a "movement along the demand curve" or a "shift of the demand curve":
1. The price of avocados rises and people buy fewer avocados.
2. A celebrity recommends avocado toast and avocado demand rises.
3. The income tax falls and people buy more avocados.
4. The price of bagels rises (a complement) and avocado demand falls.

Check the LLM's answers. The first one is the trick — it is a movement along, not a shift. If the LLM gets it wrong, ask why and see whether you can teach it.

**Exercise 3 — Predict the unintended consequence.** Tell an LLM you are considering a price ceiling on a good — pick something specific (insulin, eggs, gasoline, daycare). Ask it to predict three unintended consequences using only the demand-and-supply framework. Evaluate: does the LLM identify the shortage, the non-price rationing mechanisms, and the deadweight loss? Or does it slide into political talking points without naming the mechanisms? If the latter, push it back to the diagram.

**Exercise 4 — Build a surplus diagram in words.** Without drawing anything, describe a demand-and-supply equilibrium to an LLM in pure prose. Then ask it to identify (a) consumer surplus, (b) producer surplus, (c) total surplus, on the diagram you described. Then introduce a price ceiling below equilibrium and ask the LLM to redo the surplus identification, naming the deadweight loss. Evaluate whether the LLM keeps the geometry straight when it can't see a picture.

**Exercise 5 — Two shifts at once.** Tell an LLM: "A drought hits the Midwest, cutting U.S. corn production. At the same moment, a new federal regulation requires more ethanol (made from corn) in gasoline." Ask the LLM what happens to corn's equilibrium price and quantity. Press it on which prediction is *certain* and which is *ambiguous* — a careful answer notes that price clearly rises while quantity is ambiguous (it depends on which shift is larger). If the LLM gives confident answers in both directions, that's a tell.

## What comes next

Chapter 4 — Labor and Financial Markets applies the demand-and-supply model directly to two specific markets: the market for labor (where workers are sellers and firms are buyers) and the market for financial capital (where savers are sellers and borrowers are buyers). Chapter 5 — Elasticity refines the model by asking how *responsive* demand and supply are to price changes — a refinement that turns out to matter enormously for tax policy, business strategy, and the case for or against price controls. From there we'll spend Chapters 6–10 inside the firm — how production decisions are made, how cost structures vary, what happens in different kinds of markets.

**What would change my mind.** The claim that competitive markets maximize total surplus depends on a heroic set of assumptions: full information, no externalities, atomistic buyers and sellers, rational behavior, costless transactions. Drop any of these and the conclusion weakens. Most of the second half of the micro half of this book is about cases where the assumptions fail. The model is the right starting point. It is not the right ending point for many real markets.

**Still puzzling.** I do not have a clean account of why some markets adjust toward equilibrium quickly (commodity exchanges, financial markets in normal times) while others adjust slowly or apparently never (housing in cities with restrictive zoning, healthcare under insurance markups). Part of the answer is friction; part is information; part is institutional structure. But the textbook treatment skates over how messy actual price discovery is, and the messiness sometimes matters more than the clean diagram suggests.
