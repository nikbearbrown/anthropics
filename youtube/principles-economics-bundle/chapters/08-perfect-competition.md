# Chapter 8 — Perfect Competition

*The market with no pricing power — and why economists keep teaching it.*

A wheat farmer in central Kansas sits at her kitchen table on a Thursday morning, looking at her phone. The Chicago Mercantile Exchange has just opened. She watches the screen for thirty seconds. Hard red winter wheat: $5.82 a bushel. Yesterday it was $5.85. Last week, $5.91.

She does not call the elevator and try to negotiate. She does not threaten to take her crop to a different buyer for a better price. She does not haggle. The price is the price. If she tries to sell for $5.83, the buyer will laugh and call the next farmer. If she holds out for $5.90, the elevator will fill its capacity from her neighbors and she will be stuck with grain to store, paying interest on the loan she took out for the planting. The Chicago screen says $5.82. She sells at $5.82.

This farmer is operating in what economists call a **perfectly competitive market**, and the entirety of her strategic choice — given that the market has set the price — is *how much to produce*. That's it. Not what price to charge. Not what features to add. Not how to advertise. Just the quantity question.

Perfect competition is rare. Wheat is the textbook example. So is corn. So are some commodity metals, fish at a wholesale auction, raw lumber. So, surprisingly, are babysitting in your neighborhood and lawn-mowing services on your block — local markets where the service is undifferentiated, the customers know the going rate, and you can't charge more without losing the customer.

But economists keep teaching the perfect competition model even though most real markets aren't perfectly competitive. Why? Three reasons. First, it's the cleanest version of the theory — once you understand how a firm decides under perfect competition, the variations (monopoly, oligopoly, monopolistic competition) are all departures from this baseline. Second, the long-run prediction of perfect competition — that profits get competed away to zero — is the benchmark against which we compare what happens when entry is blocked. Third, the model produces a remarkable theorem: a perfectly competitive market, in long-run equilibrium, achieves both productive and allocative efficiency at the same time. That theorem is the centerpiece of the case for free markets, and the rest of microeconomics is partly the project of figuring out when the theorem fails.

This chapter walks through the model. By the end you'll be able to find a competitive firm's profit-maximizing output, decide when it should shut down vs. keep producing at a loss, and explain why long-run economic profits in a competitive market always trend to zero.

**Learning objectives.** By the end you should be able to: (1) state the four conditions for perfect competition; (2) find the profit-maximizing output for a competitive firm using the rule price = marginal cost; (3) calculate profit or loss at the optimum and identify which using the average cost curve; (4) determine whether a firm should shut down in the short run using the AVC test; (5) explain how entry and exit drive long-run economic profit to zero; (6) explain why a perfectly competitive industry achieves both productive and allocative efficiency.

**Prerequisites.** Chapter 3 (demand and supply, equilibrium). Chapter 7 (cost curves, MC, ATC, AVC, economic profit).

## What perfect competition means

The model rests on four conditions, and all four matter.

**Many firms producing identical products.** No firm has more than a tiny share of total industry output. The product is homogeneous — one farmer's wheat is interchangeable with another's. Buyers don't care who they buy from.

**Many buyers and many sellers.** No one — buyer or seller — is large enough to move the price. Both sides are price takers.

**Full information.** Everyone knows the going price. There are no information asymmetries, no secret deals, no quality variation.

**Free entry and exit.** No regulatory barriers, no patents, no large startup costs that prevent a new firm from entering when prices are high or an existing firm from exiting when prices are low.

When all four hold, a single firm's actions cannot move the price. The firm is a **price taker** — it accepts the market-determined price and decides only on quantity.

The four conditions are restrictive. Real markets often violate one or several. But the model is still worth running because the *direction* it predicts — toward more output, lower prices, zero economic profit — is the direction every market would head toward if competition were unimpeded. When markets fail to head that direction, you can ask which condition is being violated.

## How a price taker decides

Here's the firm's situation. The market price is $P$. The firm can sell as much as it wants at $P$ — its sales don't move the market. So the firm's *demand curve* is a horizontal line at $P$. The firm's *marginal revenue* — the additional revenue from selling one more unit — is also $P$, because every unit sells at the same price.

Profit is total revenue minus total cost. The marginal version: the firm makes additional profit on a unit if marginal revenue exceeds marginal cost, and loses on it if marginal cost exceeds marginal revenue. To maximize profit, the firm produces every unit where MR > MC and stops at the point where MR = MC.

For a perfectly competitive firm, MR = P. So the rule simplifies to:

**Produce the quantity at which P = MC.**

Find the marginal cost curve, find where it crosses the horizontal line at the market price, and that's the optimal quantity.

A worked example. The market price for wheat is $5.82/bushel. The farmer's marginal cost curve, drawn from her field's productivity, equipment costs, and labor, crosses $5.82 at 50,000 bushels. Below that quantity, MC < $5.82 — every additional bushel adds more revenue than cost. Above that quantity, MC > $5.82 — every additional bushel costs more to produce than the revenue it brings in. The profit-maximizing harvest is 50,000 bushels.

Now: at that quantity, is the farmer making a profit?

To find out, look at average total cost (ATC) at 50,000 bushels. If ATC < $5.82, the farmer is making positive economic profit (per unit, P − ATC, multiplied by 50,000). If ATC > $5.82, she's losing money, even though she's producing the profit-maximizing — or rather, loss-minimizing — quantity. If ATC = $5.82, she's at zero economic profit, which we'll see in a moment is the long-run outcome.

A useful rectangle on the diagram: the profit (or loss) is the rectangle whose width is the quantity and whose height is the gap between price and ATC.

## The shutdown rule

Suppose the farmer is losing money. Should she stop producing?

The intuition says yes. The economist says: not necessarily.

Recall the cost structure. Some costs are fixed in the short run — the farm equipment payments, the crop insurance, the property tax. The farmer pays these whether she plants the crop or doesn't. Other costs are variable — the seed, the fuel, the hired hands at harvest.

The shutdown decision in the short run depends on whether the *price* is at least covering the *average variable cost*. If P ≥ AVC, every bushel produced covers its own variable cost and contributes some money toward the fixed costs. The fixed costs are owed regardless; getting some contribution toward them is better than getting none. Even at a loss, keep producing.

If P < AVC, every bushel produced loses money even before the fixed costs are counted. Each additional bushel makes the loss bigger. Now shutdown makes sense — produce zero, eat the fixed costs, wait for next season.

The rule, formally:

- **P ≥ AVC** → produce in the short run, even at a loss, if P < ATC.
- **P < AVC** → shut down in the short run.

This rule explains a phenomenon that confuses people watching businesses lose money for years without closing. Some firms keep operating because they're at least covering their variable costs and the alternative (shutting down) saves them nothing, since the fixed costs would still be owed. The decision to *exit* the industry — to close permanently and walk away from the fixed costs — is a long-run decision, and it is determined by whether the firm can ever expect price to exceed ATC.

## The long run — entry and exit

Now zoom out from a single firm to the industry over time.

If price exceeds ATC at the profit-maximizing quantity, firms in the industry are earning positive economic profit. Positive economic profit is a signal — to the firms inside and to potential entrants outside — that this industry is producing more value than its resources could earn elsewhere. Outsiders watch this and start entering: new wheat farmers buy land, new firms incorporate. Entry shifts the *industry* supply curve to the right. The market price falls. The economic profit of the existing firms shrinks. Entry continues until economic profit is competed down to zero.

If price is below ATC, firms are losing money. Some keep producing in the short run because P ≥ AVC; some shut down. In the long run, the persistently unprofitable firms exit the industry — sell off the land, declare bankruptcy, redeploy the assets. Industry supply shifts left. Price rises. The losses shrink. Exit continues until economic profit is back to zero.

The long-run outcome of perfect competition is therefore: **economic profit equals zero**.

This sounds bad. It isn't. Zero economic profit means the firm is earning exactly what its resources could earn in their next-best use — including the implicit costs we met in Chapter 7. The owner is making a competitive return. The accountants will report substantial accounting profit. The economists will report zero economic profit. Both are correct; they're measuring different things.

The deeper consequence: in long-run equilibrium of a perfectly competitive market, *price equals the minimum of the long-run average cost curve*. Each firm produces at the bottom of its U. Output per firm is at its most efficient scale.

## Two efficiencies at once

Now the punchline. In long-run perfect-competitive equilibrium:

**Productive efficiency.** Each firm produces at the minimum of its long-run average cost curve. The industry produces the goods at the lowest possible cost. We met this concept in Chapter 2; it's the "no waste" claim.

**Allocative efficiency.** The price consumers pay equals the marginal cost of producing the last unit. This means consumers are paying exactly what it costs to make the last unit. The marginal value of the good to consumers (their willingness to pay, reflected in the demand curve) equals the marginal cost of producing it. The mix of goods being produced matches what society wants.

Both efficiencies, in one model, in one market, in long-run equilibrium. This is the result that makes economists who like markets so adamant about defending them.

It is also the result whose conditions almost never hold completely in a real market. Perfect information rarely holds. Perfect substitutability rarely holds. No barriers to entry rarely holds. Externalities (pollution, congestion) often hold and break the calculation. The next several chapters explore what happens when one or another of these conditions fails. Each failure produces a market that is *less* efficient than the perfect-competition benchmark — and the diagnosis is precisely about which condition broke.

## Synthesis

Pull back. Perfect competition is an idealization. Most real markets aren't perfectly competitive, in the same way that no real surface is perfectly frictionless. But the idealization is useful because it gives a benchmark — a clean, tractable case in which we can derive precise predictions, including the surprising and powerful theorem that competitive markets in long-run equilibrium achieve both productive and allocative efficiency.

The next chapter explores monopoly, where the price-taker assumption breaks. Then monopolistic competition, where the identical-product assumption breaks. Then oligopoly, where many-firms breaks. In each case, the key features are visible *because* perfect competition gave us a clean version to compare against.

The wheat farmer at her kitchen table is doing exactly what the model predicts: take the market price as given, produce up to where MC = P, calculate whether the harvest will cover ATC, and stay in the industry only if she expects long-run prices to keep covering her costs. The model is simple. It is also the cleanest version of how an economy without coordination problems would distribute resources — a starting point we'll modify, often substantially, in the chapters to come.

## LLM Exercises

**Exercise 1 — Find the profit-maximizing quantity.** Tell an LLM: "The market price of widgets is $20. Marginal cost of producing the Nth widget: 1st = $5, 2nd = $10, 3rd = $15, 4th = $20, 5th = $25, 6th = $30." Ask the LLM to find the profit-maximizing quantity. Verify by hand. The right answer is 4 widgets — at the 4th, MC = P = $20; the 5th would lose money. If the LLM gives a different answer, examine where its reasoning slipped.

**Exercise 2 — Apply the shutdown rule.** Describe to an LLM a small firm with: P = $15, MC at the optimum = $15 (so it's at the profit-maximizing quantity), ATC at that quantity = $18 (so it's losing money), AVC at that quantity = $13. Should the firm shut down or keep producing? Why? A correct answer says "keep producing in the short run, because P ($15) > AVC ($13), so each unit is at least covering variable cost; the firm should plan to exit in the long run if conditions don't improve."

**Exercise 3 — Identify a not-quite-competitive market.** Pick a market that *seems* competitive (gas stations, food trucks, plumbers, freelance designers). Ask an LLM to evaluate which of the four perfect-competition conditions hold, and which fail. Then ask: how does the failing condition change the outcome away from the perfect-competition prediction? The exercise tests the LLM's ability to use the model as a *diagnostic tool* rather than a sticker.

**Exercise 4 — Why zero profit isn't bad.** Ask an LLM to explain to a skeptical reader why the long-run outcome of perfect competition — zero economic profit — is *not* a sign that something is wrong. A good answer distinguishes accounting profit from economic profit and explains that zero economic profit means firms are earning a competitive return. If the LLM struggles, push it to explain the implicit-cost piece.

**Exercise 5 — Predict an industry's long-run trajectory.** Pick an industry that's currently earning very high accounting profits (some tech segment, some new pharmaceutical, some hot consumer brand). Ask an LLM to predict, using the entry-and-exit logic, what should happen to those profits over the next ten years. Then ask: what would prevent the prediction? (Hint: barriers to entry, network effects, IP protection, brand loyalty.) The exercise tests whether the LLM connects the model's predictions to the concrete features of real markets.

## What comes next

Chapter 9 — Monopoly studies the opposite extreme: a market with one seller and the pricing power that comes with it. Chapter 10 — Monopolistic Competition and Oligopoly fills in the territory between perfect competition and monopoly: many firms with differentiated products, and few firms with strategic interaction. Chapter 11 — Antitrust Policy looks at how governments respond to firms that grow too dominant. Throughout, the perfect-competition baseline is what we measure deviations against.

**What would change my mind.** The strongest version of the perfect-competition theorem — that long-run equilibrium achieves both productive and allocative efficiency — assumes no externalities, no public goods, no information asymmetries, no market power, no transaction costs. The conditions are restrictive. In domains where those conditions hold approximately (commodity markets, agricultural markets, some financial markets), the model's predictions are reasonably close to what we observe. Where they don't hold, the model is the wrong benchmark and we need different tools — which we develop in Chapters 9 through 16.

**Still puzzling.** I find the *speed* of long-run adjustment under perfect competition empirically variable in ways the model doesn't predict cleanly. Some markets adjust toward zero-economic-profit equilibrium in a year or two; others (housing, agriculture in some regions, certain professional services) take decades or appear never to reach the equilibrium. The textbook gives us "long run" as a vague horizon. The actual horizon depends on entry costs, exit costs, learning curves, and other features that differ enormously across industries.
