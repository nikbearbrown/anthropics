# Chapter 31 — The Impacts of Government Borrowing

*What happens when a government borrows on a sustained basis — to private investment, to interest rates, to trade balances, to the next generation.*

In the late 1960s, President Lyndon Johnson was pursuing two costly programs simultaneously — the Vietnam War and the Great Society's expansion of social programs. Rather than raise taxes to pay for both, his administration ran budget deficits and let the Federal Reserve accommodate the spending with monetary expansion. The combined "guns and butter" deficit substantially preceded the inflation surge that haunted the U.S. economy through the 1970s.

This wasn't unprecedented. Earlier wars and major spending programs had been deficit-financed too. What was distinctive about the LBJ era was that the deficit persisted in peacetime and started a multi-decade pattern. The U.S. has run budget deficits in nearly every year since 1969, with brief exceptions in the late 1990s. The accumulated debt has grown from about 30 percent of GDP then to over 100 percent today.

Persistent borrowing has consequences. Some are channels through which deficits affect macroeconomic outcomes. Some are constraints on future fiscal flexibility. Some are intergenerational — current beneficiaries of spending leave the bills for future taxpayers. This chapter walks through the major effects.

By the end you should understand the national saving and investment identity, the "twin deficits" relationship between fiscal and trade balances, the Ricardian equivalence argument, the crowding-out mechanism, and the question of debt sustainability.

**Learning objectives.** By the end you should be able to: (1) state the national saving-investment identity and explain how government deficits affect it; (2) describe the twin deficits hypothesis; (3) explain Ricardian equivalence and evaluate its empirical support; (4) describe the crowding-out mechanism; (5) discuss the long-run sustainability question for U.S. public debt.

**Prerequisites.** Chapter 17 (financial markets, savings and investment). Chapter 23 (international trade, S − I = NX). Chapter 30 (government budget and fiscal policy).

## The national saving and investment identity, expanded

In Chapter 23 we saw the basic identity: S − I = NX. National saving minus national investment equals net exports.

Now expand. National saving has two parts:
- **Private saving** (S_p) — households and businesses saving.
- **Public saving** (S_g) — government surplus (negative if there's a deficit).

So S = S_p + S_g.

Substituting into the identity: (S_p + S_g) − I = NX, or rearranged: S_p = I + (G − T) + NX, where (G − T) is the government deficit (spending minus taxes).

In words: **private saving funds (1) domestic investment, (2) the government deficit, and (3) net exports**. Whatever private households save has to flow somewhere — to firms investing, to the government borrowing, or to foreigners (which corresponds to net exports / capital outflows).

This identity reveals the structural connections between fiscal policy, investment, and trade. A larger government deficit must be matched by some combination of more private saving, less domestic investment, or more borrowing from abroad (smaller NX, larger trade deficit).

## The twin deficits

The U.S. has a chronic budget deficit. The U.S. also has a chronic trade deficit. The two are not coincidence.

By the identity above, when the government deficit (G − T) is large, the matching adjustment must come from private saving rising, investment falling, or net exports falling (more borrowing from abroad, larger trade deficit). Empirically, the U.S. trade deficit has tracked the budget deficit reasonably closely over decades — the **twin deficits** hypothesis.

The connection is not mechanical (one-to-one) because the other terms can adjust too. Private saving may rise modestly in response to deficit-induced anxiety. Investment may fall as crowding out happens. Net exports may bear most of the adjustment, especially if the dollar appreciates as foreign investors buy U.S. Treasuries (making U.S. exports less competitive).

The twin-deficits dynamic is one reason large fiscal deficits in the U.S. don't push interest rates up as much as the simple model would predict — foreign capital inflows partly fund the deficits. The cost of this is the trade deficit and the accumulation of foreign claims on U.S. assets.

## Ricardian equivalence

A theoretical complication. The British economist David Ricardo, writing in the early 19th century, argued that if households are forward-looking and rational, deficit-financed government spending should not affect aggregate demand at all.

The reasoning. The government cuts taxes today and runs a larger deficit. Households receive more after-tax income now. They know that to repay the resulting debt, future taxes must be higher. So they save the entire tax cut, increasing private saving by the exact amount of the deficit. The total demand effect: zero.

In this view, **deficit-financed spending and tax-financed spending are equivalent** because rational households see through the financing.

The strict version of Ricardian equivalence requires implausible assumptions: perfect foresight, perfect altruism toward future generations, no liquidity constraints, no uncertainty about future taxes. None of these holds completely. Empirical tests generally find that Ricardian equivalence is partially true — household saving does rise somewhat in response to government deficits — but the adjustment is much less than 100 percent. Households are at least partly fooled by deficit financing.

The implication: deficit-financed stimulus does have real effects, but smaller than the simple multiplier model suggests. Ricardian effects partially offset the boost. How much they offset is contested empirically.

## Crowding out

The most-discussed effect of government borrowing on the economy is **crowding out**.

When the government borrows, it competes with private borrowers for the available pool of savings. Demand for capital rises. Interest rates rise. Higher interest rates discourage private investment — businesses don't borrow as much for factories, households don't borrow as much for houses. The government's increased borrowing partially "crowds out" private investment.

The textbook diagram shows this clearly. Demand for capital shifts right (government adds to demand). Equilibrium interest rate rises. Private borrowing along the demand curve falls. The total quantity of capital may rise modestly, but private investment definitely falls.

How much crowding out actually happens depends on:
- **Slack in the economy.** In a deep recession with unemployed resources, additional government borrowing can be fully accommodated by mobilizing slack. Crowding out is minimal.
- **Foreign capital flows.** The U.S. has access to global capital markets; large deficits draw in foreign savings rather than just bidding up domestic rates.
- **Monetary policy stance.** If the Fed is committed to keeping rates low (as during 2008–2014), it can offset the crowding-out pressure.

The empirical evidence is mixed. Some studies find substantial crowding out for U.S. fiscal expansions; others find very little, especially when foreign capital is available and monetary policy is accommodating. The 2008–2014 period saw enormous government borrowing without measurable interest-rate increases, partly because the Fed was holding rates at zero.

The 2022–2023 period was different. Federal deficits remained large, the Fed was raising rates, and interest rates moved sharply higher. Some of this may have been crowding out from continued deficit-financed spending; some was Fed-driven inflation fighting. Disentangling the two is difficult.

## Debt sustainability

The longer-run question: at what point does accumulated debt become unsustainable?

There's no clean threshold. Different countries have managed very high debt (Japan over 200 percent of GDP) without crisis. Other countries have hit crisis at much lower levels (Greece below 100 percent before its 2010–12 crisis). The key factors:

**Interest rate vs. growth rate.** If the interest rate on government debt is below the growth rate of GDP, the debt-to-GDP ratio shrinks even with a deficit (the denominator grows faster than the numerator). For decades after WWII, this was true for the U.S. — debt fell relative to GDP without surpluses.

**Currency denomination.** Countries that borrow in their own currency can always pay in nominal terms (they can print the money). Countries that borrow in foreign currency face hard limits. The U.S. issues dollar-denominated debt; the dollar is the global reserve currency; the U.S. has more flexibility than most.

**Reserve currency status.** The U.S. dollar's role as the global reserve currency creates strong demand for U.S. debt. This demand finances U.S. deficits at lower interest rates than would otherwise be available.

**Political stability.** Markets price in not just the fundamentals but also confidence that the government will continue to honor its obligations. Loss of confidence can cause crisis even at apparently sustainable debt levels.

**Demographic trends.** Aging populations push up Social Security and Medicare costs. The U.S. fiscal trajectory is dominated by these long-run pressures more than by current discretionary spending.

The U.S. is currently borrowing at interest rates somewhat above its growth rate. This means the debt-to-GDP ratio will continue rising in the absence of surpluses or higher growth. The CBO projects U.S. debt-to-GDP rising substantially over the next few decades on current policy. Whether this trajectory continues, gets corrected, or eventually triggers a debt crisis is the central long-run U.S. fiscal question.

## Synthesis

Pull back. Government borrowing has multiple effects on the macroeconomy, all of which trace through the national saving-investment identity. The twin-deficits relationship connects fiscal deficits to trade deficits via capital flows. Ricardian equivalence partially offsets the demand effects of deficit spending — but not completely. Crowding out is real but variable, depending on slack, capital mobility, and monetary policy.

The longer-run question of debt sustainability depends on the relationship between interest rates and growth rates, the currency of denomination, and the trust that markets and citizens have in the government's commitment to its obligations. The U.S., with its reserve-currency status and its borrowing in its own currency, has more flexibility than most countries — but the trajectory of debt-to-GDP under current policy is rising, not stable.

The chapter we just read connects the fiscal-policy framework of Chapter 30 to the international-flows framework of Chapter 23 and the financial-market framework of Chapter 17. The interconnections are why macroeconomic policy is so complex — every decision has effects in multiple markets, and the effects depend on the state of all the others.

The next chapter takes the international view: how different countries arrange their macroeconomic policy frameworks, and what we can learn from the variation.

## LLM Exercises

**Exercise 1 — Apply the saving-investment identity.** Tell an LLM: "The government runs a deficit of 5% of GDP. Private saving is 18% of GDP. Domestic investment is 20% of GDP." Ask: what must net exports equal? Does this match the actual U.S. picture? Push the LLM to verify the math and to interpret the result.

**Exercise 2 — Argue both sides of Ricardian equivalence.** Ask an LLM to make the case that Ricardian equivalence holds (households save the tax cut) and the case that it doesn't (households spend it). Evaluate which case has stronger empirical support and what evidence would settle the disagreement.

**Exercise 3 — Diagnose crowding out.** Tell an LLM: "The U.S. ran enormous deficits in 2020–22 ($3T per year). Long-term interest rates initially fell, then rose sharply in 2022–23." Ask the LLM to decompose the rate path: how much was crowding out vs. Fed tightening vs. inflation expectations? Push for evidence-based reasoning.

**Exercise 4 — Stress-test debt sustainability.** Tell an LLM the U.S. interest rate on 10-year Treasuries is 4.5% and U.S. real GDP growth is 2%. The current debt-to-GDP is 100%. Ask: at what primary-surplus level (or deficit) is the debt-to-GDP ratio stable? Push for the calculation.

**Exercise 5 — Compare countries on debt.** Pick three countries with very different debt situations: Japan (high but stable, own currency), Greece (got into crisis), the U.S. (high and rising). Ask an LLM to identify which factors make each country's debt sustainable or not.

**What would change my mind.** The case that the U.S. can sustain rising debt-to-GDP indefinitely depends on continued global demand for U.S. assets and the dollar's reserve-currency status. If either weakens substantially, U.S. debt sustainability becomes harder. The empirical record so far is reassuring; the theoretical concern is that historical sustainability doesn't guarantee future sustainability, especially as debt rises.

**Still puzzling.** I do not have a clean account of why some countries with apparently sustainable debt situations slide into crisis, while others with apparently unsustainable situations don't. Greece's 2010 crisis hit at debt-to-GDP around 100 percent; Japan has run higher levels for decades without crisis. The difference seems to be partly about currency, partly about institutional credibility, partly about market psychology — but predictively, the line is hard to draw.
