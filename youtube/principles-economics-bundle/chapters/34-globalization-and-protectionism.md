# Chapter 34 — Globalization and Protectionism

*The political backlash against trade — and the policy debates that close this book.*

In June 2016, voters in the United Kingdom voted by a narrow margin to leave the European Union. Brexit. Five months later, in the United States, voters elected a president who had campaigned heavily on imposing tariffs on Chinese imports and renegotiating the North American Free Trade Agreement. By 2018, the new administration had imposed tariffs on $360 billion of Chinese goods, with China retaliating in kind. By 2020, the political consensus that had supported expanding trade for the previous several decades had visibly cracked.

The trade-skeptical politics did not come from nowhere. The aggregate gains from trade documented in Chapter 33 were real, but they had been distributed unevenly. Manufacturing communities in the U.S. Rust Belt had absorbed substantial losses from import competition, especially after China's accession to the World Trade Organization in 2001. Wages stagnated for non-college-educated workers (Chapter 14). Geographic inequality widened. The losers from globalization were concentrated, identifiable, and politically motivated. The winners — consumers benefiting from cheaper imports, workers and firms in export industries, capital-holders benefiting from global investment opportunities — were dispersed and invisible.

The framework from Chapter 18 — concentrated benefits and dispersed costs producing political pressure — runs in reverse here. The *losses* from trade are concentrated, the *gains* are dispersed. This produces political pressure for protection that is, in the framework of standard trade theory, "inefficient" — but politically rational from the standpoint of those most affected.

This chapter is about that political reality and the policy debates around trade. By the end you should understand the major tools of protectionism (tariffs, quotas, regulations), the standard arguments for and against trade protection, and the political-economy constraints that shape trade policy in democracies.

**Learning objectives.** By the end you should be able to: (1) describe tariffs and quotas and analyze their effects using the supply-and-demand framework; (2) evaluate the major arguments for protection (national security, infant industry, dumping, environmental, labor); (3) describe the major U.S. trade institutions and agreements; (4) explain the relationship between trade and inequality; (5) propose policies to address the concentrated losses from trade while preserving the aggregate gains.

**Prerequisites.** Chapter 3 (supply and demand). Chapter 18 (concentrated benefits and dispersed costs). Chapter 33 (gains from trade).

## How protectionism works

A **tariff** is a tax on imports. A 25 percent tariff means a $1,000 imported good costs $1,250 to bring across the border. The tariff raises the domestic price of imports, making them less competitive with domestic alternatives. The tariff revenue goes to the government.

A **quota** is a quantitative limit on imports. The U.S. might say "no more than 1 million tons of imported steel per year." Above the quota, imports are blocked. The quota constrains supply, raising the domestic price.

Both have the same general effect on the supply-demand diagram: the supply of the good available in the domestic market is restricted, the price rises, domestic producers gain (sell at higher prices), domestic consumers lose (pay higher prices), foreign exporters lose (sell less), and there's deadweight loss (some transactions that would have happened don't, with no offsetting benefit).

The math: a tariff transfers some of the consumer surplus to producers, some to the government as tariff revenue, and destroys some as deadweight loss. The deadweight loss is the social cost of the tariff. Taking the gains and losses together, the tariff makes the country worse off as a whole — even though the visible domestic-industry beneficiaries are clearly better off.

Quotas have similar effects on prices and quantities, but the "tariff revenue" goes instead to whoever has the right to import within the quota — sometimes domestic firms, sometimes foreign exporters with allocated import licenses, sometimes a mix. The total welfare effect is similar.

Other forms of protection: **non-tariff barriers** like regulatory standards (safety, environmental, technical) that disadvantage imports, **anti-dumping duties** when foreign exporters are accused of selling below cost, **countervailing duties** when foreign exporters benefit from foreign government subsidies. Each is more complex politically than direct tariffs but produces similar economic effects.

## The standard arguments for protection

Protection has been justified on multiple grounds for centuries. The major arguments:

**National security.** Some industries are essential for national defense. Domestic capacity in weapons, military electronics, energy, food, and now critical minerals matters more than the cost. Protection preserves the capacity even if it's not commercially competitive.

The argument has merit but is often overused — many industries claim to be "strategic" when their main interest is rent-seeking. The proper test is whether the specific industry would be unavailable in a crisis, not whether it serves any defense use at all.

**Infant industry.** A new industry needs time to develop scale, learning, and competitive position before it can compete with established foreign competitors. Temporary protection during this development phase eventually produces an internationally competitive industry.

The argument has theoretical support but practical problems. Temporary protection often becomes permanent. The protected industry develops political constituencies that resist removing the protection. The specific industries chosen for "infant" treatment are often selected by political rather than economic criteria. Empirically, infant-industry protection has worked in some East Asian cases (Korean steel, Japanese cars in early years) and has failed in many others.

**Anti-dumping.** Foreign producers selling below cost ("dumping") could drive domestic competitors out, then raise prices to monopoly levels. Anti-dumping duties prevent this predatory pricing.

The argument has economic content but is widely used as a protectionist tool. Most anti-dumping cases turn out to involve foreign goods sold at prices that reflect lower foreign costs rather than predatory pricing. The legal framework is gameable.

**Labor and environmental standards.** Free trade with countries that have weak labor or environmental standards creates pressure to weaken domestic standards too — a "race to the bottom." Protection (or trade conditions tied to standards) prevents this.

The argument has merit but is complicated. Lower standards in poor countries partly reflect poverty rather than choice; rising standards correlate with rising income; trade itself generates the income that supports better standards. Trade conditions targeting standards have a mixed record.

**Strategic trade.** Government support for specific industries can produce internationally competitive winners that wouldn't emerge under free trade. The economic theory (Brander-Spencer 1980s) showed this can sometimes work in industries with high economies of scale and few firms. The empirical record is mixed; selecting winners is hard.

## The case against protection

The standard economic arguments against protection:

**Higher consumer prices.** Domestic consumers pay more for the protected good. The cost is real and falls on millions of buyers.

**Misallocation.** Resources go to industries where the country doesn't have comparative advantage. The country produces less than it could.

**Deadweight loss.** Pure efficiency loss with no offsetting transfer.

**Retaliation.** Foreign countries impose protectionist measures of their own. Both countries lose.

**Political-economy distortions.** Protection creates rent-seeking incentives that consume political and economic resources.

**Weakening dynamic forces.** Less competition means less productivity pressure. Less exposure to foreign technologies and methods means less innovation.

**Hurting export industries.** Tariffs raise input costs for industries that use imported intermediates. Tariffs cause currency appreciation that hurts exporters.

The 2018–19 U.S. tariffs on Chinese imports illustrated several of these. U.S. consumers paid higher prices for affected goods. Some U.S. industries that depended on Chinese intermediate inputs suffered. China retaliated against U.S. exports (especially agricultural goods), hurting U.S. farmers. Some economic studies estimated the net cost to the U.S. economy at $40 billion or more annually.

## Trade adjustment

The political problem with free trade is that it produces concentrated losses. The standard policy response is **trade adjustment assistance** — programs to help workers and communities affected by import competition.

Major U.S. programs:
- **Trade Adjustment Assistance (TAA)** — extended unemployment benefits, retraining funds, relocation assistance for workers losing jobs to imports.
- **Generalized System of Preferences (GSP)** — tariff preferences for developing countries.
- Various sectoral programs targeted at specific industries.

The empirical record on TAA is mixed. Some workers have benefited substantially. Many have not — the retraining is often inadequate, the relocation is hard, the new jobs pay less than the old ones. Trade adjustment in the U.S. has been weak compared to similar programs in some other developed countries, contributing to the political backlash against trade.

The deeper issue is that trade adjustment requires the *winners* from trade to compensate the losers. In aggregate, the gains exceed the losses, so compensation is feasible. In practice, the political mechanism for redistributing those gains has been weak. The losers absorb concentrated costs; the winners enjoy dispersed gains; the political will to redistribute hasn't materialized at sufficient scale.

## Trade institutions

The post-WWII international trade system was built around several institutions:

**General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT)**, established 1947. A series of multilateral trade agreements that progressively reduced tariffs among signatory countries.

**World Trade Organization (WTO)**, replacing GATT in 1995. Provides ongoing negotiation, dispute resolution, and rule enforcement. China joined in 2001, opening its economy to global trade dramatically.

**Regional trade agreements** like NAFTA (1994, replaced by USMCA in 2020), the EU's single market, ASEAN, and others.

**Bilateral free-trade agreements** between specific country pairs.

The system was substantially eroded in the 2017–2024 period by U.S. tariffs, paralysis in WTO dispute resolution, and a general turn toward bilateralism and protectionism. Whether this represents a permanent change in the international trade order or a temporary disruption is one of the major open questions in international economics.

## Synthesis and a closing

Pull back. Trade produces gains for both sides, demonstrated mathematically by Ricardian comparative advantage and empirically by the post-1945 expansion of global trade and prosperity. The gains, however, are unevenly distributed. Some workers and communities absorb concentrated losses; the broader population enjoys dispersed gains. The political pressure to protect the losers, even at the cost of the dispersed gains, is real and recurring.

Standard economic analysis shows that protection imposes costs on consumers, producers using imported intermediates, exporters facing retaliation, and the country's overall productive efficiency. These costs are usually larger than the benefits to the protected industries.

Trade adjustment assistance is the textbook policy response. In practice, it has been inadequate to compensate the losers in the U.S. and similar countries. The unmet promise of compensation is part of why trade-skeptical politics have grown.

The closing chapter of this book is the closing chapter of a story that runs throughout economics: markets produce aggregate gains but distributional consequences. The aggregate gains from trade — like the aggregate gains from technology, from competition, from market efficiency — are real and substantial. The distributional consequences require institutional responses (tax policy, transfer programs, retraining, geographic mobility support, infrastructure investment) that have been politically harder to implement than the gains have been to enjoy.

The economist's task is to show both sides clearly. The aggregate gains are real. The distributional consequences are real. The trade-off requires institutional response. None of these is trivial; ignoring any of them is a kind of dishonesty.

You have now finished a tour of principles of economics. The discipline gives you a way of seeing — markets, prices, choices under scarcity, aggregate flows, institutional structures — that no other discipline provides. It does not give you all the answers; many of the most important questions involve values, politics, and contingency that economics doesn't fully capture. But it gives you tools that, applied carefully, can illuminate questions that would otherwise be confused. Use the tools. Be honest about their limits. Keep learning.

## LLM Exercises

**Exercise 1 — Calculate a tariff's effect.** Tell an LLM about a hypothetical good: domestic price $100, world price $80, domestic supply at $80 = 100 units, domestic demand at $80 = 200 units. A 25% tariff is imposed. What's the new price? New domestic production? New imports? Tariff revenue? Deadweight loss?

**Exercise 2 — Argue for and against a specific protection.** Pick a real protective measure (U.S. steel tariffs, EU agricultural subsidies, Japan's rice protections, U.S. lumber tariffs on Canada). Ask an LLM to argue both sides. Push for specifics rather than generic arguments.

**Exercise 3 — Diagnose the China shock.** Tell an LLM about the U.S. labor-market effects of China's WTO entry in 2001. Ask it to summarize the empirical evidence (Autor-Dorn-Hanson "China shock" research). What share of U.S. manufacturing job loss was attributable to Chinese import competition? What did trade adjustment do or fail to do?

**Exercise 4 — Design a trade adjustment program.** Ask an LLM to design a U.S. trade adjustment program adequate to compensate workers losing jobs to imports. Consider scale, eligibility, retraining, relocation, geographic targeting. Push for specifics that engage with the empirical limitations of past programs.

**Exercise 5 — Evaluate the Brexit decision.** Ask an LLM to evaluate, eight years after the 2016 vote, whether Brexit produced the economic outcomes that pro- and anti-Brexit economists predicted. Push for specific evidence on trade, growth, regulatory changes, immigration, financial sector. The exercise teaches the difficulty of separating policy effects from other forces.

## What comes next

You've reached the end of the book. Reading this chapter does not exhaust the topic of trade or any other; entire scholarly literatures and lifelong specialties await further study. What this book has tried to do is lay out the conceptual framework — the tools, the trade-offs, the empirical patterns — that make economic phenomena legible. From here, you can read the news with the eyes of someone who knows the framework. You can ask better questions. You can spot when arguments are missing necessary pieces.

Specific places to go further: behavioral economics (Thaler, Kahneman, Sunstein), development economics (Banerjee-Duflo, Acemoglu-Robinson), economic history (Mokyr, McCloskey), industrial organization, labor economics, international macroeconomics. The discipline is large; the principles you've learned are the entry door.

**What would change my mind.** The case that the gains from trade exceed the losses has empirical support but rests on aggregate measurements that may underweight the welfare cost of concentrated losses. If new evidence (perhaps from the COVID-era supply chain disruptions, perhaps from the China shock literature) showed that the social cost of trade-induced job loss is substantially larger than current estimates, the calculus would shift. The question is not whether trade has gains but how to weigh aggregate gains against concentrated losses.

**Still puzzling.** I do not have a clean account of why some societies have managed the political economy of trade much better than others. Northern European countries with strong social safety nets have had less trade-skeptical politics than the U.S. despite similar exposure to import competition. The institutions that absorb the losses from trade and convert them into politically tolerable transitions are documented but not predictively understood. Whether the U.S. can build comparable institutions is one of the open questions for the next generation of economists.
