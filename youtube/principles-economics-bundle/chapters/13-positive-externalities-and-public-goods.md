# Chapter 13 — Positive Externalities and Public Goods

*When the market underproduces — vaccines, basic research, lighthouses, and why your phone's GPS is free.*

In your pocket right now is a piece of technology that knows where you are on the surface of the Earth, accurate to within a few meters, anywhere in the world, in real time. It works through a constellation of about thirty satellites in medium Earth orbit, broadcasting precisely timed signals that your phone uses to triangulate its position. The whole system was built and is operated by the U.S. Department of Defense at a cost of tens of billions of dollars over decades. You use it, every day, for free.

Why did the U.S. government build GPS instead of waiting for a private company to do it?

Because no private company would have. The benefits of GPS — better navigation, safer aviation, more efficient shipping, easier logistics, the modern app economy — accrue to everyone who uses it, and almost no one pays a fee directly for the satellite service. A private company that built the satellites and tried to charge per use would face two problems. First, once the signal is broadcast, anyone with a receiver can pick it up. Excluding non-payers is hard. Second, one more user picking up the signal does not reduce the signal's availability to anyone else. There is no scarcity at the margin.

This combination — *can't easily exclude users* and *one more user doesn't subtract* — is what economists call a **public good**. The market underproduces them. Sometimes the market produces none of them. The treatment is some form of collective provision: government, philanthropy, public-private partnership, or various clever institutional designs. The diagnosis is the mirror of Chapter 12. There, externalities pushed the market to overproduce. Here, externalities push the market to underproduce.

This chapter develops the diagnosis and the policy responses. By the end you should be able to identify whether a good has significant positive externalities or public-goods features, predict the direction of the market's failure, and evaluate the main responses — patents, subsidies, government provision, voluntary mechanisms.

**Learning objectives.** By the end you should be able to: (1) define a positive externality and explain why the unregulated market under-produces; (2) explain why R&D consistently shows social returns higher than private returns; (3) describe how patents and copyrights attempt to align private and social incentives, and identify the trade-offs involved; (4) define a public good using the criteria of non-excludability and non-rivalry; (5) explain the free-rider problem and the standard responses to it.

**Prerequisites.** Chapter 9 (monopoly, patents). Chapter 12 (externalities, market failure).

## Positive externalities — the mirror case

A market transaction has a *private benefit* (what the buyer gets) and a *private cost* (what the seller pays). When the transaction also produces *benefits to third parties* — benefits no one pays for — there's a **positive externality**.

Examples:

- Vaccines. The vaccinated person benefits directly. Other people in the community benefit because the vaccinated person can no longer transmit the disease — even if they themselves are unvaccinated. This is the herd-immunity benefit. The unvaccinated free-rider gets some protection from everyone else's decisions.
- Education. The educated person earns more. Everyone around them benefits from being in a more productive workforce, a more informed electorate, a more innovative economy.
- Basic research. The scientist who develops the underlying knowledge can sometimes capture some of the value through licensing or consulting. Most of the value spills over — to other scientists who build on the work, to firms that apply it, to the public who benefits from the applications.
- Beautiful gardens visible from the street. The gardener bears the cost; passersby get a free aesthetic benefit.

The economic consequence: the unregulated market produces less of the good than would be socially optimal. The market quantity sets *private* marginal benefit equal to marginal cost. The socially optimal quantity sets *social* marginal benefit (private + external) equal to marginal cost. Because the social benefit exceeds the private, the social-optimal quantity is higher.

Draw it. Demand curve representing private marginal benefit. Supply curve. Equilibrium at the market quantity. Now draw a second demand curve above the first, shifted up by the external benefit per unit. That's the social-marginal-benefit curve. Where it crosses supply is the socially efficient quantity — higher than the market quantity, with a higher price. The gap is *underproduction*.

The treatment options mirror Chapter 12's:

- **Subsidies** — pay producers (or consumers) to produce or consume more. Vaccines are subsidized in many countries. Education is subsidized through public schools and university funding.
- **Direct public provision** — the government produces the good itself. Public schools, public health agencies, the GPS satellite system.
- **Property rights / monopoly grants** — patents and copyrights give the inventor a temporary monopoly, raising the private return enough to motivate the investment.

The third one — patents — is worth a closer look, because it sits at the messy intersection of Chapter 9 (monopoly costs) and this chapter (under-provision of positive externalities).

## R&D and the rate-of-return gap

Empirical studies of corporate R&D show a consistent pattern: the social rate of return on R&D is substantially higher than the private rate of return. Estimates vary, but typical findings put the social return in the range of 30–50 percent and the private return at 7–15 percent. In other words, every dollar a firm invests in R&D produces about $0.07 to $0.15 of profit for the firm and $0.30 to $0.50 of value for society as a whole. Most of the value spills out.

The mechanism is straightforward. A firm develops a new technology or process. The firm captures some of the value through sales, market share, or licensing. But the technology eventually informs other firms' work. Engineers leave and take ideas with them. Patents expire. Reverse-engineering happens. Knowledge diffuses. Total social value of the innovation, summed across all the firms and consumers eventually affected, is much larger than what the original inventor captures.

If firms could capture all the social value, they would invest more in R&D. They can't, so they don't. The market under-invests in research and development by a substantial margin.

Government policy responses:

**Patents.** Grant the inventor a temporary monopoly (typically 20 years from filing). The monopoly raises the private return — enough to motivate investment that wouldn't have happened otherwise. The cost: monopoly pricing during the patent period, with the deadweight losses we examined in Chapter 9. The trade-off is real. Patents work *because* they create monopoly power; they hurt *for the same reason*. Whether the dynamic gains (more innovation) exceed the static costs (monopoly pricing) depends on the industry, and is the subject of an extensive empirical literature with contested conclusions.

**Direct subsidies and public funding of research.** The U.S. National Institutes of Health spend roughly $50 billion a year. The National Science Foundation spends about $10 billion. Defense research adds tens of billions more. Universities receive hundreds of billions in research grants. The output is a large fraction of the basic-research literature that eventually gets converted to commercial applications. The argument is that basic research is too far upstream of commercial value for any single firm to fund efficiently.

**Tax credits for private R&D.** The U.S. Research and Experimentation Tax Credit lets firms reduce their tax bill in proportion to their R&D spending. Most OECD countries have similar instruments. The mechanism: lower the after-tax cost of R&D, raise the after-tax private return, get more R&D.

**University-industry partnerships.** Research consortia, joint ventures, technology transfer offices, faculty consulting arrangements. Mixed track record. Some partnerships have produced enormous value. Others have produced administrative overhead.

The combination of these instruments produces total U.S. R&D spending of about 3.5 percent of GDP, roughly half public and half private. Most other developed countries spend between 2 and 4 percent. Whether more would be better is a serious open question. The under-investment problem is well-documented in theory; the optimal level of public spending is harder to pin down empirically.

## Public goods — the extreme case

Some goods have positive externalities so extensive that private firms can't capture *any* meaningful share of the social benefit. These are **public goods**, and they are defined by two properties:

**Non-rivalry.** One person's consumption doesn't reduce the amount available for others. A lighthouse beam guiding one ship doesn't dim for the next ship. A radio broadcast heard by one listener is not less audible to the next. National defense protecting one citizen protects the next at no marginal cost.

**Non-excludability.** It's hard or impossible to prevent non-payers from consuming the good. Once the lighthouse is lit, all ships in the area benefit. Once the radio program is broadcast, anyone with a receiver can listen. Once the air is cleaned, everyone breathes it.

A good that has both properties is a *pure public good*. The market produces such goods very poorly or not at all, because the rational individual response is to wait for someone else to pay and then consume for free. This is the **free-rider problem**, and it's the central diagnostic of public-goods economics.

Suppose ten people each value clean air at $100 per year. The total social value is $1,000 per year. A clean-air program that costs $500 per year is socially worth doing. But each individual reasons: "If the others pay, the air will be clean and I won't have contributed; if the others don't pay, my $100 alone won't be enough; either way, I shouldn't pay." Everyone reasons this way. No one pays. The clean-air program isn't funded.

The standard response is **collective provision through government**. Tax everyone, fund the program, distribute the benefit. The coercion is the point — voluntary contributions don't solve the problem.

Public goods are not just things government provides. They are a *category* defined by the two properties. Some things government provides are public goods (national defense, GPS, basic research, lighthouses). Some are not (Medicare healthcare for individual recipients is rival and excludable, even though publicly funded). Some private things are partial public goods (a fireworks display in a city, free for anyone to watch). The category cuts across the public-private organizational distinction.

## A four-quadrant typology

Combining the two dimensions — rivalry and excludability — produces four categories of goods.

|  | Excludable | Non-excludable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Rival** | Private good (apple, haircut) | Common-pool resource (ocean fish, groundwater) |
| **Non-rival** | Club good (cable TV, gym membership) | Public good (lighthouse, GPS) |

Each quadrant has its own economics.

- **Private goods** are the standard case in Chapters 3 through 11. Markets handle them well.
- **Common-pool resources** suffer from over-extraction. No one owns the ocean fishery; everyone fishes; the stock collapses. The "tragedy of the commons" in Garrett Hardin's 1968 phrase.
- **Club goods** can be provided privately by charging members. The exclusion is what allows the business model.
- **Public goods** are the case this chapter has emphasized. The market under-produces; collective provision is the standard fix.

The four-quadrant classification is not a moral statement about what *should* be private or public. It's a structural statement about what kinds of provision mechanisms work for what kinds of goods. The fishery problem (common-pool) and the GPS problem (public good) are both market failures, but the failure modes and the policy responses differ in important ways.

## Synthesis

Pull back. The mirror to Chapter 12 is now in place. Where negative externalities make markets *over-produce*, positive externalities make markets *under-produce*. Both diagnoses come from the same structural insight: the price reflects only the costs and benefits to buyer and seller, and when those diverge from the costs and benefits to society at large, the market quantity is wrong.

The treatment menu is similar in shape: change the price (subsidies, taxes), change the property rights (patents, IP), or step in directly (public provision). The choice depends on the specific externality and the specific institutional context.

Public goods are the extreme case. When non-rivalry and non-excludability are both strong, the market simply cannot provide the good — the free-rider problem is too sharp. Collective provision through coercion (taxes) is the standard solution. The lighthouse, GPS, basic scientific research, national defense — all live in this category, and all are funded primarily by some level of government for reasons the model makes precise.

The R&D rate-of-return gap is the most quantitatively important application in this chapter. Society gets enormous value from research. Firms capture a small share. The gap, multiplied across decades and trillions of dollars of economic output, is the case for sustained public investment in research — even when politicians find specific projects easy to ridicule. The Voyager probe in our chapter's opening was funded by NASA in the 1970s; it taught us that Saturn has rings inside its rings, that Jupiter has a thin one of its own, and produced research that flowed back into the engineering of every telecommunications satellite that followed. The cost was substantial. The cost was also a fraction of the value that emerged from it.

## LLM Exercises

**Exercise 1 — Identify the externality structure.** Pick a good or service in your daily life that you suspect has significant positive externalities (a college degree, a flu shot, a well-maintained front yard, a Wikipedia edit, a piece of open-source software). Ask an LLM to identify (a) the private benefit, (b) the external benefit and who captures it, and (c) the direction the market's quantity is wrong. Push the LLM to be specific.

**Exercise 2 — The patent trade-off.** Tell an LLM you are advising a country's pharmaceutical regulator on patent length. Currently 20 years. Ask the LLM to argue for shortening to 10 and for extending to 30. Then ask which case is stronger and why. A good answer engages with the dynamic-incentive vs. static-deadweight-loss trade-off concretely.

**Exercise 3 — Apply the four-quadrant typology.** Give an LLM this list and ask it to classify each: a Spotify subscription, the air over a city, a salmon in a river, a pizza, a streetlight, a fenced beach access, a satellite TV broadcast, a piece of open-source code. Discuss any classifications you find counter-intuitive.

**Exercise 4 — Solve a free-rider problem.** Tell an LLM that a small condo association of 30 units needs to fund repainting of the building's exterior, which would benefit all owners and is hard to exclude individuals from. Ask the LLM to design two solutions: one based on collective coercion (e.g., HOA dues), and one based on voluntary mechanisms (assurance contracts, social pressure, etc.). Evaluate which is more likely to succeed.

**Exercise 5 — Argue the case for public R&D.** Ask an LLM to make the strongest case for substantial U.S. federal spending on basic scientific research, using the rate-of-return-gap argument from this chapter. Then ask it to make the strongest case *against*, focusing on government's poor record at picking winners and the risk of crowding out private investment. Evaluate which side is more credible and what evidence would change your mind.

**What would change my mind.** The case for substantial public R&D funding rests on the assumption that government can effectively allocate research dollars. The historical record is mixed — the NIH and NSF have funded enormous successes and also large amounts of mediocre work. If, in some specific country or moment, the institutional capacity to allocate research dollars well is absent, the case for public funding weakens. The U.S. has had unusually capable research-funding institutions for decades; not every country does.

**Still puzzling.** I do not have a clean account of why some near-public-goods (basic research, broadcast TV in the network era, infrastructure of all kinds) have been provided through wildly different mixes of public and private mechanisms in different countries — and produced roughly comparable outcomes. The institutional specifics seem to matter more than the public-vs-private organizational form, in ways the standard model doesn't capture.
