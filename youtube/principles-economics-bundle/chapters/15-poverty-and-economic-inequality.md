# Chapter 15 — Poverty and Economic Inequality

*Two different questions about how a society's income gets distributed — and what to do about each.*

In September 2011, a group of protesters set up tents in Zuccotti Park in lower Manhattan. They called themselves Occupy Wall Street. Their slogan was a number: "We are the 99 percent." The number was a reference to the income share captured by the top 1 percent of U.S. households, which by 2011 had risen to about 20 percent of all pre-tax income — roughly double its share in 1980.

The movement faded within a few months. The number stuck. By 2024, "the one percent" was a phrase used in everyday political conversation, in business journalism, and in academic research that had not existed in 1980. The Occupy movement is largely remembered as a protest that didn't change much in the short term. The framing it produced — that inequality is a feature of the U.S. economy worth talking about, distinct from poverty — has reshaped policy debates ever since.

This chapter is about that distinction and what comes from it.

Two different questions. *Poverty* asks how many people are below some threshold of acceptable living standards — how many are in absolute or near-absolute deprivation. *Inequality* asks how the total income of the society is distributed across the population — how the gap between top and bottom evolves. The two are related but not the same. A society can reduce poverty while leaving inequality high (China since 1980). A society can have low inequality with significant poverty (some low-income countries). Most policy debates conflate them, sometimes deliberately. The first move of any serious analysis is to keep them separate.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to measure both, identify what U.S. data show about each, and evaluate the major policy responses on the dimensions of effectiveness, side-effects, and ethical priors.

**Learning objectives.** By the end you should be able to: (1) define the U.S. poverty line and explain its strengths and weaknesses; (2) describe U.S. trends in poverty and inequality over the past several decades; (3) construct a Lorenz curve and interpret a Gini coefficient; (4) explain the poverty trap and how the Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC) addresses it; (5) identify the major U.S. safety-net programs and their effects; (6) articulate the equality-efficiency trade-off in a way that doesn't pretend the trade-off doesn't exist.

**Prerequisites.** Chapter 14 (labor markets, wage determination). Chapter 7 (cost concepts). Chapter 6 (utility, marginal utility).

## Poverty — measuring deprivation

The **poverty line** is a threshold income below which a household is considered to be in poverty. The U.S. poverty line, as of the early 2020s, sits at roughly $14,000 for a single-person household and roughly $30,000 for a four-person household. The exact number is updated annually for inflation.

The official U.S. poverty line was originally set by Mollie Orshansky at the Social Security Administration in the 1960s, using a method that's stayed largely the same: take the cost of a basic food budget, multiply by three (because food was estimated to be one-third of household budgets at the time), and adjust for inflation. The number has been criticized continuously since, on several grounds:

- Food's share of household budgets has fallen below one-third; the multiplier may understate the true cost.
- The line doesn't vary by region, but housing costs vary enormously between Manhattan and rural Mississippi.
- The line uses pre-tax cash income, which excludes EITC, food stamps, Medicaid, and many other in-kind benefits that materially change living standards.

The Census Bureau publishes an alternative measure (the Supplemental Poverty Measure, or SPM) that addresses some of these issues. The SPM and the official measure track each other roughly but differ on the level of poverty by a few percentage points.

U.S. poverty rates over the past sixty years tell a complicated story. Poverty fell sharply between 1959 and 1973, from about 22 percent to about 11 percent, driven by general economic growth, expansion of Social Security, and the early War on Poverty programs. From 1973 to 2019 the rate fluctuated between 11 percent and 15 percent — going up in recessions, down in expansions, but with no sustained downward trend. The COVID-19 pandemic produced sharp short-run fluctuations. The most recent (early 2020s) measure puts the official poverty rate around 11–12 percent.

Some groups face systematically higher poverty rates: children, single-parent households (especially female-headed), Black and Hispanic Americans, people with disabilities, the unemployed and underemployed. The intersections matter; a child in a single-mother household has a much higher poverty rate than a similar child in a two-parent household.

## Inequality — measuring the spread

Inequality asks a different question: not "how many are below the threshold," but "how is the total cake divided?"

Two standard measurement tools.

**Quintile shares.** Sort the population by income, divide into five equal-size groups (quintiles) from poorest to richest. Compute the share of total income each quintile receives. In a perfectly equal society, each quintile would get exactly 20 percent. In the U.S., as of recent data, the bottom quintile gets roughly 3 percent of pre-tax income; the top quintile gets about 50 percent. The top 5 percent gets about 22 percent. The top 1 percent gets about 20 percent.

**Lorenz curve and Gini coefficient.** Plot cumulative income share (vertical axis) against cumulative population share (horizontal axis). Sort population from poorest to richest. In a perfectly equal society, the curve would be a 45-degree line. In actual societies, it bows below the line — the poorer half of the population gets less than half the income.

The **Gini coefficient** measures how far the actual Lorenz curve falls below the 45-degree line, scaled from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (one household has everything). The U.S. Gini for household income, post-tax, is around 0.40. Sweden's is around 0.27. Brazil's is around 0.53. The U.S. Gini has risen substantially since the late 1970s — from about 0.35 to about 0.40, or roughly 14 percent — driven by the wage divergence we discussed in Chapter 14.

A few things the inequality numbers don't capture. They don't capture *life-cycle* differences (young and old earn less than middle-aged in any society). They don't capture *mobility* — the chance that someone in the bottom quintile this year is in a different quintile in ten years. They don't capture *consumption* differences, which are smaller than income differences because of redistribution and savings patterns. And they don't capture *non-income wellbeing* — health, education, leisure, dignity. Inequality measures are useful but incomplete.

The U.S. has unusually high inequality among rich democracies. Within the OECD, only Mexico, Chile, and Turkey have higher Gini coefficients. The Northern European countries — Denmark, Sweden, Norway, Finland — sit at the low end. The contrast between U.S. and Northern European inequality reflects different choices about labor-market institutions, taxation, and the welfare state, rather than fundamental differences in pre-tax wage distributions (which are surprisingly similar).

## The poverty trap

A practical problem arises at the intersection of poverty and labor-market policy. Suppose a government provides $20,000 a year in cash assistance to households below the poverty line, phased out as the household earns more. Specifically, every $1 the household earns reduces the assistance by $1 (a "100 percent benefit-reduction rate"). The household keeps $20,000 if it doesn't work. If it earns $20,000, it loses all $20,000 of assistance. Net change in household income: zero.

This is the **poverty trap**. The assistance program imposes an effective marginal tax rate of 100 percent on the first $20,000 of earnings — leaving the household with no financial incentive to work for any earnings below the cutoff.

Real safety-net programs typically don't have 100 percent benefit-reduction rates, but the combined rate from multiple programs (cash aid + food stamps + housing assistance + healthcare subsidies + childcare subsidies) often exceeds 70 percent in some income ranges. Households moving from welfare to work can find that they're working full-time and only marginally better off than before.

The most influential structural response is the **Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC)**, introduced in 1975 and substantially expanded since. The EITC is a wage subsidy — the government pays a credit that *increases* with earnings up to a threshold, then phases out gradually. Instead of being penalized for working, low-income workers get a bonus. The phase-out is gradual enough that the implicit marginal tax rate stays well below 100 percent.

The EITC is widely credited with substantially reducing poverty among working households and producing measurable increases in labor-force participation. It is also one of the few U.S. anti-poverty programs that has bipartisan support — the right likes it because it's tied to work, the left likes it because it transfers money to working-poor households. Its limitations: it reaches only those who work; it does little for the deeply poor with no labor-market attachment; and at higher levels of design, the phase-out rate itself can become a hidden tax.

## The U.S. safety net

The U.S. anti-poverty system is a patchwork of programs, each with its own eligibility rules, benefit formulas, and administering agencies. The major pieces:

- **Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF).** Cash assistance to low-income families with children, with work requirements and time limits. Successor to the older AFDC program; substantially smaller after 1996 welfare reform.
- **Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP).** Food stamps. Helps about 40 million Americans afford groceries, with eligibility based on income and household size.
- **Medicaid.** Health insurance for low-income individuals and families. Joint federal-state program; the Affordable Care Act expanded it substantially in participating states.
- **Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC).** Wage subsidy for low-income workers, scaled by family size.
- **Supplemental Security Income (SSI).** Cash assistance for elderly, blind, and disabled people with little income or assets.
- **Section 8 housing vouchers and public housing.** Rental assistance for low-income households.

Each program is studied separately, with its own large literature on costs, benefits, and unintended effects. The combined picture: the U.S. safety net substantially reduces poverty as measured by the Supplemental Poverty Measure (which counts in-kind benefits) but less so by the official measure (which doesn't). Estimates suggest the combined system reduces the poverty rate by roughly 5 to 10 percentage points relative to a counterfactual without any of it.

## The equality-efficiency trade-off

Now the harder question. Should we tax higher earners and transfer to lower earners more than we currently do?

The case for more redistribution: diminishing marginal utility of income (a dollar to a poor household produces more wellbeing than a dollar to a rich household), basic norms of fairness, the social-contract argument that those who benefit most from market arrangements owe back to the system, and the empirical observation that high-redistribution countries (Nordic) appear to function well on most aggregate measures.

The case against: redistribution has efficiency costs. High marginal tax rates reduce work effort, savings, investment, and entrepreneurial risk-taking. Welfare-state programs with high benefit-reduction rates produce poverty traps. The empirical question is *how big* these efficiency costs are, and the literature is contested. Most economists now think they are real but smaller than was believed in the 1980s; reasonable disagreement remains.

This is the **equality-efficiency trade-off** in its starkest form. Arthur Okun, writing in 1975, called it "the big tradeoff" — the idea that there is a "leaky bucket" by which we move income from rich to poor, with some leaking out the bottom along the way. How much leaks out per dollar transferred is the empirical question. How big a leak you accept in exchange for less inequality is the values question.

The clean version of the trade-off has limits. Some redistributive interventions (early childhood education, the EITC, healthcare access) appear to *increase* economic growth in the long run by improving human capital. Other interventions (high marginal tax rates on capital income, complex transfer programs) may reduce growth more than they reduce inequality. The trade-off exists; its size depends on what specifically you're proposing to do.

## Synthesis

Pull back. Two distinct questions. *Poverty* asks who is below an acceptable standard of living; *inequality* asks how the cake is divided. Both have measurable definitions. Both have moved over the past sixty years in ways economists understand reasonably well: poverty is roughly stable since 1973, inequality has risen substantially since 1980.

Policy responses come from three families: cash and in-kind transfers (the safety net), wage subsidies (the EITC), and tax policy (progressive income tax, capital gains tax, estate tax). Each has trade-offs. Each interacts with the others. The combined system reduces poverty but not by as much as some advocates would like and at higher cost than some critics accept.

The deeper challenge is that the underlying structural shifts that drove inequality up — globalization, technological change, declining unionization, growing market power — are largely outside what the safety net can directly address. The safety net redistributes; it doesn't change the wage distribution that gets generated in the first place. To affect *pre-tax* inequality requires either rebuilding labor-market institutions, changing trade and technology policy, or accepting the wage distribution and intensifying redistribution. The U.S. has, for the most part, chosen the third option, with results visible in the data Occupy Wall Street pointed at.

## LLM Exercises

**Exercise 1 — Compute a Gini.** Tell an LLM: "An economy has five people with incomes of $10K, $20K, $30K, $40K, and $100K." Ask it to construct the Lorenz curve (cumulative income share by cumulative population share) and estimate the Gini coefficient. Verify the math. Then ask the LLM to explain in plain English what the number means.

**Exercise 2 — Diagnose a poverty trap.** Tell an LLM about a hypothetical safety net program: "Cash aid of $15K/year, phased out at 80 cents per dollar of earnings up to $18,750 of earnings." Ask the LLM to compute the household's effective income at earnings of $0, $5K, $10K, $15K, $18,750, and $25K. Identify the income range where working an additional hour leaves the household barely better off. Then design an alternative phase-out that reduces the trap.

**Exercise 3 — Compare countries on inequality.** Pick three countries with very different Gini coefficients (Denmark ~0.27, U.S. ~0.40, South Africa ~0.63). Ask an LLM to identify what features of each country's tax system, labor-market institutions, and education system contribute to its position. Push for specifics rather than handwaving.

**Exercise 4 — Argue both sides of universal basic income.** Ask an LLM to argue for a UBI as a replacement for the existing U.S. safety net, and to argue against. Evaluate which case is stronger and identify what empirical evidence (e.g., from pilot programs) would change the answer.

**Exercise 5 — Decompose U.S. inequality growth.** Ask an LLM: "U.S. household income inequality, measured by the Gini, rose from about 0.35 in 1980 to about 0.40 today. What share of the rise is attributable to (a) wage divergence between college-educated and non-college workers, (b) growth in capital income at the top, (c) demographic change (more single-parent households, aging), (d) changes in tax policy?" Push for evidence rather than assertions. The exercise tests whether the LLM can distinguish supported claims from speculation.

**What would change my mind.** The case that redistribution has small efficiency costs depends on empirical estimates that are imprecise and sensitive to specification. If new evidence showed that high marginal tax rates substantially reduce labor supply or entrepreneurship in the U.S. context, the case for more redistribution would weaken. The recent empirical work has tended toward smaller estimated costs than was once thought, but the literature is not settled.

**Still puzzling.** I do not have a clean account of why some unequal societies remain politically stable for long periods while others tip into crisis. The Gini coefficient, by itself, predicts political volatility weakly. Other factors — perceived fairness of the process that produced the inequality, mobility expectations, intergroup composition — seem to matter as much or more, but predictively in messy ways.
