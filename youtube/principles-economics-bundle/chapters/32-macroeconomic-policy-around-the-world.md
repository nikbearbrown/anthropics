# Chapter 32 — Macroeconomic Policy Around the World

*The same problems, different institutional answers — what we can learn from how other countries do macro.*

In 1960, South Korea had a GDP per capita of about $158 — comparable to Ghana, Burma, and the Philippines. By 2024, South Korean GDP per capita exceeded $35,000. Ghana's, in 2024, was about $2,300. The Philippines' was about $4,000. Burma (Myanmar) was below $1,500. Sixty years had transformed Korea into a high-income economy while leaving its 1960 peers in low-to-middle-income status.

What changed for Korea was not luck. It was a sustained policy framework that emphasized supply-side factors — investment in education and physical capital, integration with world markets, market-oriented reforms — over the four decades from 1960 to 2000. The pattern is also visible in Taiwan, Singapore, Hong Kong (the "East Asian Tigers"), in Japan's earlier postwar transformation, and partially in China since 1980. Each country chose policies that other countries with similar starting conditions did not choose. The differences in choices produced very different outcomes.

This chapter looks at macroeconomic policy across countries — what high-income, middle-income, and low-income countries face, how their macroeconomic priorities differ, and what we can learn from the variation. By the end you should be able to identify the key factors that distinguish countries' macroeconomic situations, evaluate the policies that have worked (and haven't) in different contexts, and understand why "the right macroeconomic policy" is contextual.

**Learning objectives.** By the end you should be able to: (1) classify countries by income level using GDP per capita; (2) identify the major factors that distinguish countries' macroeconomic conditions (geography, demographics, institutions, industry structure); (3) describe the growth policies that have characterized successful middle-income transitions; (4) explain how unemployment, inflation, and trade-balance challenges differ across income levels; (5) evaluate the case for market-oriented reforms in different country contexts.

**Prerequisites.** Chapter 19 (GDP). Chapter 20 (economic growth). Chapter 21 (unemployment). Chapter 22 (inflation). Chapter 23 (international trade).

## Income classifications

The World Bank classifies countries into four groups by GNI per capita (Gross National Income, similar to GDP per capita with some adjustments for income earned abroad):

- **High-income** (~$13,845 and above as of recent updates): U.S., Germany, Japan, U.K., South Korea, Singapore, Australia, most of Western Europe.
- **Upper-middle-income** (~$4,466 to $13,845): China, Brazil, Mexico, Russia, Turkey, Argentina.
- **Lower-middle-income** (~$1,136 to $4,466): India, Egypt, Indonesia, Vietnam, Nigeria.
- **Low-income** (below $1,136): roughly 30 countries, mostly in Sub-Saharan Africa, plus some in South Asia.

These are rough categories. Within each, countries vary enormously. The high-income group includes the U.S. ($80,000) and Greece ($25,000) — both high but very different. The low-income group includes very different situations.

The classifications matter because countries at different income levels face different macroeconomic challenges. High-income countries focus on managing the business cycle, controlling inflation, and maintaining productive capacity. Low-income countries focus on enabling sustained growth in the first place.

## What distinguishes country contexts

Several factors shape what macroeconomic policy is feasible and effective in each country:

**Geography.** Landlocked countries trade less. Tropical countries face climate constraints on agriculture and disease burden. Resource-rich countries can be paradoxically poor (the "resource curse"). Coastal access, climate, and natural-resource endowments shape the economic baseline.

**Demographics.** Rapidly growing populations face challenges absorbing workers; rapidly aging populations face fiscal pressure on pensions and healthcare. The demographic profile drives long-run policy needs.

**Industry structure.** Countries with diversified industrial bases handle external shocks better than those dependent on a single commodity. Services-heavy economies face different pressures than manufacturing-heavy ones.

**Institutions.** Property rights, rule of law, contract enforcement, financial-system depth, regulatory quality, anti-corruption norms. These determine whether physical and human capital can produce growth or get extracted/wasted.

**Education and human capital.** A country's labor force quality determines its capacity to absorb new technologies and adapt to changing conditions.

**Openness to trade and capital.** Open economies access global markets and capital but face external shocks. Closed economies lose access to gains from trade.

**Geopolitical position.** Alliances, conflicts, sanctions, geographic stability all affect economic possibilities.

## The growth policy menu

Different income levels call for different growth policies. Some patterns emerge.

**Low-income countries** typically benefit from:
- Investing in basic infrastructure (roads, electricity, water).
- Expanding primary education.
- Building basic public health.
- Strengthening property rights and the rule of law.
- Reducing corruption.
- Opening to trade in stages, with appropriate sequencing.

The challenge is that these policies require functioning institutions to implement, and weak institutions are often the underlying problem. Countries that have managed to build basic institutional capacity (Botswana from the 1960s, Rwanda from the 1990s) have made dramatic progress; many others have struggled.

**Middle-income countries** face the "middle-income trap" — the tendency for countries to stall around $5,000–$15,000 GDP per capita. Catch-up growth from labor-cost advantages eventually exhausts itself; further growth requires moving up the value chain into more sophisticated production. The successful middle-income transitions (Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, more recently China in the 2000s) generally combined:

- Sustained high savings and investment rates.
- Investment in higher education.
- Export-oriented industrial policy.
- Selective integration with global supply chains.
- Gradual market-oriented reforms.
- Public investment in research, technology, and infrastructure.

Critics of this menu argue that the East Asian success stories had specific historical conditions (U.S. security guarantees during the Cold War, access to U.S. markets, substantial foreign aid initially) that aren't easily replicable. Whether the model is broadly applicable or specific to its time and place is contested.

**High-income countries** focus on:
- Managing the business cycle through monetary and fiscal policy.
- Maintaining productive capacity through R&D, education, infrastructure investment.
- Adapting to demographic change (aging, immigration).
- Managing financial-system stability.
- Adjusting to global shifts (trade competition, technology disruption).
- Supporting workers displaced by structural change.

The policy challenges are quite different at this stage. Growth rates are necessarily lower (1–3 percent rather than 5–10 percent). Inflation control matters more. Distributional questions become more salient.

## Unemployment, inflation, and trade across countries

Each major macroeconomic indicator looks different in different country contexts.

**Unemployment.** High-income countries typically have official unemployment rates of 3–10 percent and well-developed labor markets. Many low-income countries have low official unemployment but high *underemployment* — workers in subsistence agriculture or informal services with very low productivity. Sub-Saharan African countries often have official unemployment below 5 percent but per-capita output far below high-income levels because so much labor is in unproductive uses.

**Inflation.** High-income countries with credible central banks typically see inflation in 0–5 percent range. Some emerging markets have struggled with chronic high inflation (Argentina, Turkey, Venezuela). Some hyperinflations have happened in low-income or politically unstable contexts (Zimbabwe, Venezuela, Yemen). The institutional capacity to maintain monetary stability is one of the dividers between high-income and lower-income macroeconomic experiences.

**Trade balances.** Some high-income economies run persistent surpluses (Germany, Japan); others run persistent deficits (U.S., U.K.). Some low-income countries run deficits funded by aid or remittances; others run surpluses through commodity exports. The trade balance reflects the savings-investment gap (Chapter 23) modified by policy choices and structural conditions.

## Market-oriented reforms

Many countries have implemented market-oriented reforms over the past four decades — privatization, deregulation, trade liberalization, fiscal discipline. The results have been mixed.

**Successes.** China since 1980 (gradual market liberalization plus state-led development). India since 1991 (reform package that ended the License Raj). Eastern European countries since 1990 (transition from central planning to market economies). Many Latin American countries had successful disinflation in the 1990s through reform packages.

**Failures and partial failures.** Russia in the 1990s (rapid transition created enormous corruption and inequality). Some Latin American reforms produced growth but also social instability. Some African reform packages didn't produce the expected growth.

The lesson seems to be that reforms work when:
- They build on existing institutional capacity (or build it).
- They sequence appropriately (e.g., institutions before deregulation).
- They include social safety nets to manage the transition.
- They avoid creating new corruption opportunities.

Reforms fail when they ignore institutional preconditions, sequence poorly, or fail to address the political economy of the transition. There's no universal market-reform package; the right approach depends heavily on the country's specific situation.

## Synthesis

Pull back. Macroeconomic policy is not one thing applied uniformly across countries. The challenges, priorities, and feasible interventions all differ by income level, institutional capacity, and historical context.

High-income countries focus on cycle management, inflation control, demographic adaptation, and supporting workers through structural change. Middle-income countries face the question of how to move up the value chain without stalling. Low-income countries struggle with the institutional foundations needed to grow sustainably at all.

The successful catch-up cases — Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, China for several decades, Vietnam, parts of Eastern Europe — offer lessons but no formula. Each combined sustained investment in human and physical capital with selective integration into global markets. Each had specific institutional and historical conditions that supported its policy choices. The countries that have struggled — many of Sub-Saharan Africa, parts of Latin America, much of South Asia for long periods — have generally failed at one or more of these dimensions.

The conclusion: macro policy works best when it accounts for context. The U.S. Federal Reserve and the Bank of Korea face very different challenges. The Indian Ministry of Finance faces different challenges than the German Ministry of Finance. Universal recommendations ("balance the budget," "control inflation," "open to trade") are starting points; the specific policy mix depends on the country's situation.

The next two chapters close the book by returning to international trade in detail — how it works, what it does for the countries involved, and the political pressures that periodically challenge it.

## LLM Exercises

**Exercise 1 — Classify and compare countries.** Pick three countries from different income tiers and ask an LLM to compare them on (a) GDP per capita, (b) unemployment rate, (c) inflation rate, (d) trade balance. Explain why each indicator looks the way it does given the country's situation.

**Exercise 2 — Diagnose a development success.** Pick a country that has successfully transitioned from low-income to middle- or high-income status (Korea, Singapore, Taiwan, Vietnam, Botswana). Ask an LLM to identify the policies and conditions that enabled the transition. Push for specifics rather than generic claims.

**Exercise 3 — Explain a development failure.** Pick a country that has stagnated or regressed economically over decades (Argentina, Venezuela, Zimbabwe, parts of Sub-Saharan Africa). Ask an LLM to diagnose the structural, institutional, and policy factors involved. Where do reasonable economists disagree about the causes?

**Exercise 4 — Apply the middle-income trap.** Tell an LLM about a hypothetical country at $8,000 GDP per capita with growth slowing from 7% to 3% per year. Ask: what policies could move it back to high growth? What's the empirical evidence on which approaches have worked?

**Exercise 5 — Evaluate a market-reform package.** Tell an LLM about a hypothetical country considering reform: privatize state-owned enterprises, liberalize trade, deregulate financial services, reduce fiscal deficit. Ask: in what order should these be done? What sequence produces the best outcome? Push the LLM to engage with the empirical record on reform sequencing.

**What would change my mind.** The case that the East Asian growth experience offers a replicable development model rests on assumptions about whether the historical conditions (Cold War security guarantees, U.S. market access) can be reproduced. If new evidence showed that the East Asian success was largely contingent on those conditions and not on the specific policies, the universal applicability of the model would weaken.

**Still puzzling.** I do not have a clean account of why some countries with weak institutional capacity have produced rapid growth (China for decades) while others with stronger institutional foundations have stagnated. The relationship between institutional quality and growth is correlational and contextual, not deterministic. The mechanism that allowed China to grow despite governance challenges that would have crippled smaller economies is not fully understood.

## LLM Exercise — Chapter 32: Macroeconomic Policy Around the World (Policy Brief Project)

**Project:** Policy Brief.
**What you're building this chapter:** the comparative-policy analysis — how do other countries handle the same problem your policy addresses, and what does the cross-country evidence say?
**Tool:** **Claude Project** "Policy Brief" — appends a section.

---

**The Prompt:**

```
Chapter 32 of my Policy Brief project. Prior sections in this Claude
Project. Chapter 32 taught: income classifications (high-income,
upper-middle-income, lower-middle-income, low-income; per-capita
GDP and PPP-adjusted measures); what distinguishes country
contexts (institutions, geography, demographics, history of
colonialism, integration with global trade); the growth-policy
menu and what's worked across countries; cross-country variation
in unemployment, inflation, and trade; market-oriented reforms
and their mixed record.

Write the brief's "Comparative Policy Analysis" section in 300–500
words.

1. **Three country comparisons.** Pick three countries that have
   implemented your policy (or close variants) and report what
   happened. Be specific: country, policy, year of implementation,
   measured effects, what was different about the context. Cite
   sources where possible (use [verify] tags for any specific
   claims).

   Example pairings:
   - Carbon tax: Sweden (1991), British Columbia (2008), France
     (2014, gilets jaunes backlash).
   - Minimum wage: UK (1999 introduction), Germany (2015
     introduction), Seattle (2014 city-level).
   - Antitrust: EU vs. Google (multiple cases), South Korea vs.
     Apple's payment system, Australia vs. Facebook on news.

2. **What the cross-country evidence says.** Consolidate: what
   does the evidence across the three cases suggest about the
   policy's likely effects in the US? Where does the evidence
   converge (most cases show X)? Where does it diverge (different
   contexts produce different results)?

3. **The transferability question.** Which features of the foreign
   experience transfer to the US case, and which don't? Different
   institutional contexts, different starting conditions, different
   complementary policies all matter.

End with one sentence on whether the cross-country evidence
strengthens or weakens the brief's conclusion. Honest cross-
country evidence often complicates rather than simplifies the
domestic policy debate; the brief should not paper over this.
```

---

**What this produces:** A 300–500 word section grounded in real cross-country evidence. The three-country comparison adds empirical weight to the brief that purely theoretical analysis can't.

**How to adapt this prompt:**

- *For ChatGPT / Gemini:* Works as written.
