# Chapter 4 — Labor and Financial Markets

*The same diagram applied to two markets that don't look like markets — the one for hours, and the one for dollars across time.*

In 2020, the U.S. Census Bureau ran the numbers and reported that 22 percent of the country's population was at least 60 years old. More than 74 million people. The single largest cohort, the baby boomers — born between 1946 and 1964 — was hitting retirement and the medical conditions that come with it: heart disease, arthritis, Alzheimer's, the everyday wear of long lives. The Affordable Care Act, in the years just before, had pulled millions more people into the formal healthcare system.

The result, in the labor market for nurses, was not a slow drift. It was a surge. Hospitals couldn't hire fast enough. Wages for registered nurses rose. Nursing schools couldn't expand fast enough to meet enrollment. Travel-nurse agencies, which existed before the surge but at modest scale, became enormous and began paying nurses two or three times the staff salary to cover short-term contracts.

What you are looking at, in that surge, is the demand-and-supply model from Chapter 3 — the same two curves, the same equilibrium, the same shift logic — applied to a market that most people don't think of as a market at all: the market for hours of skilled human work.

This chapter does that move. We take the demand-and-supply machinery and apply it to two specific markets where it does an unusual amount of work. The first is the **labor market** — where workers are sellers of their time, and firms are buyers. The second is the **financial-capital market** — where savers are sellers of money-now and borrowers are buyers, and the price is the **interest rate**. By the end you should see why the same diagram explains why nursing wages rose in 2020, why student loan interest rates are what they are, and why a usury law operates the same way as a rent-control law.

**Learning objectives.** By the end you should be able to: (1) draw a labor-market demand-and-supply diagram and explain what shifts each curve; (2) predict how aging demographics or new automation technology will change wages and employment in a specific occupation; (3) analyze the minimum wage as a price floor, including its predicted and observed effects; (4) draw a financial-capital market with the interest rate on the price axis and identify who's on each side; (5) analyze a usury law as a price ceiling and predict its effects.

**Prerequisites.** Chapter 3 (demand and supply, equilibrium, shifts, price controls). Comfort with reading a two-curve diagram.

## Labor as a market

Start by being precise about what's traded. In a labor market, what's bought and sold is **hours of work** — specifically, hours of a particular kind of work, by people with a particular set of skills, in a particular geographic area. The "market for nurses in the Minneapolis-St. Paul metro area in 2020" is a labor market. The "market for software engineers in the San Francisco Bay Area in 2025" is another. They are not the same market because the workers in one cannot easily substitute into the other, and the buyers in one (hospitals) are not the buyers in the other (tech firms).

The price in a labor market is the **wage** — dollars per hour, or its annual equivalent.

The supply curve in a labor market is the relationship between the wage and the number of people willing to do that work. Higher wage, more people willing — the same upward slope as any supply curve. A nurse who would not work for $40 an hour might work for $60. A software engineer who would not move to San Francisco for a $120,000 salary might move for $250,000. The general shape holds.

The demand curve is the relationship between the wage and the number of workers firms want to hire. Higher wage, fewer hires — the same downward slope. A hospital that would hire 200 RNs at $40/hour might hire only 150 at $60. The reason is on the firm's side: hiring decisions are made by comparing the value the worker produces (the *marginal revenue product*, which we'll formalize in Chapter 14) to the wage. As the wage rises, the worker's contribution has to be larger to justify the cost. Some marginal positions get cut.

The two curves cross at an equilibrium wage and equilibrium employment level. At the equilibrium, every employer who wants a nurse at the going wage can hire one, and every nurse who wants a job at the going wage can find one. *Frictional* unemployment — people moving between jobs — exists, but the *systematic* match between the number of workers wanting jobs and the number of jobs available comes out at the crossing.

Now: what shifts the curves?

Demand for labor (the firm side) shifts with the demand for the firm's *output*. Hospitals want more nurses when more patients show up. Tech firms want more engineers when there's a software boom. This is what the 2020 nurse surge actually was: aging demographics + ACA expansion shifted the demand for healthcare itself, which shifted the demand for nurses to the right. Higher equilibrium wage. Higher equilibrium employment.

Demand for labor also shifts with technology. New technologies that make a worker more productive shift demand for that worker's labor right. New technologies that *replace* the worker shift demand left. The relationship is messier than the textbook treatment suggests, because most technologies do both at once — they replace some tasks and amplify others. ATMs were predicted to wipe out bank tellers in the 1980s; the number of bank tellers actually rose for a while because ATMs made each bank branch cheaper to run, so banks opened more branches. Eventually, online banking did what ATMs were predicted to do. The lag was decades.

Supply of labor shifts with population, with the cost of training, with cultural and policy shifts in who works. The number of women in the U.S. paid labor force roughly doubled between 1950 and 2000 — supply curve for many occupations shifted dramatically right over that span, holding wages lower than they would otherwise have been even as demand grew. Immigration shifts supply right. Aging out of the labor force shifts supply left.

### The minimum wage as price floor

The model from Chapter 3 makes a sharp prediction about the minimum wage. A minimum wage that sits above the equilibrium wage in a competitive labor market creates a surplus of labor — more people want to work at that wage than firms want to hire. The textbook name for that surplus is **unemployment**.

The empirical record is messier. Modest minimum-wage increases — say, a one- or two-dollar bump in a metro area — often produce no measurable effect on employment, or a small effect. Larger or more sudden increases can produce visible employment effects. Why the gap between the model's clean prediction and the messy data?

A few reasons. First, many local labor markets are not competitive in the textbook sense. They have a small number of large employers (a hospital network in a small town, a meat-packing plant in a rural region), and those employers exercise some monopsony power — they can set wages somewhat below what a competitive market would produce. A minimum wage in such a market can *increase* employment, by raising the wage closer to where it would have been if the employer didn't have wage-setting power. Second, real labor markets have search costs and frictions, so workers and firms don't always reach equilibrium quickly. Third, firms can absorb modest wage increases by raising prices a little, accepting slightly lower margins, or making small productivity adjustments — the demand curve for labor in the short run is not perfectly elastic.

The model is right about the *direction* of the effect — a sufficiently high minimum wage in a competitive market will reduce employment. The model is right *less robustly* than the rent-control case because the assumptions of the simple labor-market diagram are on shakier ground. We will return to this in Chapter 14, where we look at labor markets more carefully.

## Money across time

Now the second market: financial capital.

This one feels less like a market because the thing being traded is abstract. What changes hands is a present amount of money, in exchange for a promise of a future amount. A saver gives the bank $1,000 today; the bank gives the saver back $1,030 a year from now. A borrower takes $1,000 today; pays back $1,080 a year from now. The price of *money-now* in terms of *money-later* is captured by a single number — the **interest rate**.

The interest rate is the price in this market. Plot it on the vertical axis. On the horizontal axis: the quantity of financial capital — dollars supplied by savers, dollars demanded by borrowers, in some unit of time.

The supply curve is upward-sloping. The higher the interest rate, the more people are willing to save (and the less they spend now). At 1 percent, you might keep your $1,000 in a checking account; at 5 percent, you'd put it in a CD; at 10 percent, you might forgo a vacation to invest more.

The demand curve is downward-sloping. The higher the interest rate, the fewer borrowers find their projects worth the cost. A business that would borrow $100,000 to expand at 4 percent might decline at 9 percent. Mortgage applications fall when rates rise. Car loan demand falls when rates rise.

The two curves cross at an equilibrium interest rate and equilibrium quantity of capital flowing from savers to borrowers. This is the picture of a financial market in its simplest form.

What shifts the curves?

Supply (savings) shifts with income — when households earn more, they typically save more. With expectations — if people expect a recession, they save more. With demographics — older populations approaching retirement save heavily. With government tax policy — when retirement savings get tax preference, supply shifts right. With foreign capital flows — capital from abroad seeking U.S. returns shifts the U.S. supply curve right.

Demand (borrowing) shifts with the perceived returns to investment — when business opportunities look good, firms borrow more. With consumer expectations — if households are confident, they borrow to buy houses and cars. With government borrowing — when the federal government runs large deficits, it competes with private borrowers for the available pool of capital, shifting demand right. With foreign borrowing — when U.S. firms borrow abroad, the domestic demand curve shifts left.

A worked example. In the early 2020s, the Federal Reserve raised short-term interest rates aggressively to fight inflation. The mortgage rate, which is closely tied to longer-term Treasury yields, rose from roughly 3 percent in 2021 to roughly 7 percent in 2023. What does the model predict for housing demand?

Use the four-step. Original equilibrium: low rate, large quantity of mortgage credit. The change: short-term rate rose, pulling longer-term rates with it; mortgage rates climbed. From the borrower's side, the cost of borrowing rose. This is a movement *along* the demand curve — fewer borrowers want mortgages at the higher rate. Quantity of new mortgages drops. New home sales fell sharply through 2022 and 2023.

The model handled this case neatly. It also missed something: existing homeowners, locked into 3 percent mortgages, didn't want to sell because they'd have to take out a 7 percent mortgage on their next house. The supply of *existing* homes for sale collapsed. The demand-and-supply model for *new* mortgages predicted what we saw. The fuller picture — that mortgage rates also affect housing supply through this lock-in mechanism — required more than the simple model contains.

### Usury laws as price ceilings

Usury laws are old. They cap the interest rate a lender can legally charge — historically aimed at predatory lenders charging triple-digit annual rates. Some states still cap consumer interest at 10, 18, or 36 percent. Federally, certain consumer credit categories have caps.

A usury cap that sits below the market interest rate is a price ceiling. Same diagram as Chapter 3's rent control. The model predicts a shortage: at the capped rate, more borrowers want loans than lenders are willing to make. Some borrowers — usually the riskiest, the ones most likely to default — get rationed out. They either get no loan, or they get pushed into the gray market (payday lenders charging effective triple-digit rates anyway, structured around the cap).

The same trade-off the rent-control diagram showed up in housing shows up here: redistribution toward the borrowers who still get loans, away from lenders, with deadweight loss in the form of loans not made and projects not financed. Whether the trade-off is worth making — whether predatory lending creates harms large enough to justify the rationing — is a question outside the model. The model is doing what it does well: making the structural consequences visible.

## Synthesis

Pull back. The model from Chapter 3 — two curves, equilibrium, shifts, surplus, price controls — extends to markets where the thing traded is not a physical good. In labor markets, the price is the wage, the seller is the worker, the buyer is the firm, and the curves shift with output demand on the firm side and with population, training, and institutions on the worker side. In financial-capital markets, the price is the interest rate, the seller is the saver, the buyer is the borrower, and the curves shift with income, expectations, demographics, government deficits, and foreign capital flows. The same logic applies in both. The same predictions hold, with the same caveats about competitive assumptions.

A useful way to read the chapter: every time you see a price tag in the world — a salary, a mortgage rate, a tomato sticker, an Uber surge multiplier — you are looking at the equilibrium of two curves you cannot see directly. The price contains a lot of information about both. Learning to read it is one of the durable payoffs of an economics education.

A more sober read: the simple model is most reliable when the underlying market resembles its assumptions — many buyers, many sellers, no one with much pricing power, low information asymmetry, low friction. Some markets look like this (commodities, public stocks). Many do not (large-employer towns, healthcare markets with insurance intermediaries, executive compensation markets, the global market for elite-school degrees). When the market doesn't look like the assumption, the simple diagram still gestures in the right direction more often than not, but the magnitude of the prediction — and sometimes the sign — gets unreliable. Knowing the difference is the skill.

## LLM Exercises

**Exercise 1 — Predict the labor market.** Pick an occupation that's likely to face significant change in the next ten years (radiologists, truck drivers, registered nurses, software engineers, K-12 teachers). Ask an LLM to predict whether equilibrium wages and employment will rise or fall, and to identify which curve shifts and why. Then push: ask it to identify the *strongest counterargument* to its own prediction. Evaluate whether the LLM is doing genuine reasoning or restating standard talking points.

**Exercise 2 — Minimum wage with two views.** Tell an LLM you want to think carefully about minimum-wage effects. Ask it to write two short analyses: one assuming a perfectly competitive labor market, and one assuming the local labor market has significant monopsony power (one or two large employers). Compare the predictions. Use this exercise to internalize that the model's predictions depend on which assumptions are doing work.

**Exercise 3 — Interest rate as a price.** Give an LLM this task: explain to a high-school student what an interest rate *is*, in plain English, by analogy to the price of any other thing. Evaluate the explanation. Does the LLM treat the interest rate as the price of money-across-time? Does it explain why both savers and borrowers respond to it? If the analogy is weak or the explanation circular, push it to revise.

**Exercise 4 — Crowding out.** Tell an LLM: "When the federal government runs a large deficit, it borrows from the same pool of capital that private businesses borrow from." Ask it to predict what happens to the interest rate and to private investment using the demand-supply-of-capital diagram, and to use the four-step method. Then ask: under what conditions does this *crowding out* effect not happen? (Hint: foreign capital inflows, slack in the economy.) Evaluate whether the LLM names the conditions clearly.

**Exercise 5 — Usury cap stress test.** Describe to an LLM a hypothetical state that has just imposed a 15 percent annual interest rate cap on payday loans. Ask it to predict three consequences using the demand-supply diagram. Then ask: which borrowers benefit, and which borrowers are *worse off* than before the cap? A good answer notices that the borrowers who used to get loans at 200 percent rates (a real number for some payday loans) and now get no loans at all are not obviously better off, even though the cap was supposed to help them. Evaluate whether the LLM gets to that uncomfortable conclusion.

## What comes next

Chapter 5 — Elasticity asks how *responsive* demand and supply are to price changes — a refinement that turns out to matter for tax policy, surge pricing, exchange-rate effects, and the case for or against many price controls. The labor-market and capital-market examples we built here will return in Chapter 14 (Labor Markets and Income), Chapter 17 (Financial Markets), and the macroeconomic chapters from 19 onward, where the same diagrams scale up to the whole economy.

**What would change my mind.** The labor-market and capital-market predictions in this chapter rest on the assumption that workers and firms, savers and borrowers, can move reasonably freely on the curves. If labor markets are dominated by a few large employers, or capital markets are dominated by a few large lenders, the simple model fails by *direction* and not just by magnitude. We get to monopsony in Ch 14 and to financial-market structure in Ch 17.

**Still puzzling.** I do not have a clean way to explain why long-term interest rates and short-term interest rates sometimes move together and sometimes don't. The textbook story is that long rates equal expected short rates plus a risk premium, but the empirical evidence on this is mixed and the term-structure literature is one of the messier corners of economics. I'll point at the puzzle in the macro chapters without claiming to solve it.

## LLM Exercise — Chapter 4: Labor and Financial Markets (Policy Brief Project)

**Project:** Policy Brief.
**What you're building this chapter:** how your policy ripples into the labor market and the financial market, even if it's "not really about" either.
**Tool:** **Claude Project** "Policy Brief" — appends a section.

---

**The Prompt:**

```
Chapter 4 of my Policy Brief project. Prior sections in this Claude
Project. Chapter 4 taught: labor as a market with demand from
employers (derived from product demand) and supply from workers;
the equilibrium wage as the price; financial markets as the market
for money over time, with the interest rate as the price; how
shifts in either market ripple through the rest of the economy.

Even if your policy isn't directly about labor or finance, it has
labor-market and financial-market consequences. Trace them.

Write the brief's "Labor and Financial Market Effects" section in
300–500 words.

1. **The labor market effect.** Identify the labor markets the
   policy affects. For each: who's hurt (jobs lost, wages
   suppressed, hours reduced), who's helped (jobs gained, wages
   raised), and the magnitude — your best estimate, with
   uncertainty named. Be specific about which workers (low-wage,
   high-skill, particular industries, particular geographies).

2. **The financial market effect.** Identify the financial markets
   the policy affects. Bond yields? Stock prices in the affected
   industry? Corporate borrowing costs? Capital flows? For each:
   the direction of the effect and the mechanism.

3. **The general-equilibrium feedback.** Most policy analyses stop
   at the first-order effect. The chapter's anchor claim: markets
   connect, so first-order labor effects produce second-order
   spending effects, which feed back into demand for the policy's
   primary market. Trace one such feedback loop for your policy.

End with one sentence on the labor or financial effect that's most
likely to surprise the policy's advocates. The point isn't to
oppose the policy — it's to make the brief honest about effects
the advocates haven't named.
```
