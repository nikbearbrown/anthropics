# Chapter 19 — The Macroeconomic Perspective

*Zooming out from individual markets to the whole economy — what we measure, why it matters, and what the numbers don't capture.*

In October 1929, the U.S. stock market collapsed. Over the next four years, the U.S. economy contracted by roughly 30 percent in real terms. Industrial production fell by half. Unemployment rose from about 3 percent to about 25 percent. Bank failures wiped out the savings of millions of households. By 1933, more than 9,000 banks had closed, taking $7 billion of deposits with them.

This was the Great Depression, the deepest macroeconomic crisis of the twentieth century. It killed nobody directly, but it reshaped American politics for two generations, gave rise to the modern welfare state, and produced the first systematic government efforts to measure and manage the macroeconomy.

The Depression also produced the analytical apparatus this chapter introduces. *Gross Domestic Product* (GDP) — the most-used economic statistic in the world — was developed in the 1930s by Simon Kuznets at the U.S. Department of Commerce, partly because nobody had any way to track how big the contraction was while it was happening. The Bureau of Labor Statistics' systematic measurement of unemployment dates from this era. The standard concepts of "recession" and "expansion" were formalized by the National Bureau of Economic Research, also in the 1930s and 1940s.

Until you can measure something, you can't track it; until you can track it, you can't manage it. This chapter is about what macro economists measure and why. By the end you should be able to read a GDP report, distinguish nominal from real changes, identify what the GDP number captures and what it leaves out, and recognize where the U.S. economy sits in the business cycle.

**Learning objectives.** By the end you should be able to: (1) define GDP and identify its four expenditure components (C + I + G + NX); (2) calculate real GDP from nominal GDP and a price index; (3) define the GDP deflator and explain its role; (4) describe the business cycle and identify peaks, troughs, recessions, and expansions; (5) compute and compare GDP per capita across countries; (6) explain at least three things GDP does not measure that affect economic welfare.

**Prerequisites.** Chapter 1 (markets, the circular flow). Chapter 18 (public spending and economic policy).

## Gross Domestic Product — the headline number

**Gross Domestic Product (GDP)** is the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a given year. The four words "all," "final," "produced," and "within" each carry weight.

*All*: every good and every service that gets transacted, weighted by its market value.

*Final*: only sales to the ultimate consumer count; intermediate goods (the steel that goes into a car, the flour that goes into bread) are not counted separately to avoid double-counting.

*Produced*: only newly-produced goods this year. Sales of used houses, used cars, or financial assets are not counted; they're transfers of existing wealth, not new production.

*Within a country*: GDP measures production happening on the country's soil, regardless of who owns the firms. A Toyota factory in Tennessee is part of U.S. GDP. A U.S.-owned factory in Mexico is part of Mexican GDP, not U.S. GDP. (The related measure that includes income earned abroad by U.S. residents is *Gross National Product* (GNP), but most macro analysis now uses GDP.)

GDP can be measured three equivalent ways:

**Expenditure approach.** Add up everything bought in the economy. Four categories:
- **Consumption (C)** — household spending on goods and services. Roughly 70 percent of U.S. GDP.
- **Investment (I)** — business purchases of capital (machines, buildings) plus residential construction plus changes in inventories. Roughly 17 percent of U.S. GDP.
- **Government purchases (G)** — federal, state, and local government spending on goods and services (but *not* transfer payments like Social Security). Roughly 17 percent of U.S. GDP.
- **Net exports (NX)** — exports minus imports. Negative for the U.S. (we import more than we export). Roughly −4 percent of U.S. GDP.

**GDP = C + I + G + NX**.

**Income approach.** Add up everything earned in the economy. Wages and salaries to workers, profits to firms, rents to property owners, interest to lenders, taxes to government. By accounting identity, the income approach gives the same total as the expenditure approach. Every dollar spent is somebody else's dollar earned.

**Production approach.** Add up the value-added by every firm in the economy (the firm's revenue minus the cost of the inputs it bought from other firms). Also gives the same total, by construction.

The three approaches give the same total, because they're three ways of looking at the same flow. In practice, the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) primarily uses the expenditure approach for its quarterly GDP reports.

## Nominal vs. real — adjusting for inflation

The GDP number you'll see in a news headline is one of two things — *nominal GDP* (in current dollars) or *real GDP* (adjusted for inflation). The distinction is crucial.

**Nominal GDP** measures production in current-year prices. If U.S. nominal GDP rises from $25 trillion to $26 trillion, it could mean:
- The country produced 4 percent more stuff at the same prices (real growth of 4 percent),
- The country produced the same stuff at 4 percent higher prices (real growth of 0 percent), or
- Some combination.

To know which, you need to strip out the price changes.

**Real GDP** measures production in constant dollars — the prices of some chosen base year. To compute real GDP from nominal GDP, you divide by a price index (the **GDP deflator**) that captures the average level of prices in the economy.

The GDP deflator in the base year is set to 100. In a year when prices have risen 10 percent above the base, the deflator is 110. To get real GDP in base-year dollars: nominal GDP × (100 / deflator). A nominal GDP of $26T with a deflator of 110 gives real GDP of $23.6T.

The reported quarterly U.S. GDP growth rate is *real* — it strips out price changes. A quarter of "0.6 percent real GDP growth" annualized at 2.4 percent is the headline number tied to the macroeconomy's actual production.

The deflator captures inflation in the prices of all goods produced domestically. A related measure — the **consumer price index (CPI)** — captures inflation in the prices of goods consumers buy. The two move together but not identically; we'll see CPI in detail in Chapter 22.

## The business cycle

Real GDP doesn't grow smoothly. It moves in cycles — periods of expansion punctuated by contractions, then back to expansion.

The standard vocabulary:

- **Peak** — the highest point of GDP before a contraction begins.
- **Recession** — a contraction in real GDP. The conventional definition: two consecutive quarters of negative growth. The official U.S. designation is made by the National Bureau of Economic Research using a broader basket of indicators.
- **Trough** — the bottom of the recession; the point from which expansion begins.
- **Expansion** — the period of growing real GDP from trough to peak.

The U.S. economy has experienced about 11 recessions since World War II. Most have been relatively short — typically 6 to 18 months — followed by expansions of several years. The 1990s expansion (March 1991 to March 2001) was the longest peacetime expansion in U.S. history at the time, exceeded only by the 2009–2020 expansion that ended with COVID.

Recessions vary enormously in depth. The 2008–2009 Great Recession was the deepest since the Great Depression (real GDP fell about 4 percent from peak to trough). The COVID recession of early 2020 was very short (two quarters) but very sharp (real GDP fell about 10 percent). Most others are smaller — a 1–2 percent real GDP decline followed by recovery.

Why do recessions happen? This is the core question of macroeconomics, and we'll spend the next ten chapters working on it. The very short answer involves *aggregate demand* shocks (consumers and firms collectively pulling back on spending), *aggregate supply* shocks (oil price spikes, pandemics, supply chain disruptions), and the financial-system feedback loops we touched on in Chapter 17. The longer answer is the subject of macroeconomic theory.

## GDP per capita and standards of living

For comparing across countries or across time, *total* GDP can be misleading because countries differ enormously in population. China's GDP is the world's second-largest in absolute terms; its GDP per capita is about $13,000, well below most of the developed world.

**GDP per capita** is GDP divided by population. It's the most common single measure of a country's average standard of living.

Approximate GDP per capita figures (in U.S. dollars, mid-2020s):
- Switzerland: ~$95,000
- United States: ~$80,000
- Germany: ~$55,000
- Japan: ~$35,000
- China: ~$13,000
- India: ~$2,500
- Nigeria: ~$2,000

These numbers tell you something real about average living standards. They also miss enormous amounts.

Comparing across countries also requires adjusting for differences in what a dollar can buy. A dollar in India buys substantially more than a dollar in the U.S. because Indian prices for non-traded goods (housing, services, food) are much lower. Adjusting for this gives **purchasing power parity (PPP)** GDP, which is what most cross-country comparisons in international agencies use. India's PPP GDP per capita is roughly three times its market-exchange-rate GDP per capita.

## What GDP doesn't measure

GDP is a measure of market production. Many things contribute to wellbeing without being market production. GDP misses several:

**Non-market production.** Household production (cooking, cleaning, childcare done by family members) doesn't show up in GDP. If you cook your own dinner, no GDP. If you go to a restaurant, GDP. If you pay a babysitter, GDP. If you watch your own kids, no GDP. The category is enormous — estimates suggest household production is roughly 20–30 percent the size of measured GDP.

**Distribution.** GDP per capita is an average. It says nothing about how income is distributed. A country with extreme inequality and a country with broad-based prosperity could have the same GDP per capita and very different welfare distributions.

**Quality and innovation.** A 2024 smartphone produces vastly more value than a 1990 smartphone but might count as similar GDP if priced similarly. Quality improvements are partially captured (through hedonic adjustments to price indexes) but imperfectly.

**Externalities and depreciation.** Production that pollutes generates measured GDP. The damage from the pollution doesn't subtract from GDP. Resource extraction (timber, oil, fish) generates GDP but the depletion of the natural-capital stock isn't subtracted. *Net* domestic product would subtract depreciation of physical capital; *green* GDP measures attempt to subtract environmental depreciation; neither has fully replaced GDP in standard reporting.

**Leisure and life satisfaction.** A country where people work 50 hours a week has more GDP than one where people work 35, ceteris paribus. The 35-hour society may have higher welfare. GDP misses this.

**The sustainability of growth.** A country drilling its oil reserves at unsustainable rates has high current GDP and a future shortfall. A country investing in education and infrastructure has lower current GDP and more future productivity. GDP captures only the present.

These limitations don't make GDP useless. They make it a partial measure that should be supplemented by other indicators (life expectancy, educational attainment, environmental quality, time use, distributional measures). Aggregate measures like the United Nations' Human Development Index combine GDP per capita with health and education indicators; subjective wellbeing surveys add another dimension. The single number "GDP" is a useful summary; it's not the whole story.

## Synthesis

Pull back. The macroeconomic perspective starts with one big question: how big is the economy, and how is it changing?

GDP is the standard answer. It's an annual flow of market production. It can be measured by what's spent (C + I + G + NX), what's earned (wages + profits + rents + interest + taxes), or what's produced (sum of value-added across firms). The three give the same total because they're three views of the same circular flow.

To compare across time, distinguish nominal from real GDP. To compare across countries, use GDP per capita and adjust for purchasing-power parity.

The business cycle — the regular fluctuation of GDP around its long-run trend — is the central macroeconomic phenomenon, and the next several chapters will be about understanding why recessions happen and what (if anything) can be done about them.

GDP misses a great deal. It misses non-market production, distribution, externalities, leisure, and sustainability. The standard recommendation is to use GDP alongside other indicators, not as a replacement for them.

The Great Depression is what gave us GDP. Before the 1930s, neither the federal government nor private economists had any systematic measure of how the U.S. economy was doing. Once Kuznets and his colleagues at the BEA had built the apparatus, it became possible to track recessions in something close to real time and to evaluate whether interventions were working. The next chapter takes the long view — economic growth over decades and centuries, the most important macroeconomic phenomenon there is.

## LLM Exercises

**Exercise 1 — Decompose a country's GDP.** Pick a country — the U.S., Germany, India, your home country. Ask an LLM to find or estimate the breakdown of GDP into C, I, G, and NX shares. Push for current data. Then ask: how does this composition compare to other countries, and what does it suggest about the country's economic structure?

**Exercise 2 — Compute real from nominal.** Tell an LLM: "Country A has nominal GDP of $1 trillion in 2024 and $1.05 trillion in 2025. The GDP deflator was 100 in 2024 and 103 in 2025." Ask it to compute real GDP for both years and the real growth rate. Verify by hand.

**Exercise 3 — Identify a recession in real time.** Find recent economic news (real GDP changes, employment changes, financial-market moves). Ask an LLM whether the data described constitute (or are heading toward) a recession by the standard NBER criteria. Push for which specific indicators it's using.

**Exercise 4 — Critique GDP as a welfare measure.** Pick a specific case where GDP would be misleading as a welfare metric — a country recovering from a hurricane (rebuilding raises GDP), a country that destroys its forests for export, a country where most economic activity is in the informal sector. Ask an LLM to walk through what GDP captures and what it misses. Evaluate the LLM's specificity.

**Exercise 5 — PPP comparison.** Tell an LLM the GDP per capita of two countries at market exchange rates and ask it to estimate the PPP-adjusted comparison. For example: India's market-exchange GDP per capita is about $2,500; the U.S. is about $80,000. The PPP-adjusted ratio is closer to 7 times rather than 32 times. Push the LLM to explain *why* the adjustment is so large for India relative to the U.S.

Chapter 20 — Economic Growth zooms out to the long-run question: why have some countries grown rich while others have not, and what determines a country's growth rate over decades and centuries? **What would change my mind.** The case that GDP is a useful (if imperfect) summary of economic performance depends on the assumption that market production correlates closely enough with welfare. In some contexts (war reconstruction, disaster recovery, environmental degradation), that correlation breaks. If those contexts came to dominate the global economy, GDP would need to be substantially supplemented or replaced.

**Still puzzling.** I do not have a clean explanation for why subjective measures of life satisfaction in the U.S. have stagnated or declined since the 1990s, even as real GDP per capita has continued to rise. The disconnect — the "Easterlin paradox" in its modern form — has multiple candidate explanations (rising inequality, social changes, status effects, measurement issues), none of which is fully satisfying.
