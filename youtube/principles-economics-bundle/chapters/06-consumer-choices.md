# Chapter 6 — Consumer Choices

*Where the demand curve actually comes from — and where the rational consumer model breaks.*

In the middle of the 2008–2009 financial crisis, when consumer spending across the United States cratered and family budgets tightened in ways economists were still trying to track in real time, one category of spending went *up*.

Not necessities. Not health care. Not housing.

Lipstick.

The "lipstick effect" had been observed in earlier downturns, but the 2008 data made it specific: an 18 percent uptick in U.S. cosmetics spending during a year when overall discretionary spending fell sharply. Estée Lauder's Leonard Lauder had been talking about the pattern since the 2001 recession. Why did people whose disposable income was shrinking buy *more* of a small luxury?

The standard demand curve from Chapter 3 — quantity goes down when income goes down — would not have predicted this. The standard curve treats consumers as a black box that responds to prices in a downward-sloping way. To explain the lipstick effect, you have to open the box. You have to ask *how* people decide what to buy.

This chapter opens the box. We start with the textbook model — utility, marginal utility, the rule a rational consumer is supposed to follow — and use it to derive the demand curve from first principles. We then apply the same machinery to choices over time (saving, borrowing, investing in your own skills). Then, because the model is too clean to be the whole truth, we hand-off to behavioral economics — the systematic ways real consumers depart from the textbook prediction, and what those departures teach us.

By the end you should see why the demand curve slopes the way it does, why people save less than the model predicts, and why a 5 percent off coupon often outperforms a 5 percent price drop.

**Learning objectives.** By the end you should be able to: (1) define utility and explain why economists use it as a model rather than a measurement; (2) apply the equimarginal rule and find a utility-maximizing choice on a budget constraint; (3) decompose a price change into its substitution effect and income effect; (4) draw an intertemporal budget constraint and explain how the interest rate affects saving; (5) name three behavioral patterns that violate the standard model — loss aversion, present bias, framing effects — and explain why each matters.

**Prerequisites.** Chapter 2 (budget constraint, opportunity cost, marginal). Chapter 3 (demand curve). Chapter 5 (elasticity).

## The textbook model — utility maximization

Start with the rule the textbook says a rational consumer is supposed to follow.

A consumer has a fixed income, faces fixed prices for goods, and gets some satisfaction — call it **utility** — from each good consumed. The first slice of pizza brings a lot of utility. The second, somewhat less. The fifth, much less. The pattern from Chapter 2 — diminishing marginal utility — applies here.

The consumer's job is to allocate her budget across goods to maximize total utility, given the prices and the budget. The condition that solves this problem is called the **equimarginal rule**: at the optimum, the consumer is buying so that the *marginal utility per dollar* is equal across all goods.

In symbols: MU₁/P₁ = MU₂/P₂ = ... for every pair of goods being consumed.

Why does this rule produce the optimum? Suppose it doesn't hold — say, the marginal utility per dollar of pizza is higher than that of beer. Then the consumer can take a dollar away from beer and spend it on pizza. The dollar, applied to pizza, gives more utility than it gave when applied to beer. The consumer is better off. She keeps doing this until the per-dollar marginal utilities are equal, and then no further reallocation helps. That's the optimum.

This is also where the demand curve comes from. When the price of a good rises, the marginal utility per dollar of that good falls (same MU, divided by a bigger P). To restore the equimarginal balance, the consumer buys less of the good (which raises its MU again as it climbs back up the diminishing-utility curve). Higher price, lower quantity demanded. The downward-sloping demand curve, derived from a rule about how rational consumers allocate.

This is a beautiful piece of theory. It is also too clean to capture how people actually decide, and we'll get to the messier reality below. But the structure is worth holding because it gives a precise meaning to what a price change *should* do, and that becomes the baseline against which we measure how real behavior departs.

### Substitution and income effects

When the price of one good changes, the consumer's choice shifts in a way that economists decompose into two effects.

**Substitution effect.** When pizza gets relatively more expensive than beer, the consumer substitutes toward beer — even if she's no poorer overall, the relative price has shifted. This effect always pushes toward the cheaper good.

**Income effect.** When pizza gets more expensive, a fixed budget no longer goes as far. The consumer is *effectively* poorer (her real income has fallen) and must consume less of something. For *most* goods (normal goods), this pushes consumption down further. For *inferior* goods (ramen, generic-brand cereal), the income effect can push the other way — when you're effectively poorer, you eat *more* ramen because you can no longer afford the things you'd eat instead.

For most goods, both effects work in the same direction: a higher price means less is bought. For a few unusual goods called Giffen goods — extreme inferior goods that take up a large share of poor households' budgets — the income effect can theoretically dominate, producing an upward-sloping demand curve. The empirical evidence for Giffen goods in modern economies is weak. The point is that the standard downward-sloping demand curve is the typical case, not a logical necessity.

## Choices across time

The same machinery applies when the choice is between consuming now and consuming later. This is **intertemporal choice**, and it covers everything from "should I save for retirement" to "should I take out student loans" to "should I refinance the mortgage."

The diagram is just a budget constraint with a different pair of goods on the axes: *consumption now* on one axis, *consumption later* on the other. The interest rate determines the slope. If you save $1 today at 5 percent interest, you can consume $1.05 a year from now. The slope of the intertemporal budget constraint is therefore −(1 + r) consumption-later per unit of consumption-now.

A higher interest rate makes the line steeper — saving becomes more attractive because each dollar saved today produces more consumption later. The standard model predicts that, all else equal, a higher interest rate should produce more saving.

The standard model also predicts that Americans, as a group, should save more than they do. The U.S. personal saving rate has hovered around 4–8 percent of disposable income for decades, with brief spikes during recessions. Many other developed countries — Germany, Japan, France — save substantially more.

Why the gap? Some of it is institutional (Social Security and Medicare reduce the precautionary motive in the U.S.). Some of it is cultural (debt-financed consumption is more accepted in the U.S.). And some of it is the third thing this chapter is about — the systematic departures of real behavior from the rational-consumer baseline.

## Where the model breaks — behavioral economics

Through the 1970s and 1980s, two psychologists — Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky — ran experiment after experiment showing that people's actual decisions reliably violate the predictions of utility maximization. Not randomly. *Predictably*. Their work, eventually recognized with a Nobel for Kahneman in 2002, became the foundation of **behavioral economics**, which integrates these findings into the standard model rather than pretending they don't exist.

Three patterns matter most for an introductory course.

### Loss aversion

People feel losses more sharply than they feel gains of the same size. Losing $100 produces about twice as much pain as gaining $100 produces pleasure. The standard model says a $100 swing is a $100 swing; the data say it's not.

Loss aversion explains a lot. It explains why people hold losing investments too long ("if I sell, I lock in the loss"). It explains why "$5 off" framing often outperforms "save $5" framing — the loss-of-the-discount feels worse than the gain-of-the-discount feels good. It explains why default options matter so much in retirement plans — opting *out* of contributing feels like a loss; opting *in* feels like a gain.

It also helps explain the lipstick effect. In a recession, big purchases that signal status (a new car, a vacation) become impossible. Small purchases that signal something similar (a tube of expensive lipstick, a designer coffee) become more important *because* they're still affordable. The loss of large indulgences is partially offset by clinging harder to the small ones.

### Present bias

People weigh near-term costs and benefits much more heavily than future ones, and they do so in a *time-inconsistent* way. Asked today whether to exercise tomorrow, you say yes. Asked tomorrow whether to exercise today, you say no. Same question, same person, different answers depending on when "now" is.

Present bias explains chronic undersaving. It explains why people pay subscriptions for years for services they don't use. It explains why diet and exercise interventions that work in the short run usually fail in the long run. The standard intertemporal-choice model assumes people discount the future at a constant rate — meaning they value a dollar tomorrow vs. a dollar today the same way they value a dollar in 366 days vs. 365 days. People don't. They put a much heavier discount on "anything later than right now" and a much lighter discount on the difference between two future dates.

This is why automatic enrollment in 401(k) plans dramatically increases participation. The decision is removed from the present moment. The same workers, asked to actively enroll, often defer the decision indefinitely.

### Framing and reference dependence

The same options, presented differently, produce different choices. A medical treatment described as "90 percent survival rate" is chosen more often than the same treatment described as "10 percent mortality rate." A price labeled "regular $100, today $80" produces more sales than a price simply labeled "$80." The reference point — what people compare to — shapes the choice as much as the options themselves.

Standard economics says the choice depends on the options. Behavioral economics says the choice depends on the options *and how they are framed*. Marketers, lawyers, doctors, and policymakers have known the second thing for a long time. Economics is catching up.

### Reading the lessons

Behavioral economics doesn't say the standard model is *wrong*. It says the standard model is incomplete — a useful first approximation that breaks in predictable ways. The strong reading: real choices depart from utility maximization in directions that can be measured, modeled, and (within limits) exploited or corrected. Default choices in retirement plans, calorie labels on menus, "nudges" to encourage organ donation — these are policy applications of behavioral findings.

The weak reading: people are not exactly rational, but they're not so irrational that the standard model is useless. The standard model still gets the *direction* of most price effects right. It just gets the *magnitude* wrong in systematic ways, and behavioral economics tells us which ways.

I find both readings useful. A policy designer should know the behavioral patterns. An undergraduate trying to understand why prices and quantities move the way they do still benefits from learning the standard model first.

## Synthesis

Pull back. The demand curve from Chapter 3 was a black box. This chapter opened it.

Inside, in the textbook version, sits a consumer running the equimarginal rule — allocating her budget so the marginal utility per dollar is equal across all the goods she buys. The downward-sloping demand curve falls out of that allocation logic. The income effect and the substitution effect, working together, deliver the standard prediction in most cases.

But the consumer in the box is not quite the consumer in the world. The real consumer is loss-averse, present-biased, and sensitive to framing in ways the textbook ignores. Behavioral economics adds those patterns back in, producing a fuller picture: the demand curve still slopes down in most cases; people still respond to prices; but the magnitudes, the time consistency, and the equivalence of "$5 off" with "$5 saved" all break in measurable ways.

A practical reading: when you see a pricing decision, a saving decision, or a behavior-change campaign, the textbook model tells you the baseline. The behavioral patterns tell you where the baseline will be wrong, and in which direction. The lipstick effect, the chronic-undersaving puzzle, and the framing effect on tax forms all sit somewhere between the textbook model and the messy reality. Knowing both gives you something neither alone can.

## LLM Exercises

**Exercise 1 — Apply the equimarginal rule.** Tell an LLM your weekly entertainment budget and the prices of three things you spend it on (streaming, books, concert tickets, whatever). Ask it to walk through the equimarginal rule and identify the optimal allocation, given your subjective valuation of each. Then ask: where in this exercise does the LLM rely on numbers you supply, and where does it have to assume a utility function? Notice how much the answer depends on inputs you can't observe directly.

**Exercise 2 — Decompose a price change.** Tell an LLM about a recent price increase you noticed in something you regularly buy. Ask it to decompose your behavioral response into a substitution effect and an income effect. Push: which effect was larger in your case, and how can the LLM tell? The exercise teaches you to feel the two effects working separately.

**Exercise 3 — Find your present bias.** Describe to an LLM a decision you've put off (saving more, exercising, applying for a job). Ask it to explain the decision in terms of present bias — what's the "now self" doing that the "later self" disagrees with? Then ask the LLM to suggest one structural change (an automatic enrollment, a commitment device) that would help. Evaluate whether the suggestion is genuinely structural or just willpower advice.

**Exercise 4 — Loss aversion in pricing.** Tell an LLM you're a marketer choosing between two equivalent offers: "Save $20 if you order today" or "Pay $20 more if you wait." Ask which framing will produce more orders and why, using loss aversion. A good answer explains *why* the second framing — invoking a loss — is psychologically more powerful, even though the math is identical.

**Exercise 5 — Behavioral nudge design.** Tell an LLM you want to design a policy that helps people save more for retirement, using only the behavioral findings from this chapter. No mandates, no taxes, no education campaigns. The policy must be a structural change to defaults, framings, or timing of decisions. Evaluate the LLM's proposal: does it match the empirical evidence (e.g., automatic enrollment in 401(k) plans dramatically increases participation)? Or does it default to "tell people to save more"?

## What comes next

Chapter 7 — Production, Costs, and Industry Structure flips to the supply side: where supply curves come from, in the form of firms making production decisions about how much to produce, how to combine inputs, and how their cost structure shapes their behavior. Chapters 8 through 11 then explore how firms behave in different competitive environments — perfect competition, monopoly, oligopoly, monopolistic competition, and the antitrust policy that regulates them.

**What would change my mind.** The behavioral findings I've cited (loss aversion, present bias, framing) are robust in lab experiments and in some field experiments. They are weaker in some real-market settings where competition forces firms toward something close to rational pricing. If a market is large, repeat-play, and well-informed, the standard model's predictions improve substantially; behavioral effects compress. The frame of "behavioral patterns matter everywhere" is too strong; "behavioral patterns matter most where information and competition are weak" is more accurate.

**Still puzzling.** I do not have a clean account of why some behavioral biases are sticky and others wash out with experience. Loss aversion appears stable; present bias is partly correctable through commitment devices; framing effects sometimes persist and sometimes fade with feedback. The mechanism that determines which biases stick is, as far as I know, not fully settled.
