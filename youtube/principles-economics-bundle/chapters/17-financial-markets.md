# Chapter 17 — Financial Markets

*How savings find their way to the projects that need them — and what happens when the system breaks.*

In 2006, the total value of U.S. home equity reached $13 trillion. Three years later, in 2009, it had fallen to about $6 trillion — a destruction of roughly $7 trillion of household wealth in 36 months. The financial crisis that produced this collapse pushed the U.S. into the deepest recession since the 1930s, triggered emergency interventions by the Federal Reserve and the Treasury, and reshaped financial regulation for the next decade.

The crisis happened because something went wrong with one specific market — the market for mortgage-backed securities — but the consequences flowed through every other financial market in the economy. Banks failed. Credit markets froze. Money-market funds threatened to "break the buck." Stock markets fell roughly 50 percent. The interconnections that normally make the financial system useful — that match savers to borrowers, that price risk, that allocate capital — became channels through which one market's failure spread to all the others.

This chapter is about that system. The financial market is the institutional machinery that takes savings — money that households and firms aren't currently spending — and channels it to entities that want to borrow or raise capital. When it works, it is one of the most powerful productivity-enhancing inventions in human history. When it doesn't, it can take down whole economies.

By the end of this chapter you should know how firms raise capital (four main ways), how the major financial instruments differ in their risk-return profile, why diversification matters, and why the "I can pick winning stocks" intuition almost always fails.

**Learning objectives.** By the end you should be able to: (1) describe the four ways a firm can raise financial capital; (2) explain the differences between debt (bonds) and equity (stocks) financing; (3) calculate bond yields and explain the inverse relationship between bond prices and interest rates; (4) describe the risk-return trade-off across major asset classes; (5) explain the efficient-market hypothesis and the random-walk theory of stock prices.

**Prerequisites.** Chapter 4 (interest rates, financial capital market). Chapter 6 (intertemporal choice). Chapter 16 (information asymmetry).

## How firms raise money

A firm that wants to invest — build a factory, develop a product, expand into a market — needs financial capital. Four main sources.

**Reinvested profits.** The firm uses its own retained earnings. Cheapest in some sense (no external claim to pay) but limited to what the firm has earned.

**Bank loans.** The firm borrows from a bank. The bank intermediates between depositors (savers) and borrowers, taking on the credit-evaluation work and bearing some of the default risk. Loans typically have specific terms — interest rate, repayment schedule, sometimes collateral.

**Bonds.** The firm issues bonds — promises to pay back a specified amount on a specified date, with periodic interest payments along the way. Bond buyers (savers) get a defined cash flow. The firm gets a lump sum now in exchange for those future payments.

**Stocks.** The firm sells equity — fractional ownership claims on the company's future profits and assets. Stock investors don't get fixed payments; they get a share of whatever the company is worth and earns. Common stock typically also confers voting rights.

The choice among these sources depends on the firm's stage, its profitability, the cost of each option, and the strategic implications. Early-stage startups rely heavily on equity (founders, angels, venture capital) because they don't have the cash flows to support debt. Mature, profitable firms often issue bonds (cheap interest rates, no equity dilution). Growing public companies use a mix.

The aggregate flow of capital through these channels is enormous. The total value of U.S. corporate stocks is in the tens of trillions of dollars. The U.S. corporate-bond market is similarly large. Bank loans, including small-business and corporate loans, run into trillions. The financial system is the circulatory system of the modern economy.

## Bonds — promises to pay

A bond is a contract. The issuer (a firm or government) borrows a stated amount (the **face value** or **principal**) and promises to pay the lender back on a specified **maturity date**, with periodic interest payments (the **coupon**) along the way.

A simple example. A corporation issues a 10-year bond with $1,000 face value and a 5 percent coupon. The buyer pays $1,000 today. The corporation pays the buyer $50 a year (5 percent of $1,000) for 10 years, then $1,000 at the end. Total paid: $1,500 over 10 years on a $1,000 investment.

Bonds trade in secondary markets after they're issued. The price changes as interest rates change. If new bonds are being issued at 6 percent, an old 5 percent bond is less attractive — the buyer would rather get the higher rate. So the old bond's price falls. If new bonds are being issued at 4 percent, the old 5 percent bond is more attractive, and its price rises.

This is the most important fact about bonds: **bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions**. When the Fed raises rates, existing bonds lose value. When the Fed cuts rates, existing bonds gain value. The longer the bond's remaining maturity, the larger the price swing for any given interest-rate change.

The **yield** is the return an investor actually gets, accounting for the price they paid and all the future cash flows. If you buy a $1,000 face-value bond paying a $50 coupon for $1,000, the yield is 5 percent. If you buy the same bond for $900 (because rates have risen), you collect $50/year coupons but only paid $900, so your yield is higher than 5 percent. The yield is an inverse function of the price.

Bond risk comes in two main forms. **Default risk** — the issuer might not pay back. U.S. Treasury bonds are considered essentially default-free (the U.S. government can always print dollars to pay them). Corporate bonds carry default risk that varies by issuer; rating agencies (S&P, Moody's, Fitch) assign letter grades from AAA (very safe) to D (in default). **Interest-rate risk** — even default-free bonds lose value if interest rates rise after you buy them. Long bonds are more interest-rate-sensitive than short ones.

## Stocks — claims on whatever's left

A stock is fundamentally different from a bond. The stockholder doesn't have a claim to a fixed amount. They have a claim to a fraction of whatever's left over after the firm pays its bills, its bondholders, and its taxes — the **residual claim**.

The stockholder benefits in two ways: through **dividends** (cash payments the firm distributes to shareholders) and through **capital gains** (the appreciation of the stock's price over time). Some companies pay generous dividends and grow slowly (utilities, mature consumer companies). Others pay no dividends and grow fast (tech startups, biotechs in development). The mix is the company's choice and a feature of its business model.

Stocks carry more risk than bonds for two reasons. First, the residual-claim status means stockholders eat losses first. If a company goes bankrupt, bondholders typically recover something; stockholders typically recover nothing. Second, stock returns are inherently volatile — they depend on a company's profits, which depend on competition, technology, the economy, and chance, all of which fluctuate.

The reward for the higher risk is, on average, higher returns. U.S. stocks have returned about 7 percent per year (real, after inflation) over very long periods — substantially higher than the 1 to 2 percent real return on long-term Treasury bonds. The stock-bond return gap is called the **equity risk premium**, and it compensates investors for the extra volatility.

## Diversification — the only free lunch

Different stocks (and different bonds) move differently. Some industries do well when others do poorly. Technology stocks may rise when energy stocks fall. Foreign markets may move differently than U.S. markets. By holding a portfolio of many different securities, an investor can reduce risk *without* reducing expected return. This is **diversification**, and it's sometimes called the only free lunch in finance.

The mathematics behind it: the variance of a portfolio depends both on the variance of each component *and* on how the components move together (covariance). If two assets are perfectly correlated (always move the same direction), holding both reduces nothing. If two assets are uncorrelated (move independently), holding both reduces the portfolio's variance. If two assets are negatively correlated, holding both reduces variance even more.

The practical implication: a portfolio of fifty randomly chosen stocks has substantially less risk than any one of those stocks alone. A globally diversified portfolio across stocks, bonds, real estate, and other assets has even lower variance.

**Mutual funds** and **exchange-traded funds (ETFs)** exist primarily to make diversification easy. Buy one share of an S&P 500 index fund and you own a tiny piece of 500 large U.S. companies. Buy one share of a global bond fund and you own a slice of dozens of governments' debt. The fund pools investors' money, buys the securities, and charges a small fee for the service.

## The random walk and efficient markets

Now the question that comes up in every introductory class: can you beat the market by picking winning stocks?

The honest answer, as far as the empirical record shows: **almost certainly not, on average, after costs**.

The argument is in two parts.

First, the **efficient-market hypothesis** says that current stock prices already reflect all publicly available information. If there's a reason to think a stock is undervalued, professional traders have already bought it (raising the price). If there's a reason to think it's overvalued, they've sold it (lowering the price). In equilibrium, prices reflect the best collective judgment about each company's future cash flows.

Second, the **random walk theory** says that future stock prices change in response to *new information*, which by definition can't be predicted in advance. Past prices contain no information about future prices. The path of a stock price is, statistically, a random walk.

Both ideas are simplifications. Markets are not perfectly efficient — there's plenty of evidence that prices sometimes diverge from fundamentals, that some traders have private information, that bubbles and crashes happen. But the simplifications are right enough to predict what we observe: most actively managed mutual funds underperform low-cost index funds over long periods, after fees. Most individual stock-pickers do worse than the market. Most "expert" forecasters of individual stock prices have track records no better than chance.

This has practical consequences. The standard advice to most investors — diversify broadly, hold for the long term, minimize fees, don't try to time the market — is the advice the efficient-market hypothesis implies. It's boring advice. It's also the advice that wins, on average, over long periods.

## The financial system's other side — when it goes wrong

The 2007–2009 financial crisis we opened the chapter with was a failure of the financial system in several places at once.

The trigger was the U.S. housing market. Home prices had risen rapidly through the 2000s, partly fueled by mortgage lending that, with hindsight, was clearly imprudent — adjustable-rate mortgages with teaser rates that jumped after two years, "no-documentation" loans where borrowers' incomes weren't verified, sub-prime borrowers being approved for loans they had little chance of repaying. As long as home prices kept rising, default rates stayed low because borrowers in trouble could refinance or sell. Once prices stopped rising and started falling, the underlying loans began to default in numbers no one had modeled.

The mortgages had been packaged into mortgage-backed securities and sold to investors all over the world. The packaging mechanism, with sophisticated tranching and credit-default swap insurance, had been advertised as making the resulting securities very safe. When the underlying loans started defaulting at unanticipated rates, the securities lost value catastrophically — and the banks and investors holding them suffered losses they hadn't expected.

The losses cascaded. Banks short of capital cut lending to businesses. Businesses short of credit cut hiring and investment. Households short of home equity cut consumption. The real economy shrank. The Fed and the Treasury intervened on a massive scale — emergency lending, capital injections, the takeover of Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, the bailout of AIG. The intervention prevented a full-scale collapse of the banking system; it also generated lasting political controversy about who paid the costs and who absorbed the losses.

The institutional response was the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform Act of 2010. Higher capital requirements for banks. New consumer-protection regulations. The Volcker rule restricting bank proprietary trading. Resolution authority for failing financial institutions. The reforms aimed to reduce the probability of the next crisis and to limit the cost when it comes. Whether they have succeeded is an empirical question we won't fully know until the next major test.

## Synthesis

Pull back. The financial system intermediates between savers and borrowers, channeling capital to its highest-value uses. The four major financing channels — reinvested profits, bank loans, bonds, stocks — each serve different needs at different stages of a firm's life. Bonds are debt with defined cash flows; stocks are equity with residual claims; both involve trade-offs between risk and return. Diversification reduces risk without reducing expected return — the only free lunch. The efficient-market hypothesis says individual stock-picking almost certainly doesn't beat the market on average; the empirical evidence is consistent with this prediction.

The system has structural vulnerabilities. The same interconnections that make it useful for spreading risk and matching capital to projects make it vulnerable to cascading failure when one piece breaks. The 2007–2009 crisis is the recent reference case; previous crises have a similar structure (the savings-and-loan collapse of the 1980s, the Asian financial crisis of 1997, the Mexican peso crisis of 1994, the Long-Term Capital Management collapse of 1998). The pattern is consistent enough that financial economists treat crises as a feature of the system, not an aberration. Regulatory responses try to make the next crisis less catastrophic without sacrificing the system's normal-time productivity.

For an individual investor, the lessons of this chapter are short. Diversify. Don't try to beat the market. Use low-fee index funds. Hold for the long term. Treat any "hot tip" as a warning sign rather than an opportunity. The investing strategies that win, on average, over long periods are the boring ones.

## LLM Exercises

**Exercise 1 — Compute bond price and yield.** Tell an LLM: "A bond pays $50/year for 5 years and returns $1,000 at maturity. The going market interest rate is 4%." Ask it to compute the bond's present value (its price) at the 4 percent rate, and what the yield would be if the bond were instead trading at $950. Verify by hand.

**Exercise 2 — Risk-return trade-off in plain language.** Ask an LLM to rank the following from lowest to highest expected return and from lowest to highest risk: U.S. Treasury bonds, U.S. stock index fund, corporate bonds (BBB-rated), savings account, emerging-market stock fund, U.S. residential real estate. Are the rankings the same? Push the LLM to be explicit about how risk is being defined.

**Exercise 3 — Test the efficient-market hypothesis.** Ask an LLM to design a test of whether U.S. stock markets are efficient. The test must be (a) implementable with publicly available data, and (b) able to distinguish "markets are efficient" from "markets are inefficient." Push: most simple tests don't work because past returns are very noisy. What would?

**Exercise 4 — Diagnose your own portfolio.** Tell an LLM about your own savings or investment portfolio (or a hypothetical one). Ask it to evaluate whether the portfolio is well-diversified, whether the fees are reasonable, and whether the asset allocation is appropriate for your stated time horizon and risk tolerance. Push back on any generic advice that doesn't engage with the specifics.

**Exercise 5 — Trace a crisis cascade.** Ask an LLM to walk through the 2007–2009 financial crisis, starting from one specific event (e.g., the failure of Bear Stearns in March 2008 or Lehman Brothers in September 2008), and show how it propagated through interconnected markets. Identify which channels were most damaging and which institutional features (Fed liquidity facilities, deposit insurance, capital requirements) limited or amplified the spread.

**What would change my mind.** The case that markets are "efficient enough" that individual stock-picking doesn't pay rests on aggregate evidence about the underperformance of active managers. If a robust strategy emerged that consistently beat market returns over long periods after costs and risk adjustment, the case would weaken. The history of "factor investing" — value, momentum, quality, low-volatility — has produced some persistent excess returns, but with substantial debate about whether the excess returns reflect risk premia (which the efficient-market hypothesis would predict) or genuine anomalies (which would refute it).

**Still puzzling.** I do not have a clean account of why financial crises seem to happen at regular intervals despite the regulatory responses to previous crises. Each generation of regulation appears to address the failure mode of the last crisis without preventing the next one — which has a different structure. The financial system seems to evolve faster than the regulatory framework can adapt. The mechanism that produces this dynamic is probably about innovation outside the regulated perimeter, but the predictive accuracy is weak.
