# Chapter 7 — Production, Costs, and Industry Structure

*Where the supply curve actually comes from — what a firm spends, and why "cost" is more complicated than the bill it pays.*

In 1994, Jeff Bezos drove from New York to Seattle in a beat-up car, writing a business plan in the passenger seat while his wife drove. The plan: an online bookstore. The advantages: no physical store, no inventory limit, no geographic constraint on customers. The disadvantages: shipping costs, the complete absence of any infrastructure for what he was proposing, and the small detail that no one had successfully sold books online before.

Twenty-five years later, every independent bookstore in America had either disappeared or learned to live in the gaps Amazon left. Borders, the second-largest bookstore chain in the country, went bankrupt in 2011. Barnes & Noble, the largest, was acquired by a hedge fund in 2019 after years of decline. Amazon, meanwhile, was selling not just books but virtually everything, with its bookstore origins absorbed into a $1+ trillion company.

What happened? The simple answer — "Amazon undercut everyone on price" — is true and uninteresting. The interesting question is *why* Amazon could undercut everyone on price. The answer is in the *cost structure* of online retail versus brick-and-mortar retail. And the cost structure is in this chapter.

We've spent the last few chapters mostly with consumers — what they want, how they choose, where the demand curve comes from. Now we flip to the producer side. The supply curve is not given. It comes from firms making decisions about how much to produce, given what their costs look like. And firms' costs look the way they do because of two things: the *production function* (how inputs become outputs) and the *factor prices* (what those inputs cost).

By the end of this chapter you should be able to look at any business and locate it in a cost-and-output diagram — fixed cost here, variable cost there, marginal cost climbing or falling — and explain why a hardware store charges more than Amazon for the same screwdriver, and why a small bakery survives next to an industrial bread factory.

**Learning objectives.** By the end you should be able to: (1) distinguish between accounting profit and economic profit, and identify implicit costs; (2) draw a production function and explain the law of diminishing marginal product; (3) graph and label the seven short-run cost curves (TFC, TVC, TC, AFC, AVC, ATC, MC) and explain how they relate to each other; (4) distinguish short-run from long-run cost curves; (5) identify economies of scale, diseconomies of scale, and constant returns to scale on a long-run average cost curve.

**Prerequisites.** Chapter 1 (specialization, division of labor). Chapter 2 (opportunity cost, marginal). Chapter 3 (supply curve).

## What "cost" really means

A firm pays bills. Wages to workers. Rent for the factory. Money for raw materials. Utilities, insurance, software licenses. Every dollar that leaves the firm's bank account is what economists call an **explicit cost**. Accountants track these. They show up on the income statement.

But explicit costs are not the whole story. Consider a small startup founded by two engineers who left $250,000-a-year jobs to work on it full time. The startup pays them nothing for the first year. The accountant looking at the books sees zero salary expense. The economist looks at the same situation and sees $500,000 a year of *implicit cost* — the income the founders gave up by working on this instead of their old jobs. The startup is "free" in the accounting sense; expensive in the economic sense.

**Implicit costs** are the opportunity costs of resources the firm uses but doesn't pay for explicitly. The factory the firm owns and doesn't rent — implicit cost equal to the rent it could have charged. The owner's time spent running the business — implicit cost equal to what the owner could have earned doing something else. The capital invested in the business that could have been earning interest in a bond market.

This produces a useful distinction:

- **Accounting profit** = Total Revenue − Explicit Costs.
- **Economic profit** = Total Revenue − Explicit Costs − Implicit Costs = Accounting Profit − Implicit Costs.

A business with positive accounting profit can have zero or negative economic profit. The two engineers' startup might be making $400,000 a year in accounting profit and *losing* $100,000 in economic profit because the implicit cost of their forgone salaries is $500,000. From an economic perspective, they're worse off than if they had stayed at their day jobs. From an accounting perspective, they're rich.

Economic profit is what determines whether a firm should stay in a business in the long run. Zero economic profit doesn't mean zero income — it means the firm is doing exactly as well as it would do in its next-best alternative use of resources. Negative economic profit, sustained, means the firm is destroying value relative to what it could be doing instead, and rational owners eventually reallocate.

## The production function — inputs to outputs

Now the second piece. How do inputs become outputs? The relationship is called the **production function**. Inputs are typically grouped into labor (people-hours), capital (machines, buildings, equipment), and sometimes raw materials and land. Output is whatever the firm produces.

A central distinction: **short run** vs. **long run**. In the short run, at least one input is fixed — usually capital. The firm has whatever factory and equipment it currently owns; it can hire more workers but it can't build a new factory overnight. In the long run, *all* inputs are variable — the firm can change everything, including its capital stock.

This distinction matters because it changes the shape of the cost curves.

In the short run, with capital fixed, adding more labor produces more output — for a while. The first additional worker dramatically raises output. The second adds less. The fifth adds even less. Eventually, more workers crowd the same fixed capital and the marginal addition shrinks toward zero. This pattern — that the additional output from one more unit of a variable input falls as more of it is added, holding everything else fixed — is the **law of diminishing marginal product**, and it is the production-side analogue of diminishing marginal utility.

A worked example. A small typing-services firm has one computer (capital, fixed in the short run) and varies the number of typists (labor, variable). With one typist, the firm produces 5 documents per hour. With two typists sharing the one computer, output rises to 7 — the second typist is helpful (suggesting edits, organizing the queue), but can't fully use the machine when the first one is on it. The marginal product of the second typist is 2 documents. With three typists, 8 documents — marginal product of 1. With four typists, still 8 — marginal product of 0; the fourth typist literally can't get to the keyboard. Diminishing marginal product, made visible.

The fix in the long run: get a second computer. Then two typists each produce 5 documents, total output 10. The capital constraint, eased.

## Cost curves — the supply-side toolkit

Combine the production function with factor prices and you get costs. The full toolkit takes a moment to build, but each piece pays its keep.

**Total fixed cost (TFC)** — costs that don't depend on output. The factory rent, the equipment payments, the salaried managers. In the short run, these are paid whether the firm produces 0 units or 10,000.

**Total variable cost (TVC)** — costs that change with output. Raw materials, hourly labor, energy. Zero when output is zero; rising as output rises.

**Total cost (TC) = TFC + TVC**. The total bill at every level of output.

Now the per-unit measures, which are where the action is.

**Average fixed cost (AFC) = TFC / Q**. Falls as output rises — the same fixed cost spread over more units. This is why scale matters.

**Average variable cost (AVC) = TVC / Q**. Often U-shaped — falls at first as fixed factors are better utilized, rises later as diminishing marginal product kicks in.

**Average total cost (ATC) = TC / Q = AFC + AVC**. Also U-shaped, with the minimum further to the right than AVC's minimum because AFC keeps falling.

**Marginal cost (MC) = ΔTC / ΔQ** — the cost of producing one more unit. This is the most important number on the firm's spreadsheet, because every production decision a rational firm makes is a comparison of *marginal cost* to *marginal revenue*.

There is one geometric fact about these curves you should know: the marginal cost curve crosses both the AVC and ATC curves *at their minimum points*. This is not a coincidence. When MC is below the average, the average is being pulled down. When MC is above the average, the average is being pulled up. The MC curve crosses an average curve precisely where the average is flat — at its minimum.

This is a piece of arithmetic dressed up as a graph. If your average grade is 80 and you take a test and get a 70, your average drops. If you take a test and get a 90, your average rises. The new test is the "marginal" data point; the average follows it down or up. Same logic for cost curves. *Marginal pulls average*.

## The long run — economies of scale

In the long run, the firm chooses the best combination of all inputs for each level of output. This produces a **long-run average cost curve (LRAC)** that is typically U-shaped at the firm level — but the U is much wider than the short-run version.

The LRAC has three regions, each with a name.

**Economies of scale.** As the firm scales up, average cost falls. Each unit gets cheaper to produce. This happens because larger firms can specialize labor, invest in better equipment, spread fixed costs over more output, and negotiate volume discounts on inputs. Most industries have *some* economies of scale at small output levels. Software has enormous economies of scale (one program can be copied infinitely at near-zero marginal cost). Mass-market manufacturing typically has substantial economies. Specialty restaurants and craft producers often have very limited economies of scale.

**Constant returns to scale.** A range over which scaling doesn't change average cost — the firm has reached the size at which it captures the available scale advantages, and additional output costs the same per unit.

**Diseconomies of scale.** Beyond some size, average cost begins to *rise*. The firm becomes too big to coordinate. Communication overhead grows quadratically with the number of employees. Bureaucracy slows decisions. Top management can't see what's happening on the front line. Big companies often hit diseconomies of scale somewhere; the question is at what size.

Now back to Amazon. The question was why Amazon could undercut everyone. The answer is in the cost structure.

A traditional bookstore has high fixed costs per book sold. Real estate, store staff, lighting, heating, the inventory tied up in books that may not sell. The store has economies of scale up to the point where it fills the building, but the building constrains it. A bigger building costs more rent. Above some size, average cost stops falling.

An online retailer has very different fixed costs. Warehouses (cheaper per square foot than retail). Software (a large one-time fixed cost, near-zero marginal cost). A logistics network that costs huge amounts to set up and very little to use once built. Once Amazon had built the warehouse network, the fulfillment software, the customer database, and the recommendation engine, the marginal cost of selling one more book was very small.

Amazon's average cost per book fell precipitously as the firm grew, in a way the brick-and-mortar bookstore's never could. This is what "Amazon used economies of scale to crush the industry" actually means, in the technical sense of this chapter. It wasn't a willingness-to-lose-money story (though Amazon did lose money for years). It was a *cost-structure* story.

## Synthesis

Pull back. The supply curve from Chapter 3 is a black box like the demand curve was, and this chapter has opened it. Inside sits a firm with a production function, paying its inputs, watching its short-run cost curves climb as diminishing marginal product kicks in, and choosing — in the long run — its scale based on where its long-run average cost curve sits.

A practical reading. Every business has a cost structure. The cost structure has fixed pieces and variable pieces. The fixed pieces matter most when output is small (high AFC = high ATC). The variable pieces matter most when output is large (AFC has fallen to nothing; AVC is what's left). Decisions about scale, pricing, and entry are all decisions about how to position the firm on its cost curves.

The scale question — economies vs. diseconomies — also matters at the *industry* level. Industries with large economies of scale tend toward concentration: a few big firms dominate, because small firms can't compete on price. Industries with limited economies of scale stay fragmented: many small firms, each at the bottom of their U-curve. Restaurants, hairdressers, and specialty shops are fragmented. Auto manufacturing, semiconductors, and cloud computing are concentrated. The cost curve, more than anything else, predicts the structure.

We are now ready to ask the question that will run through the next four chapters: given that a firm's cost structure looks the way it does, how should the firm decide how much to produce, and how does the answer differ depending on what kind of market it operates in? That's where we go next.

## LLM Exercises

**Exercise 1 — Find the implicit costs.** Pick a small business you know — a friend's startup, a family member's restaurant, a freelance design business. Describe it to an LLM in detail, including the explicit costs. Ask the LLM to identify three implicit costs the owner is not paying themselves for. Then ask the LLM to estimate the *economic* profit of the business — accounting profit minus implicit costs. Evaluate whether the LLM is asking the right questions about opportunity cost.

**Exercise 2 — Build the cost curves from a production function.** Tell an LLM: "A firm has fixed costs of $1,000/month. Each worker costs $2,000/month. Output per worker is: 1st worker, 100 units; 2nd, 80; 3rd, 50; 4th, 30; 5th, 10." Ask the LLM to compute total cost, average total cost, and marginal cost at each output level. Verify by hand. Then ask: at what output level is average total cost minimized? Where does marginal cost cross average total cost?

**Exercise 3 — Diagnose the cost structure of a real industry.** Pick an industry you find interesting (streaming services, ride-sharing, coffee shops, semiconductor manufacturing). Ask an LLM to describe the cost structure: what's fixed, what's variable, what's the shape of the long-run average cost curve. Then ask: does the industry tend toward concentration or fragmentation? Does the LLM's prediction match what you actually observe in the industry?

**Exercise 4 — Diseconomies in big tech.** Tell an LLM: "Amazon, Google, and Apple are at very large scale. Are they currently in the diseconomies-of-scale region of their long-run average cost curves?" Press the LLM to use specific evidence — coordination costs, organizational complexity, slowing growth in revenue per employee. Push back if the answer is too confident in either direction.

**Exercise 5 — Predict an industry shake-up from cost-curve thinking.** Ask an LLM to identify a current industry where a new entrant is using a different cost structure to challenge incumbents — Tesla in autos, Stripe in payments, OpenAI in AI services, your pick. Ask it to walk through what the new cost structure looks like and why it should win on long-run average cost. Then ask: what could go wrong with the prediction? What assumption would have to fail?

## What comes next

Chapter 8 — Perfect Competition shows how a firm with no pricing power decides how much to produce. Chapter 9 — Monopoly shows the same decision when the firm is the entire market. Chapters 10 and 11 fill in the in-between cases: monopolistic competition, oligopoly, and the antitrust policy that tries to keep markets from concentrating too far. The cost curves we built here will be the recurring tool — a firm's cost structure does not change when the market structure changes; what changes is the demand curve the firm faces.

**What would change my mind.** The claim that economies of scale predict industry concentration depends on the assumption that scale is the dominant cost lever. In some industries, regulation (banking), capital intensity (steel), or network effects (social media) drive concentration more than scale. In others, factors that *fragment* — strong local preferences, regulatory variation, taste differentiation — matter more than scale economies. The framework gives a starting point. Industry-specific factors decide the verdict.

**Still puzzling.** I do not have a clean account of why some firms manage diseconomies of scale better than others. Some companies grow to enormous size while keeping coordination costs in check (Google for a long time; Amazon, in some periods); others run into severe diseconomies at much smaller size. The organizational-economics literature has stories — culture, modularity, principal-agent design — but the predictions are not crisp.
