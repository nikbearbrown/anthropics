## Candidate 01 — Build the Scaffold Classifier: Map Any AI Tutoring Feature Against Three Architectures with Claude

- Source: ai-for-learning-experience-design/chapters/03-the-scaffold.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: The product says "AI tutor." The question is which of three machines you're actually buying: one that constrains the AI, one that supervises the AI, or one that supports the human. They have different evidence, different failure modes, and different equity profiles. Most procurement teams can't tell them apart from a demo.
- The artifact: A screen-recording of Claude receiving a description of an AI tutoring product (feature list, marketing copy, or a short demo transcript) and classifying it against the three scaffold architectures: (1) Constrain the AI — AI only has access to a bounded, teacher-authored content set; (2) Supervise the AI — AI runs freely, a human reviews before or after delivery; (3) Support the Human — AI enhances the human tutor's capability without replacing them. Claude outputs a classification table: Architecture | Evidence Present / Absent | Specific Feature | Risk if Misidentified. A Manim animation renders the three architectures as labeled nodes on a decision tree — the product's features snap into position as evidence points.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Classify this AI tutoring product against three scaffold architectures: (1) Constrain the AI — the AI has access only to teacher-authored, bounded content; (2) Supervise the AI — the AI operates broadly and a human reviews output before or after student delivery; (3) Support the Human — the AI enhances a human tutor's work without replacing the tutor. Product description: [paste feature list or marketing copy]. For each architecture: Is evidence present or absent? What specific feature provides that evidence? What is the risk if a buyer misidentifies which architecture this is? Output as a classification table. Then name the architecture with the strongest RCT evidence as of 2025."`
- Read / check: Verify all three architectures appear as rows. Confirm the evidence column cites specific features, not general impressions. Check that the "Risk if Misidentified" column names a concrete consequence (equity disparity, content accuracy failure, tutor displacement) — not a generic caution. Confirm the Manim tree is legible and the snap-in animation sequence is readable at video resolution.
- Human supplies: A product feature list or marketing copy for a real or realistic synthetic AI tutoring product (Khan Academy's Khanmigo, Carnegie Learning MATHia, or a fictional "EduBot Pro" stand-in). Synthetic is fully sufficient for the video.
- Output medium: screen-recording mp4 (terminal session) + Manim (three-architecture decision tree animation)
- The change: Run the same classification on a second product and show how different architectures have different equity implications. Prompt: "For a school district where 30% of students use phones on shared data plans, which architecture introduces the least routing bias risk? Why?" Show Claude's answer labeling the equity-architecture connection.
- Teardown angle: The word "scaffold" covers three different machines with different costs, risks, and evidence bases. Procurement that can't name the architecture is procurement that can't evaluate the risk. The classification is the due diligence.
- Exclusions: Cut the contingency-fading-transfer theoretical detail beyond the three-architecture taxonomy; cut the GPT Tutor / GPT Base RCT narrative (covered in teachers book); cut vendor market dynamics.
- Score: 10/10

## Candidate 02 — Build the Layer 1 / Layer 2 Withdrawal Test: Check Whether Your AI Tool Created a Crutch with Claude

- Source: ai-for-learning-experience-design/chapters/01-two-layers-of-change.md + chapters/02-the-crutch-effect.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: The DataWise 101 pilot showed learning gains. The withdrawal test showed the gains vanished without the tool. The tool wasn't teaching — it was doing the cognitive work the learner was supposed to practice. The dashboard never measured the difference.
- The artifact: A screen-recording of Claude receiving a learning program design (objectives, AI-tool touchpoints, assessment design) and running a Layer 1 / Layer 2 audit. Layer 1 = designer's workflow (did AI accelerate design without replacing designer judgment?). Layer 2 = learner's experience (did AI scaffold learning or substitute for the cognitive effort that produces learning?). For each AI touchpoint in the program, Claude outputs: Touchpoint | Layer | Scaffold or Crutch Test | Withdrawal Test Question. A Manim animation shows two parallel timelines — Layer 1 (design workflow) and Layer 2 (learner experience) — with AI touchpoints marked, and a vertical "withdrawal bar" that sweeps through both: what disappears when the tool is removed at each point?
- Prompt seed: `claude "Audit this learning program design for Layer 1 / Layer 2 crutch risk. Layer 1 = the designer's workflow (AI tools used during design). Layer 2 = the learner's experience (AI tools the learner interacts with). Program design: [paste design including objectives, AI touchpoints, assessment plan]. For each AI touchpoint, run the withdrawal test: 'If this tool were removed today, what cognitive work would the learner have to do that they are currently not doing?' If the answer is 'nothing — the learner still has to think,' classify as Scaffold. If the answer is 'the learner would have to do the thinking the tool is currently doing,' classify as Crutch Risk. Output: Touchpoint | Layer | Classification | Withdrawal Test Result | Recommended Design Change."`
- Read / check: Verify the withdrawal test is applied to each AI touchpoint, not just the most obvious ones. Confirm the Scaffold / Crutch Risk classification is grounded in the specific cognitive work being offloaded, not a surface-level judgment. Check that the Manim withdrawal bar animation is timed correctly — not too fast to read at video resolution.
- Human supplies: A learning program design description (synthetic: a 6-week professional upskilling course using AI feedback on written responses + AI-generated practice scenarios + AI-summarized readings). Real-data upgrade: an LX designer inputs their own current program in development.
- Output medium: screen-recording mp4 (terminal session) + Manim (two-layer timeline with withdrawal sweep animation)
- The change: Show a redesign pass — for each Crutch Risk touchpoint, Claude generates one structural change that converts it to a Scaffold: "What is the minimum change that preserves the AI time-saving while restoring the cognitive work to the learner?" Compare the before/after touchpoint list.
- Teardown angle: The crutch effect is invisible at the dashboard level. Quiz scores stay up. Time-to-completion stays down. Engagement stays high. The only signal is the withdrawal test — and most program designs never run it. The audit makes the test explicit.
- Exclusions: Cut the DataWise 101 extended narrative; cut the dependency vs. agency philosophical framing; cut the Bastani et al. learning-loss data (covered in teachers book).
- Score: 10/10

## Candidate 03 — Build the BKT Progress Bar Decoder: Expose What "Skills Remaining" Actually Measures with Claude

- Source: ai-for-learning-experience-design/chapters/07-adaptive-systems.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: The progress bar says "3 skills remaining." That number is a probability. It was calculated with four parameters someone chose. The threshold that decides "mastered" was set somewhere between 0.80 and 0.95 — and the learner who sits at 0.94 drills indefinitely. Most LXDs don't know which number is running their product.
- The artifact: A screen-recording of Claude running the Bayesian Knowledge Tracing four-step worked update — P(L₀) = 0.20, P(T) = 0.15, P(G) = 0.25, P(S) = 0.10, sequence: correct, correct, incorrect, correct — showing the posterior walk step by step, ending at 0.82 (below 0.95 threshold). Claude then runs a sensitivity analysis: what happens to the final posterior if P(T) is set to 0.30? If the mastery threshold is set to 0.80? If P(S) is 0.05? A Manim animation shows the posterior walk as a line chart — the threshold as a dashed horizontal line — with the over-practice zone shaded red between the final posterior and the threshold.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Run the Bayesian Knowledge Tracing update for this skill with these parameters: P(L₀)=0.20, P(T)=0.15, P(G)=0.25, P(S)=0.10. Answer sequence: correct, correct, incorrect, correct. Show the posterior probability of mastery after each answer, applying: (1) the likelihood update from the observed response; (2) the learning opportunity bump. Round to two decimal places. Then run three sensitivity analyses: (A) same sequence but P(T)=0.30; (B) same sequence but mastery threshold = 0.80; (C) same sequence but P(S)=0.05. For each, report the final posterior and whether it clears the mastery threshold. Label what the designer's choice — not the data — determined."`
- Read / check: Verify the four-step posterior walk matches the chapter's worked example values (approximately 0.55, 0.84, 0.51, 0.82). Confirm the three sensitivity analyses produce different outcomes. Check that the Manim chart correctly positions the threshold line and labels the over-practice zone.
- Human supplies: Nothing — the BKT parameters and worked example are from the chapter. Fully synthetic.
- Output medium: screen-recording mp4 (terminal showing the step-by-step update) + Manim (posterior walk line chart with threshold and over-practice zone)
- The change: Add a second scenario — a learner who mastered the wrong generalization (answers correctly by applying a misconception consistently). Show that BKT's four-parameter model has no way to distinguish this learner from one with genuine mastery. Label: "The progress bar is a thermometer, not a diagnosis."
- Teardown angle: The progress bar is a probability computed from parameters a human chose. The threshold is a designer decision, not a measurement. Before granting any mastery indicator authority — over pacing, assessment windows, teacher belief — name the threshold, the parameters, and the knowledge component map it depends on.
- Exclusions: Cut IRT technical derivation beyond the S-curve concept; cut Deep Knowledge Tracing neural network architecture; cut the DKT re-evaluation benchmark controversy beyond one sentence.
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 04 — Build the Distractor Audit: Catch AI-Generated Quiz Items That Look Wrong but Don't Teach with Claude

- Source: ai-for-learning-experience-design/chapters/09-ai-generated-content-and-feedback.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: The AI generated four distractors. They all look wrong. None of them encode a real misconception. A student who picks the wrong answer learns nothing — because the distractor was plausible, not diagnostic. The quiz discriminates without teaching.
- The artifact: A screen-recording of two prompt rounds. Round 1: Claude generates 4 multiple-choice items with distractors for a topic (confidence intervals, cellular respiration, contract formation — pick one) with no misconception guidance. Round 2: Claude generates the same 4 items, but each distractor is required to encode a named, real learner misconception from a provided misconception inventory. Claude outputs a comparison table: Item | Distractor | Plausible Only / Diagnostic | Misconception Encoded (if any). A Manim animation renders a 2×2 matrix — Diagnostic vs. Plausible (y-axis) / Looks Wrong vs. Looks Right (x-axis) — with items from both rounds plotted as labeled dots.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Generate 4 multiple-choice questions on confidence interval interpretation for introductory statistics students. Round 1: generate freely, no constraints on distractors. Round 2: generate the same 4 items, but each incorrect option must encode one of these known misconceptions: (a) 'the interval contains 95% of the data values'; (b) 'there is a 95% chance the true mean is in this specific interval'; (c) 'a wider interval is always more informative'; (d) 'the interval is about the sample, not the population.' For each distractor in Round 2, label which misconception it targets. Then generate a comparison table: Item | Distractor | Plausible Only or Diagnostic | Misconception Encoded."`
- Read / check: Verify Round 2 distractors genuinely encode the named misconceptions — not just rephrase them. Confirm the comparison table distinguishes Plausible Only from Diagnostic correctly. Check that the Manim 2×2 matrix is legible and labeled — the item dots should be visually distinct between rounds.
- Human supplies: A misconception inventory for the topic (the four confidence interval misconceptions listed in the prompt seed are the synthetic stand-in). Real-data upgrade: an LXD or subject-matter expert provides a real misconception inventory from their domain.
- Output medium: screen-recording mp4 (two-round terminal session) + Manim (2×2 diagnostic matrix animation)
- The change: Run a third round — add a subject-matter expert review step (simulated by a second Claude prompt) that validates whether each Round 2 distractor "maps to a misconception a real learner actually holds, not just one that sounds plausible in the abstract." Show one distractor get revised after the review.
- Teardown angle: A distractor that discriminates without diagnosing produces data without insight. The fix is a design pattern — generate distractors against a misconception inventory, verify each maps to a real cognitive error. This is the step the generation pipeline cannot perform on its own.
- Exclusions: Cut the BABEL generator story; cut essay scoring and automated feedback; cut the measurement theory validity chain beyond one sentence.
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 05 — Build the Equity Routing Audit: Find the Proxy Chain That Creates Digital Tracking with Claude

- Source: ai-for-learning-experience-design/chapters/08-algorithmic-routing-and-equity.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: The adaptive system doesn't have demographic data. It has response latency, hint usage, and session continuity. Those three features reconstruct income and device access with enough accuracy to route students into remediation they never leave. No protected attribute anywhere in the feature list. Disparity in the output anyway.
- The artifact: A screen-recording of Claude receiving a feature list for a hypothetical adaptive learning system and tracing the proxy chain: which features correlate with protected attributes, how those features feed the routing decision, and what the downstream task-diet divergence looks like after 6 weeks. A Manim animation shows the DataWise 101 scenario — two learners, same 62% diagnostic score — diverging to 40% vs. 8% higher-order task diet over 6 weeks, with the proxy chain labeled at each decision point.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Trace the proxy chain for this adaptive learning system's routing algorithm. For each feature, identify: (1) what protected attribute it correlates with (income, device type, language background, disability status); (2) how the correlation enters the routing decision; (3) what the cumulative task-diet consequence is for a learner whose proxy signature consistently reads as 'struggling.' Then run the two-learner simulation: both score 62% on a diagnostic. Learner A has smooth, fast sessions. Learner B has high latency, broken sessions. - Human supplies: The feature list is synthetic (the DataWise 101 scenario). A real LXD can substitute their own adaptive system's feature list from the vendor documentation.
- Output medium: screen-recording mp4 (terminal session) + Manim (two-learner divergence timeline animation)
- The change: Add a second prompt: "What three feature engineering changes would reduce the proxy chain risk without degrading the system's overall accuracy? For each, name the specific feature modification and the protected-attribute correlation it addresses." Show Claude's three recommendations and label the design trade-off each one makes.

The audit's product is not a certificate of fairness — it is a named design judgment about which unfairness to refuse.
- Exclusions: Cut the ABROCA statistical detail beyond the concept; cut the Kleinberg impossibility theorem beyond one sentence; cut the Ofqual A-level episode detail.

## Candidate 07 — Build the Assessment Validity Audit: Find Which Kane Inference GenAI Broke with Claude

- Source: ai-for-learning-experience-design/chapters/10-assessment-redesign.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: The rubric worked perfectly. The markers applied every criterion correctly. And 94% of GPT-4 written answers went undetected, earning higher grades than the real students. The rubric wasn't broken. The inference was broken — and it broke for every student, not just the ones who cheated.
- The artifact: A screen-recording of Claude receiving an assessment design (task description, rubric, stakes, context) and running Kane's four-inference validity audit: (1) Scoring — is the performance correctly converted to a score?; (2) Generalization — does this score predict performance across the test domain?; (3) Extrapolation — does test-domain performance predict real-world capability?; (4) Decision — does the capability claim warrant the grade/credential/progression? For each inference, Claude outputs: Inference | Holds / Weakened / Broken by GenAI | Mechanism | Structural Fix. A Manim animation renders the four-link validity chain — intact links in green, broken links in red, with the GenAI attack point animated as a cut in the chain.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Run a Kane four-inference validity audit on this assessment. Task: [paste assessment description, rubric, and stakes]. For each of Kane's four inferences — Scoring, Generalization, Extrapolation, Decision — evaluate: (1) does this inference hold for an honest student completing the task independently?; (2) is this inference weakened or broken if a student submits AI-generated work without the examiner detecting it?; (3) what is the mechanism of the break?; (4) what structural change to the task design would restore the inference? Classify each inference as Holds / Weakened / Broken. Identify which inference GenAI primarily attacks in this assessment."`
- Read / check: Verify all four inferences appear in the output. Confirm the "Weakened/Broken" classification correctly targets Extrapolation as the primary GenAI attack point for typical unproctored essays (per the chapter). Check that the "Structural Fix" column suggests task redesign, not detection or policy (per the chapter's argument). Verify the Manim animation's red-link placement matches the audit output.
- Human supplies: An assessment description (synthetic: 8-page undergraduate essay on a humanities topic, unproctored, submitted digitally, with a standard rubric). Real-data upgrade: an LXD inputs an actual assessment from a course they support.
- Output medium: screen-recording mp4 (terminal session) + Manim (four-link validity chain with break animation)
- The change: After the audit, run a second prompt: "Generate a structurally redesigned version of this task where the Extrapolation inference holds — even at 0% detection rate. The redesign cannot rely on proctoring or AI-use policy." Show Claude's structural redesign and label the specific mechanism that restores the inference.
- Teardown angle: GenAI breaks the inference, not the rule. The inference breaks for every student — not just those who cheated. Detection is an arms race; redesign is the exit. The Kane audit is the tool that converts a moral panic into a design diagnosis.
- Exclusions: Cut the University of Reading study's grade-by-module detail; cut the Liang et al. 61.3% false-positive finding beyond one sentence; cut the Texas A&M–Commerce and Vanderbilt cases beyond naming them.
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 08 — Research the Evidence Landscape: What Do RCTs Actually Show About AI Tutoring Outcomes with Claude

- Exclusions: Cut the Bayesian methodology detail; cut the GPT Tutor / GPT Base numbers (covered in teachers book); cut meta-analysis methodology beyond what's needed for the checklist.
- Score: 8/10
