*The photo wasn't a lie. It just showed a property that didn't exist at the showing.*

---

The photo showed a bright, unobstructed kitchen view. At the showing, the buyer saw the power lines and the neighboring structure the image had removed. The agent hadn't changed the square footage or the list price. Nothing in the MLS data sheet was inaccurate. But the image had changed the buyer's expectation of what they were walking into — and when the showing didn't match the photo, the transaction's momentum went with it.

That gap — between a legally accurate listing and a materially misleading one — is what this chapter is about. The tools that create it are not malfunctioning. They are doing exactly what they were designed to do: make properties look as good as possible in photographs. The compliance question is not about tool capability. It is about where making a property look good becomes making a property look like something it is not.

The answer is not obvious from intuition alone. It requires a framework, and that framework has three components: what the law and professional standards actually require, what "material" means in the specific context of real estate advertising, and how to classify specific editing decisions before an image reaches a platform.

---

## What the Rules Actually Say

Start with the specific regulatory landscape, because the specifics matter and are easy to misstate.

California AB 723, enacted in 2025 and effective in 2026, creates disclosure requirements for digitally altered real estate images in advertising materials. The statute targets images that have been modified to change the depiction of the property itself — not color correction or exposure adjustment, but alterations that add, remove, or change physical features of the property or its surroundings. The precise language and final codification details should be verified against the enrolled bill and any implementing guidance before relying on specific provisions for client advice.

The practical effect for California agents: any image used in listing advertising that has been digitally altered beyond standard photographic correction needs disclosure identifying it as altered. "Virtually staged" or "digitally enhanced" labels on the image, or disclosure in the listing remarks, are the operative mechanisms. The statute does not prohibit altered images. It requires transparency about what has been altered.

The national principle is older and not California-specific. NAR's Code of Ethics Article 12 requires that agents present a "true picture" in advertising. The First Photo Rule — which governs what image must appear as the primary listing photo in MLS-syndicated advertising — has its own policy details that vary by MLS and should be verified against the applicable MLS rules before implementation. The underlying principle is consistent across jurisdictions: advertising that creates a materially false impression of the property creates misrepresentation liability regardless of whether a specific AI-image statute applies.

The phrase that does the most work across all of these frameworks is "material." A material alteration is one that changes a buyer's understanding of something that matters to the purchase decision — not a technical distinction, but a practical one. The test is: if a buyer saw this image before the showing and then saw the actual property, would they feel misled? Not merely disappointed by the difference between a professional photo and reality, but misled about something they would have weighed in their decision.

## The Classification Problem

Here is the practical challenge. Image editing exists on a spectrum, and the compliance question is not whether an image has been edited — virtually all professional listing photos are edited — but where on the spectrum a specific edit falls.

The spectrum has three zones.

**Ordinary photographic correction.** Exposure adjustment, color temperature correction, straightening, minor noise reduction, removal of a temporary object left in frame by accident — a moving box the photographer didn't notice, a car that obscured the driveway that has since moved. These edits bring the image closer to what the property actually looks like under normal viewing conditions. They are universally understood as standard photography practice and do not implicate disclosure requirements.

**Virtual staging.** Adding furniture, rugs, artwork, and decorative objects to an empty room. This is the most common AI-assisted editing application in residential real estate, and it is legally permissible under the frameworks described above — provided it is disclosed. The disclosure requirement exists because a staged image creates an impression that the property includes furnishings it does not include, and a buyer who understands that the image is virtually staged can calibrate their expectations accordingly. The failure mode is not staging itself; it is staging without disclosure, which converts a useful visual tool into a potentially misleading representation.

**Material alteration.** Removing or changing physical features of the property or its surroundings that would be visible at the showing: power lines, neighboring structures, construction activity, landscaping condition, exterior defects, views obstructed by trees or buildings. These edits change what the buyer expects to see when they arrive, and the difference between the image and reality is not a staging disclosure issue — it is a misrepresentation issue.

The worked example from the chapter's opening case falls squarely in the third zone. The power lines and neighboring structure are not temporary. They are permanent features of the property's visual context that a buyer would see at every showing, from every angle the photo was taken from. Removing them doesn't show the property under better conditions — it shows a property that doesn't exist. A buyer who relies on the image in deciding to tour, make an offer, or commit to purchase has been given inaccurate information about a material feature.

## Why Platform Availability Is Not Permission

One of the most common misjudgments in this space is treating a platform feature as implicit permission. If Zillow Showcase or a virtual staging app offers a tool that removes unwanted background elements, the availability of that feature might seem to imply that using it is acceptable practice. It doesn't.

The platforms that offer AI editing tools are in the business of providing useful features to agents and sellers. They are not in the business of certifying that every use of those features is consistent with the agent's professional obligations, Fair Housing requirements, or applicable state law. The terms of service for listing media platforms typically place compliance responsibility with the user. The platform offering a background removal tool does not assume the misrepresentation liability that arises if the tool is used to remove a material feature from a listing photo.

This is not a technical distinction. It is the same principle that governs AI-generated text: the tool produces an output. The licensed professional is responsible for what that output represents when it reaches a client, a buyer, a platform, or a transaction file.

The practical implication: before using any AI image editing feature, the agent classifies the edit against the three zones described above and applies the appropriate disclosure requirement. The platform's availability of the feature is not part of that classification. The classification is based on whether the edit changes material reality, not on what the software is capable of doing.

## The Four-Edit Test

There is a concrete procedure that makes the classification usable in practice. Before any edited image is published, apply it to each edit the image received.

**Edit 1: Did this change the exposure, color, or technical quality of the image without altering the physical content?** If yes, no disclosure required. Proceed.

**Edit 2: Did this add furniture, decor, or staging elements that are not present in the property?** If yes, disclosure is required. The image must be labeled as virtually staged. The original unstaged version should be retained. Proceed with disclosure.

**Edit 3: Did this remove, alter, or add a physical feature of the property itself or its immediate surroundings — structures, landscaping, views, defects?** If yes, the edit is a material alteration. The image should not be published in this form for listing advertising. Either the edit is reversed, or the image is published alongside the unedited version with explicit disclosure of the specific alteration, and the agent should consult with their broker about the specific MLS and legal requirements that apply.

**Edit 4: Would a buyer who relied on this image to make a decision about touring or offering feel materially misled when they saw the actual property?** This is the judgment check. If the answer is yes, or even possibly yes, the image does not go to market in its edited form.

The buyer-at-showing test in the fourth check is the most important one, because it converts a legal question into a practical question with a clear answer. Legal standards are interpreted by regulators, attorneys, and courts. But the agent applying this test doesn't need a legal interpretation — they need to ask themselves honestly: if someone came to this showing having seen only this image, would they feel misled? That question is answerable without a law degree, and it is the right question.

## The Archive Requirement

There is a practical obligation that follows from the disclosure framework that agents frequently overlook: the original, unedited images must be retained.

The disclosure that an image is "virtually staged" or "digitally enhanced" is meaningful only if the original version is accessible. If a buyer, a lender, an inspector, or a regulator later asks what the unaltered property looked like, the agent who has only the edited version has a problem. The archive is not a formality. It is the documentation that makes the disclosure real.

The archive also matters for the material alteration case in a different way. If an agent publishes an image that turns out to have crossed the material alteration line — perhaps because the original photographer processed it and the agent didn't catch the edit — the ability to produce the original image and demonstrate that the agent was not aware of the alteration is a relevant fact in any subsequent inquiry.

## Where This Fits in the Larger Practice

The listing media chapter is the most visible instance of a principle that runs through every chapter in this book: the agent is accountable for what reaches the client, regardless of how it was produced.

In the listing copy context, that accountability is about Fair Housing compliance and factual accuracy. In the document summary context, it is about source verification and professional interpretation. In the listing media context, it is about the difference between a property that looks its best and a property that looks like something it is not.

The tool makes enhancement easy. It also makes material alteration easy, and it does not distinguish between them. That distinction is the agent's job. The four-edit test, applied consistently before any image reaches a platform, is the habit that keeps the tool in the first category and out of the second.

## What Would Change This Chapter

The chapter would need revision in either direction as the regulatory landscape develops. If California AB 723's implementation guidance or subsequent enforcement actions clarify the specific standard for what constitutes a disclosure-triggering alteration, the classification framework would be updated against that guidance. If national MLS rules or NAR policy develop a uniform standard that supersedes current variation by market, the advice would be updated accordingly.

The underlying principle — that images creating materially false impressions of a property create misrepresentation liability — is not regulatory. It is the general law of misrepresentation applied to a specific medium. That principle does not change with the tools. The tools that make alteration faster and easier do not change the standard; they make it more relevant, more frequently, to more agents than before.
