*Earning the Right to Say "I Liked It" or "I Didn't."*

---

For thirty-eight years, until she stopped in 2017, Michiko Kakutani was the chief book critic of *The New York Times*. She read thousands of books. She gave Norman Mailer some of the worst reviews of his career. She gave Jonathan Franzen some of the best of his. Her negative reviews were credited with sinking sales of major novels; her positive reviews with launching them. She won the Pulitzer Prize for Criticism in 1998 and did not show up to accept it in person. She did not give interviews. She did not go to literary parties. She did not take calls from authors.[^1]

[^1]: Kakutani's tenure at the *Times* ran from 1979 to 2017. Her [final review](https://www.nytimes.com/2017/07/27/books/review-democracy-orange-revolution-mariusz-szczygiel.html) ran in July 2017.

The thing I want you to notice about Kakutani is not the authority — the years, the prizes, the unquestioned influence. That is the result. The thing to notice is the position. Her position: a book review is an act of judgment, made by someone whose job it is to read more carefully than the average reader, on behalf of readers who will then decide whether to spend their time. The reviewer's job is not to be the author's friend. It is not to be the publishing industry's promoter. It is to read the book carefully and report what she found.

That move — judgment, anchored in evidence, delivered to a reader who needs it to decide — is what this chapter is about. Not Kakutani's authority. The move itself.

---

## The distinction that matters

You react to everything you encounter. You liked the film or you didn't. You recommend the restaurant or you don't. You scroll on or you stay. That is reaction.

A review is what happens when you take that reaction and make it visible, defensible, and useful to someone else.

The defensibility is the center of it. A reaction does not have to be defensible — you can feel however you feel, and no one can tell you you're wrong. A review claims its judgment is *better-supported than the opposite judgment*. That is a different kind of claim, and it is what the writer is on the hook for. You are not just reporting a feeling. You are making an argument that the feeling is justified, and you are making it to a reader who has not yet experienced the work and is trying to decide whether to.

Three things do the work. Let me be precise about each.

*Criteria.* A reaction says *I liked it*. A review says *here is what I was measuring it against, and here is how it measured up*. The criteria can be named explicitly or implied through the analysis. The stronger reviews tend to name them. Criteria specific enough to rule something out are real criteria. Criteria so generic they could apply to every work in a genre are not doing useful work.

*Evidence.* A reaction can be vague — *it just felt flat*. A review cites specifics: scenes, sentences, structural decisions the work made, performances that did or didn't work. The evidence is what proves the criteria were actually applied to this work and not to some imagined version of it.

*Judgment.* A reaction stops at the feeling. A review names the verdict — *this is worth your time*, *this is not*, *this is worth your time if you are the kind of reader who comes to this with a specific thing*. The verdict is supposed to be useful to a reader who hasn't encountered the work yet.

A review with criteria but no evidence is a thesis statement. A review with evidence but no criteria is a description. A review with both but no judgment is a literature paper. You need all three doing work together.

*Figure 9.1 — The three moves of a review*

To make this concrete, compare two short attempts to review a restaurant. The first: *Service was slow. Food was okay. Three stars.* The second: *We arrived at 7:45 on a Wednesday and three tables ahead of us hadn't been bussed. Our appetizers took thirty-five minutes. The bread was good. The pasta was overcooked in a way that suggested it had been held under a heat lamp. The standard I'm applying here is weeknight service consistency and whether the kitchen can execute its own menu — and on those standards, this was a structural failure, not a single bad night.* The first is a reaction. The second is a review. The second one a reader can actually use.

| Element | Reaction | Review |
|---|---|---|
| Criteria | Absent or vague — *it just didn't work for me* | Named, drawn from the work's genre — *weeknight service consistency*, *the horror film's pacing of dread and revelation* |
| Evidence | Generic impressions — *the food was okay*, *it dragged* | Specifics from the encounter — the 7:45 arrival, the thirty-five-minute appetizer, the overcooked pasta |
| Judgment | A feeling, reported — *three stars*, *I liked it* | A verdict a reader can act on — *structural failure, not a single bad night* |
| Usefulness to an undecided reader | None — the reader still has to guess | High — the reader can predict what they will encounter and decide |

## Why the genre exists

The review as a form is not new. Pierre Bayle and the seventeenth-century journals of the Republic of Letters were reviewing books for an emerging European reading public. Newspaper criticism reached something like its modern shape in the nineteenth century. Film criticism followed once there were films.

What changed in the last twenty-five years is not the genre. It is who gets to practice it. Once, a small number of writers — Kakutani at the *Times*, Pauline Kael at the *New Yorker*, Roger Ebert at the *Chicago Sun-Times* — produced most of the reviews most people read. Now anyone with an account can post to Yelp, Goodreads, Letterboxd, Rotten Tomatoes, Amazon. The distribution of the reviewer exploded.

This is mostly good. More voices is better than fewer. The downside is that most user-generated reviews are weaker on the three moves I named — criteria, evidence, judgment — than the professional tradition was. A typical Yelp review is almost entirely reaction. The assignment this chapter is building toward asks you to write in the older tradition: not because it is more prestigious, but because those moves are what make a review useful to someone who was not you, experiencing the thing you experienced, on the specific night you experienced it.

## The decision before drafting

Two things to decide before you write anything, and both are harder than they look.

The first is the work itself. Pick something you can engage with *closely*. Closely means: you can describe a specific scene, quote a specific sentence, name the specific structural choice in the third episode. If you cannot do those things, you have not been close enough. Watch the film again. Reread the chapter. Listen to the episode twice. The evidence has to come from your actual encounter with the work, not from your memory of having been in its general vicinity.

A sub-decision that students rarely think about explicitly: picking something you loved is fine. Picking something you hated is also fine. The most interesting reviews tend to come from mixed feelings — a film that succeeded in some ways and failed in others, a book whose first half worked and second half didn't. The mixed work forces finer distinctions. The all-good or all-bad work tempts you toward generalization, and generalization is where reviews go to die.

The second decision is the criteria. Every work belongs to a genre, and every genre has conventions readers expect the work to meet, exceed, or violate deliberately. Your criteria come from the genre.

Here is the diagnostic. Complete this sentence before you draft anything:

*The criteria I am using to evaluate this work are [name them], and these criteria apply because the work belongs to [name the genre or tradition].*

If the sentence doesn't complete concretely, you have not yet decided what you are measuring. Do not draft until it does.

The weak versions sound like: *I am evaluating whether it was good*, or *I am evaluating whether it was interesting*. Those are not criteria — they are descriptions of having criteria, without having them. *Did the horror film's pacing execute the genre's promised tension between dread and revelation?* is a criterion. *Did the novel earn its late shift in narrative perspective by establishing, earlier, whose interiority the reader had access to?* is a criterion. *Did the documentary podcast's reliance on a personal narrator work as a structural argument, or was it a substitute for archival evidence the reporter didn't have?* is a criterion. Notice what those have in common: each one is specific enough that a work could fail it. A criterion that no work could fail is not a criterion.

## The Kakutani move

The most useful single piece of craft advice for this genre — and I want to give it to you as a specific technique, not a vague aspiration — is this: ask, of the work, what it seemed to be *trying to do*, and then evaluate whether it succeeded on *those* terms. Not your preferred terms. Not the terms of the genre you wish the work had been in. The work's own terms.

That move rules out one of the most common review failures: criticizing a romantic comedy for not being a war drama, criticizing a horror novel for not being literary fiction, criticizing a podcast about cooking for not addressing systemic problems in agriculture. All three of those are real failure modes in published criticism, and they all come from the same error: the reviewer's preference has been substituted for the work's intent.

The discipline is to first establish, as a factual matter, what the work was going for — from genre conventions, from the work's own internal signals, from authorial statements where they are relevant — and then to measure execution against that.

The move has a limit, and the limit is worth naming clearly. Some works have aims you can legitimately object to, independent of whether they achieved them. A film that is competently made but traffics in racist stereotypes is competently made *and* doing harm. A novel that achieves its propagandistic effect efficiently is achieving propaganda efficiently. The Kakutani move does not require you to find that morally neutral. It just requires that the two judgments — execution and intent — be kept separate, so that you are not calling the work badly made when what you actually mean is that you disagree with its purpose.

In practice, you can hold both in the same review: *the work succeeded at what it set out to do, and what it set out to do is problematic for the following specific reasons.* Both of those claims can be supported with evidence. Conflating them — calling a work badly made because its purpose is objectionable — is one of the fastest ways to lose a reader who shares your values but notices the conflation. Keep the two analyses separate and let each stand on its own evidence.

## The two tonal failures

Most student reviews fall into one of two tonal traps, and both have the same cause.

The first is the enthusiastic positive. The writer loved the work; the prose tells you so repeatedly; the review is more effusive than evidentiary. The reader, sensing the writer has not done the analytical work, becomes skeptical even of judgments they might otherwise have shared.

The second is the cheap takedown. The writer hated the work; the prose is sarcastic and clever; the review is more entertaining than evidentiary. The reader, even one who also disliked the work, withdraws.

Both failures share a single cause: the writer trusted the feeling to do the argument's work. The feeling won't. The argument lives in the criteria and the evidence. The feeling is the engine; it is not the rendering.

The fix is to write toward a *skeptical* reader — not a hostile reader, but an undecided one. A reader who has not seen the film, has not read the book, is genuinely trying to decide whether to spend the time. That reader will not be persuaded by enthusiasm or sarcasm. They will be persuaded by specifics they can verify against their own future experience of the work. Write for that reader, not for the reader who already agrees with you.

A small observation about negative reviews. They are easier to write entertainingly than positive reviews. Skepticism comes equipped with linguistic resources — the sarcasm, the raised eyebrow, the apt quotation deployed to deflate — that praise does not. The result is that many writers default to negative pieces because they are more fun to produce. That is fine, occasionally. As a habit, it builds a reviewer who has lost the ability to write praise convincingly, which is half the job. The best critics — Kakutani, Roger Ebert, Pauline Kael, Wesley Morris — could do both, and did. Both require evidence and criteria. Both require the discipline to write toward the reader who hasn't decided yet.

## The LLM problem in this genre specifically

The review is the genre where a language model is most likely to produce something that *reads like a review* without doing review work, and the reason is structural.

A model has been trained on millions of reviews. It has internalized their surface features with high fidelity. Ask it to write a review of a film and it will produce prose with all the genre conventions: the opening hook, the brief summary, the passage of analysis, the verdict. It will look professional. It will, almost always, lack two things.

The first is engagement with the specific work. The model has not watched the film this week. It has processed other reviews of the film, and its output averages those reviews into something coherent. If the prior consensus was wrong — and consensus often is — the model's review will be wrong in the same direction, confidently.

The second is criteria specific to the work. The model knows the vocabulary of criticism but reaches for generic evaluative language when specific criteria would do more work. *The film's handling of inherited trauma* becomes *the film's emotional depth*. The specific version was doing real analytical work. The general version is decoration.

Where the model is genuinely useful:

After you have a draft, ask it to identify which of your claims are not supported by specific evidence in the draft. Models are accurate at spotting *I liked* sentences without a *because*, and they will identify which arguments are floating without support.

After you have your criteria, ask it to argue against them — *here are the standards I am using; what would a reviewer using different standards say?* The pushback exposes the assumptions underneath your criteria, which makes your evidence more precise.

After your verdict, ask it to construct the strongest case for the opposite verdict. *Make the strongest argument that this film is overrated, even if I disagree.* This sharpens your own argument, especially if you push past the model's tendency to soften the opposition. When it softens: *Don't balance it. Make the strongest case for the opposite verdict. Pretend you genuinely hold that view.*

Where the model does damage:

Any part of the review where the evidence is supposed to be your own specific encounter with the work. The model has not had that encounter. What it produces in that position is a composite of other people's encounters, averaged. That is not criticism. It is a simulation of criticism.

Quote generation. The model will sometimes produce plausible-sounding quotations from a book or film you have described. The fabrication risk is high. If you are using quoted material, go back to the source and get the actual quote.

| Task | Verdict | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Identify unsupported claims in your draft | Use it | Models are accurate at spotting *I liked* sentences without a *because*. They flag the floating claims you stopped seeing. |
| Argue against your criteria | Use it | Pushback on your standards exposes the assumptions underneath them, which makes your evidence more precise. |
| Construct the strongest opposite verdict | Use it | Sharpens your own argument — push past the model's tendency to soften the opposition. |
| Revise prose toward more specific criteria | Use it, cautiously | The model can flag generic evaluative language; you still decide what the specific criterion is. |
| Draft any part of the review | Avoid | The evidence has to come from your encounter with the work. The model's draft averages other people's encounters. |
| Generate quotes from a work you've read | Avoid | Fabrication risk is high. Go back to the source for any quoted material. |

## What would change my mind

If careful studies showed that reviews structured around explicit criteria were rated by readers as less useful than reviews organized around the reviewer's voice and impressions — that naming the criteria killed the prose, distanced the reader, made the verdict feel mechanical rather than earned — I would revise the central emphasis of this chapter. The evidence from working criticism points the other way: the critics who lasted, and whose work readers returned to years later, tended to make their criteria visible, even when they did so with a light hand. Ebert's criteria for what a film was trying to do were legible across decades of his writing. Morris's criteria for how a work handles race and genre are visible in the structure of every review. Kael's criteria, eccentric and original, are what made her rereadable. But my claim could be wrong, and I want to name where it could be tested.

## LLM Exercise — Chapter 9

**What you're building:** a draft of your review's analytical scaffold — criteria, evidence list, verdict — before you write the prose.

**Tool:** [Claude.ai](https://claude.ai/) chat. A Claude Project is recommended if you want to keep your notes on the work in one place across drafting and revision.

**The prompt:**

```
I'm writing a 1,200–1,500 word review of [name the work — film, book,
podcast, TV series, play, or game] for a college writing class.

I have engaged closely with the work. Below I'm pasting my raw notes
on what I noticed.

I am NOT asking you to write the review. I am asking you to help me
build its analytical scaffold.

Do the following in order, one step at a time, waiting for my
response between steps.

1. Based on my notes, what criteria do I seem to be using to evaluate
   this work — even if I haven't named them explicitly? List them.
   For each, name the genre or tradition the criterion comes from.

2. For each criterion you just listed, identify the strongest piece
   of evidence in my notes that supports a positive judgment on that
   criterion, and the strongest piece of evidence that supports a
   negative judgment. If I don't have evidence for one direction,
   say so — that means I need to engage with the work more closely
   before drafting.

3. What is the work trying to do? Describe it in two sentences,
   based on my notes and on the work's apparent genre. (This is the
   Kakutani move: evaluate against intent before evaluating against
   what you wish the work had been.)

4. Construct the strongest case AGAINST my emerging verdict — the
   review someone with opposite taste would write, using my own
   evidence where they could.

Do not draft the review. Do not write the opening. The drafting is
mine.

Here are my notes:
[paste your notes]
```

**What this produces:** a clean set of named criteria, an evidence inventory organized by criterion, a statement of what the work is trying to do, and a counter-review you can argue with. After this, you draft the review yourself.

**How to adapt this prompt:**

- *For ChatGPT or Gemini:* works as written. Both will sometimes try to draft the review after step 3. Push back.
- *For Cowork:* useful if your notes on the work are extensive. Keep them in a file Cowork can read alongside the prompt.
- *For Claude Code:* not the right tool.

**The instructive failure to expect:** when you ask the model to construct the opposite case, it will often soften the opposition to seem balanced. Push: *"Don't balance it. Make the strongest case for the opposite verdict. Pretend you genuinely hold that view."* The strong version is the one worth thinking through.

##  AI Wayback Machine
The ideas in this chapter didn't appear from nowhere. **Wesley Morris** was working on criticism that argues for itself — Pulitzer-winning reviews of film, music, and culture that make the criteria visible on the page rather than hidden behind taste — decades before most people had heard of evaluation or review as a teachable genre.

**Run this:**

```
Who was Wesley Morris, and how does their work on evidence-based criticism
in film and culture writing connect to writing a review that grounds
judgment in evidence? Keep it to three paragraphs. End with the single
most surprising thing about their career or ideas.
```

Then search **"Wesley Morris"** on Wikipedia. See what the model got right, got wrong, or left out.

**Now make the prompt better.** Try one of these:

- Ask it to break down the structure of a specific Wesley Morris review — pick one from his *Still Processing* podcast era or his recent *NYT Magazine* essays — paragraph by paragraph.
- Ask: "What does Morris do when he wants to argue that a popular work is bad — and what does he refuse to do?"
- Add the constraint: "Answer using one Morris-style sentence — long, accumulating, syntactically alive — that would actually appear in a Morris review."

What changes? What gets better? What gets worse?
