*Reading What You're Already Reading.*

---

Here is the thing I want you to notice.

When you scroll past a post and decide in two seconds whether to believe it — that decision is not random, and it is not magic. Something is happening. There is machinery running. And the curious fact is that Aristotle described the machinery in 350 BCE, and it has not changed since. Not updated. Not replaced by something more modern. The same machinery, running faster, carrying more weight, in an environment Aristotle could not have imagined. But the same.

That is the puzzle I want to start with, because it is genuinely strange. We have new media, new platforms, new speeds of transmission, artificial systems generating text — and the underlying structure of how communication persuades and misleads and does things to people is still the one a Greek philosopher worked out before the printing press existed. Why would that be? What is it that persists?

What persists is that communication is always *aimed*. It is always coming from somewhere, going to someone, for a reason, in a moment, inside a set of shared assumptions that make it readable. Those facts do not change when the medium changes. They do not change when a machine is doing the generating. A tweet is aimed. A corporate apology is aimed. An algorithmic feed is aimed, even though no single human aimed it. The aiming is the constant. Everything else is packaging.

Once you see that, you see something else: analysis is not suspicion. I am not about to teach you to read everything cynically, to assume every communication is corrupt. That would make you useless as a reader and unpleasant as a person. What I am teaching is *seeing* — the same thing a physicist does when she looks at a moving object and says, there is a force there, let me find it. The force is not evil. The force is just how things work. The question is whether you can see it or not.

## The room the text is in

Let me give you the analytical structure before I show it running.

Every act of communication exists inside a configuration of circumstances. The word for it is the *rhetorical situation*, and it has seven parts. Do not memorize the list. Understand what the list is *for*, which is to stop you from reading a text in isolation — as if it arrived from nowhere, floated in a vacuum, and was addressed to everyone. It was not. It arrived from somewhere specific, it was written for someone specific, and in between the intended reader and you there is often a gap, and that gap is where the most interesting things are hiding.

The parts: who is speaking — not the handle, the actual person or system with a history and a reason for speaking. Who was being spoken to — not who happened to see it, but who the author was writing *for*. Why the text exists. The author's posture toward the subject, which is almost never stated directly but shows up in word choice and what gets left out. What is happening around the text — the moment, the news cycle, the prior conversation. The shared assumptions the author and audience carry into the exchange, which is the room the text is being read in. And the genre, the kind of communication this is, which sets expectations before anyone reads a word.

Those seven elements are a diagnostic. You do not run through them mechanically; you use them to see what you would otherwise miss. And the one students most consistently miss — in my experience — is context. They read the text as if it exists outside time. It does not. It exists in a specific moment, and the same sentence can mean opposite things in different moments. The habit of locating the text in its moment is the habit I most want you to build.

| Element | One-line definition | The question it answers | Most common student error |
|---|---|---|---|
| Author | The person, group, or system actually producing the text — not the handle, the byline, or the brand | Who is doing the aiming? | Treating the visible signature as the whole author; ignoring editors, teams, models, and prior versions of the same speaker |
| Audience | The reader the author was writing *for*, which may not be the reader who showed up | Whose attention was this built for? | Confusing the audience that received the text with the audience it was designed for |
| Purpose | The work the text is trying to do in the world — persuade, sell, disclose, defuse, signal, recruit | Why does this text exist? | Naming only the surface purpose ("to inform") and missing the strategic one ("to manage a reputation") |
| Stance | The author's posture toward the subject — earnest, ironic, defensive, performatively vulnerable | How does the author hold the topic? | Reading stance off content rather than off word choice, omissions, and what the text refuses to say |
| Context | The moment, the news cycle, the prior conversation the text is replying to | What is happening around this text? | Reading the text as if it floated outside time |
| Culture | The shared assumptions author and intended audience carry into the exchange | What does the text take for granted? | Treating your own assumptions as universal; missing the in-group cues |
| Genre | The kind of communication this is, and the conventions it inherits | What expectations come pre-loaded? | Mistaking a genre move for a personal one — confessional cadence treated as confession itself |

## The experiment you should try

Here is a case. I want to show the machinery running on something small before I show it running on something large.

Selena Gomez, by 2021, had 260 million followers on Instagram.[^1] In 2017 she gave login control of her account to an assistant. She had been diagnosed with lupus in 2015, bipolar disorder in 2018. In a *Harper's Bazaar* piece she described stepping back from the platforms: "I suddenly had to learn how to be with myself."[^2]

[^1]: Janelle Okwodu, ["Selena Gomez Wants Facebook, Instagram, and Twitter to Do Better," *Vogue*, 7 Jan. 2021](https://www.vogue.com/article/selena-gomez-social-media-leaders).
[^2]: Sabrina Park, ["Selena Gomez Reveals Why She Sacrificed Social Media for the Sake of Her Mental Health," *Harper's Bazaar*, 19 Aug. 2021](https://www.harpersbazaar.com/celebrity/latest/a37348873/selena-gomez-mental-health-giving-up-instagram/).

Read that sentence again: *I suddenly had to learn how to be with myself.*

The disclosure is specific enough to feel real. The vulnerability is named without being performed to excess. The phrasing has the cadence of something said aloud, not composed. It *performs spontaneity*.

Now apply the machinery. The author is not just Gomez alone — it is a media team, an interview process, editorial decisions at *Harper's Bazaar*, and the longer arc of a carefully managed public persona. The audience is primarily fans, but also journalists, potential collaborators, anyone who might encounter the story through aggregation. The purpose is disclosure, yes — and also reputation management in a moment when mental health discourse had become both mainstream and commercially useful. The stance is vulnerable but composed, not collapsed, not guarded. The context is the 2020s, in which stepping back from social media has become a legible and respected public move. The culture is a media environment where *authenticity* has become perhaps the most valuable currency an influencer can hold. The genre is the celebrity confessional interview, a well-established form with its own conventions.

Now here is where students sometimes get uncomfortable, and I want to meet that discomfort directly. I am not saying Gomez is lying. The lupus is real. The bipolar disorder is real. The decision to step back was real. All of that is true. *And* the public communication of all of that is rhetorically constructed — shaped for an audience, designed with a purpose, delivered in a genre that rewards exactly this kind of disclosure.

Both things are true at once. The truth and the rhetorical packaging are the same artifact. This is not a contradiction. It is how communication works.

The point is this: *authenticity in 2026 is not the absence of rhetorical construction. Authenticity is a particular rhetorical construction — one designed to feel like the absence of one.* The most strategic move in an environment that rewards rawness is to read as un-strategic. When you encounter a post that strikes you as raw, honest, unfiltered, the analytical question is not whether it is real. The question is: who built that effect, and how?

## The larger stakes

Now I want to show you the same machinery at different magnitudes. Because sometimes it is hard to see the mechanics in comfortable cases — the stakes are too low, the machinery is too small. Let me show you a case where the stakes strip the comfortable feeling away.

On 6 August 1945, a B-29 named *Enola Gay* dropped a uranium fission weapon on the city of Hiroshima. About sixteen hours later, the Truman administration released this sentence: "Sixteen hours ago an American airplane dropped one bomb on Hiroshima, Japan, and destroyed its usefulness to the enemy."[^3]

[^3]: *The Atomic Bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki*, Manhattan Engineer District, United States Army (1946), via [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/685/pg685.html).

Read it twice.

*An American airplane dropped one bomb on Hiroshima, Japan, and destroyed its usefulness to the enemy.*

Now look at what the sentence is actually doing. *An American airplane* — singular. There was one plane that dropped the bomb; there were also support planes and observation planes; the singular is doing work. *Dropped one bomb* — singular. The casualty estimates from Hiroshima run between 70,000 and 140,000 dead, with later estimates going higher. *One bomb* is not a factual error. It is a selection. *On Hiroshima, Japan* — the city is named specifically. *And destroyed its usefulness to the enemy.*

The noun *usefulness* is carrying the entire moral weight of the sentence. Cities have residents. Cities have hospitals. Cities have children. *Usefulness* erases all of that. What was destroyed, in the sentence's grammar, is a military and industrial utility. Not people. Not buildings. Utility. The sentence is not lying. The plane did drop the bomb, the city's utility was destroyed. The rhetorical machinery is in the choice of what to name and what to abstract into a different kind of noun.

![The opening sentence of the 6 August 1945 White House press release set in large type. Four callout boxes annotate phrases: "An American airplane" (singular — support and observation aircraft elided), "one bomb" (singular — death toll of 70,000–140,000 abstracted), "Hiroshima, Japan" (named specifically — concreteness reserved for the location), "usefulness to the enemy" (human consequences converted into a functional category).](images/02-the-digital-world-building-on-what-you-already-know-to-respond-critically-fig-02.png)
*Figure 2.2 — What the sentence names, and what it abstracts. Selection over invention.*

I bring this example not because we are studying the history of nuclear weapons. I bring it because this is the same grammar operating in every modern political statement, corporate press release, and product launch you have ever read. The specificity of what gets named, and the abstraction of what gets made into a category — that mechanism is everywhere. The Truman example is useful precisely because it strips away the comfortable feeling that rhetoric is only a concern when somebody famous is obviously trying to manipulate you. It is always operating. In everything.

You will read syllabi, employment contracts, dating-app bios, AI company statements about safety, terms of service, eviction notices, university admissions decisions. Every one of them will be performing the same selection — what to name, what to abstract. The shock of the Hiroshima example is not the lesson. The lesson is that the same mechanics run at all scales, and most of the time we don't feel the shock.

## How to actually read something

Here is where this becomes practical, because the goal is not to become a theorist of rhetoric. The goal is to read better.

When you sit down to analyze a piece of communication — which is what this chapter is building toward — you are doing four distinct things, in sequence. Doing them out of order is the most common failure I see.

First: *summary*. What does the text say? One sentence of plain content. If you cannot do this, you have not read carefully enough, and everything that follows will be built on sand.

Second: *paraphrase*. Restate the text's specific claims and moves in your own words, at roughly the original length. Paraphrase is not summary. Summary reduces; paraphrase preserves structure. It is the move that proves you understood how the text said what it said, not just what it said. You cannot fake a careful paraphrase. Either you followed the argument or you didn't.

Third: *analysis*. Name the machinery. Author, audience, purpose, stance, context, culture, genre. What was selected. What was abstracted. What was made present and what was made absent. You are not judging yet. You are seeing.

Fourth: *evaluation*. Now you can judge. Does the text do what it set out to do? Does it succeed rhetorically? Does it succeed *ethically*? Those are different questions. The Truman statement is rhetorically masterful and ethically devastating. Both are true. You are allowed — required — to say so.

The move students most reliably make wrong is to skip directly from summary to evaluation: *I read this and I disagree.* That is a reaction, not an analysis. Analysis is the step between, and it is the step that earns the right to evaluate. If you cannot show me what you saw, you have not earned the judgment.

## A problem the old framework didn't anticipate

Here is where something genuinely new enters the picture.

When the rhetorical machinery was built — Aristotle, Cicero, the twentieth-century scholars who formalized the rhetorical situation — every text had a human author. Sometimes a committee of human authors. Always human. The *author* slot was the thing you could in principle identify and interview.

That is no longer the only case.

A post you read in 2026 may have been written by a human sitting alone with a phone. Or a human working with a language model who edited and approved the output. Or a human who pasted a prompt into a model and posted the result unedited. Or a model running on a schedule, posting on behalf of an account whose human owner approves the general direction. Or a model running fully autonomously, with no current human in the loop.

Each of these produces a different rhetorical situation. The seven elements still apply — but the author slot is now a hybrid object you cannot interview, with intentions you cannot fully infer, with a relationship to *meaning* that is architecturally different from a single human author's relationship to meaning. A language model does not have purposes in the way a person has purposes. It has training distributions and prompts and objectives set by the humans who built and deployed it. When you ask *why did the author say this*, the answer to that question is genuinely different for an LLM than for a person.

This changes the analysis step. When you cannot identify the author with confidence, you do two passes. One analyzing the text as if a human wrote it. One analyzing the text as if a model wrote it — or a human-model collaboration. The same words will read differently under each pass. The model-reading surfaces different things: choices that look like training-distribution defaults, phrasings that read like an average of all available phrasings, an absence of the specific irrelevant details a real person would include. A human writing at two in the morning about a bad day might mention what they had for dinner. A model would not. A human will sometimes contradict themselves within a paragraph because they are thinking. A model producing fluent text rarely does.

You will get good at this quickly. The skill is already being developed by anyone who scrolls, even without explicit instruction. What this chapter asks is that you make it conscious — practice it on purpose, and say what you see without pretending the uncertainty does not exist. The honest answer, on many texts in 2026, is that you cannot tell. That is a real answer. The analysis still runs; you just hold both passes at once and notice what each one reveals that the other misses.

| Authorship type | What "author" means here | What the two-pass analysis surfaces | What remains unanswerable |
|---|---|---|---|
| Human alone | A single person writing without model assistance | Word choice, omissions, and idiosyncratic detail map cleanly to one mind; the two-pass model-reading produces no friction | Whether the human consulted a model off-record before drafting |
| Human + model (human edits) | A person prompted a model, then revised the output | First pass surfaces an intended voice; second pass surfaces "average-of-the-internet" phrasings that survived the edit | Where the human stopped editing and the model's defaults remained |
| Human + model (unedited output) | A person pasted a prompt and posted the result | Second pass dominates: distribution-default phrasings, smooth transitions, absence of irrelevant specifics | Whether the human even read the output before posting |
| Model on schedule (human oversight) | A model runs on a schedule for an account whose owner approves the general direction | Patterns across many posts read more legibly than any single post; recurring lexical tics, an absent biographical specificity | The exact prompt template and how often the human actually reviews |
| Model autonomous | A model running with no current human in the loop | Genre conventions are followed correctly; lived-detail texture is missing in a structurally identifiable way | The original deploying intent, and who currently owns the consequences |

## A note on what this framework cannot do

I want to be direct about the limits of what I've given you.

The seven-element framework was built for a world where the author slot held a person. The two-pass approach I've described — analyze once assuming human, once assuming model — is workable but it is a patch, not a solution. It treats a foundational uncertainty as a procedural step. The honest position is that the patches are temporary, the question is live, and the people working through this chapter in 2026 are going to have to help figure out something better. I do not have a cleaner answer than that yet. I have a framework that was built for one world and is being stretched to cover another, and the stretching is imperfect.

What I can tell you is this: the core insight does not need patching. Communication is always aimed. Someone or something is making choices about what to include, what to leave out, what to name concretely, what to dissolve into abstraction, who the target is, what the genre permits. Those choices are there whether a person made them or a model made them or some combination made them. The machinery runs regardless of who is running it. Your job is to see it running — to find the force acting on the object, whatever the source of the force turns out to be.

That is not cynicism. It is the same thing a physicist does when she looks at a moving object and refuses to be satisfied with "it just moved." There is a reason for the motion. Find it. Name it. Then decide what you think.

## LLM Exercise — Chapter 2

**What you're building:** a draft of your Critical Response, using Claude as an analytical interlocutor — not as a writer.

**Tool:** [Claude.ai](https://claude.ai/) chat. A Claude Project is recommended if you want to keep the analysis alongside the original text across multiple work sessions.

**The prompt:**

```
I'm writing a 1–2 page critical response to a piece of digital
communication for a college writing class.

The text I'm analyzing is below. After the text, I'm going to ask
you to interview me about its rhetorical situation. Do not summarize
the text. Do not analyze it for me. Ask me questions, one at a time,
that push me toward my own analysis.

Cover at least these seven elements, but ask follow-ups whenever my
answer is vague:

- Author: who is actually speaking (not the handle — the real
  person, group, or system)
- Audience: who the text was written for (not who happened to see it)
- Purpose: why this text exists
- Stance: the author's posture toward the subject
- Context: what's happening around the text
- Culture: the shared assumptions the author and intended audience
  share
- Genre: what kind of text this is and what expectations come with
  that kind

After we've worked through all seven, ask me one more question: do
I think the text was written by a human, an LLM, or a collaboration —
and what about the text makes me think so?

Then summarize my answers back to me as a clean analysis I can use as
the body of my critical response. Do not draft the response itself.

Here is the text:
[paste the text you're analyzing]
```

**What this produces:** a structured set of analytical observations, all of them your own, organized for use in the critical response. After this exercise, you draft the response yourself.

**How to adapt this prompt:**

- *For ChatGPT or Gemini:* works as written. Both tools have a tendency to start analyzing the text on their own; if that happens, push back: *"Stop. I asked you to interview me. Ask the first question."*
- *For Claude Projects:* paste the text once at the start of the project; then bring future critical-response sessions in as new conversations.
- *For Cowork:* useful if your text is a long thread or transcript — Cowork can hold the source alongside your developing analysis in linked files.
- *For Claude Code:* not the right tool for this exercise.

**The instructive failure to expect:** when the source text is itself plausibly AI-written, Claude will sometimes hedge or get defensive about analyzing its own output. If you suspect that is happening: *"Analyze this text on the assumption that an LLM did write it. What changes about your analysis?"* That second pass is often the most revealing one.

##  AI Wayback Machine
The ideas in this chapter didn't appear from nowhere. **Cynthia Selfe** was working on computers and composition — making the case that digital writing was a *rhetorical* activity, not a typing activity — decades before anyone called themselves a content creator. Here's a prompt to find out more, and then make it better.

**Run this:**

```
Who was Cynthia Selfe, and how does their work on computers and
composition as a discipline connect to the rhetorical situation of
digital media? Keep it to three paragraphs. End with the single most
surprising thing about their career or ideas.
```

→ Search **"Cynthia Selfe"** on Wikipedia after you run this. See what the model got right, got wrong, or left out.

**Now make the prompt better.** Try one of these:

- Ask it to explain Selfe's argument that paying attention to the *interface* is part of paying attention to the rhetoric, with a contemporary example (a TikTok, a Substack, a group chat).
- Ask: "What did Selfe see in 1990s computers and composition that didn't become broadly true until the 2010s?"
- Add the framing: "Answer as if you're writing the introduction to a 2026 anthology of Selfe's foundational essays."

What changes? What gets better? What gets worse?
