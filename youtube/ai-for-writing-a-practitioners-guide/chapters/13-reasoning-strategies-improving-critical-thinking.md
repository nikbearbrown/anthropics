Sometime in the fourth century BCE, in the city of Athens, Plato sat down to describe an imaginary cave.

Inside the cave are prisoners who have been chained from childhood, facing a wall. Behind them, somewhere they cannot see, there is a fire. Between the fire and the prisoners, people walk by carrying objects. The objects cast shadows on the wall the prisoners face. The prisoners have named the shadows. They have become expert at predicting their movements. They take them for reality.

One prisoner is released. He turns. He sees the fire. He sees the objects casting the shadows. He is brought out of the cave entirely, into sunlight. The sun is painful. He realizes the world he inhabited was a projection, not the thing itself.

He goes back to tell the others. They do not believe him.[^1]

[^1]: Plato, *The Republic*, Book VII, "The Allegory of the Cave," trans. B. Jowett. Available via [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1497/1497-h/1497-h.htm#link2H_4_0010).

Now I want to point at something specific about what Plato is doing here, because it is easy to miss while you are following the story.

Plato is not writing fiction. He is making a philosophical argument about the nature of knowledge — about how the unexamined mind mistakes its representations for reality, and about what it costs to leave that comfortable confusion behind. The concept he is trying to convey is genuinely hard to think about directly. The distinction between appearances and forms, between the world as we encounter it and the world as it actually is — you cannot just state it. The reader needs to feel it.

So Plato finds a different situation that shares the relevant *structure* with the philosophical situation he wants you to understand. The prisoners are to the shadows as the unexamined mind is to its representations. The fire is to the real objects as the real objects are to their deeper nature. The man who escapes is to the others as the philosopher is to the multitude. The literal situations are completely unlike. The structure is identical. And because the cave is concrete and the epistemology is abstract, the cave carries the argument across.

That move — finding two situations that are structurally similar and using the familiar one to illuminate the unfamiliar one — is one of six reasoning strategies this chapter is going to take apart. You have used all six before, in your writing and in your thinking, without ever naming them. This chapter names them. Once you can name them, you can use them deliberately.

## What a reasoning strategy is

A reasoning strategy is a structural pattern for organizing evidence into an argument. It is not the evidence itself — the facts, the data, the examples. It is not the conclusion. It is the *shape* of the relationship between the evidence and the conclusion.

The distinction matters because most writing problems in argument are not evidence problems. You have the evidence. The question is how to organize it so the conclusion follows visibly. That is what the strategies do.

Here is a useful comparison: the reasoning strategies are to writing what structural joints are to carpentry. A mortise-and-tenon joint, a dovetail, a lap joint — each holds wood together in a specific way suited to specific stresses. A carpenter who knows only one joint can build some things. A carpenter who knows six builds anything that needs to be built. The strategies are the joints. The argument is what you are building.

The six: analogy, cause and effect, classification and division, comparison and contrast, problem and solution, and definition. Real arguments rarely use only one. The strategies are tools in the same toolbox. You use the one — or the several — that fit the work you are trying to do.

## Analogy

The mechanism: you take a situation the reader already understands and map it onto a situation the reader does not yet understand, in order to transfer understanding from the first to the second.

The situations are unlike in most respects. That is not a problem. The analogy does not claim they are the same in most respects. It claims they share one specific structural relationship, and that the shared structure is the thing the reader needs to grasp.

Plato's cave works because the relationship between a prisoner and the shadow-world — familiarity, expertise, comfort, total dependence on something that is not the actual thing — is structurally identical to the relationship between an unexamined mind and its representations. Strip the literal details away and the two situations have the same shape. The shape is the argument.

When analogies work, they make genuinely difficult ideas tangible by giving the reader something concrete to hold. When they fail, they introduce confusion because the mapping does not hold — the reader follows the analogy into a corner where the two situations stop being structurally similar, and now the analogy has made the original concept harder to follow than if no analogy had been used.

Four points of discipline.

Pick an analog the reader already understands. The analog must be more familiar than the subject. If you explain a difficult concept by comparing it to something equally difficult, you have added a second difficult thing, not a clarification.

State the mapping specifically. Not *this is like that*. Say what the structural relationship is. "Just as prisoners mistake shadows for the objects that cast them, a mind that never examines its assumptions mistakes its representations for the world itself." The reader needs to see the specific correspondence you are claiming, not infer it.

Name where the analogy breaks down. Every analogy has limits. The careful writer names them. The careless writer extends the analogy past the point where it holds, and a hostile reader finds the over-extension and uses it to dismiss the entire argument. Naming the limits preempts that dismissal. It is also, incidentally, a signal to the reader that you know exactly what the analogy is doing and are not confused about whether the two situations are actually the same.

Stop the analogy when the mapping stops holding. The most common failure: a writer finds an analogy that works for one aspect of the argument and then forces every subsequent aspect through the same analogy, including aspects it was never designed to cover. The analogy becomes a trap. Plato does not make the mistake of then explaining every philosophical concept by extending the cave. The cave does its specific job and stops.

## Cause and effect

Cause-and-effect reasoning identifies why something happened or what consequences follow from a particular event or condition. It is the most common strategy in policy writing, science writing, and journalism, because most of what those genres do involves explaining mechanisms.

In 2015, a pipeline ruptured near Refugio State Beach on the California coast and released over 100,000 gallons of crude oil into the Pacific. Researchers at UC Davis documented the consequences: coastal birds, sea lions, and elephant seals rescued and rehabilitated; many more uncounted; broader ecosystem effects lasting years.[^2] The cause-and-effect chain: a corroded section failed; oil spread along the coastline; wildlife was poisoned and oil-coated; animals died or were rescued; the rescued animals required months of rehabilitation; the ecosystem will take years to recover. Each item causes the next. Remove any link and either the chain breaks or the reader cannot follow it.

[^2]: Kat Kerlin, ["Wildlife Experience High Price of Oil," UC Davis, 2 June 2015](https://www.ucdavis.edu/news/wildlife-experience-high-price-oil/).

Three distinctions matter enormously in this strategy, and public discourse routinely collapses all three.

Correlation versus causation. Two things can happen together without one causing the other. Ice cream sales and drowning rates both rise in summer. The sun causes both, through separate pathways. A causal claim requires showing a mechanism, not just co-occurrence. "X happened and then Y happened" is not the same as "X caused Y," even when the timing is consistent and the correlation is strong.

Proximate versus distal cause. The proximate cause of the Refugio spill is the pipeline rupture. A distal cause is the regulatory regime that allowed an aging pipeline to remain uninspected. Both are real causes. Both matter for different arguments. A proximate cause explains what happened. A distal cause often explains why it was allowed to happen. A strong argument is explicit about which kind of cause it is invoking.

Necessary versus sufficient. A necessary cause is one without which the effect could not have occurred. A sufficient cause is one whose presence alone is enough to produce the effect. The pipeline corrosion was necessary — without it the pipeline does not rupture — but not sufficient, because a corroded pipeline that is monitored and repaired does not necessarily rupture. Many causes are necessary without being sufficient. Conflating them produces arguments that appear to demonstrate more than they do.

The writer who can specify which kind of cause they are invoking is doing reasoning that most of what you read in a newspaper is not doing. That specificity is not pedantry. It is the difference between an argument that can be checked and one that cannot.

| Distinction | What it is | Diagnostic question | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Correlation vs. causation** | Two things move together; one of them may or may not cause the other. | Can I name the mechanism by which X produces Y, or do I only have co-occurrence? | Ice-cream sales and drowning rates both rise in summer. The sun causes both, separately. Co-occurrence alone is not a causal claim. |
| **Proximate vs. distal** | The immediate cause versus the upstream condition that allowed the immediate cause to operate. | Am I asking what happened, or why it was allowed to happen? | At Refugio in 2015, corrosion (proximate) ruptured the pipeline; a regulatory regime that left aging pipelines uninspected (distal) let the corrosion go unchecked. |
| **Necessary vs. sufficient** | A necessary cause must be present for the effect; a sufficient cause is enough on its own to produce it. | If I removed X, would Y still happen? If I had only X, would Y happen anyway? | Pipeline corrosion was necessary for the Refugio rupture, but not sufficient — a corroded pipeline that is monitored and repaired does not necessarily rupture. |

*Specifying which kind of cause you are claiming is part of the argument.*

## Classification and division

Classification groups disparate things into a larger category. Division breaks a single thing into parts. They are inverse moves that share the same underlying logic: both are claims about which relationships matter.

Classification is what this chapter does when it groups six distinct reasoning patterns into one category called reasoning strategies. Division is what an anatomy textbook does when it breaks the body into systems, or what a project plan does when it breaks a project into phases.

The thing easy to miss: the categories are not given by nature. They are choices. And the choices are the argument.

The biological taxonomy — Kingdom, Phylum, Class, Order, Family, Genus, Species — reveals certain relationships among living things and obscures others. A different classification organized around ecological role rather than morphology would reveal different relationships. Neither is wrong. They are useful for different purposes. But whichever you use, the classification is a claim about which relationships are worth seeing.

The test for any classification or division you build: does this grouping produce insight I could not have produced by treating the items separately, or by grouping them differently? If yes, the strategy is doing real work. If no, you are organizing material without arguing anything, and organization without insight is not argument.

## Comparison and contrast

Comparison examines similarities. Contrast examines differences. Together they produce insight that neither subject examined alone would generate, because seeing what is alike and what is different reveals something about both subjects that looking at either one independently would not show.

The strategy is most powerful when the two subjects share a category — similar enough that the contrasts are meaningful — but differ within it in ways that illuminate what each is doing.

Consider Shakespeare's *Romeo and Juliet* alongside *Macbeth*. Both are tragedies. Both end in the death of the central characters. Both engage themes of fate and human weakness. The similarities establish that comparison is legitimate — they belong in the same conversation. The contrasts are where the insight lives. *Romeo and Juliet*'s tragedy is driven by external forces: family feud, plague, a letter that doesn't arrive, a chain of misunderstandings. *Macbeth*'s tragedy is driven internally, by the protagonist's own ambition and moral collapse. Same genre, same ending, completely different mechanism. Putting them side by side makes that difference visible in a way that examining either play alone would not.

One practical point about architecture. There are two ways to organize a comparison: point-by-point, which alternates between the two subjects for each feature; or subject-by-subject, which fully describes one subject before fully describing the other. The choice is architectural, not cosmetic. Subject-by-subject works better when the reader needs full context on each subject before the contrasts can land. Point-by-point works better for shorter comparisons where the reader can hold both subjects in mind simultaneously. The choice changes what the reader holds in their head while reading, which changes what the comparison feels like when it lands.

## Problem and solution

Problem-and-solution is the structure the proposal genre runs on, but it appears far beyond proposals. Opinion writing, analytical reports, scientific papers, policy documents — wherever someone is arguing that a difficulty exists and that something should be done about it.

The move has four beats: name the difficulty, explain its causes and consequences, propose a response, evaluate whether the response actually addresses the difficulty that was named.

The strategy's main failure mode is the most common one in all of policy writing: rushing to the solution before the problem has been adequately specified. A solution to an under-specified problem is a solution to nothing — it may address a symptom rather than a cause, or it may address the wrong version of the problem entirely.

The practical discipline is simple. Before writing the solution, write one paragraph that specifies the problem precisely enough that the solution can be evaluated against it. What is the problem? Who does it affect, and how? What causes it? What would a successful solution look like — what would change that is currently not changing? Once you can answer those questions, you know what the solution has to do. The solution then earns or does not earn the work it claims to be doing. If you cannot specify the problem that precisely, the solution section will be doing work you cannot verify.

## Definition

The definition strategy specifies what a contested term means. Most consequential arguments rest on terms whose meanings are not settled, and the argument is often won or lost at the level of the term.

Consider *patriotism*. The dictionary gives you *love of one's country* — the denotation, the literal meaning. The connotation, the political and emotional freight the word carries, varies enormously by speaker and context. Some use it to mean support for existing leadership in times of crisis. Some mean active participation in civic institutions. Some mean specifically *critical* engagement — the claim that loving a country means demanding better of it. These are not the same definition. They lead to different arguments. They authorize different actions. A writer who uses *patriotism* without specifying which sense they mean is writing an argument that will be evaluated differently by every reader, depending on which sense they bring to it. No single reader will encounter the argument the writer intended to make.

Four moves for a strong definition argument.

Distinguish denotation from connotation. Name the literal meaning; then acknowledge that the word carries contested freight. The reader needs to know you know the term is contested.

Specify your working definition. *In this essay, I am using "patriotism" to mean...* The reader now knows what you mean. They may disagree with the definition — that is fine — but they cannot misunderstand it.

Defend the working definition. Why is your definition the right one for this argument? What does it allow you to see that other definitions would obscure? This is not a matter of choosing a definition arbitrarily and declaring it the one true meaning. It is a matter of explaining why this particular specification serves the argument.

Handle competing definitions fairly. Other definitions exist and have their own logic. The strongest writers acknowledge the alternatives and explain why their working definition is more useful for the particular argument at hand. The reader who holds a different definition does not feel dismissed; they feel engaged.

## The strategies in combination

The strategies are most powerful when used together, each doing different structural work. A single sustained argument might use all six.

Here is a complete example. Suppose you are arguing that high schools should require a course in AI literacy.

Open with an analogy: just as twentieth-century high schools required driver's education because cars were becoming pervasive and dangerous to operate without instruction, there may be a case for requiring AI literacy instruction because language models are becoming similarly pervasive in the spaces where students work and think. The analogy does not prove the conclusion. It frames the kind of argument this is — a case from precedent — and lets the reader feel the structural situation before any data is presented.

Use definition to specify what AI literacy means for this argument — not how to write a prompt, but how to evaluate AI-generated output for accuracy, bias, and appropriate use. Without this, the debate about whether to require the course is happening among people who mean different things by the term.

Use cause and effect to explain what is currently happening in the absence of such instruction: rising rates of students relying on AI-generated content without evaluating it, with downstream effects on the skills students are actually building.

Use comparison to draw on what is already known from other required courses — driver's education, civics — about what works and what does not when schools require instruction on something contested.

Use problem and solution as the overall frame: here is the problem, specified precisely; here is the proposed response; here is why the response addresses that problem rather than some adjacent one.

Use classification to organize what the course would contain: three categories of AI literacy, each requiring different instruction.

That is six strategies in one argument. None of them decorative. Each doing specific structural work the others are not doing. The analogy sets the frame. The definition prevents equivocation. The cause-and-effect establishes the need. The comparison supplies historical evidence. The problem-and-solution governs the overall architecture. The classification organizes the proposal. Remove any one and the argument is noticeably weaker.

The Plato cave with which we opened was already doing this. It was an analogy serving a definition argument. The analogy carried a definition of *genuine knowledge* that could not easily be stated without it. Both strategies, one move.

Now go back to the last thing you wrote. The strategies are already there. This chapter is asking you to see them.

## A note about the model

The reasoning strategies are the dimension of writing where the model is most likely to simulate the work without doing it.

Where it genuinely helps: identifying which strategies a draft uses. Paste a draft and ask the model to identify every instance of each strategy and quote the specific passage. It is mechanically good at this, and the inventory helps you see what you have and what you have not. Ask it to stress-test an analogy — at what point does the mapping between subject and analog stop holding? The model often catches the failure modes you have not noticed because you are too close to the analogy. Ask it to find where a cause-and-effect chain treats correlation as causation, or conflates necessary and sufficient causes. It is reasonably good at flagging soft spots.

Where it does damage: ask it to improve the reasoning and it produces prose that looks more reasoned, often by adding qualifications and hedges that flatten the argument's specific commitments. Ask it to generate analogies and it produces analogies in abundance, most of them generic. The model's analogies feel borrowed because they are — they come from the statistical shape of analogical writing, not from one person's specific knowledge of two domains. Ask it to validate a causal chain and it will sometimes confidently endorse a chain that is actually flawed, particularly when the flaw is in the middle of a long argument.

The rule that covers all of these: use the model to interrogate your reasoning, not to produce it or approve it. The interrogation is genuinely useful. The production and the approval are not.

## LLM Exercise — Chapter 13

**What you're building:** a reasoning-strategy inventory and audit of a draft you have already written, so you can see your own reasoning patterns and choose more deliberately on the next draft.

**Tool:** [Claude.ai](https://claude.ai/) chat. A Claude Project is recommended.

**The prompt:**

```
I'm taking a college writing class. The class has just introduced six
reasoning strategies: analogy, cause and effect, classification and
division, comparison and contrast, problem and solution, and
definition.

Below I'm pasting a draft of a longer essay I've already written.

Do the following in order, one step at a time, waiting for my response
between steps.

1. Identify every instance in this draft of each of the six reasoning
   strategies. For each instance, quote the specific passage and
   name which strategy it is. Be specific — don't conflate adjacent
   strategies.

2. For each instance, evaluate whether the strategy is doing real
   work in the argument or whether it is filler. A strategy is doing
   real work if removing the passage would weaken the argument. A
   strategy is filler if the passage could be cut without loss.

3. Identify three places in the draft where I COULD have used a
   reasoning strategy and didn't — where an argument would have been
   stronger if I had explicitly used analogy, classification, etc.

4. Identify any reasoning failures in the draft: places where I
   treated correlation as causation, where I forced an analogy beyond
   its useful range, where I used a term without defining it, where
   I claimed necessity when I had only shown sufficiency.

Do not rewrite the draft. Do not produce a revised version. The
revision is mine to do.

Here is the draft:
[paste your draft]
```

**What this produces:** an inventory of the reasoning strategies you have used, a critique of which are doing real work, suggestions for where you could have used more, and a list of specific reasoning failures. After this, you revise the draft yourself.

**How to adapt this prompt:**

- *For ChatGPT or Gemini:* works as written.
- *For Cowork:* useful if the draft is long — tell Cowork to read the file before the conversation begins.
- *For Claude Code:* not the right tool.

**The instructive failure to expect:** the model is sometimes overconfident about identifying causal failures. It will flag a cause-and-effect passage as treating correlation as causation when in fact you have provided enough evidence elsewhere in the essay to support the causal claim. Read every flagged item carefully. Accept only the critiques that hold against your full argument, not just against the specific passage quoted.

##  AI Wayback Machine
The ideas in this chapter didn't appear from nowhere. **Stephen Toulmin** was working on *The Uses of Argument* — a model that broke real-world reasoning into claim, data, warrant, backing, qualifier, and rebuttal — decades before "critical thinking" became a curriculum goal, and decades before most people had heard of reasoning strategies as a teachable set.

**Run this:**

```
Who was Stephen Toulmin, and how does their work on the Toulmin model
of argument and applied reasoning connect to reasoning strategies for
everyday and academic argument? Keep it to three paragraphs. End with
the single most surprising thing about their career or ideas.
```

Then search **"Stephen Toulmin"** on Wikipedia. See what the model got right, got wrong, or left out.

**Now make the prompt better.** Try one of these:

- Ask it to walk through Toulmin's six elements — claim, data, warrant, backing, qualifier, rebuttal — on a real argument you've had recently.
- Ask: "Toulmin called formal logic 'a-temporal, a-personal, a-everything.' What did he think real reasoning needed that formal logic refused to provide?"
- Add the constraint: "Diagram the structure as a Toulmin schema — claim at top, data and warrant beneath, qualifier and rebuttal visible."

What changes? What gets better? What gets worse?
