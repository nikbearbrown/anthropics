Here is the first sentence of a short story written in 1898:

> The leaves were so still that even Bibi thought it was going to rain.[^1]

[^1]: Kate Chopin, "The Storm" (written 1898, published 1969 in *The Complete Works of Kate Chopin*). Available via [The Kate Chopin International Society](https://www.katechopin.org/the-storm/).

I want to stay with that sentence before we go anywhere else. Twenty words. Let me show you what is inside them.

A casual reader sees weather. A storm is coming. The sentence is preparatory and unremarkable.

A close reader sees something different.

The sentence opens in passive construction: *the leaves were*. Agency belongs to the leaves, which have no agency, because leaves are vegetable. From the first word, the construction signals that something is going to happen *to* the world of the story rather than *from* its human characters. The story has not named a single person yet and it has already made a claim about how cause works here.

The word *still* is doing specific work. It names a state of stopped motion. The expectation of motion is implicit — leaves move when wind moves them — and *still* foregrounds the absence, which is what you notice about something that is unexpectedly not happening. The opening word is about an absence that predicts what is coming.

Then: *even Bibi thought it was going to rain*. The word *even* signals that Bibi is someone whose perception is normally limited or unreliable — someone whose noticing is not the default evidence for anything. We do not yet know who Bibi is, but the construction has characterized the household: there is a child in it, the child's perception is limited, and the narrator respects it enough to invoke it. We have been inside the register of this household from the third word.

And then: *going to rain* is colloquial. Household speech, not a nineteenth-century narrator being formal. The narrator has slid into the household's register before we have been introduced to anyone in it.

All of that from twenty words. Not all of it is equally important to every reading. But none of it is accidental. Chopin is making choices, and the choices are doing work. The question that textual analysis asks is: *what work is this choice doing, and what does the work reveal?*

## What the story does

"The Storm" runs about 2,500 words. A married woman named Calixta has an extramarital encounter with a former lover while a thunderstorm keeps her husband and son sheltered at a nearby store. The story does not condemn her. The story does not punish her. The marriage continues intact afterward; she is more at peace, not less. Chopin wrote it in 1898 and it was not published until 1969, partly because its frank treatment of female desire made it unpublishable in her lifetime.

The story has been read in multiple ways: as feminist allegory, as a meditation on desire and social constraint, as a portrait of post-Reconstruction Louisiana, as a study in figurative language where the storm functions as a metaphor for the human encounter it makes possible. Each reading is defensible. Each is supported by specific evidence in the text.

What I want you to notice is how those readings were produced. Each one came from someone attending to the specific choices of the text — the language, the structure, the images — and asking what those choices add up to. The storm-as-metaphor reading comes from a reader who noticed that the storm's intensification and the encounter's intensification track each other, sentence by sentence. The social-constraint reading comes from a reader who noticed that the story's permissions and prohibitions are inscribed in the language used to describe them.

Different readers, attending to different things, producing different interpretations. All defensible. All working from the text outward, not from a thesis downward into the text.

That outward movement — from specific textual choices to an interpretive claim — is the mechanism of textual analysis.

## What close reading actually means

The phrase *close reading* is used loosely enough that I want to make it specific.

Close reading is the practice of attending to what a text does at the level of word, sentence, paragraph, and structure — not just at the level of plot, theme, or topic. It is attending to the *exact* choices a writer makes and asking what those choices are accomplishing.

The opposite is reading for plot or summary: attending to what happens and to whom, following the arc. This is necessary reading. But it is not the reading that produces textual analysis, because it does not attend to the choices that make this story what it is rather than something else.

Here is the difference demonstrated on the same text. A plot-level reading of the storm in "The Storm" says: there is a thunderstorm; the characters are separated by it; the encounter takes place during it. A close reading attends to this passage:

*The rain was coming down in sheets obscuring the view of far-off cabins and enveloping the distant wood in a gray mist. The playing of the lightning was incessant. A bolt struck a tall chinaberry tree at the edge of the field. It filled all visible space with a blinding light and the crash seemed to invade the very boards they stood upon.*

And then attends to the passage that follows immediately — the description of the encounter itself — and notices that the vocabulary is the same. *Sheets*. *Blinding*. *Invading*. Applied equally to the meteorological and the human. The storm is not a frame around the encounter. It *is* the encounter, in parallel language. Chopin has made the physical world and the human world the same event, occurring in the same sentences.

From that observation, an interpretive claim follows: the story is suggesting that this desire is as natural, as irresistible, and as amoral as weather. The storm has no opinion about itself. The story has no opinion about Calixta's desire. The parallel construction is the argument.

![Two-column parallel diagram of the storm passage and the encounter passage with shared vocabulary connected across columns](images/19-print-or-textual-analysis-what-you-read-fig-02.png)
*Figure 19.2 — The storm is not a frame for the encounter. It is the encounter, in parallel language.*

That is what close reading produces. An observation at the level of specific language choices, followed by an inference about what those choices are doing. The observation has to be real — it has to be in the text. The inference has to follow from the observation. The chain from word to claim is what textual analysis is made of.

## Why different readers find different things

Here is the question I have not yet answered. If the text is doing specific things, why do trained readers disagree about what it is doing?

The literary theorist Stanley Fish proposed an answer: *interpretive communities*. Readers are not neutral. Every reader brings to a text a set of assumptions, training, cultural background, prior reading, and political commitments that shape what they attend to. These are not individual quirks — they are shared by communities of readers who have been trained in the same frameworks, who are asking the same kinds of questions, who have been formed by the same conversations.

A feminist critic reading "The Storm" in 2024 will attend to different choices than a Catholic moral theologian reading it in 1898. Both are doing real reading. Both will find real things in the text. They are not finding the same things, because they are asking different questions.

Three implications for your own textual analysis.

First: your reading will be shaped by what you bring to the text. You should acknowledge that framing, at least implicitly. Not confessionally — you do not need to begin the essay with a paragraph about your background. But the essay should name the kind of question it is asking. *I am reading this as a text about social constraints on desire* is an implicit announcement of the interpretive community you are working from. It lets the reader know what kind of reading they are getting.

Second: other interpretive communities have read your text before you. The strongest textual analyses engage with those prior readings — not necessarily to refute them, but to locate your reading in relation to them. You are not reading in isolation. You are reading inside a conversation. The essay should show that you know what the conversation has already said.

Third: the discipline that keeps interpretation from becoming mere opinion is *specific textual evidence*. Your reading is a claim. The claim has to be supported. The support has to come from the text — from what the text actually does at the level of specific language. A reading that cannot be anchored in specific passages, with close-reading work on each passage, is an impression, not an analysis.

## What the thesis looks like

The thesis of a textual analysis is the interpretive claim your reading is going to defend. It is the answer to: *what is this text doing, and what does its doing reveal?*

Several kinds of claim are available, and they commit the writer to different kinds of evidence.

A *formal claim* attends to structural or stylistic choices. *Chopin's storm is not a frame around the affair but a mechanism for it — the meteorological and the human are doing the same work in the same passages.* This claim commits the writer to showing the parallel construction, sentence by sentence. The evidence is linguistic.

A *thematic claim* attends to what the text is about, argued through what it does. *"The Storm" is best read not as a story about adultery but as a story about the social structures that construct the category of adultery — and about what happens when those structures are temporarily suspended.* This requires showing specifically where the story's language encodes those structures. The evidence is still linguistic, but organized around a social argument.

A *historical claim* attends to what the text is doing in its specific moment and how the moment shapes the reading. *"The Storm" reads differently in 1898, in 1969, and in 2024, and the history of its reception is itself an analytical object.* This requires historical context and evidence of how the story was received or suppressed.

A *comparative claim* attends to how the text does what a tradition does, by setting it beside another text. *Chopin's treatment of female desire in "The Storm" differs from Edith Wharton's treatment in specific ways that illuminate what each writer is making possible.* This requires close reading of both texts.

All of these are legitimate. The choice depends on what the text and your reading actually support. The wrong choice is the thesis that sounds analytical but makes no specific claim: *Chopin uses the storm as a symbol for the emotions of the characters.* That sentence could be said about almost any story with weather in it. It commits the writer to no specific evidence, names no specific claim, and cannot be wrong. A thesis that cannot be wrong is not a thesis. It is a sentence shaped like one.

| Thesis type | What it attends to | Example thesis | Evidence commitment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Formal | Structural and stylistic choices — sentence shape, diction, repetition, parallel construction | Chopin's storm is not a frame around the affair but a mechanism for it — the meteorological and the human are doing the same work in the same passages. | Sentence-by-sentence demonstration of parallel construction; linguistic evidence. |
| Thematic | What the text is about, argued through what it does at the level of language | "The Storm" is best read not as a story about adultery but about the social structures that construct the category of adultery — and what happens when those structures are temporarily suspended. | Linguistic evidence organized around a social argument; passages where the structures are encoded. |
| Historical | The text in its specific moment, and how the moment shapes the reading and its reception | "The Storm" reads differently in 1898, in 1969, and in 2024 — and the history of its reception is itself an analytical object. | Historical context; evidence of how the story was received, suppressed, or recovered. |
| Comparative | How the text does what a tradition does, by being set beside another text | Chopin's treatment of female desire in "The Storm" differs from Edith Wharton's in specific ways that illuminate what each writer is making possible. | Close reading of both texts; specific moments where the treatments diverge. |
| *Empty thesis* (the kind to avoid) | Sounds analytical but makes no specific claim | *Chopin uses the storm as a symbol for the emotions of the characters.* | None. Could be said about almost any story with weather in it. Cannot be wrong, so it is not a thesis. |

*Table 19.1 — The thesis is a commitment. It determines what evidence you need and what you can leave out.*

## A mechanical point with real implications

When you write about a literary text, use the present tense. *Chopin's narrator observes*, not *Chopin's narrator observed*. The convention has a reason: the text continues to do what it does every time it is read. The story is, in a real sense, always happening on its pages. Past tense implies it stopped.

The convention applies even when the events are historical in setting. *In* Hamlet, *the prince hesitates*, not *the prince hesitated*. The action is in the text.

The exception is when you are discussing composition or publication history: *Chopin wrote "The Storm" in 1898; it was not published until 1969*. Here the past tense is correct because you are describing historical events with a before and after.

Working rule: past tense for what happened to the text in the world; present tense for what the text does on the page.

## How the analysis is structured

A textual analysis has five structural moments, and they are not interchangeable.

The introduction names the text, the author, and the date. It contextualizes the text briefly — what kind of work it is, what interpretive questions it has raised. It names the interpretive question you are pursuing and often ends with a tentative thesis. Note that *tentative* — the full thesis earns its confidence from the body. The introduction should not overclaim before the evidence is in.

The brief summary covers the text's basic content in a paragraph or two, enough that a reader who has not seen the text can follow the analysis. Keep it short. The summary is scaffolding. It is not the analysis.

The body is the close reading organized around your interpretive claim. Specific passages are quoted. The analysis works on each passage at the level of words and sentences. The close-reading work accumulates across sections, building the evidential foundation for the interpretation. This is where the work lives. An analysis whose body consists of summary and assertion rather than close reading of specific passages has not yet become a textual analysis.

The engagement with other readings names the critical conversation your essay is entering. Not exhaustively — the relevant question is: what would a reader doing a *different* kind of reading of this text see? Where would they agree? Where would the evidence pull them somewhere else? You locate your reading in relation to those others without needing to refute them all. The goal is to show that you know your reading is a reading, not the reading.

The conclusion restates the interpretation with the weight of the evidence behind it, and opens outward. What does this reading reveal about the text, the author's work, the genre, the cultural moment? The conclusion is not the thesis again. It is the thesis after the evidence has earned it.

## Choosing the text

For a 2,000 to 3,000 word analysis, you want a text short enough to engage closely and rich enough to repay sustained attention. A short story works well. A poem can work even better — poems are often short enough to close-read word by word. A literary essay, a memoir chapter, a speech: all workable.

The practical test for richness: can you find two or three passages in the text that are doing something more than what a surface reading sees? If yes, the text has enough interior complexity to sustain an analysis. If every sentence is doing exactly what it appears to be doing — if the text is transparent — there is not much for close reading to find.

The practical test for length: can you read the whole thing? For a short story or a poem, yes. For a novel, you are choosing a chapter or a section, not the whole. Name that choice and defend it. The defense should explain why this section is representative or structurally significant enough to bear the interpretive weight you are putting on it.

## Where the model cannot follow you

The textual analysis is the genre where the model is most likely to produce interpretation that looks like analysis and is not.

The reason is structural. The model has been trained on many readings of many canonical texts. When you ask it to analyze a text, it produces an average of those readings — a synthesis of what others have said, rendered in the vocabulary of close reading. The result has the grammar of analysis, the structure of an interpretive claim, the vocabulary of formalist criticism. It does not have the one thing that gives textual analysis its value: a reading produced by your specific encounter with the specific text.

Where the model is genuinely useful. After you have done your own close reading, paste a passage and ask the model to identify word repetitions, image patterns, or structural moves you may have missed. It is mechanically good at spotting patterns across a text that a human reader might not catch on a single pass — you are then the one who interprets them. Ask it to construct the strongest counter-interpretation to the reading you are developing. The pushback almost always sharpens the analysis, because it forces you to specify what your reading accounts for that the counter-reading does not. Ask it where your text sits in the broader critical conversation — what major interpretive frames have been applied to it — and then verify whatever it tells you, because the model sometimes invents critics or misattributes claims.

Where the model does damage. Ask it to produce a reading and you get competent averaged synthesis, not your encounter with the text. Ask it to supply quotations and it sometimes fabricates passages that do not appear in the work — every quotation you use must be verified against the source. Ask it to revise your prose toward greater analytical fluency and it will convert specific observations into vaguer sentences that sound like analysis without performing close-reading work. *The author uses imagery to suggest* is the model's default sentence for this move. That sentence is the death of textual analysis. Every time you find yourself writing it, go back to the specific passage and start again.

The model has read everything. It has not read your text the way you read your text — with your specific encounter, your specific attention, your specific moment of noticing that the storm vocabulary and the encounter vocabulary are the same vocabulary. That moment of noticing is what the essay is evidence of. You cannot outsource it.

## LLM Exercise — Chapter 19

**What you're building:** the analytical scaffold for your textual analysis — patterns observed, interpretive thesis, engagement with other readings — before drafting.

**Tool:** [Claude.ai](https://claude.ai/) chat.

**The prompt:**

```
I'm writing a 2,000–3,000 word textual analysis of [text title] by
[author], for a college writing class.

I have read the text closely. Below I'm pasting the text (or a link
to it).

Do the following in order, one step at a time, waiting for my response
between steps.

1. Identify three to four patterns in the text — patterns of word
   repetition, image repetition, structural choice, or rhythmic move
   that recur across the text. Quote the specific passages where each
   pattern appears. Do not yet interpret.

2. Ask me four diagnostic questions about which patterns I think
   matter most and what interpretive claim each one would support.
   Push back if my interpretation seems thin.

3. After I respond, propose a one-sentence thesis statement that
   commits to a specific reading of the text supported by the
   patterns I identified.

4. Once I have a working thesis, suggest where my reading sits in
   the broader critical conversation about this text. Where would
   feminist critics agree or push back? Formalists? Historicists?
   Suggest specific kinds of reading, not specific scholars I have
   not verified.

5. Construct the strongest counter-interpretation to my reading.
   Where could a reader, looking at the same text, reach a different
   conclusion? Quote the textual evidence that would support that
   counter-reading.

Do not draft the analysis. Do not write the introduction. The reading
is mine.

Here is the text:
[paste or link]
```

**What this produces:** a pattern inventory, a working thesis, a position in the critical conversation, and a counter-interpretation you will need to address. After this, you draft the analysis yourself.

**How to adapt this prompt:**

- *For ChatGPT or Gemini:* works as written.
- *For Cowork:* useful if the text is in a file you want to keep accessible across sessions.
- *For Claude Code:* not the right tool.

**The instructive failure to expect:** when the model identifies patterns, it will sometimes quote passages that do not appear in the text as written, or attribute a pattern to a passage that does not actually contain it. Verify every quoted passage against the source. *Where exactly in the text does this appear?* The verification is part of the discipline.

##  AI Wayback Machine
The ideas in this chapter didn't appear from nowhere. **Louise Rosenblatt** was working on *Literature as Exploration* (1938) and the transactional theory of reading — the argument that meaning lives between the reader and the text, not inside the text waiting to be uncovered — decades before most people had heard of textual analysis as a formal practice.

**Run this:**

```
Who was Louise Rosenblatt, and how does their work on transactional
reader-response theory connect to textual analysis as a critical
reading practice? Keep it to three paragraphs. End with the single
most surprising thing about their career or ideas.
```

Then search **"Louise Rosenblatt"** on Wikipedia. See what the model got right, got wrong, or left out.

**Now make the prompt better.** Try one of these:

- Ask it to explain Rosenblatt's distinction between *efferent* and *aesthetic* reading — with a specific text (a poem, a news article, an instruction manual) as the example.
- Ask: "Rosenblatt's transactional theory predates reader-response by decades but is often forgotten. What did her version say that Wolfgang Iser's and Stanley Fish's later versions left out?"
- Add the constraint: "Answer in the form of a high-school English teacher explaining to a student why their reading of a poem *is* the poem's meaning — and also isn't."

What changes? What gets better? What gets worse?
