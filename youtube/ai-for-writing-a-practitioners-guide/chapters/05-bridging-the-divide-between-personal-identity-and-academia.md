*The Shift From "Write What You Know" to "Write What the Genre Expects."*

---

Here is a feeling most writers can place exactly.

You sit down to write something academic — a literary analysis, a history paper, a book review. You start a sentence. At the third or fourth word you feel that the sentence is going wrong. Not wrong in any way you can name precisely. Just wrong. You delete it. You try another opening. Same feeling. The sentences you are producing do not sound like *you*, and the sentences that sound like you do not sound like *the kind of writing this is supposed to be*.

That feeling is real. It is telling you something true. And I want to explain what it is telling you, because once you understand what the feeling is tracking, you can navigate deliberately instead of stumbling.

What the feeling is tracking is a border. You are standing at the edge of a new genre, and genres have physics. They have rules about what can exist inside them and what cannot. The wrongness you are sensing is not personal failure — it is your ear detecting a mismatch between what the genre permits and what you have learned to do. That is actually useful information. The question is what to do with it.

---

## The first mistake to undo

Most students arrive at the academic genres carrying a belief that is wrong in a way that makes everything harder: the belief that "academic writing" is a single thing. A uniform voice. One set of rules. The belief gets reinforced by every handbook that positions academic writing as a monolithic register, a kind of official English against which everything else is measured.

This is not accurate, and I want to be specific about why, because the mistake has consequences.

A philosophy paper does not read like a chemistry lab report. A history paper does not read like an economics working paper. A literary analysis does not read like a sociology paper. A paper for an upper-division seminar reads differently from a paper for a general-education survey, even in the same field, even at the same institution. The register shifts by subfield, by journal, by professor, by century. Anyone who tells you there is *one* academic voice is describing their own training — at best.

What the academic genres share is a family resemblance, not an identity. Some shared features: citation discipline, some level of analytical structure, a commitment to defining terms before arguing about them, some degree of syntactic formality. But inside that family the cousins are genuinely distinct from each other. The historian writes with footnotes and archival evidence. The philosopher foregrounds the first-person argument. The chemist buries the author in passive constructions. The literary critic tracks very close to the text and moves outward. Each community of readers has agreed, over time, on a set of conventions that make its particular analytical work possible.

This matters for the transition you are making because the project is not what you might think it is. You are not trying to force your voice into a single academic mold. You are doing something more local and more interesting: for each new genre you encounter, figure out which moves you already have that travel, and which moves the genre cannot hold. That is a different task, and it is ultimately more learnable than "write academically."

## What you already have

Here is something the handbooks usually understate.

The instincts you developed in personal writing are not personal-writing instincts. Several of them are good-writing instincts, and good writing is the same problem in every genre.

Consider what those instincts actually are. Your eye for specificity — the habit of rendering something precisely rather than vaguely. Your feel for the telling detail, the one concrete thing that does the work of ten abstract things. Your sentence-level rhythm — the felt sense of when a sentence has earned its length and when it has gone on too long. Your honesty about what you do and don't yet understand. Your willingness to slow down when something is genuinely interesting and speed up when it isn't.

Now ask: is any of these a memoir move? Not really. A lab report written by someone with these instincts is a better lab report than one written by someone without them. The lab report will not look like the memoir — the forms are genuinely different. But the same underlying attention is at work in both. The attention that produces a specific, honest, rhythmically alive sentence in personal writing is the same attention that produces a specific, honest, rhythmically alive sentence in academic analysis.

The reason I am being explicit about this is that writers who struggle most with this transition are often not the ones whose personal writing was weakest. They are frequently the ones whose personal writing was strongest, and who then overcorrected when they hit the academic genres — as if everything that made the earlier work good had to be thrown out and replaced by something foreign. That overcorrection is its own kind of loss. The goal is not to suppress what you have. The goal is to figure out what travels and what doesn't, and to be precise about the distinction.

| Move | Personal writing | Academic writing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Eye for specificity | The exact slant of light on the kitchen floor; the brand on the cereal box. | The exact passage on page 47; the year the policy changed; the precise statistic and its denominator. |
| Telling detail | One concrete image that carries the emotional weight of a paragraph. | One piece of textual or archival evidence that carries the analytical weight of a paragraph. |
| Sentence-level rhythm | Long sentence to ride the feeling; short sentence to land the turn. | Long sentence to unfold the qualification; short sentence to land the claim. |
| Honesty about limits | "I don't know what my grandmother was thinking that afternoon." | "The evidence supports X; what it does not yet support is Y." |
| Slowing down for the interesting | Three paragraphs on the doorway because the doorway is the moment. | Three paragraphs on one passage because that passage is where the argument turns. |

## What does not travel

There are things that worked in the literacy narrative that will actively cause problems in academic writing, and they are worth naming without softening.

The first is using personal experience as primary evidence. In the literacy narrative, the fact that you were there — that this happened to you, that you remember it this way — was the evidence. Your memory was the source. The genre asked you to be the source. Academic genres are structured around evidence external to you: published research, primary documents, observable data, prior arguments in the field. You are no longer the source. You are the argument-maker working from sources. The moment you reach for your own experience as the basis for an analytical claim in a literary analysis, you have walked outside the genre's walls.

The second is leaving a question productively unresolved as a finishing move. In the cultural artifact essay, ending in a question mark was sometimes exactly right. The personal essay is allowed to wander, to arrive at complexity and sit in it. Academic arguments are expected to arrive somewhere — even if the arrival is qualified, even if you are saying "the evidence supports X but not yet Y." The analytical genre requires resolution of its central claim. The personal genre is allowed to defer resolution as a kind of honesty. These are different contracts with the reader, and honoring the wrong contract in the wrong genre breaks the reader's trust.

The third is using the first person as an authority move rather than an argument move. "I think this poem is about grief" is a start. But in academic writing the *I* does not itself establish anything. What follows the *I* still has to be made. "I think this poem is about grief, and here is what I can show you from the poem, and here is where prior readers have looked, and here is where my reading departs from theirs and why" — that is the academic move. First person is not forbidden in academic writing; the widespread belief that it is has produced decades of unnecessary passive-voice constructions. But the first person in academic writing carries different weight than the first person in memoir. In memoir, *I* is the authority. In the analytical essay, *I* is the argument-maker. The argument still has to be made.

None of this is arbitrary. The conventions of an academic genre exist because a community of readers has agreed, over time, that those conventions make certain kinds of work possible. The requirement to cite sources is not bureaucratic formalism — it is how the reader checks the chain of reasoning. The requirement to define terms is not pedantry — it is how you ensure that the disagreement you are having is a real disagreement about the subject rather than a confusion about what words mean. The conventions are doing something. Once you understand what they are doing, you can use them instead of imitating them blindly or refusing them on principle.

| What worked in Unit 1 | What this becomes in Unit 2 |
| --- | --- |
| Personal experience as primary evidence | External sources as primary evidence — published research, primary documents, observable data, prior arguments. |
| Interiority as resolution (sitting in complexity) | An arrived-at claim with qualified resolution — "the evidence supports X but not yet Y." |
| "I" as authority ("I remember it this way; that is the source") | "I" as argument-maker ("I think X; here is what I can show you, and here is where prior readers have looked"). |
| Ending in a question mark as a finishing move | Arriving somewhere, even if the arrival is hedged or partial. |
| Memory as the source of the claim | The chain of reasoning as the source of the claim — citation makes that chain checkable. |

## The two ways writers fail this transition

Writers navigating this move tend to fail in one of two directions, and the two failures are almost mirror images.

The first is overshooting the register. The writer senses that the genre wants something different from personal writing and reaches for what they imagine *sounds academic*: long Latinate vocabulary, passive constructions, heavy nominalization, sentences that go through three subordinate clauses before landing a verb. The prose that results is harder to read than necessary. On a quick read it sounds competent. On a careful read, it does not do any work.

What overshooting looks like in practice: a paragraph that sounds analytical but does not advance an argument. A source that is cited but whose specific claim is never used. *Thus* and *moreover* and *furthermore* that suggest logical connection without producing any. The prose is decorated. The analytical work is absent.

The best academic writing in every field achieves the genre's analytical work in the clearest possible sentences. The historian writing well does not write "the occurrence of hostilities was precipitated by the incapacity of diplomatic mechanisms to produce resolution." They write "the war began because the diplomats ran out of moves." The analysis is the same. The prose is not.

What is happening when a writer overshoots is that they have mistaken the surface features of academic writing — the signals of complexity — for the analytical work those features are supposed to carry. The complexity in good academic prose exists because the ideas are complex. Strip the surface features off a good academic paper and the work is still there. Strip them off an overshoot and there is nothing underneath.

The second failure is the opposite: refusing the translation. The writer treats the academic register as a betrayal of their authentic voice, violates the genre's conventions deliberately, and convinces themselves this is integrity. The instinct behind the refusal is usually sound — the academic genres do ask you to adapt, and adaptation can feel like putting on a mask. But the sound instinct has been generalized into a counterproductive principle. You are not being asked to become someone else. You are being asked to walk into a room with its own conventions and do some work there. The conventions are not the room's aesthetics. They are the room's load-bearing structure. Refusing them is refusing the work.

The fix for both failures is the same, and it is simpler than either failure sounds: read carefully in the genre you are about to write before you write. Not to imitate. To understand what the genre is actually doing when it makes the moves it makes. Once you understand what the conventions are *for*, you can use them intentionally.

## Five moves that cut across all the academic genres

Several moves recur across everything you will write in the academic genres ahead, regardless of the specific form. I want to name them once, here, clearly.

*The strong opening.* Every academic genre rewards an opening that does specific work fast — names the subject, signals the angle, tells the reader what they are about to read and why. The formal opening signals that something will come. The strong opening begins doing it immediately. The difference is not about length. A single sentence can be either.

*The earned claim.* Academic writing asks you to make claims about texts, events, problems, or evidence outside yourself. The claim has to be earned by the analysis that follows. A claim about a poem that arrives in the first sentence, before any reading of the poem, has not been earned yet. The claim earns its place by arriving after the evidence it depends on has been laid down. This is not a sequence you apply once — it is a discipline you practice in every paragraph.

*The named source.* Generic appeals to authority — *some scholars argue*, *research has shown*, *experts believe* — are not citation. They are the shape of citation without its substance. A real citation looks like this: *Anzaldúa argues in* Borderlands *that the border is not a line between two countries but a wound.* Or: *Wei et al. (2022) found that, in a sample of 400 undergraduate writers...* The specificity is not formalism. The specificity is what makes the claim checkable, and the checkability is the point. A reader who wants to test your reasoning needs to be able to find the chain. Generic authority appeals break the chain.

*The handled counterargument.* Academic genres reward the writer who can name the strongest objection to their own claim and then address it. Not a weak objection that collapses easily — the strongest version of the opposing case. The writer who pretends the counterargument does not exist reads to academic audiences as someone who has not thought hard enough. The writer who finds the strongest objection and genuinely grapples with it reads as someone who can be trusted. The distinction is visible on the page in about twenty seconds.

*The honest unresolved.* You will not resolve every question your paper raises. Flagging precisely where your analysis reaches its limit — where the evidence is not yet sufficient, where the question remains genuinely open — is stronger than manufacturing a false resolution. Good academic writing is not confident about everything. It is confident about what it has actually shown and honest about what it has not. This is not weakness. It is the move that earns you credibility with the reader who knows the literature and knows what your evidence can and cannot support.

| Move | What it does | What it replaces from personal writing | The failure if absent |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Strong opening | Names the subject, signals the angle, starts doing the work in the first sentence. | The atmospheric scene-setting that delays arrival. | A formal opening that signals something will come and never quite does. |
| Earned claim | Lets the claim arrive after the evidence it depends on has been laid down. | Memory as self-authorising authority. | A claim asserted in the first sentence and never supported. |
| Named source | Specifies the author, work, and exact argument being engaged. | "I remember reading somewhere that…" | Generic "some scholars argue" appeals that break the chain of reasoning. |
| Handled counterargument | Names the strongest objection to the claim and answers it. | The personal essay's permission to wander past opposing views. | Reads to a knowledgeable audience as not having thought hard enough. |
| Honest unresolved | Flags exactly where the analysis reaches its limit. | The personal essay's permission to sit in complexity. | Manufactured resolution that overclaims what the evidence supports. |

## What the language model does to this transition

Here is a specific warning about a specific problem, because the problem is specific.

Language models are very good at producing prose that sounds academic. This is not the same as producing prose that does academic work, and the gap between the two is exactly the gap you are navigating in this transition.

The model has been trained on thousands of academic papers. It has internalized the surface features — the Latinate vocabulary, the hedging phrases, the long clausal sentences, the citations distributed throughout. When you ask it to make something "more academic," it will produce prose with all of those features. The prose will read as competent at a glance. On careful reading, it frequently does not do the work.

What not doing the work looks like: a paragraph that sounds analytical but does not advance an argument. A source cited but never actually used. An assertion hedged in every direction but never grappled with. *Thus* and *moreover* connecting sentences that have no logical connection. The surface features of academic writing are exactly what the model produces reliably — and that is exactly the failure mode we just named: the overshoot.

What makes this dangerous at this particular stage is that the surface features of academic writing are unfamiliar enough that a writer in transition may not notice they are hollow. In the literacy narrative, you could feel when something was wrong because the register was close to your own speech. Academic prose does not sound like your own speech, which means the feeling-of-wrongness that usually catches this kind of problem does not fire. The prose sounds different from what you produce, you take that difference as a sign that it is working, and it is not.

The fix is the same fix it always is with models. Use the model to interrogate your argument, not to write your paragraphs. Ask the model to argue against your claim. Ask it to identify which of your sources you are actually using versus name-dropping. Ask it to find the weakest evidence in your analysis. Ask it to name the strongest objection to your central claim. The model is reliable at those tasks. It is unreliable at writing your academic paragraph in a way that earns its keep. Know which mode you are using it in, and do not mistake the fluency of its output for the quality of the reasoning underneath.

| Use the model for this | Don't use the model for this |
| --- | --- |
| Argue against my central claim — find the strongest objection. | Write my paragraph in academic register. |
| Identify the weakest evidence in my analysis. | Translate my voice into "more academic" prose. |
| Show me which of my sources I'm actually using vs. name-dropping. | Fill in the citations I haven't read. |
| Stress-test my definitions: do the terms I've set up support the claim? | Produce the academic-sounding version of a paragraph I haven't yet thought through. |
| Ask: where would a careful reader push back hardest, and why? | Generate hedging phrases and transitions to dress up an absent argument. |

## The tension this chapter cannot fully resolve

I want to be direct about a real tension in what I have been describing, rather than paper over it.

Academic writing, in some of its forms, does ask you to suppress moves that are genuinely yours. The first-person voice as primary authority. The digression that follows curiosity rather than argument. The admission that you do not know how something ends. These are not available in every academic genre. Pretending otherwise is a form of dishonesty that becomes visible the first time a paper is marked down for using them.

But here is what I keep seeing. The students who struggle most with this transition are not the students whose personal writing was weakest. They are often the students whose personal writing was strongest — the ones who developed real voices and then overcorrected when they hit the academic genres, suppressing the strength instead of carrying it forward. That overcorrection is its own kind of loss, and I do not have a clean pedagogical solution to it. The advice in this chapter is partial. Some of this is going to have to be figured out in specific drafts, in specific conversations about specific paragraphs.

What I can tell you is the underlying principle, and I think it holds. The moves that made your personal writing work are not fundamentally opposed to the moves that make academic writing work. The eye for specificity. The resistance to vague abstraction. The willingness to say exactly what you mean rather than retreating into hedged generality. The habit of slowing down when something is genuinely interesting. These travel. The forms change. The underlying attention is the same attention.

The adaptation is not capitulation. The genre is a room. You do not have to be the same person in every room. But you are still the one who walked in.

## LLM Exercise — Chapter 5

**What you're building:** a clear-eyed map of which moves in your existing personal-writing voice will travel into academic writing and which will not. The map becomes a reference document you can return to throughout the rest of the book.

**Tool:** [Claude.ai](https://claude.ai/) chat. A Claude Project is recommended — you will reference this map for the rest of the semester.

**The prompt:**

```
I'm transitioning from personal writing (literacy narrative,
cultural artifact essay) to academic genres (literary analysis,
research papers, position arguments, rhetorical analysis).

Below I'm going to paste one paragraph of personal writing I've
already done. I want you to do four things, in order:

1. Identify the specific moves in this paragraph that ARE my voice —
   sentence rhythm, word choice, structural moves, tonal habits.
   Name them as specifically as you can.

2. For each of those moves, tell me whether it would travel into
   academic writing as-is, travel with modification, or have to be
   left behind. Explain why for each one.

3. Identify any moves in this paragraph that, in an academic genre,
   would be FAILURE modes — appeals to my own authority that don't
   have external evidence, claims that work in memoir but wouldn't
   work in an argument, etc.

4. Suggest one specific question I should ask myself before drafting
   any academic paragraph, drawn from what you saw in this analysis.

Do not rewrite my paragraph. Do not produce an "academic version" of
it. The exercise is diagnostic, not generative.

Here is the paragraph:
[paste one paragraph from your literacy narrative or cultural
artifact essay]
```

**What this produces:** a personalized map of your voice's portability across genres. Save the output. Keep it open in another tab when you draft academic assignments. Reference move 4 — the diagnostic question — every time.

**How to adapt this prompt:**

- *For ChatGPT or Gemini:* works as written.
- *For Cowork:* if you want a permanent reference document, tell Cowork to save the model's response as `voice-map.md` in your project folder.

**The instructive failure to expect:** when you ask the model to identify failure modes, it will sometimes try to soften them. *"You might consider..."* instead of *"This would fail because..."* If you notice the softening: *"Be more direct. Tell me exactly what would fail and why."* Diagnostic feedback that hedges is not yet useful diagnostic feedback.

**Preview of next chapter:** Chapter 6 is memoir and personal narrative — closer to Unit 1's mode than to the academic genres ahead. Treat it as the last chapter where your voice's first-person authority is the primary asset. After Chapter 6, the genres pivot toward evidence external to you.

most surprising thing about their career or ideas.
