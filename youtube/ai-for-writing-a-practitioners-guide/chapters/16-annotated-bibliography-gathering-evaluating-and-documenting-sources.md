*Proving You Read What You're Citing.*

---

Here is a thing that happens all the time in research papers, and that almost nobody talks about directly.

A student has done six weeks of research. Fifteen articles, four book chapters, one government report, two long-form magazine pieces. Notes in a sprawling document. A developing argument. A stack of PDFs with filenames that made sense in week two and are now largely mysterious.

The paper gets written. Citations accumulate. Somewhere around citation nine, the student is no longer quite sure what source nine actually said. The title sounds right. The PDF is on the desktop. The citation goes in.

This is not fraud. It is the ordinary outcome of a specific kind of information overload combined with a workflow that never forced the student to demonstrate — to themselves or to anyone else — that they actually read what they cited. The citation format does not require this. The notes do not require it. The first draft does not require it.

The annotated bibliography requires it.

That is the first thing the genre is doing: creating a document where you have to prove, annotation by annotation, that you read what you are citing. Not that you scanned it. Not that you read the abstract and trusted the middle. That you read it carefully enough to summarize its argument specifically, to evaluate its credibility honestly, and to explain in writing where it fits in the argument you are building.

The second thing the genre is doing is less obvious but more important. The act of writing the annotations often changes the argument.

When you read a source, you have a developing thesis, and you are looking for what the source can do for the thesis. When you annotate the source, you have to explain not just what the source says in your favor but what it actually says — including what it says that complicates your position, what its limits are, how it sits in relation to the other sources. That requirement produces a different kind of reading. And that different kind of reading sometimes shows you that your argument needs to move.

The student who builds a serious annotated bibliography often ends up with a better thesis than the one they started with. This is not an accident of the assignment. It is the assignment working exactly as intended.

## What the annotation contains

An annotated bibliography entry has two parts: the citation and the annotation. The citation is the formal element — author, title, publication, date, in whatever style is required. The annotation is the paragraph that follows.

The annotation does three things. Not one. Not two. Three, and all three need to be present even when they are not labeled.

The first is *summary*. One or two sentences that capture what the source actually argues. Not what the title suggests. Not what you wish it said. What it argues — with the key finding or method named. The summary must be specific enough that a reader who has not seen the source can understand its main contribution.

The failure mode here is the summary that describes a topic rather than an argument. *This article discusses the relationship between social media and mental health* is a topic description. *This article reports a longitudinal survey of 5,000 American teenagers showing that passive social media consumption — scrolling without posting — is more strongly associated with depression scores than active posting* is a summary. The difference is specificity. A summary names what the source found or argued, not what it was about.

The second is *evaluation*. Two or three sentences assessing the source's credibility and quality. Who wrote it? What are their credentials, and are those credentials relevant to this specific claim? Where was it published, and what does that publication venue imply about editorial standards? Is it peer-reviewed, journalistic, governmental, advocacy-driven? What is its date, and does the date matter for the claim it makes? Where might the source be biased, and does the bias affect the specific finding you are drawing on?

The failure mode here is metadata without judgment. *This article was published in 2021 in a peer-reviewed journal* is metadata. *This article was published in 2021 in the Journal of Health Economics, a peer-reviewed venue that requires statistical transparency in submissions; the primary limitation is that the sample is drawn entirely from one metropolitan area, which may reduce generalizability to rural populations that are central to my argument* is an evaluation. The evaluation makes a judgment about whether and how the source should be trusted for the particular use you are making of it.

The third is *reflection*. Two or three sentences explaining how the source fits into your developing argument. What does it support? What does it complicate? Which other sources does it speak with or against? Where in the paper will it likely appear?

The failure mode here is vague relevance. *This source is relevant to my topic* says nothing. *This source provides the specific misclassification rates I will use in my third section, and it pairs with Sadasivan et al. on technical failure modes; together they show that detectors fail in two different ways for two different reasons, compounding each other* is a reflection. The reflection places the source at a specific location in a specific argument.

Put all three together and the annotation tells a reader three things simultaneously: what the source says, whether to trust it for this use, and what it is doing in your paper. That is the complete picture.

| Component | What it does | Failure mode | Strong version |
|---|---|---|---|
| Summary | Names what the source actually argues, with the key finding or method specified. | Describes the topic instead of the argument. *This article discusses social media and mental health.* | Names the specific finding. *A longitudinal survey of 5,000 American teenagers showing passive scrolling is more strongly tied to depression scores than active posting.* |
| Evaluation | Judges credibility for the specific use you are making of the source. | Records metadata without making a judgment. *Published in 2021 in a peer-reviewed journal.* | Engages the limitation that matters. *Peer-reviewed in the Journal of Health Economics, but the sample is one metropolitan area, which may not generalize to the rural populations central to my argument.* |
| Reflection | Places the source at a specific location in your developing argument. | States vague relevance. *This source is relevant to my topic.* | Names function and pairing. *Provides the misclassification rates I use in Section 3; pairs with Sadasivan et al. on technical failure modes to argue detectors fail in two compounding ways.* |

## A worked example

The fastest way to see what the genre wants is to read one annotation that does the job.

This one is from a paper arguing that AI-detection software in college courses fails in ways that disproportionately harm marginalized students:

> Liang, Wenxin, et al. "GPT detectors are biased against non-native English writers." *arXiv preprint*, 2023. https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02819.
>
> Liang and colleagues at Stanford evaluate seven commercial GPT-detection tools by testing them against essays written by native English-speaking eighth graders and TOEFL essays written by non-native English speakers. They find systematic bias: detectors misclassify non-native essays as AI-generated at rates substantially higher than for native-English essays — 61% vs. 5% for one widely-deployed tool. The paper is an arXiv preprint and not peer-reviewed, but the methodology is transparent, the authors are at Stanford's NLP group with established credentials, and the findings have been widely cited in subsequent work in the field. This source is the empirical centerpiece of the case that detectors fail in ways that fall disproportionately on already-marginalized students. It pairs with Sadasivan et al. on technical failure under paraphrasing attacks; together they argue that detectors fail in two different ways for two different reasons, producing compound harms.

That is under 200 words. Look at what is happening in it.

The summary is specific enough that you know what the study measured (seven tools, two populations), what it found (61% vs. 5%), and what the paper claims from that finding (systematic bias). You could not have written those numbers without reading the paper.

The evaluation names the preprint status, which is a real limitation — no peer review — and then addresses it directly rather than ignoring it. It names the credentials and the citation record as compensating factors. It does not pretend the limitation is not there. It makes a judgment about how to weight the source given the limitation.

The reflection places the source at a specific location — *the empirical centerpiece* — and names a specific pairing with another source, explaining what the pairing accomplishes. A reader of the annotation knows exactly how the paper will use this source before the paper is written.

To produce that annotation, the student had to read the paper closely enough to find the 61% vs. 5% numbers, had to know enough about academic publishing to assess what the preprint status implies, and had to have thought enough about the full bibliography to understand how this source and Sadasivan et al. work together. The annotation is evidence of the work. That is the point.

## Why the evaluation is always the weakest

Of the three components, the evaluation is the one most annotations short-change. The pattern is consistent enough that it is worth naming specifically.

The student names the publication venue without explaining what it implies. *Published in Nature* is metadata. *Published in Nature, a generalist science journal with exceptionally high rejection rates, which implies the finding passed unusually rigorous peer review but also that the journal selects for novelty — which may mean this finding is more striking than robust* is an evaluation.

The student notes the author's credential without engaging with its relevance to the specific claim. *The author is a professor at Johns Hopkins* is metadata. *The author is a professor of epidemiology at Johns Hopkins whose expertise is directly relevant to the mechanistic claims in Section 2 but not to the policy recommendations in Section 4, which I should treat with more caution* is an evaluation.

The student records the date without asking whether it matters. *Published in 2019* is metadata. *Published in 2019, before the emergence of large language models at scale; the author's claims about AI-assisted writing may therefore not apply to the current environment I am analyzing* is an evaluation.

The pattern in all three is the same: the student records a fact about the source without making a judgment from it. Evaluation is judgment, not record-keeping. The question the evaluation component is trying to answer is: *what kind of object is this source, and how should it be weighted for the specific use I am making of it?* The answer is never the same for every source. A pharmaceutical company's internal research report on its own drug is a different kind of object than independent academic research on the same drug, even if both have credentials and publication venues. The evaluation should name the difference and explain what it means for how you will use the source.

| Fact about the source | Metadata version (record only) | Evaluation version (judgment) |
|---|---|---|
| Publication venue | *Published in Nature.* | *Published in Nature, a generalist science journal with exceptionally high rejection rates — the finding cleared rigorous peer review, but the journal also selects for novelty, which can mean the result is more striking than robust.* |
| Author credentials | *The author is a professor at Johns Hopkins.* | *The author is a Johns Hopkins epidemiologist whose expertise is directly relevant to the mechanistic claims in Section 2 but not to the policy recommendations in Section 4 — which I should treat with more caution.* |
| Date | *Published in 2019.* | *Published in 2019, before large language models scaled. The claims about AI-assisted writing may not apply to the current environment I am analyzing.* |

*The evaluation asks: what kind of object is this source, and how should it be weighted for the specific use I am making of it?*

## The bibliography that never changes its mind

There is a failure mode I want to name directly, because it is the most common and the most invisible to the student producing it.

The student develops a tentative thesis in week two. The student then searches for sources that support the thesis. The bibliography ends up with six sources that all argue for the position the student held on day fourteen of the project. Every annotation ends with some version of *this source supports my argument*. The paper gets written. The argument is exactly the argument the student had before they started reading.

This bibliography has not failed in any technical sense. The citations are correctly formatted. The annotations contain summaries, evaluations, and reflections. The sources are real and credible. But the student has not been changed by the reading. The reading was confirmatory from the start.

The test for whether this has happened is simple: find the source that complicates your argument most seriously. If you cannot find it in your bibliography, it is not because such a source does not exist. It is because you did not look for it, or found it and left it out.

The counterargument source — the one that argues most directly against your tentative position — is the source students most consistently skip. The skip is visible to any reader who has seen a few bibliographies. An instructor reading a list of six uniformly supportive sources can tell immediately that the student has not yet engaged with the opposition.

Include the strongest opposing voice you can find. Annotate it honestly. The evaluation must engage seriously with why it is credible. The reflection must name where in the paper you will engage with it and what you will have to do to address its challenge.

This does two things. It strengthens the eventual argument, because you have engaged with the best case against your position rather than ignoring it. And it changes the character of your reading — once you are genuinely looking for what the other side would say, you read everything differently, including the supporting sources, because you are attending to what each source claims precisely enough to understand what it would take to refute it.

## What the bibliography looks like as a whole

Five to seven sources for a research paper of ten to twelve pages is the standard ask. How you distribute those sources across types matters as much as which sources you choose.

One or two peer-reviewed journal articles in the most directly relevant subfield provide the empirical anchor — the specific data or findings your argument will rely on most heavily.

One or two longer works — book chapters or substantial reports from credentialed organizations — provide the theoretical or historical context your argument needs.

One or two pieces from major journalism or magazine venues with editorial standards are useful for framing the public significance of your question, for recent developments that academic publication has not caught up to, and for making your argument legible to a reader who is not inside the academic subfield.

One counterargument source — the strongest opposing voice you can find, from a credible venue. This is not optional.

Optionally, one primary source: a dataset, a government document, a piece of legislation, a published interview transcript. Primary sources provide the closest possible proximity to the events or phenomena your argument is about.

A bibliography drawn entirely from one source type signals limited engagement with the range of evidence available. All peer-reviewed articles means you may be missing the contemporary public dimension of your question. All journalism means you may be lacking the methodological rigor that distinguishes your argument from opinion. The distribution is itself an argument — it shows the reader that you have thought about what kind of evidence your claim requires and where that evidence lives.

| Source category | What it provides that other types cannot | Essential vs. optional | Warning sign if absent |
|---|---|---|---|
| Peer-reviewed journal articles (1–2) | The specific empirical data or vetted methodology your argument leans on most heavily. | Essential for any argumentative paper making an empirical claim. | The paper rests on impressions rather than evidence; reviewers cannot locate the data behind your numbers. |
| Longer works — book chapters, reports from credentialed organizations (1–2) | Theoretical depth and historical context that journal articles are too narrow to provide. | Essential when the argument depends on framing, definitions, or a longer arc. | The argument floats above its tradition; you cannot say which conversation you are joining. |
| Major journalism or magazine pieces (1–2) | Recent developments, public significance, and prose legibility for readers outside the subfield. | Essential when the question is contemporary; optional for purely historical topics. | The paper feels sealed inside the academy; nothing connects it to the world the reader lives in. |
| Counterargument source (1) | The strongest opposing voice, treated honestly rather than caricatured. | Always essential. Never optional. | The bibliography reads as confirmation-only; the instructor sees that you skipped the opposition. |
| Primary source — dataset, government document, legislation, transcript (0–1) | Closest possible proximity to the events or phenomena your argument is about. | Optional, but disproportionately strengthens any paper that can use one. | All of your evidence is filtered through other people's interpretation; you never touch the thing itself. |

*A bibliography's source distribution is an argument about what kind of evidence the claim requires.*

## Citation style — the minimum

You need to know which citation style your assignment requires and apply it consistently. The major choices are not interchangeable.

MLA is standard in literature, languages, and some humanities. In-text citations use author and page number: (Smith 42). The bibliography is called Works Cited.

APA is standard in psychology, education, social sciences, and many sciences. In-text citations use author and year: (Smith, 2023). The bibliography is called References.

Chicago comes in two versions: author-date, which resembles APA, and notes-and-bibliography, which uses footnotes plus a bibliography. History and some humanities use Chicago; book publishing uses it widely.

For the mechanics of formatting — where the period goes, whether the journal title is italicized, how to handle a source with five authors — use the style manual, use Zotero, or use a reliable generator. Do not memorize the minutiae. What you need to internalize is which style is required and how to read a citation in that style, so you can verify that your citations are correctly formatted.

Zotero is free, open-source, and handles citation formatting automatically once set up. The fifteen minutes of setup repay themselves very quickly on a project of this length. Hand-formatting citations is a cost you do not need to pay, and the errors introduced by hand-formatting are exactly the kind of thing that erodes a reader's confidence in your precision on everything else.

## Why the model cannot read for you

Two places where the model is genuinely useful in annotated bibliography work, and three where it does damage.

After you have read a source and taken your own notes, paste the notes and ask the model to help compress them into a 100–150 word summary. The model is good at compression. You revise for your own voice and judgment. The compression is useful; the judgment is yours. Separately, paste your current source list and ask what positions or source types are underrepresented. The model is often good at flagging obvious gaps in a bibliography's distribution.

Where it does damage. Ask the model to generate an annotation of a source you have not read and it will produce a plausible-sounding annotation based on the title and citation that may bear little resemblance to what the source actually argues. The plausibility is the danger — it is not detectable as wrong unless you have read the source yourself. Ask the model to generate citations and it invents plausible-sounding references that may not exist; every citation must point to a source you have located and read. Ask the model to improve your annotations to sound more academic and it will convert your specific observations into vaguer prose that sounds more conventional and is less useful. The specific observation in your own voice is the annotation. The generalized academic version is not.

The principle that covers all three: the model cannot read what you have not read, and an annotation is evidence of reading. When the model generates the annotation, it is generating the evidence without producing the underlying fact the evidence is supposed to attest to. The bibliography becomes a document that claims you read what you did not read. This is a stronger version of the problem the genre exists to solve. The whole point of the annotation is that it forces the actual reading. Using the model to generate annotations defeats the only mechanism the genre has.

## LLM Exercise — Chapter 16

**What you're building:** the annotated bibliography for your developing research paper, sharpened by structured interrogation of each annotation.

**Tool:** [Claude.ai](https://claude.ai/) chat. A Claude Project is recommended for long projects.

**The prompt:**

```
I'm building an annotated bibliography for an argumentative research
paper. The format requires a citation in MLA/APA and then a 100-200
word annotation that summarizes, evaluates, and reflects on each
source.

Below I'm pasting my draft annotations for [N] sources. I have read
each source carefully. The annotations are my first drafts.

Do the following, one source at a time. For each annotation:

1. Does the summary accurately capture what the source actually
   argues, or am I sliding into a related claim? Be specific.

2. Does the evaluation engage with the source's credibility
   seriously? What's missing — author credentials, venue type,
   methodological caveats?

3. Does the reflection place the source in my developing argument
   with enough specificity? Do I name which other sources it
   connects to and how?

4. Is the annotation between 100-200 words? If it's outside the
   range, where can I tighten or expand?

Do not rewrite my annotations. Identify what needs fixing. The
revision is mine.

Here are my draft annotations:
[paste them]
```

**What this produces:** specific revision targets for each annotation. After this, you revise the annotations yourself.

**How to adapt this prompt:**

- *For Cowork:* useful if your annotations are spread across multiple files — have Cowork read them before the conversation begins.
- *For ChatGPT or Gemini:* works as written.
- *For Claude Code:* not the right tool.

**The instructive failure to expect:** when the model evaluates your reflections, it sometimes produces vague suggestions like *make the connection more specific*. Push back: *Where specifically should I make the connection more specific? Quote the sentence you mean.* The specificity is the part of the feedback that helps.

##  AI Wayback Machine
The ideas in this chapter didn't appear from nowhere. **Suzanne Briet** was working on *Qu'est-ce que la documentation?* (1951) — the foundational text on what a document *is*, what a citation does, and what is lost when we treat documentation as clerical work — decades before most people had heard of the annotated bibliography as a critical practice.

**Run this:**

```
Who was Suzanne Briet, and how does their work on documentation theory
and the logic behind citation systems connect to the annotated
bibliography as a critical, not clerical, act? Keep it to three
paragraphs. End with the single most surprising thing about their
career or ideas.
```

Then search **"Suzanne Briet"** on Wikipedia. See what the model got right, got wrong, or left out.

**Now make the prompt better.** Try one of these:

- Ask it to explain Briet's famous example: an antelope in a zoo *is* a document, but an antelope in the wild *is not*. What is the principle behind the distinction?
- Ask: "Briet argued that documentation is fundamentally an *organizing* practice. What does that imply about how an annotated bibliography should be ordered — chronologically, by argument, by something else?"
- Add the framing: "Answer as a 1951 librarian giving a talk on what citation could become in the next century — but written in 2026."

What changes? What gets better? What gets worse?
