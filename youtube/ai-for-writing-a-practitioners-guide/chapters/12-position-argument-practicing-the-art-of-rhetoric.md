*Defending a Claim a Reader Might Reject.*

---

On May 22, 1964, Lyndon Johnson stood at the podium in Michigan Stadium at the University of Michigan, six months into a presidency he had not campaigned for, and gave a graduation address that he and his speechwriters knew would be quoted for decades. He opened with humor. He addressed the graduates. He widened his audience to the nation. And then he did something the genre of presidential graduation address does not require: he staked out a position.

> The Great Society rests on abundance and liberty for all. It demands an end to poverty and racial injustice, to which we are totally committed in our time.[^1]

[^1]: Lyndon B. Johnson, ["Remarks at the University of Michigan, May 22, 1964"](https://www.lbjlibrary.org/exhibits/great-society-speech). LBJ Presidential Library archives.

The sentences are short. The commitments are specific. Many in the audience and in the country did not believe poverty could be ended. Many did not believe racial injustice was the federal government's business. Many did not believe *abundance and liberty for all* was a coherent goal, let alone an achievable one. Johnson knew all of this. He stated the position anyway, in language that would not allow him to back down later.

That is the move. *Stating a position on a contested issue with enough specificity that you cannot retreat from it.* Everything in this chapter is built on that.

---

## What a position argument actually is

Let me make the definition precise, because the genre has close neighbors and the distinctions matter.

A position argument takes a clear stance on a contested issue and defends it with reasoning and evidence. A *proposal* recommends a specific intervention to a specific problem — its structure is problem-solution, and it ends with a recommendation. A position argument can recommend nothing; it can simply argue that something is the case, or that something should be understood differently. An *analytical report* holds its conclusion back while the evidence accumulates, and calibrates its voice toward apparent neutrality. A position argument commits to the conclusion near the top and advocates for it — not shouting, but openly, with the advocacy visible.

What separates the position argument from a rant, an opinion piece, or a manifesto is precision on four things. The position is genuinely debatable — if everyone reasonable already agrees, you do not have a position argument, you have a description of consensus. The defense rests on evidence and reasoning, so the reader is asked to follow a case, not to defer to the writer's authority. The strongest objection is engaged and addressed, not ignored or strawmanned. And the advocacy is restrained enough to be persuasive to a skeptic — not to the reader who already agrees with you, but to the reader who doesn't and might.

| Dimension | Position argument | Proposal | Analytical report |
|---|---|---|---|
| Structure | Claim, defense, objection, conclusion | Problem, evidence, solution, implementation | Question, evidence, accumulating analysis, conclusion |
| Where the claim appears | Near the top (thesis-first) or late (delayed-thesis), but committed by the end | At the end — the recommendation is the destination | Held back until the evidence has been laid out |
| Voice register | Measured advocacy — visible commitment, restrained adjectives | Practical, action-oriented, often institutional | Calibrated toward apparent neutrality |
| Relationship to objections | Engages the strongest objection in its strongest form | Anticipates implementation concerns, not philosophical opposition | Often catalogs competing readings without picking one |
| What the reader is asked to do | Accept a contested claim on the strength of the case | Adopt or fund a specific intervention | Understand the state of knowledge on a question |

*Three close neighbors. The position argument is the one that openly advocates for a contested claim and engages the strongest opposition. Use this to confirm you are in the right genre before drafting.*

That last requirement is the one that does the most work to constrain how the piece is written, and I'll return to it throughout.

## The issue: what makes something arguable

The single most consequential decision in a position argument is what to argue. Choose wrong and the rest of the draft is fighting uphill regardless of how well you write.

Three conditions are required for a genuine debatable issue, and all three must hold.

The issue is contested in the live discourse. Not in your friend group. In the broader conversation you are writing into — news coverage, academic literature, policy debate, professional discussion. If the issue is not contested there, you have a settled question, and your job is to report the settlement rather than argue it.

Reasonable, informed people can disagree. This is the harder filter, and it rules out more than it looks like it does. Some issues are contested in public discourse because of motivated bad-faith disagreement rather than because people who have examined the evidence reach different conclusions. Whether the Earth is spherical. Whether vaccines are safe. Those are contested in some public conversations, but the contestation is not based on genuine evidential difficulty. The position argument is built for issues where the controversy is real — where people who understand the evidence and are arguing in good faith can reach different conclusions. When the controversy is manufactured, you are not writing a position argument; you are explaining why reasonable people should not be confused.

You can hold a position you are willing to defend publicly against its strongest opposition. The genre will require you, in the objection section, to take on the most compelling case against your claim. If you secretly aren't sure you believe your own argument, that section will collapse and the reader will feel it.

Now here is the most practical thing I can tell you about issue selection: specificity is what converts a topic into an arguable position. 

*Should universities require undergraduates to complete a course on the history of their own institution?* — workable. *Is AI changing education?* — not workable. It is too general to argue because no claim could be specific enough to be falsified.

*Is the use of AI detectors in college courses doing more harm than the AI-generated work they are intended to catch?* — workable. *Should we address polarization in American politics?* — not workable. *Should U.S. federal elections adopt compulsory voting with a small civil penalty for non-participation, on the Australian model?* — workable.

The pattern is consistent: specificity makes an issue arguable. Generality kills it. The general topic is where you start. The workable issue is what you produce after narrowing it to the point where a claim could be right or wrong.

| Too general or settled (not workable) | Narrowed to an arguable position (workable) |
|---|---|
| Is AI changing education? | Should first-year writing programs require that every drafted assignment be accompanied by an LLM-use disclosure, on the model of conflict-of-interest statements in scholarly journals? |
| Is climate change real? | Should U.S. federal building codes require new single-family construction in the wildland-urban interface to meet Class A wildfire-resistance standards as a condition of mortgage insurance? |
| Should we address polarization? | Should U.S. federal elections adopt compulsory voting with a small civil penalty for non-participation, on the Australian model? |
| Is social media bad for teenagers? | Should U.S. public schools confiscate student smartphones for the duration of the school day, on the New York City model rolled out in 2024? |
| Are AI detectors useful? | Is the use of AI detectors in college writing courses doing more harm than the AI-generated work they are intended to catch? |
| Should colleges teach the humanities? | Should the general-education humanities requirement be replaced with a single research-methods course that satisfies the same credit hours? |

*The pattern is consistent — specificity converts a topic into an arguable position. The left column is where you start; the right column is what you produce after narrowing.*

## The claim: committing in a sentence

Once you have the issue, you need to state your position. A claim is a declarative sentence that names the issue and commits you to a side.

Here is what the failure looks like, because it is almost universal in first drafts:

*AI detection software has become controversial in college courses and raises many important questions about fairness and academic integrity.*

That is a topic description. It does not commit to anything and therefore cannot be defended or refuted. A reader who finishes that sentence knows only that the writer has noticed that the issue exists.

Here is a real claim:

*Universities should not use AI detection software to screen student writing because the detectors' false-positive rates exceed their accuracy gains and the resulting harms fall disproportionately on non-native English speakers.*

The difference is commitment. The real claim names the issue, takes a side, and gestures at the reasoning. It is unambiguously contestable — a reasonable reader could reject each of its three moving parts. And it commits the writer to defending a specific claim, not a vague concern.

Three tests for a real claim.

*Specificity.* The terms are defined precisely enough that the reader knows exactly what is being argued. *Non-native English speakers* is specific; *marginalized groups* is not. *False-positive rates exceed accuracy gains* is falsifiable; *harms students* is not. If you can substitute a different term without changing the meaning, the original term was not doing work.

*Defensibility.* You can imagine the evidence that would support the claim, and you can imagine the evidence that would refute it. If you cannot imagine what would change your mind, you do not have a claim; you have a conviction. The reader can tell the difference, and the reader who can tell the difference will not trust you.

*Stake.* The claim matters. Answering the question one way or the other changes something the reader should care about. Stakes are not optional — they are what give the reader a reason to keep reading past the first paragraph.

## Structure: two options

There are two standard structures for a position argument, and the choice between them is not aesthetic. It follows from who your reader is and how your argument works.

*Thesis-first* states the claim in the introduction. Supporting arguments develop in the body. Objections are addressed. The conclusion restates the claim with the accumulated weight of evidence behind it. Most writers default to this structure. It works. It is the right choice for most arguments, most audiences, and most time constraints.

*Delayed-thesis* begins with the issue, the stakes, and the strongest cases on each side. It walks the reader through the evidence. The writer's own position appears only late — at the point where the accumulated evidence makes the position feel earned rather than announced. This is harder to execute. When it works, it produces a stronger sense of having reasoned toward a conclusion with the reader rather than having declared one at them. When it fails, it produces a long introduction that the reader loses patience with before the argument begins.

Delayed-thesis earns its difficulty in specific situations: when announcing the conclusion up front would put off a skeptical reader before the evidence has had a chance to work; when the issue is genuinely hard and the position you reach is itself a product of reasoning the reader needs to follow; when your audience is analytically patient.

Thesis-first is right most of the rest of the time. When your audience expects a clear claim early. When the argument depends on multiple supporting moves that need to be cumulative. When attention is constrained — which describes most writing environments a college graduate will enter.

Neither is wrong. The choice is a function of your reader and your argument, not your taste.

## The objection section: the move most drafts skip

The single most common failure in student position arguments is the absence — or the perfunctory handling — of the objection section.

This is where you name the strongest cases against your claim and address them. *Strongest*, not *most convenient to dismiss*. The reader who is skeptical of your position is reading specifically to see whether you have engaged with what they would say. If you have not, you have lost them — not because they think you are wrong, but because you have shown you have not done the work of taking the opposition seriously.

What strong objections look like in practice: for the AI-detection claim above, the weakest objection is *but academic dishonesty is bad and we should address it*. You can dismiss that in a sentence. The strongest objection is something like: *even imperfect detectors raise the cost of casual academic dishonesty enough that they reduce overall cheating; the policy produces specific harms to specific populations, but it also produces specific goods this argument isn't counting, and the full accounting might favor the policy.* That is a real objection. A position argument that does not engage with it will not persuade anyone who already finds it compelling.

Two moves that do the work in the objection section.

*Concession before refutation.* Acknowledge the parts of the objection that are correct. *The concern about academic dishonesty that motivates the detector policy is real and worth taking seriously.* This does not weaken your position. It signals you are arguing in good faith and that your refutation has already done the work of seeing what is true on the other side. Readers who disagree with you are looking for the concession. When they find it, they keep reading.

*Refutation that names specific evidence.* After the concession: *the concern is real, but this particular intervention does not address it well, because [specific evidence]. A better response to the same underlying concern would be [alternative].* The refutation has to do real work. Gesturing past the objection — *while this view has some merit, ultimately my position stands* — is not a refutation. It is a demonstration that the objection was too strong to answer.

One term worth knowing precisely: *steel-manning*. To steel-man an opposing position is to represent it in its strongest form — the version the most serious critics actually hold, not the version you could most easily defeat. The opposite move is *straw-manning*: arguing against a weakened or distorted version of the opposition. The reader who holds the strong version of the opposing view will recognize a straw man immediately, and they will stop trusting the writer. Steel-man the opposition. It is harder. The argument you produce is more persuasive because of it.

## Voice: measured advocacy

The position argument can have heat in it. You are committed to a contested view, and the reader will be able to tell. That is a feature, not a problem.

The trap is confusing heat with aggression. A piece of writing that is sarcastic, dismissive, or contemptuous toward the opposing side will fail with precisely the reader it most needs to persuade. The reader who already agrees with you will enjoy the contempt. The reader who doesn't will withdraw, and they are the only reader the position argument is designed to change.

Measured advocacy is the move. State the claim clearly — *I believe* and *I think* are usually unnecessary in a position argument; the entire document is your thinking and the reader already knows it. Render evidence specifically — statistics with sources, examples with names, reasoning with steps visible enough to follow. Name the opposing view fairly and in its strongest form. Restrain the adjectives. *Devastating*, *outrageous*, *clearly*, *obviously* — these reach for emotional force when the argument should be reaching for evidence. The reader senses the substitution and trusts the prose less.

Allow the conclusion its force without overstatement. The position you have defended is the position you have defended. Do not claim the evidence shows more than it shows. A conclusion that overshoots the evidence is a gift to the skeptical reader: it gives them a reason to reject not just the overstatement but everything behind it.

One observation about rhythm, because it matters more in advocacy writing than in most other genres. Position arguments benefit from sentence variety — long sentences for accumulation of evidence and reasoning, short sentences for landing the point. The rhythm does rhetorical work. A draft with uniform sentence length sounds like it was written in a single register at a single pace, which makes it feel mechanical. Read the draft aloud. The places where the rhythm goes flat are usually the places where the argument goes flat.

## The LLM problem in this genre

The position argument is the genre where the temptation to use a language model badly is highest, and understanding why is itself useful.

Models produce prose that sounds like an argument. They are less reliable at producing prose that *is* an argument. The difference is in the specific commitment the genre requires. A position argument works when the writer is visibly invested in a particular claim, has engaged with the specific evidence bearing on it, and has grappled genuinely with the strongest cases against it. The model tends to flatten exactly that investment. The output has the surface features of advocacy — it states a position, marshals some points, acknowledges some opposition — without the underlying engagement that makes advocacy persuasive to a skeptic.

The objection section is where the damage is most visible. Models soften opposition. When asked to generate objections, they will produce versions that are more polite, more diffuse, and less sharp than what serious critics actually argue. The rhetorical effect is the worst possible one: the reader who holds the strong version of the opposition reads the softened version and concludes the writer either doesn't understand the opposing view or has deliberately misrepresented it. Either reading destroys credibility.

Where the model is genuinely useful before you draft:

Stress-testing the claim. Before you commit to a position, ask the model to name four objections a hostile reader would raise. Ask it to make them sharp, and push back when it softens them: *don't worry about being fair to my view — make the strongest case the opposition would make.* The model generates sharper opposition than most writers generate alone, and the list is good raw material for the objection section.

Building the steel-manned opposition. Ask the model to write a one-paragraph version of the strongest case against your claim. Read it. Notice what it does that your draft has not yet engaged with. The gap between the model's steel-man and your current objection section is your revision target.

Where the model is genuinely useful after you have a draft:

Identifying unsupported assertions. Paste the draft and ask the model where claims are being made without supporting evidence — where you are asserting rather than demonstrating. Models are accurate at this. The list will be longer than you expect.

Where the model does damage:

Drafting any part of the argument, including the introduction. The model will produce something defensible-looking that lacks the specific commitment the genre requires. The flatness is not a surface problem. Editing rarely repairs it because the problem is underneath the prose, not in the prose.

Generating sources or statistics. Models produce confident citations that are sometimes fabricated. The position argument depends on evidence the reader can verify. Every citation has to be one you have read.

| Task | Verdict | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Stress-test the claim before drafting | Use it | The model surfaces objections sharper than most writers generate alone; raw material for the objection section. |
| Build the steel-manned opposition | Use it | Asking for the strongest one-paragraph case against your claim produces a target the draft has to actually engage. |
| Identify unsupported assertions in a finished draft | Use it | Models are accurate at flagging where you are asserting rather than demonstrating; the list will be longer than you expect. |
| Revise the objection section for prose flow | Cautious use | Sentence-level smoothing is fine; do not let it soften the objection itself into something more polite than what serious critics argue. |
| Draft any section of the argument | Do not | The output has the surface features of advocacy without the specific commitment the genre requires; editing rarely repairs the flatness. |
| Generate sources or statistics | Do not | Models produce confident citations that are sometimes fabricated; every citation must be one you have read. |

*Consult this before you open the model. The line is between work that sharpens your own commitment and work that substitutes for it.*

## What would change my mind

If a careful comparison of position arguments produced with extensive LLM assistance to arguments produced without it showed that the assisted versions were rated by readers as more persuasive on genuinely contested issues, I would revise the central concern of this chapter. The current evidence — admittedly partial, since the question is recent — points the other way: arguments where the writer is visibly invested in the specific claim tend to land harder with skeptical readers, and the model tends to flatten that investment. But the tools are changing fast, and the claim should be kept open and tested as they do.

## LLM Exercise — Chapter 12

**What you're building:** the analytical scaffold for your position argument — sharpened claim, evidence map, objection list — before you draft the prose.

**Tool:** [Claude.ai](https://claude.ai/) chat. A Claude Project is recommended.

**The prompt:**

```
I'm writing a 1,500–2,500 word position argument for a college writing
class. The genre requires a debatable claim, evidence-based defense,
and engagement with the strongest objections.

The issue I'm writing on: [name it in one sentence]
My tentative claim: [state it in one sentence]
The sources I have so far (with citations): [paste]

Do the following in order, one step at a time, waiting for my
response between steps.

1. Stress-test my claim. Is it specific enough? Is it debatable? Is
   it defensible with the evidence I have? Ask three diagnostic
   questions, one at a time. Push back on vagueness.

2. Construct the three strongest objections a hostile reader would
   raise against my claim. Do not soften them. After listing them,
   tell me which one I am least prepared to address, based on the
   sources I have shared with you.

3. For each of my supporting points, identify the strongest piece of
   evidence in my sources that supports it. If a point has weak or
   no supporting evidence in my sources, flag it — I may need more
   sources before I can defend that point.

4. Once I've responded, propose a one-sentence claim that incorporates
   the sharpening from steps 1–3.

Do not draft the argument. Do not write the introduction. The drafting
is mine to do.
```

**What this produces:** a sharpened claim, three sharp objections with a diagnosis of which you are least prepared to answer, and an evidence map by supporting point. After this, you draft the argument yourself.

**How to adapt this prompt:**

- *For ChatGPT or Gemini:* works as written.
- *For Cowork:* useful for arguments with many sources. Keep them in linked files Cowork can access.
- *For Claude Code:* not the right tool.

**The instructive failure to expect:** when the model constructs objections, it will sometimes hedge or refuse to fully argue the opposing side. Push back: *"Don't worry about being fair to my view. Make the strongest case the opposition would make. The fairness is my problem in revision."*

##  AI Wayback Machine
The ideas in this chapter didn't appear from nowhere. **Stuart Hall** was working on essays that argued positions in the open — "Encoding/Decoding," "The Whites of Their Eyes," "Notes on Deconstructing the Popular" — at the founding moment of cultural studies, decades before most people had heard of the position argument as a teachable genre.

**Run this:**

```
Who was Stuart Hall, and how does their work on encoding/decoding and
the position essay as cultural critique connect to writing a position
argument that engages a real opposition? Keep it to three paragraphs.
End with the single most surprising thing about their career or ideas.
```

Then search **"Stuart Hall"** on Wikipedia. See what the model got right, got wrong, or left out.

**Now make the prompt better.** Try one of these:

- Ask it to explain Hall's "encoding/decoding" model and how it changed the way scholars think about audience reception of media.
- Ask: "Hall famously wrote, 'There is no escape from politics of representation.' What position argument did that sentence open — and what did it close?"
