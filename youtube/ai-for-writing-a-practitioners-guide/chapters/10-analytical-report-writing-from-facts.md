*Letting the Facts Argue.*

---

In January 2018, a woman named Luciana Borio sat down to write a report. She was the director of medical and biodefense preparedness at the U.S. National Security Council. She had spent her career thinking about when, not if, the next major pandemic would arrive. The report she produced that month examined the country's readiness. It identified gaps. It named specific weaknesses in the federal response infrastructure. Every claim in it was supported by evidence.

Two years later, in the first months of 2020, most of what Borio's report had identified turned out to be the exact infrastructure that failed. By the end of that year, the United States had recorded more COVID-19 deaths than any other country in absolute terms — this despite having one of the most sophisticated medical systems in the world, spending more per capita on health care than any other nation, and having, two years earlier, a document that specified exactly what would break.[^1]

[^1]: Trevor Garcia's student analytical report, anthologized in the OpenStax *Writing Guide with Handbook*, aggregates comparative pandemic figures from the *Wall Street Journal*, *Washington Post*, Worldometer, and Just Security.

The document failed not because it was wrong. It failed because the people who needed to act on it didn't. The gaps it named remained unfilled for two years until the gaps became the story.

Here is the puzzle: Borio's report was right. It was specific. It was supported. It was verifiable. And it was ignored. So what is the point?

I will give you the answer now, and then spend the rest of the chapter explaining why the answer is not as discouraging as it sounds. The point of an analytical report is that it creates an object — a specific, checkable, revisable record of what the evidence said at a particular moment — that exists independently of whether anyone acts on it in the short term. The report did not prevent the pandemic failure. But it meant that the failure had a before. It meant that when the question *did anyone see this coming?* was asked, the answer was not silence. It meant that the gap between what was known and what was done was legible. And legibility is the first condition of accountability.

That is what the genre is for. Let the facts argue. Then let the argument stand in the record.

---

## What "let the facts argue" actually means

The phrase sounds simple. It is not. Facts do not argue. Facts sit there. The argument is made by a person who assembles facts, organizes them into a structure, draws inferences from the structure, and renders the whole thing in prose clear enough that a reader can follow the reasoning and check it. The analytical report is the genre that names this process as the primary job. Everything else — the voice, the structure, the citation style — is in service of one goal: making the inferential path from evidence to conclusion visible and reproducible.

This distinguishes the analytical report from what other genres do with facts. An op-ed uses facts as support for a stance the writer already holds. A personal essay uses facts as texture in a first-person narrative. A biography uses facts to reconstruct a life. The analytical report uses facts as the *primary material of argument*. The conclusion is supposed to emerge from the analysis, not precede it.

I will not pretend this is always what happens in practice. Writers are not blank slates. You will come to your topic with a tentative view, and that view will shape what you search for and what you notice. That is not cheating. It is how reasoning works. The discipline the genre requires of you is not *have no prior view* — that is impossible. The discipline is *let the evidence revise the prior view, and show the reader the revision*. If you arrive at your topic thinking pandemic failure happened for reason X, and then your evidence shows reasons Y and Z are equally important, the report has to reflect the complication. The evidence does not serve your thesis. Your thesis serves the evidence.

This is the thing that distinguishes an analytical report from advocacy with footnotes. The direction of authority runs from the evidence to the conclusion, not the other way. When you find yourself reaching for a fact because it supports the conclusion you have already decided on, rather than updating your conclusion because the facts point somewhere, you have left the genre.

## The interpretation problem

Most analytical report instruction begins with the distinction between fact and opinion, and the distinction is useful enough that I will state it before explaining why it is not sufficient.

A fact is a statement whose truth can be verified by checking external evidence — observation, measurement, document. *The United States spends more per capita on health care than any other high-income country* is a fact; it can be verified against OECD data. *The U.S. health care system is inefficient* is an opinion; it depends on what you mean by efficient and against which baseline you measure.

The problem is that almost no non-trivial claim in an analytical report is purely one or the other. Consider: *The U.S. response to COVID-19 was slower than India's in the early months of 2020.* Is this a fact or an opinion? It depends on what *response* means, what *slower* means, what *early months* means, and how you measure each. Once those terms are specified, the claim becomes verifiable. Before they are, it is contestable.

This is the situation the analytical report actually lives in. The claims that do real analytical work are interpretations of evidence — anchored in fact, requiring judgment to make. The genre's solution is not to eliminate interpretation. It is to specify the terms clearly enough that a reader can check the work.

Here is the precise move: every time you make a claim that is doing analytical work, ask whether you have defined your terms specifically enough that a hostile reader could verify the claim against the same evidence. If yes, you have an interpretation. If no, you have an opinion dressed in factual language. The difference is not always large. The discipline of locating it is what separates careful analysis from confident assertion.

## Three biases you cannot eliminate, only compensate for

Three biases operate on every analytical report. They cannot be removed. The discipline is to know they are present.

The first is confirmation bias: the tendency to seek and weight information that confirms what you already believe. It operates most heavily during topic selection and source selection. Without noticing it, you will find sources that agree with your tentative thesis, and you will weight them more heavily than sources that complicate it. The fix is not to arrive without a tentative thesis — that is impossible. The fix is to deliberately seek the best case against your tentative conclusion and engage with it explicitly. The strongest analytical reports do this visibly. They cite the most credible opposing source and grapple with it, not because they had to, but because doing so makes the eventual conclusion harder to dismiss.

The second is source bias: the position from which a source produces its information. A pharmaceutical company's report on its own drug carries a different kind of bias than independent academic research on the same compound. A government agency's data on its own performance has a different quality than an external watchdog's analysis of the same numbers. The bias is not a disqualifier — biased sources still produce verifiable information. The bias has to be named in the report so the reader can weight the evidence accordingly. A reader who cannot see the provenance of the evidence cannot evaluate the argument.

The third is what psychologists call false consensus: the tendency to assume that other people share your views more widely than they actually do. In an analytical report, this shows up when the writer treats a contested claim as obvious. The fix is to imagine a specific hostile reader — not a generic skeptic, but a person who would actively dispute the report's emerging thesis — and write toward what would persuade that person. Not what persuades you. You are already persuaded. The question is what persuades someone who isn't.

## Why the topic is always too big

There is a nearly universal failure mode in analytical reports, and it happens before any drafting begins. The topic is too large.

*The U.S. response to COVID-19* is too large. The report that works narrows it: *the U.S. response in 2020, focusing on three specific causes — reductions in expert personnel, equipment shortages, and policy decisions that delayed action*. The narrowing is what made the report writable. Three causes, each with specific evidence, each carrying a specific inference. Together, they add up to a conclusion the evidence can actually support.

*Climate change* is too large. *The carbon footprint of a specific manufacturing process in a specific industry* is workable. *Social media's effect on teenagers* is too large. *The relationship between one platform's algorithmic recommendation patterns and a specific measurable mental-health indicator in a specific age group over a specific period* is workable.

Here is the diagnostic test. Finish two sentences:

*This report analyzes [specific subject] by examining [three to five specific dimensions].*

*The evidence suggests [tentative direction of conclusion].*

If you cannot finish both sentences cleanly, the topic is still too large. The inability to complete the sentence is information. It is telling you that you do not yet know what you are analyzing. Do not draft. Narrow first.

The narrowing feels like a loss. You had a large, important topic, and now you have a smaller one. The trade is that the smaller topic is actually writable, actually arguable, actually verifiable. A report that examines three specific causes with specific evidence will teach a reader more than a report that gestures at a large subject with shallow coverage of twelve angles. Depth beats breadth in this genre. The important thing is not the size of the subject. The important thing is whether the inferential path from evidence to conclusion is visible. You cannot make that path visible in a topic you cannot fit on a map.

## What the five sections are doing

The analytical report has five parts, and each is doing a specific job.

The introduction names the subject, establishes why it is worth analyzing, and sets up what the report will examine. Sometimes it holds the full thesis until after the analysis. That is a legitimate choice. The introduction's job is to make the reader willing to follow the analysis, not to preempt it. If you announce the conclusion before earning it, you have written an op-ed with footnotes.

The background gives the reader what they need to follow the analysis — history, prior research, definitions of terms that will recur. The discipline here is ruthless: if a background section does not load directly into the analysis that follows, it is filler. Cut it.

The analysis is the body of the report. Each section develops one cause, one effect, one comparison, one sub-problem. Each section makes a claim, presents evidence, and draws an inference. This third move — the inference — is the one writers most consistently omit, and its absence is the most common structural failure I see. A section that presents evidence but does not state what follows from the evidence has done informational work, not analytical work. Every analysis section must answer the question *so what*. The *so what* is the inference. The inference is the analysis.

The synthesis pulls the body together. What does the evidence, collectively, suggest? Where does it converge and where is there remaining ambiguity? If the thesis was held back from the introduction, it lands here, with the full evidential structure behind it. If it was stated in the introduction, the synthesis restates it with the scaffolding now visible. The reader can see both the claim and what holds it up.

The conclusion states what follows from the analysis — implications for further research, for practice, for understanding — briefly, without recapping the body. The conclusion is not a summary. It is where the analysis has brought you.

## The voice

The analytical report is conventionally described as written in a neutral or objective tone. That framing is misleading enough that I want to say what it actually means.

Neutral does not mean without a voice. You are not impersonating a machine. Neutral means calibrated to be trustworthy to a reader who does not yet share your tentative conclusion. The voice is still yours. The calibration is the discipline.

Avoid first-person assertion where it adds nothing. *I think* and *I believe* are usually unnecessary in an analytical report — the entire report is your thinking, so adding the tag implies the claim is personal belief rather than evidential argument. Use first person when it describes something you specifically did: *I interviewed*, *I observed*, *I measured*. Those are genuinely useful because they describe your role in producing evidence.

Restrain adjectives. *Devastating*, *shocking*, *outrageous* are signals that you are reaching for emotional force instead of evidentiary force. Cut them. The facts, properly assembled, carry more force than your adjectives do. And the adjectives signal that the evidence may not be doing enough work on its own, which makes a careful reader trust you less.

Hedge precisely. *Suggests*, *indicates*, *is consistent with*, *does not yet establish* — these verbs are the genre's grammar for calibrated uncertainty. Use them. Their opposites — *proves*, *demonstrates beyond doubt*, *makes clear* — are rarely warranted, and when they are not warranted and you use them anyway, you have borrowed credibility you have not earned. The precision of your hedging is part of the argument. A reader who trusts your *suggests* in paragraph four will trust your *establishes* in paragraph twelve. A reader who has seen you overclaim once is on guard for the rest of the document.

Cite specifically. *According to a 2020 Pew Research Center survey, 64 percent of American adults report...* lands. *Studies show* does not. *Studies show* is the verbal equivalent of a citation you forgot to look up. It signals that you know a source exists somewhere but cannot be bothered to find it, and it gives the hostile reader exactly the opening they need to dismiss the claim.

Define every contested term before you use it analytically. *Recession*, *misinformation*, *bias*, *progress*, *efficiency* mean different things in different contexts. If your report hinges on one of these terms, specify the meaning the report is using before the analysis begins. Otherwise you will use the term in two different senses in two different paragraphs without noticing. The hostile reader will notice.

## What makes a source reliable

Not all sources are equal in what they establish, and the distinctions matter.

The strongest sources: peer-reviewed academic publications, major government data repositories with documented methodologies, reporting from outlets with editorial standards and fact-checking infrastructure, and research reports from established nonpartisan institutions. The political or institutional position of the source organization is relevant even for strong sources — Brookings and AEI both produce serious empirical work and hold different political orientations, and a reader weighing your evidence should know that. Name it.

Usable with qualification: trade publications, reporting from sector-specific outlets, analysis from credentialed named experts writing under their own names. Use these with explicit citation of the credential and acknowledgment of the potential interest.

Not usable as primary evidence: anonymous sources, social media posts (unless the post itself is the subject of analysis), material from advocacy organizations used as evidence of fact rather than as evidence of what the organization argues.

One category requiring special attention in 2026: language model outputs. Models generate plausible-sounding citations that may not exist. If you use a model at any stage of research, every citation it produces must be verified against the actual source before you cite it. The model's output is a lead to investigate, not a source to cite. The rule is absolute: every citation in your report must be one you have read.

---

## Where the model helps and where it doesn't

The analytical report is the genre where the model is most likely to produce something that looks like the work without doing it, and I want to say specifically what I mean.

The model can produce paragraphs with the texture of reasoning — *suggests* appears where it should, *evidence indicates* is placed correctly, the hedging is calibrated — but the inference was not earned from your evidence. The model has inferred from the statistical shape of analytical writing. You need to infer from your specific sources. Those are different operations, and the difference is not visible at a surface reading.

Where the model genuinely helps: stress-testing your interpretation. Paste an analytical paragraph and ask for the strongest alternative interpretation a reader who disagreed with your conclusion would offer of the same evidence. The model is reliable at generating that challenge, and the challenge almost always improves the paragraph. Ask it where your argument is relying on premises you have not explicitly defended — invisible assumptions are the most common structural failure in analytical writing, and an external reader, even an artificial one, can flag them faster than you can find them yourself. Paste a source passage alongside your paraphrase and ask whether your version has imported any claim the source does not make — model-paraphrase drift is a real problem, and having a check on it is useful.

Where the model does damage: generating sources (the citations will sound real and some will not exist), drafting the analysis (the inferential work looks present and isn't), and polishing the prose (the calibration you built in gets quietly upgraded — *suggests* becomes *shows*, *is consistent with* becomes *confirms*, and you do not notice until the reader who knows the literature points it out).

The rule is the same rule it always is: the model can help you think about work you have done. It cannot do the analytical work the genre requires, and when it simulates doing it, the simulation passes a quick read and fails a close one.

## LLM Exercise — Chapter 10

**What you're building:** a sharpened thesis and an evidence-organized outline for your analytical report, before you begin drafting.

**Tool:** [Claude.ai](https://claude.ai/) chat. A Claude Project is recommended if you want to keep your developing research notes alongside the analysis.

**The prompt:**

```
I'm writing a 2,000–3,500 word analytical report for a college writing
class. The genre uses fact-based analysis to examine causes, effects,
comparisons, or problems and possible solutions.

The subject I'm analyzing is: [name in one sentence]

The dimensions I'm planning to examine are: [list 3–5]

The sources I've gathered (with full citations) are pasted below.

Do the following in order, one step at a time, waiting for my
response between steps.

1. Read my source list. For each source, identify what kind of source
   it is and where it sits politically or institutionally — what its
   position is, in a sentence. Flag any source whose position I
   should explicitly name in my report.

2. Based on my subject and my dimensions, ask me four diagnostic
   questions about whether the dimensions are sharp enough. Where am
   I being vague? Where could a hostile reader make my categories
   collapse? Where am I assuming a comparison is fair without
   defending it?

3. Once I've answered, propose a tentative thesis sentence — one
   sentence — that names the subject, the dimensions analyzed, and
   the direction the evidence points. Do NOT overstate the certainty.

4. For each of my dimensions, identify the strongest piece of
   evidence in my source list that supports the tentative thesis and
   the strongest piece that complicates it. If a dimension has only
   supporting evidence and no complicating evidence, flag it — that
   may mean I need more sources before I can write that section
   credibly.

Do not draft the report. Do not write the introduction. The drafting
is mine.

Here are my sources:
[paste source list]
```

**What this produces:** a position on each of your sources, a sharpened set of dimensions, a tentative thesis, and an evidence map. After this, you draft the report yourself.

**How to adapt this prompt:**

- *For ChatGPT or Gemini:* works as written.
- *For Cowork:* useful if your sources are PDFs or long articles you have downloaded — tell Cowork to read the files before starting the conversation.
- *For Claude Code:* not the right tool.

**The instructive failure to expect:** when the model identifies a source's political position, it will sometimes be overconfident — describing the source as more biased or less biased than it actually is. Verify by reading the source's editorial pages or about page. The model's first guess at institutional position is reasonable but not authoritative.
