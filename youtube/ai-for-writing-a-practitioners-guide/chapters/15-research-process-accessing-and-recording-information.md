*How to Find What You Don't Yet Know You're Looking For.*

---

Here is a scene that will be familiar. You sit down with a topic and a deadline. You open a tab, type some words into a search engine, get back ten million results, click the third or fourth one, read it for five minutes, lose track of what you were looking for, follow a link to something tangentially interesting, lose more time, look at the clock, panic gently, and start again with slightly different keywords.

This is how most students research. It is also the process of someone who has not been shown that research is a craft with specific techniques, a recordable structure, and failure modes that are entirely predictable. Most of what goes wrong in a research paper was already going wrong in the first two hours of the search, before a single word of the paper was written.

The interesting thing about those failure modes is that they are all fixable, and they are all fixable in the same way: by converting the random walk into a deliberate sequence. That sequence is what this chapter is about.

## Two kinds of research

There are two broad approaches to academic research, and strong argumentative papers usually draw on both.

Library research is research into what others have already discovered, written, and published. Journal articles, books, government data, archived documents, news reports, well-curated databases. The library is much broader than the building — the relevant databases, digital archives, and high-quality freely available material online are all part of it. This is what most students mean when they say *research*, and it is where most student papers spend most of their time.

Field research is research you conduct yourself by observing, interviewing, surveying, or measuring. It produces primary sources — original data nobody else has collected on your specific question. Field research is harder to execute than library research for most student projects, but it can be the most important contribution of a strong paper precisely because it produces evidence the existing literature does not yet contain.

The choice between them is topic-dependent, and the discipline is to make it deliberately. Most papers use predominantly library research. Some benefit substantially from a small field component — a few interviews, a short observation, a brief survey — added to the library base. The question is not which is more prestigious. The question is which one produces the evidence your specific argument needs.

## The library research sequence

Most students search randomly — open the search bar, type a phrase, read whatever comes up. The specific alternative I want to describe produces better results in less time, and it has five steps in a fixed order.

**Generate keywords before you search.** Before you open a database, sit down and list the words and phrases you expect to find in good sources on your topic. Include synonyms. Include words that mean roughly the same thing but operate in different fields — *food sustainability* and *food security* mean slightly different things and the literatures using each are sometimes disjoint. Finding both requires knowing both terms. The point is that keyword generation is a thinking task, not a searching task. Do it before you open the search bar, not during the search when the results are pulling your attention in multiple directions at once.

**Search multiple databases.** Single-database research produces single-database results. The same query in JSTOR, Google Scholar, Web of Science, and a discipline-specific database will return overlapping but importantly different result sets. Your institution's library gives you access to most of these. Use at least three for any question you are taking seriously. The sources that appear in one database but not the others are sometimes the most useful, because they represent corners of the literature your competitors have not found.

**Read with two passes.** First pass: skim the abstract or executive summary, look at the conclusion, check the bibliography. This takes two minutes. Decide whether the source is worth reading in full. Second pass: full read of the sources that survived the first filter. This takes twenty minutes. Most students conflate the two passes and end up doing neither well — spending too long on sources that should have been filtered out, and not long enough on the ones that deserved full attention. The separation is the discipline.

**Follow the citation trails.** Every good academic source has a bibliography. The works it cites are themselves leads. The bibliography of a strong recent article is a map of the conversation that article is entering. If you find one strong recent source on your topic, its bibliography points you to the older work it is engaging with. Trace those citations and you are not doing library research anymore — you are following the intellectual history of the problem. This is one of the most productive research techniques and one of the least taught. It costs nothing except the willingness to open a bibliography and read it like a list of leads rather than a list of works cited.

**Save everything in a structured way.** Every source you find needs a full record: the citation, the URL or DOI, the date you accessed it, your credibility assessment, the specific claim or evidence you took from it, and where it fits in your developing argument. This is the research log, and it is the subject of an extended section later in this chapter. For now: the record has to be made while the source is in front of you, or you will spend time later trying to reconstruct what you read and why you thought it mattered.

## Evaluating sources — what the standard checklist catches and what it misses

A widely taught framework for source evaluation goes by the acronym CRAAP: Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose. It is a useful starting checklist, and I will describe each component. Then I want to describe what it does not catch, which is where most source-evaluation failures actually live.

*Currency* asks when the source was published or updated. In fields that move quickly — AI, climate, public health — a five-year-old source may be substantially outdated. In others — Shakespeare scholarship, ancient history — a fifty-year-old source may still be authoritative. The question is not the date alone; it is whether the date matters for the claim you are using the source to support.

*Relevance* asks whether the source actually addresses your specific question. The temptation is to use a high-quality source that is on a related but different topic — to treat *adjacent* as if it were *on point*. Resist it. A strong source that does not address your question is not evidence for your argument, no matter how well-regarded it is.

*Authority* asks who wrote the source and what the publishing venue's editorial process is. Peer-reviewed journal, major newspaper, and university press each imply different scrutiny. These controls are not guarantees of accuracy — peer review catches some errors and misses others — but they are evidence of the process the work underwent.

*Accuracy* asks whether the source's specific claims can be verified against other sources, whether it cites its own evidence, whether the methodology is defensible. A source that makes strong claims without citations is a different kind of evidence than one that is carefully grounded.

*Purpose* asks why the source was produced — to inform, persuade, sell, or advocate. Each purpose carries its own bias. The bias is not disqualifying; a piece written to persuade can still be evidence. But the purpose has to be named and the bias factored into how you use the source.

CRAAP catches most obvious problems. It misses three things that matter.

*Institutional bias of journals.* Even peer-reviewed journals have editorial perspectives. Two journals covering overlapping territory may publish in consistently different positions on contested policy questions. Reading the editorial board and the recent tables of contents reveals this. The CRAAP checklist does not ask.

*Author networks.* Some scholars cite each other heavily because they are collaborators, students of the same mentor, or ideological allies. Their citation network may not represent the field — it may represent a corner of it. Reading the acknowledgements sections and the bibliography overlap across your sources reveals this pattern.

*Settled framing on contested questions.* Some sources present contested questions as if they are settled — as if the opposing view has already been dismissed and the only task is to elaborate the winning position. The framing is itself a form of bias. You catch it by reading the source against scholars who would disagree with it. If you have only read sources on one side of a genuine debate, you have not done the research; you have done the confirmation.

| Dimension | What to look for | Where to look | What failure looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Currency** | Publication or last-update date appropriate to a field that moves at the source's speed | Front matter, citation entry, journal issue date, page footer | A five-year-old source used in a fast-moving field as if it were current |
| **Relevance** | Direct engagement with your specific question, not an adjacent one | Abstract, table of contents, conclusion, the sub-claims the source defends | An adjacent source treated as on-point because it is high quality |
| **Authority** | Author credentials, institutional affiliation, editorial process of the venue | Author bio, journal masthead, publisher page, peer-review statement | A confident source mistaken for a credentialed one |
| **Accuracy** | Verifiable claims, internal citations, methodology that can be inspected | In-text citations, methods section, footnotes, the source's own bibliography | Strong claims with no evidence chain back to a primary source |
| **Purpose** | Why the source was produced — to inform, persuade, sell, or advocate | Funder acknowledgements, "about" page, opening framing, author affiliations | Advocacy used as if it were neutral analysis |
| **Institutional bias** *(beyond CRAAP)* | Editorial perspective of the journal or publisher | Editorial board, recent tables of contents, the journal's history on contested topics | Treating peer review as if it dissolved every bias the venue carries |
| **Author networks** *(beyond CRAAP)* | Whether your sources cite each other heavily because they are a corner of the field, not the field | Acknowledgements, bibliography overlap across your sources, repeated co-authors | Reading one citation cluster and calling it the literature |
| **Settled framing** *(beyond CRAAP)* | Whether the source presents a contested question as if it were already decided | The opening framing, whether opposing positions are engaged or dismissed | Confirmation dressed as research — only one side of a real debate read |

## Field research — when and how

Some topics benefit from primary research you conduct yourself. Here are the main methods at student scale.

*Observation* means going somewhere — a classroom, a public space, a digital community — and taking structured notes on what you see. The discipline is in separating observation (what you saw) from interpretation (what you think it meant). Write them in separate columns or separate passes. The distinction is what makes observation a research method rather than a personal essay. Participant observation, where you participate in what you are observing, gives you access an outsider cannot reach. Non-participant observation, where you watch without participating, preserves an analytical distance the participant loses. Neither is superior; the choice depends on the question.

*Interviews* mean asking open-ended questions of one or more people and recording their answers. The questions should be open-ended where possible — *describe a time when...*, *what was your experience of...* — rather than yes/no, which produces yes/no answers. Record with permission, or take careful written notes. Transcribe key passages you might want to quote before you lose the context.

*Surveys* mean asking a defined population the same set of questions to produce comparable data. Survey design is its own craft. For most student projects, three to ten well-crafted questions administered to a defined group beats a longer survey administered to whoever happens to respond. The respondents have to be a defined population for the results to generalize to anything.

Two requirements before you begin field research. Get permission — tell people what the research is for, how the data will be used, and let them refuse or retract. And check whether your institution requires IRB review before you begin. Most informal student research falls below the threshold, but check anyway. Research involving children, prisoners, or potentially distressing topics usually requires it. The check is part of the practice, not a bureaucratic obstacle.

## The research log

The research log is the document that holds the whole project together. Without it, you carry everything in your head, lose some of it, and rebuild the rest under deadline pressure. The rebuilding is expensive, and the work you lose is usually the best-sourced and most carefully read material from the beginning of the project.

A useful log has three components per source.

The first is source information: the full citation in your required format, the URL or DOI, the date you accessed it, whether you have the full text or only an abstract, your initial credibility assessment. This is the administrative record. It takes two minutes per source. Without it, you will spend forty minutes later trying to find the same source again.

The second is specific claims and evidence: what the source actually says that you might use. Direct quotes with page numbers for passages you might want to cite. Paraphrases with page numbers for claims you might restate in your own voice. Statistics or specific data points with the source's own citations noted. This is not a summary of the whole source. It is a record of the parts of the source that are load-bearing for your argument.

The third is synthesis notes: where this source fits in the argument you are developing. Which other sources it agrees with, contradicts, or extends. What questions it leaves unanswered. What it makes you think about that you had not been thinking about before you read it.

Here is what a log entry looks like in practice, compressed, for a paper on AI-detection software in college courses:

> **Source:** Sadasivan, Vinu et al. "Can AI-Generated Text Be Reliably Detected?" *arXiv preprint*, May 2023. https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11156. Accessed 12 Oct. 2024. Preprint, not yet peer-reviewed; authors at University of Maryland CS department.
>
> **Claims/Evidence:** Authors test multiple AI-detection systems against adversarial rewrites. Find that light paraphrasing reduces detection accuracy from ~95% to ~50%. "Even the best-known detectors of LLM-generated text can have their accuracy reduced... by simple paraphrasing attacks" (p. 2).
>
> **Synthesis notes:** Pairs with Liang et al. 2023 on biased false-positive rates for non-native English speakers. Together these form the empirical spine of the position argument: detectors fail against motivated cheaters and produce false positives against innocent non-native speakers.

That entry took longer to write than dumping a URL into a notes file. It saves time later by a factor that is hard to overstate. When you are drafting three weeks from now, you will find exactly what you need without re-reading everything from the beginning. The discipline pays off in exactly the moments when you have the least time to pay it.

| Source information | Specific claims and evidence | Synthesis notes |
|---|---|---|
| Full citation in required format (MLA / APA / Chicago) | Direct quotes with page numbers — passages you might cite | Where this source fits in the argument you are building |
| URL or DOI | Paraphrases with page numbers — claims you might restate | Which other sources it agrees with |
| Date you accessed it | Statistics or data points with the source's own citations noted | Which other sources it contradicts or qualifies |
| Full text in hand, or abstract only | Tables or figures you might reproduce, with permissions noted | Questions the source leaves unanswered |
| Initial credibility assessment (CRAAP + the three misses) | The methodology used, if it bears on how you cite the claim | What new thinking it prompted that you had not been thinking about |

## Plagiarism — the traditional question and the version that matters now

Plagiarism in the traditional sense is presenting someone else's work as your own. It can be deliberate or unintentional. The unintentional versions are often more common and just as damaging: paraphrasing without citing, copying a key phrase without quotation marks, summarizing someone's argument without attribution. The fix is consistent: cite everything that is not yours. Summarize a source's ideas — cite. Paraphrase a passage — cite. Quote a phrase — use quotation marks and cite. When in doubt, cite.

The question that matters now is what counts as plagiarism when the source is a language model. The honest answer is that there is no settled convention across institutions. Some treat LLM use as plagiarism unconditionally. Some treat it as plagiarism only when the output is presented as the student's own without disclosure. Some treat it as permissible with disclosure in a footnote or cover note.

In the absence of settled convention, the practical discipline is transparency. If you used an LLM to brainstorm, draft, edit, or interrogate your work, say so. The disclosure protects you. It also makes the LLM's contribution part of the visible work rather than a hidden ingredient, which is the more honest relationship to have with any tool you actually used.

Some moves that are clearly problematic: submitting LLM-generated text as if it were your own writing; asking an LLM to paraphrase a source for you and then citing the source as if you had read it; using LLM-generated text as evidence in an argument that requires the evidence to be your own analysis. Some moves that are usually fine: using an LLM to brainstorm research directions and then doing the research yourself; using an LLM to identify gaps in a draft and filling those gaps yourself; using an LLM as an interrogation partner that asks you questions about your developing argument.

The line between the two categories is fuzzier than I would like. The discipline: ask, of each LLM use, whether you would be comfortable telling your instructor exactly how you used this tool. If yes, probably fine. If no, you have a problem.

## Where the model helps and where it damages the research process

Library research is the part of the writing workflow where LLMs are most easily integrated and most easily misused, because the appearance of having found sources is easy to produce and the reality of having read them is hard to fake from the outside.

Useful: keyword expansion. The model is good at suggesting search terms you have not thought of. Use the suggestions as a starting point for database searches, then verify the results yourself. The model is generating vocabulary, not retrieving literature.

Useful: source triage. Paste an abstract and ask whether the source is likely to be useful for your question. The model often gives a reasonable assessment in seconds. This is a first filter, not a substitute for reading.

Useful: research log organization. After you have read a source, dictate or type your notes to the model and ask it to help organize them into the three-component log structure. The thinking is yours. The model formats it.

Useful: citation formatting. The model is reasonably good at converting citations between MLA, APA, and Chicago. Verify against the official style guide before submitting.

Damaging: asking the model to find sources. The model will invent plausible-sounding citations. This is not an occasional bug — it is a structural property of how these models work. The model does not distinguish between citations it retrieved and citations it fabricated. You cannot tell from the output which is which. Use it for keyword generation, then run those keywords in actual databases.

Damaging: asking the model to read sources you have not given it. If you ask the model about a paper you have not shared with it, you get an answer based on training data, which may or may not match the actual source. What you get is the model's reconstruction of what the paper probably says, not what it says.

Damaging: outsourcing synthesis. The model can produce synthesis-shaped prose without doing synthesis thinking. If you cannot identify, in a model-produced synthesis paragraph, what the specific claim is and why each source belongs in it, the paragraph is decorative. Read every synthesis claim the model produces against your own developing understanding of the literature. If the synthesis claim is not one you can defend from your own reading, it is not yours to use.

| Use case | What the model actually does | What the model does *not* do | Risk if you treat the output as finished work |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Useful — keyword expansion** | Generates plausible synonyms and cross-field terms you have not thought of | Retrieve actual sources for those terms | None, provided you run the keywords in a real database before trusting any title |
| **Useful — source triage** | Reads an abstract you paste in and offers a fast usefulness assessment for your question | Read the full paper or the sources the abstract cites | Triage replaces the second-pass read, and weak sources slip past the first filter |
| **Useful — log organization** | Takes notes you dictate or paste and structures them into the three-column research log | Generate the claims, evidence, or synthesis itself — the thinking remains yours | The model invents a "synthesis" line you did not write and you fail to notice |
| **Useful — citation formatting** | Converts entries between MLA, APA, and Chicago; fixes punctuation and casing | Verify that the source itself exists or that the metadata is correct | A correctly *formatted* citation for a nonexistent source ends up in your bibliography |
| **Damaging — generating sources** | Produces plausible-sounding citations indistinguishable from real ones | Retrieve them from a database — fabrication is a structural property, not an occasional bug | Invented citations enter your bibliography; instructor finds them in five minutes |
| **Damaging — reading sources you have not shared** | Reconstructs what a paper "probably says" from training data | Read the actual paper | You cite a position the author does not hold or a result they did not publish |
| **Damaging — drafting synthesis** | Produces synthesis-shaped prose with the right rhythm and connectors | Do the synthesis thinking — connect claims you understand to evidence you read | A paragraph you cannot defend ends up in the draft because it sounds right |
| **Damaging — global prose revision** | Rewrites your paragraphs in a more polished register | Preserve your specific argument or your specific voice | Argument quietly drifts; precision dissolves into smoothness; you lose what was yours |

## What would change my mind

If careful tracking of student research projects showed that students who used structured research logs produced papers no stronger than students who used ad-hoc note-taking — that the log discipline was overhead without payoff — I would revise the chapter's central emphasis. The evidence from teaching points the other way: students who keep logs produce stronger papers and lose less work. But the claim should remain testable, and I want to name where the evidence could go the other way.

The remaining puzzle I cannot resolve cleanly: some students with messy, ad-hoc research processes produce wonderful papers because their thinking is strong. Some students with immaculate logs produce mechanical papers because the log organized data but did not produce insight. The log helps. It does not substitute for thinking. I do not yet know how to teach the difference in a textbook section, and I am not sure it is teachable that way at all.

## LLM Exercise — Chapter 15

**What you're building:** the research log for your current project, structured for use across the full research process.

**Tool:** [Claude.ai](https://claude.ai/) chat, with [Cowork](https://claude.ai/) for file management if you have many sources.

**The prompt:**

```
I'm conducting research for an academic paper. The topic is [name it]
and my tentative thesis is [state it].

I have read the following sources (citations and notes below).

Do the following, in order, one step at a time, waiting for my
response between steps.

1. For each source I've listed, suggest the three things that need
   to be in my research log: the source's specific claims I should
   record, the source's specific evidence I should record, and the
   source's position in the larger conversation.

2. Identify gaps in my source list. Where in the literature have I
   not yet looked? Suggest specific keywords or specific scholars I
   should search for. Do not invent citations — only suggest search
   directions.

3. For each source, identify how it relates to the others. Where do
   they reinforce each other? Where do they contradict? Where could
   the synthesis between two sources produce an argument neither
   makes alone?

4. Based on my tentative thesis and the source set, what is the
   single most important piece of evidence I am missing? What
   specific kind of source — primary research, statistical data, an
   opposing scholarly voice — would most strengthen the paper?

Do not draft the paper. Do not produce a thesis. The research is mine.

Here are my sources:
[paste source list with brief notes]
```

**What this produces:** a structured research log, an evidence-gap analysis, and a clear direction for the next round of source-finding. After this, you continue the research yourself.

**How to adapt this prompt:**

- *For Cowork:* if your sources are in separate PDFs, have Cowork read them before the conversation. Cowork can also maintain the running research log as a file you update across sessions.
- *For ChatGPT or Gemini:* works as written.
- *For Claude Code:* not the right tool for the conversation, but useful for managing the file structure of a longer research project.

**The instructive failure to expect:** when you ask the model to suggest gaps in your source list, it will sometimes name specific scholars who do not exist or attribute specific positions to scholars who did not hold them. Treat every named scholar as a search lead to verify, not a citation to use.

##  AI Wayback Machine
The ideas in this chapter didn't appear from nowhere. **Zora Neale Hurston** was working on *Mules and Men* — a field-research project that documented Black Southern folklore on its own terms, decades before participatory anthropology was a recognized method and decades before most people had heard of the research process as a craft.

**Run this:**

```
Who was Zora Neale Hurston, and how does their work on ethnographic
field research and the work of recording a community in its own voice
connect to the research process — accessing and recording information
from real sources? Keep it to three paragraphs. End with the single
most surprising thing about their career or ideas.
```

Then search **"Zora Neale Hurston"** on Wikipedia. See what the model got right, got wrong, or left out.

**Now make the prompt better.** Try one of these:

- Ask it to describe the specific scene in *Mules and Men* where Hurston enters a Florida turpentine camp, and what she did differently than her Columbia anthropology training would have prescribed.
- Ask: "Hurston's anthropology training came from Franz Boas. What did she keep, and what did she throw out, when she did her own fieldwork?"
- Add the constraint: "Answer using at least one direct quotation from *Mules and Men* — Hurston's voice on the page."

What changes? What gets better? What gets worse?
