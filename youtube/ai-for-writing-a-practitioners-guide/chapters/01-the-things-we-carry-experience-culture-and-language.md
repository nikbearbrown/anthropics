*What You Already Have Before You Write a Word.*

---

Here is something that should bother you, if you think about it carefully enough.

You already know how to write.

I don't mean this as encouragement. I mean it as a factual claim that requires an explanation, because if it's true — and I think it is — then the existence of this course, and of writing courses in general, becomes a puzzle rather than an obvious thing. What are we actually doing here? What is being taught, and to whom, and why does anyone need it?

Let me sharpen the puzzle before I try to dissolve it. You have been producing written language for the better part of your life. Not occasionally, and not for practice — for real, with real audiences, with real consequences when it didn't work. You have sent a message to someone you were falling in love with and rewritten it three times because the first version was too much and the second was too little and you needed it to land in exactly the right place. You have written a caption for a photograph where the joke had to land in under ten words or it was worse than nothing. You have written in a group chat in a voice that the people in that chat recognized as yours, that was different from the voice you used with your parents, that was different again from the voice you used in the essay you turned in for your English class. You have been doing this work constantly, across dozens of contexts, with genuine skill.

So why does almost every student who walks into a writing class arrive convinced they cannot write?

I've thought about this for a long time, and I think the answer is that somewhere along the way, most students absorb a distinction that isn't real. The writing in your pocket — the writing that actually works on the people it needs to work on — gets classified as not-quite-writing. Informal. Doesn't count. "Real writing" gets located somewhere else, in some other register you haven't learned yet, guarded by a credential. The practical consequence is that you walk into a class carrying a large and functional set of tools and you don't know you're carrying them, because you've been told the tools don't count.

This chapter is about putting them back in the ledger. Not as a motivational exercise. As a practical one, because the skills you already have are the foundation that everything in this course gets built on. You can't build on a foundation you haven't looked at.

## The list inside the list

There's a story by Tim O'Brien — a Vietnam veteran writing about his time with the 23rd Infantry Division — called "The Things They Carried."[^1] The story is built on a list: what did the soldiers carry in their packs? Some items are obvious. Rifle, ammunition, rations, poncho, socks. Those are the standard-issue items. They appear on every list because they have to. They don't tell you anything about who the soldier is.

[^1]: Tim O'Brien, *The Things They Carried* (Houghton Mifflin, 1990).

Then O'Brien starts listing the individual items. One soldier carries letters from a woman named Martha, folded so many times the paper has gone soft. Another carries a Bible. Another carries comic books. One carries a thumb taken from the body of a fifteen-year-old enemy soldier. And one — and O'Brien writes this in exactly the same grammatical structure as the canteens and the ammo — carries his grandmother's distrust of the white man.

That last item is the one I want you to sit with for a moment. O'Brien puts a psychological inheritance — a specific distrust shaped by a specific history — in the same list as physical objects. He uses the same verb. *Carrying* is not just what your hands do. It's also what you bring into a room that you didn't consciously choose to bring, that affects everything you do there, that you may not even be fully aware of as a thing you're doing.

The pack is both.

You carry things in that wider sense into every writing situation. Some are standard-issue: you've had teachers, you've written papers, you've been graded. Every student in every class shares roughly those items. But most of what shapes how you write isn't on the standard-issue list. It's the specific list. The language your family spoke at the dinner table, and whether it changed when company came over. The teacher in third grade who put a red X through your poem and the one in seventh who wrote *this is good* in the margin and meant it. The Discord server where you developed a voice that has nothing to do with the voice you use with adults. The accent you grew up speaking and whether anyone ever told you to lose it.

Those items are in the pack whether you name them or not. They will affect what you write this semester. The question is whether you've thought carefully enough about what's in there to actually use it.

## The room is not neutral

Now I want to say something that writing instruction tends to skip over, probably because it is uncomfortable.

You are not the only one carrying a pack into the room. The instructor has one. Every other student has one. And the room itself has a kind of acoustic — what sounds correct in that room, what sounds marked or wrong, what gets heard as sophisticated and what gets heard as careless — is shaped by all of those packs together, and not equally. Some packs set the default. Others sound like departures from it.

This is not a complaint. It's a description of how language communities work, and pretending classrooms are exempt from it is just false. Any community recognizes its own members by how they communicate. The shorthand a group of doctors use in a hospital cafeteria — *the workup, the pre-op, the bounce-back* — marks them to each other. Skateboarders, lawyers, competitive chess players, military historians — every community has a register that sounds natural to the inside and like a code to the outside. You already know this, because you already belong to several communities, and you navigate the differences in their registers every day without thinking about it.

What you're being asked to learn in this course is a new register: the one academic writing uses. It has actual conventions — how to make a claim, how to deploy evidence, how to acknowledge counterarguments, how to cite sources so a reader can check your work. These conventions are worth learning. They are not arbitrary. They do real intellectual work. And the credential you're here to earn is partly a signal that you've learned them.

| Community | Written form | Key conventions |
|---|---|---|
| Hospital medicine | Patient charts, progress notes, sign-out lists | Compressed shorthand (*workup*, *pre-op*, *bounce-back*); fixed headings (HPI, A/P); legally durable timestamps |
| Law | Briefs, motions, memos | Numbered points and headings; citation to authority in fixed form; concession-and-counter structure |
| Skateboarding | Forum posts, zines, video captions | Trick names as nouns and verbs; first-person scene voice; brand and spot references that mark the insider |
| Academic writing | Papers, articles, reviews | An explicit claim near the top; evidence with citation; a counterargument acknowledged and answered |

But here is the distinction I want you to carry through this entire semester: *entering* a new discourse community is not the same as *replacing* your old ones. That word — replacing — is the wrong word, and when writing instruction implies it, it does damage. What it produces is a writer who is technically profished in academic prose and has lost the voice that made them interesting. The sentences parse. Nothing is alive in them.

The better model is accumulation. Each community you learn to write for adds a layer to what you can do. The 2 a.m. message doesn't go away. The group chat voice doesn't go away. They sit underneath the new layer, supporting it, and the interesting writing — the writing that does something the fully-conventionalized academic version can't do — usually comes from a writer who is drawing on more than one layer at once.

The pack doesn't get emptied. It gets heavier. That is the whole architecture of what we're doing here.

## The thing in every pack now that wasn't there five years ago

There is one item that is now in almost every student's pack that simply wasn't there when I started thinking about how to write this chapter. And I'm not going to be indirect about it, because being indirect would be a kind of intellectual dishonesty.

You have used a large language model to write something. Maybe once. Maybe continuously, for years. You have seen what that output looks like. You have had to make some decision about what to do with it — use it, change it, discard it, argue with it, copy it without reading it carefully. That history is in your pack now. It is part of your literacy story whether you've named it yet or not.

Let me tell you what I think is actually going on with these tools, because there is a lot of noise in the conversation about them and I want to try to get at the signal.

A large language model is very good at producing text that sounds like writing. It has been trained on an enormous amount of text, and it has learned what writing sounds like with a fidelity that is genuinely startling. What it has not learned, because it cannot learn it, is what you specifically think, what you specifically carry, what you specifically know about the thing you are writing about. It can produce a plausible version of a generic writer writing a generic essay. It cannot produce a plausible version of you, because it has never been you.

This matters for a reason that will become clearer as the course goes on, but I'll state it now as a hypothesis: the thing that makes a piece of writing interesting — the thing that makes a reader feel like they're in contact with a mind, not just processing information — is specificity. The specific item in the pack. The specific turn of phrase that could only have come from someone who grew up in a particular place with a particular family speaking a particular language in a particular moment. When you hand that task to a model, you get fluent prose with the specificity averaged out. It sounds like writing. It is writing the way a photo of a meal is food.

None of this means the tool is useless. It means the tool is useful for specific things and not useful for others, and knowing the difference is the new literacy this moment requires. The chapters ahead treat LLM use as integrated rather than forbidden or ignored. Every chapter ends with an exercise that uses Claude or another model. Those exercises are designed to use the model the way a good interlocutor works — something to think against, something that can generate a draft you then have to argue with, something that can ask you questions that reveal what you haven't figured out yet. They are not designed to produce output you submit as your own.

The difference between those two uses is large. It will become clearer as the chapters progress. For now, the only thing I am asking is this: *notice*. Notice when you reach for the model and why. Notice what you ask it for. Notice what you accept without reading it carefully, and what you push back on. Notice when it makes you a better writer — when it gives you a first draft that shows you what you were trying to say — and when it makes you a more confident producer of prose that doesn't actually work.

| Using the model as an interlocutor | Using the model as a ghostwriter |
|---|---|
| Ask it to push back on your argument and name the strongest objection | Ask it to write the argument and submit what it gives you |
| Paste your messy draft and ask which sentence is doing the most work | Ask for a polished version and copy the polished version |
| Ask it to interview you with one question at a time about a topic you barely know | Ask it for three paragraphs on a topic you barely know |
| Use its first attempt as a thing to disagree with — write the disagreement in your own words | Use its first attempt as the final answer and patch typos |
| Ask it to surface what your draft assumes that you haven't said out loud | Ask it to fill in the parts of your draft that are missing |

The noticing is the first skill. Everything else is downstream of it.

## What the pack is not

I should prevent a misreading, because it is a tempting one.

This chapter is not arguing that everything you carry is equally useful in every context. It is not arguing that the writing that works in a group chat also works in a formal essay, or that the voice you found on the internet is the voice you should use when you want a hostile reader to take you seriously. Those are different contexts with different demands, and pretending otherwise would be as wrong as pretending the demands don't exist.

The argument is more specific than that. It is against a particular pedagogical move that happens — explicitly sometimes, but more often by implication — in writing instruction: the suggestion that the correct thing to do with the writing you already do is to set it aside, because it's informal, or incorrect by standard conventions, or belongs to a community that the academic world doesn't recognize as legitimate. That move is wrong in a specific way. It's wrong because it severs you from the only thing that writing actually runs on, which is your knowledge of what language can do and your felt sense of when it's working.

Learning formal conventions and keeping your existing voice are not in tension. They are both things you need, and the task of a writing education is to teach you to hold them at the same time and to develop the judgment to know when to deploy which one. A writer who knows only one register is a writer with one tool. The pack is supposed to get heavier, not lighter.

## Three things to do before the next chapter

None of these is a traditional assignment. They are acts of noticing, and they will make everything that follows more useful.

The first: sit down and spend ten minutes writing five items in your pack in O'Brien's wider sense. Not objects. Inherited things. The phrase your family uses that nobody outside the family uses. The first piece of writing you remember being proud of, and why. The moment, if there was one, when you decided you were not a writer. The voice you developed in some online context that is not the voice you use with authority figures. You know what's in there. Write it down.

The second: which item do you think will be most useful in college-level writing, and which will be most in the way? You are probably going to be wrong about both answers. That's fine. The point is having a guess at the start so you have something to measure against what you actually learn.

The third: be honest about the model in your pocket. When did you first use one to write something? What was the thing? What did you do with what it produced? You don't have to share this with anyone. But you should know the answer, because your relationship to that tool is going to be a live question throughout this course.

You are about to spend a semester learning to write in registers that are not yet fully yours. The claim this chapter is making is that you do that more effectively when you know what you already have. Pack honestly. The rest is craft.

## A note about AI

The chapter's title is about what you carry. The model carries nothing.

A large language model can produce prose about identity, culture, language, and lived experience in a register that sounds like a writer who has carried specific things into specific rooms. It has not. The biographical specificity this chapter asks you to put in the ledger — the phrase your family uses when someone is being dramatic, the moment you decided you were not a writer, the voice you found in some online space — those are not in the model's pack because the model has no pack. It has the synthesized averages of millions of other people's packs, which is not the same thing.

Where the model genuinely helps: after you have written your list of five items, you can paste the list and ask it to push back on which items you might be underweighting. Models are sometimes useful as a sparring partner against your own modesty about what you carry. You can ask it to suggest kinds of writing your daily life involves that you haven't counted yet — birthday cards, group-chat moderation, captions that had to land — and check the suggestions against your actual life.

Where the model does damage: writing the items for you, or "improving" the way you describe them. The pack is the one thing in this course that has to be yours specifically. A reflection on your literacy that has been smoothed by the model is no longer a reflection on your literacy. It is a reflection on the model's idea of someone like you.

## LLM Exercise — Chapter 1

**What you are building:** a written inventory of your writing pack, produced through an interrogation rather than free-writing. The first piece of self-knowledge that the longer assignments ahead will draw on.

**Tool:** [Claude.ai](https://claude.ai) chat. A Claude Project works well if you plan to return to this material across the semester — the model will have access to your earlier answers when later exercises reference them.

**The prompt:**

```
I'm starting a college writing course. The course's opening chapter
asks me to inventory what I'm "carrying" into the classroom — the
language, literacy, and cultural experiences that will shape how I
write.

I want you to interview me. Ask me one question at a time. Wait for my
answer before asking the next. Cover at least these areas, but follow
up on anything I say that seems to have more underneath it:

- the language(s) spoken in my household growing up, and any
  differences between them
- the first writing I remember producing for a reader other than a
  teacher
- a moment a teacher made me feel like a writer, and a moment a
  teacher made me feel like I wasn't one
- the place online where I learned to write in a voice
- a piece of writing by someone else that I've returned to more than
  once
- the first time I used an LLM to help me write something, and what
  that felt like

After we've gone through these, summarize what you've learned about my
"pack" in 200–300 words. Do not write any prose for me. Do not tell
me what to do with this information. Just give it back to me as a
clean inventory I can carry into the rest of the course.
```

**What this produces:** a 200–300 word inventory of your specific literacy history, generated through your own answers, written in the model's voice but built entirely from your specifics. Save it. Later chapters will ask you to draw on it.

**Adapting the prompt:**

For ChatGPT or Gemini, the prompt works as written. For Cowork, tell it to save each of your answers to a file called `my-pack.md` as the conversation progresses, so you can revise the inventory at the end of the course. For Claude Code: wrong tool — this is a conversation, not a code task.

**The instructive failure to expect:** the model will sometimes ask all the questions at once instead of one at a time. When that happens, say: *"Stop. One question at a time. Ask me only the first one and wait for my answer."* The discipline of refusing premature output is part of the literacy you are about to develop. Notice that you had to enforce it.

##  AI Wayback Machine
The ideas in this chapter did not appear from nowhere. **Geneva Smitherman** was doing the work of naming African American English as a complete and durable carried language tradition — *Talkin and Testifyin*, 1977 — in explicit defiance of the claim that Standard American English was the only legitimate vehicle for thought. Decades before the discourse about "code-switching" entered mainstream conversation, Smitherman was documenting what students actually carry into classrooms, and what it costs them when they are told to leave it at the door.

**Run this:**

```
Who was Geneva Smitherman, and how does their work on Black English and
the language varieties we carry into the classroom connect to the idea
that what students bring with them into a writing course is an asset
rather than a problem to be corrected? Keep it to three paragraphs.
End with the single most surprising thing about their career or ideas.
```

Then search **"Geneva Smitherman"** on Wikipedia. See what the model got right, got wrong, or omitted.

**Now make the prompt better.** Try one of these:

- Ask the model to explain what "signifying" means in Smitherman's framework, with a concrete example from speech or written text.
- Ask: "What is the cost — political, cognitive, rhetorical — of being asked to leave your home variety at the classroom door?"
- Add a constraint: "Answer in a register that respects the language variety you're describing."

What changes when you add the constraint? What gets better? What gets worse? Why might that matter for the claim this chapter is making?
