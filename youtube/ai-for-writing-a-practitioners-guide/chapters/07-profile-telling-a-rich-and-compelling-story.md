*Finding the One Sentence That Holds a Person Together.*

---

On the morning of March 7, 1965, a twenty-five-year-old organizer named John Lewis was at the front of a line of six hundred people walking across the Edmund Pettus Bridge in Selma, Alabama. The crossing was peaceful. On the far side of the bridge, Alabama state troopers in gas masks were waiting. When the front of the line reached them, the troopers attacked with batons and tear gas. Lewis was beaten so badly his skull was fractured.

Fifty-five years later he died in Washington, D.C., having served seventeen terms in the U.S. House of Representatives. When the Librarian of Congress, Carla Hayden, wrote his remembrance, she had a six-decade public life to work with. The Freedom Rides, the march to Montgomery, the friendship with Robert Kennedy, the forty-five-entry arrest record, the Presidential Medal of Freedom, the lying in state. Six decades.

She chose two words — Lewis's phrase *good trouble* — and built the entire piece around them.[^1]

[^1]: Carla D. Hayden, ["Remembering John Lewis: The Power of 'Good Trouble,'" *Library of Congress Blog*, 19 July 2020](https://blogs.loc.gov/loc/2020/07/remembering-john-lewis-the-power-of-good-trouble/).

Here is the puzzle I want you to sit with: why is the piece better because of what it leaves out?

The profile that tried to cover everything would have produced something comprehensive and, for exactly that reason, forgettable. You would have learned the shape of a life. You would not have felt the weight of it. Hayden chose two words and built everything around them. She gave you less. You understood more.

That is not a paradox. That is the mechanism of the genre. And the mechanism is what this chapter is about.

## What the profile is not

I need to be precise about what a profile is, because it gets misunderstood — including by people who write them.

A profile is not a biography. A biography tries to be comprehensive. Its job is to give you the full record: the chronology, the relationships, the context, everything that happened and why. A biography that leaves out half its subject's life is a bad biography. A profile that leaves out half its subject's life is just a profile. Selectivity is not a deficiency in the genre. It is the genre.

A profile is not a hagiography. The job is not to make the reader admire the subject. The job is to show the reader one specific thing about a person that they would not otherwise have seen. Sometimes that produces admiration. Sometimes it produces something more complicated. What the writer is after is not the reader's approval of the subject — it is legibility. A reader who finishes the profile should understand something they did not understand before.

Once you have those two cleared away — it is not biography, it is not flattery — you can see what is actually there.

## The three things a profile is made of

A profile has three structural components. They are not interchangeable, and they do not function in the same way.

The first is the *spine*: the central insight about the subject, the thing the profile is arguing. Note that word — arguing. The spine is not a fact. A fact about Lewis is "he was a civil rights leader." That is true. It is not a spine. A spine sounds more like this: *Lewis spent his life converting personal physical danger into political moral force, and he never stopped offering his body as the instrument of that conversion.* That is an insight. It is arguable. It commits the writer to specific evidence and forces the rejection of evidence that doesn't serve it. It is the kind of claim a reader could disagree with. That capacity for disagreement is how you know you have a spine and not just a fact.

The second is the *angle*: the specific lens through which the spine becomes visible. The angle for Hayden's Lewis piece is "good trouble" — Lewis's own phrase for the deliberate, principled willingness to be arrested, beaten, or killed in service of a cause. The angle is not the spine. The angle is the entry point that makes the spine accessible. A different writer could have used a different angle — Lewis's return to Selma every year, his relationship to the younger generation of organizers who found him too cautious, his handwritten notes to constituents — and arrived at a different but equally defensible spine. The angle is the writer's contribution. It is the decision that makes this profile this profile and not some other profile.

The third is the *evidence*: interview, field observation, and secondary research. Evidence is what earns the right to make the claim. Without it you have an opinion. With it you have an argument.

There is a useful way to think about the direction of construction here. The writer builds from the top down — from spine to angle to evidence selection, deciding what material the spine requires and what it excludes. The reader experiences it from the bottom up — encountering the evidence first, moving through the angle, arriving at the spine. What this means in practice is that the spine has to be identified before the draft, even if it was discovered after the research. The spine is the decision that governs every other decision in the piece.

## Where the angle comes from

Most student profiles fail in the same way, and once you can see the failure you can see it approaching before it has happened.

The student picks an interesting subject. Conducts an interview. Takes some notes. Gathers some background. Returns to the desk and writes a profile that covers the subject's life in roughly chronological order, includes several direct quotes, and concludes with a paragraph about inspiring qualities. The profile is competent. It says nothing the reader could not have found on the subject's LinkedIn page.

The failure is not execution. The failure is that the student never identified the angle. Without an angle, there is no principle for selecting evidence. Every fact about the subject seems equally relevant, because no particular argument is being made. The result is a list of facts that adds up to description — *here is who this person is* — rather than claim — *here is the one thing about this person that, once you see it, you cannot unsee*.

Here is a practical test. After the interview, the field notes, and the secondary research, try to finish this sentence in twenty words or fewer:

*The thing I want my reader to understand about my subject that they would not otherwise have seen is...*

If you cannot finish it in twenty words, you have material. You do not have an angle. Material is necessary. It is not sufficient.

Where does the angle come from in the material? Almost without exception: from the surprise. The thing the subject said that you did not expect. The moment the field notes caught that the interview did not mention. The piece of secondary research that made a small detail suddenly significant. The boring interpretation of your subject — the obvious interpretation, the interpretation that any reasonable person would arrive at with no reporting — is not the angle. The angle is what you found that contradicts or complicates the obvious interpretation.

If the angle does not surprise you, it probably will not surprise your reader.

## Three kinds of research, and what each one is for

The profile requires three kinds of material. Each does something the others cannot do, and they are not substitutes for each other.

The interview gives you the subject's own voice. The actual words, in the actual rhythms, that no secondary source can replicate. Most student profiles contain too few interview quotes, and the quotes they do include are too tidy — which is usually because the student did not record the conversation. When you record and transcribe, you get the false starts, the digressions, the moment the subject went on a tangent that contradicted something they said earlier. That is where the angle often lives. The transcript is not a polished document. It is a mine. The vein you are looking for is the moment the subject said something they did not mean to say, or revealed something they did not know they were revealing.

The field observation gives you the things the subject would not think to tell you. They did not know you were watching when they shifted their weight during the difficult question. They did not notice that they rearranged the same three objects on their desk four times. Half an hour of careful field notes will produce more usable material than two hours of interview, because the interview is mediated — the subject is choosing what to present — and the field notes are not.

There is a discipline to field notes that takes practice. Do not interpret in the notes. Do not write *she seemed nervous*. Write *she shifted weight from her left foot to her right twice while answering, and straightened the pen on her desk*. The interpretation is your job at the desk. The notes are for the observable specifics that nobody else would have caught, because nobody else was standing where you were standing at that moment.

The secondary research sets the context that makes your angle land — the trend, the statistic, the historical pattern that gives your subject's particular position its weight. The secondary research is most powerful when it is not about your subject specifically but about the world your subject is surviving inside. The profile of the last independent video rental store in rural Alabama is not interesting because of what prior articles have written about the store's owner. It is interesting because of what the secondary research reveals about rural economic decline, the collapse of physical media, and streaming's conquest of everything. The secondary research is the wall the subject is standing against. Without that wall, the stubbornness is just stubbornness. With it, the stubbornness is an argument.

| Research type | What it produces | What it cannot produce | Common student error |
|---|---|---|---|
| Interview | The subject's own voice — the actual words, rhythms, and digressions a recorder catches | The things the subject would not think, or chooses not, to say | Few quotes, tidied up; the writer did not record, so the surprise was lost |
| Field observation | Unrepeatable sensory specifics — gestures, objects, the room, the body in time | A second observer's version; a moment you weren't there for | Interpretation rather than observation — "she seemed nervous" instead of "she shifted weight twice" |
| Secondary research | The wall the subject is standing against — the trend, statistic, or pattern that gives the particular its weight | The texture of this person in this room on this day | Background on the subject specifically, rather than the world the subject is surviving inside |

## The tonal trap

There is a specific way that profiles go wrong at the sentence level, and it is almost impossible to see in your own first draft.

The trap is overt admiration. The subject is admirable; the writer wants the reader to admire them; the prose tells the reader, repeatedly, how admirable the subject is. *She was remarkable.* *His dedication was inspiring.* *Her resilience in the face of adversity was extraordinary.* The result reads like a press release. The reader, sensing that they are being told what to feel rather than shown the subject, withdraws.

The fix is not to be less admiring of the subject. The fix is to render the subject specifically enough that the admiration becomes the reader's conclusion rather than the writer's instruction. If the subject is courageous, do not write *she was courageous*. Write the specific moment of courage — what she did, what the cost was, what she said before and after. Let the reader name the trait. The naming is what creates the felt response. Your stating it preempts the reader's work.

Gay Talese's "Frank Sinatra Has a Cold" — published in *Esquire* in April 1966, still one of the finest pieces of literary nonfiction in English — is built around the fact that Talese could not get an interview with Sinatra and watched him from across rooms.[^2] The writer's distance *is* the angle. But Talese never explains his own emotional state. He reports his position — where he was standing, what he could and couldn't see, what he was and wasn't permitted to do — and lets the reader make every inference from that. The first person earns its place when it is doing structural work. It does not earn its place by telling the reader what the writer felt.

[^2]: Gay Talese, ["Frank Sinatra Has a Cold," *Esquire*, April 1966](https://classic.esquire.com/article/1966/04/1/frank-sinatra-has-a-cold).

The test is simple: would this sentence still be necessary if the writer were not in it? If no, cut it.

## How to draft

Once the angle is identified, there is a sequence that works reliably for this genre.

Open with a scene. Not a summary of the subject's career. Not a thesis statement about the angle. A scene: a specific place, a specific moment, sensory detail, the subject doing something. Hayden's Lewis piece opens with Lewis at the Library of Congress — a specific visit, a specific room — not with a recap of his congressional career. The Sinatra piece opens at a bar in Beverly Hills. Every strong profile I have read begins inside something specific and earns its way to the general. The movement is always from particular to principle, never from principle to illustration.

While you draft, keep the angle sentence at the top of your document — not for the reader, for you. Every time you add a piece of material, ask: does this advance the angle, or does it just add information? The material that advances the angle stays. The material that only adds information goes into a notes file. You will mourn it briefly. The profile will be better.

Close by returning. Come back to the opening scene, or to a parallel moment, that lets the reader feel the spine without your announcing it. The close should not contain the sentence *and that is why this person matters*. The close should be a specific image or exchange that lands with the weight of everything you have built toward it. Hayden closes with Lewis's own words about good trouble — not her characterization of his legacy, but his voice, in his idiom, carrying the argument the profile has earned. The profile ends with the subject. That is almost always right.

## The cost of the angle, honestly stated

I want to be direct about a real cost, because ignoring it produces dishonest profiles.

Every profile with a strong angle has excluded most of what is true about its subject. Lewis was not only the person who converted danger into moral force. He had a complex relationship to the Democratic Party establishment. He had positions some of his constituents found insufficient. He had a personal history the "good trouble" framing does not touch. Hayden's profile does not lie about Lewis. But it does not contain all of Lewis.

The line between *a profile with a strong angle* and *a profile that has reduced its subject to a thesis* is harder to draw than I would like. Both operations look the same on the surface: selection, emphasis, omission. What distinguishes them is the writer's honesty about whether the material being excluded is inconvenient or genuinely irrelevant to the argument being made.

There is no clean rule here. What I can say is that the angle should come from the material, not precede it. If you decide what the angle is before you do the research and then collect evidence for it, you are building a case rather than discovering something. The discipline of doing the research first, sitting with the material, and finding the surprise in it is what keeps the profile honest. The angle that surprised you is usually more trustworthy than the angle you arrived with.

A profile that selects in order to argue is doing the genre's legitimate work. A profile that selects in order to flatter is PR. The two look identical until you ask: did the writer exclude this because it didn't serve the argument, or because it complicated the tribute? The answer to that question is the difference between reporting and promotion, and the reader can usually feel which one they are reading.

## Why the LLM cannot do this for you

The profile is the genre where the model is most easily misused, and I want to say specifically why.

A language model can produce prose that has the texture of a profile — the rhythm of scene-setting, the balance of quote and commentary, the appearance of a specific subject — from nothing but a Wikipedia page and a stock vocabulary. The result will be grammatically competent. It will not be a profile, because a profile depends on exactly the three kinds of research the model cannot have done: the interview transcript with its unexpected digressions, the field notes with their unrepeatable sensory specifics, the secondary research selected for the particular angle this particular writer is pursuing.

The model has never been in the room. It cannot describe how the subject moved when the difficult question landed, because it was not there. It cannot reproduce the quote that mattered because the subject went off-script, because nothing went off-script for the model. The thing that makes a profile a profile — the evidence of a human observer paying attention in a specific place at a specific time — is the thing the model structurally cannot provide.

Where the model genuinely helps: after you have the transcript, paste it and ask the model to identify the three moments that surprised it most — moments where the subject said something you wouldn't have predicted. A rested reader is better at flagging the genuinely unexpected than a fatigued writer who has been in the material for two weeks. After you have the field notes, ask the model which details you recorded twice and which you recorded only once. The repeated noticing is usually the noticing that matters. After you have your angle, ask the model to make the strongest case against it. The counterargument improves the angle even when it does not change it.

Where the model does damage: writing the opening scene, producing the quotes, "improving the flow" of your prose. The last one is particularly insidious. The model's version of improved flow is a version that sounds like every other AI-assisted profile submitted that semester — competent, smooth, and with no felt sense of a particular human having been in a particular room. The specific roughness of your observation is not a flaw to be smoothed. It is the evidence that you were there.

## LLM Exercise — Chapter 7

**What you're building:** an angle for your profile, identified through structured interrogation of your raw research materials.

**Tool:** [Claude.ai](https://claude.ai/) chat. A Claude Project is recommended — you may want to keep your transcript and notes alongside the developing analysis.

**The prompt:**

```
I'm writing a profile of [subject — person, place, group, or event]
for a college writing class. The profile is supposed to be 1,200–1,800
words, built around an admirable trait my subject exemplifies.

I have completed:
- An interview with the subject (transcript pasted below)
- Field observation notes (pasted below)
- Secondary research (pasted below)

I am not asking you to write the profile. I am asking you to help me
find the angle.

Do the following, in order, one step at a time. After each step, wait
for my response before moving to the next.

1. Read the interview transcript. Identify the three moments that
   surprise you most — moments where the subject says or does
   something you wouldn't have predicted from a description of who
   they are.

2. Read the field notes. Identify the three details that appear most
   specifically — details so concrete I clearly noticed them in real
   time rather than reconstructing them later.

3. Read the secondary research. Identify the one piece of context
   that, if combined with my interview and field notes, would make a
   specific argument possible about my subject.

4. Propose three candidate angles, each in a single sentence of
   twenty words or fewer. For each, say what evidence it would
   require and what material I would have to leave out.

5. After I tell you which angle I'm choosing, propose one
   counter-angle — the case for why I might be wrong about which
   angle is strongest.

Do not draft the profile. Do not write the opening scene. The writing
is mine to do.

[Interview transcript:]

[Field notes:]

[Secondary research:]
```

**What this produces:** three candidate angles, each defensible, each requiring different material from your research; plus a counterargument once you choose. After this, you draft the profile yourself.

**How to adapt this prompt:**

- *For ChatGPT or Gemini:* works as written. Both will sometimes try to draft a scene after step 4; refuse the offer.
- *For Cowork:* useful if your research materials live in separate files — tell Cowork to read your transcript, field notes, and research files before starting the conversation.
- *For Claude Code:* not the right tool.

**The instructive failure to expect:** when you propose your chosen angle, the model may reflexively endorse it. Push back: *"Don't tell me my angle is good. Tell me what's wrong with it."* The reflex toward validation is one of the model's worst habits in writing-coach mode. The discipline of demanding pushback is part of the literacy.

##  AI Wayback Machine
The ideas in this chapter didn't appear from nowhere. **Lillian Ross** was working on *Picture* (1952) — a long-form profile of John Huston shooting *The Red Badge of Courage* that became the template for the modern New Yorker profile — decades before most people had heard of the profile as a genre.

**Run this:**

```
Who was Lillian Ross, and how does their work on the long-form profile
and "fly on the wall" interviewing connect to writing a profile that
captures a person rather than a résumé? Keep it to three paragraphs.
End with the single most surprising thing about their career or ideas.
```

Then search **"Lillian Ross"** on Wikipedia. See what the model got right, got wrong, or left out.

**Now make the prompt better.** Try one of these:

- Ask it to explain Ross's technique of using a subject's own dialogue, almost unedited, to characterize them — and the cost of that technique when the subject is unsympathetic.
- Ask: "How did Ross's *Portrait of Hemingway* (1950) blow up — and what did the profile do that the standard celebrity profile of the time refused to do?"
- Add the constraint: "Answer using one specific Ross technique that an interviewer learning to do profiles today should steal."

What changes? What gets better? What gets worse?
