*The Voice You Were Born Into and the Voice You're Asked to Borrow*

---

In the early 1820s, a Cherokee silversmith named Sequoyah figured something out that nobody in his nation had figured out before. He invented an alphabet.

Not adapted one. Not borrowed one. *Invented* one — eighty-six characters, each representing a syllable of spoken Cherokee, built by a man who could not read or write any other language. He had watched white settlers writing and reading English. He had not learned the marks. He had done something harder: he had inferred the *principle*. Marks on paper correspond to sounds. That is the whole idea. The specific marks are arbitrary — you can make your own.

Within a few years, literacy swept through the Cherokee Nation. A newspaper launched in 1828, printing in both English and Cherokee. A nation that had been entirely oral became, in a single generation, a nation whose laws, sermons, letters, and journalism were written down in its own language.


![Within a decade of Sequoyah completing the syllabary, the Cherokee Nation had a bilingual newspaper. The pri](images/03-language-identity-and-culture-exploring-employing-embracing-fig-01.png)
*Figure 3.1 — Within a decade of Sequoyah completing the syllabary, the Cherokee Nation had a bilingual newspaper. The principle — marks correspond to sounds, and the marks are arbitrary — is the chapter's opening argument made visible.*

Now: why did he bother? He could have just learned English. Many of his contemporaries did exactly that.

He bothered because he understood something important. Being literate in someone else's language is not the same as being literate in your own. The settlers had used writing to make treaties, then violated them. They had used it to make laws that did not apply to him. The technology was being used against his people, and the only real defense was to take the technology — the *principle* of it — and use it to write in Cherokee.

That is the problem this chapter is going to work through. It shows up in grammar. It shows up in the history of what gets called "correct" English. It shows up in what happens when you ask a language model to edit a draft you wrote in your own voice. The details vary. The underlying structure is the same every time: *whose language are you inside, and what does that cost you?*

## What culture actually means

The word *culture* is going to do a lot of work here, so let's fix its meaning now, because it gets used sloppily.

You've probably heard it in two ways. There's the high-arts sense — museums, opera, "a cultured person." And there's the pop sense — internet culture, "cancel culture," fandom. Neither of those is what I mean.

What I mean is the anthropological sense: the shared beliefs, practices, references, foods, fears, jokes, gestures, and ways of mourning and celebrating that a group of people inhabit together. Two features of culture in this sense matter.

First, it isn't optional. You are inside several cultures simultaneously before you can name any of them — the culture of your family, your region, your generation, your religion, the communities you joined online. You didn't choose them the way you choose a restaurant. You grew up inside them.

Second, they interact. A student once asked me: which one counts as *my* culture? All of them, at the same time. And the more interesting question is what happens at the edges where they rub against each other.

W. E. B. Du Bois named one specific kind of interaction in 1903. He called it *double consciousness*. This was the experience, for Black Americans, of always seeing yourself through two sets of eyes simultaneously — the eyes of the dominant white culture and the eyes of your own community. Two sets of standards. Two ways of being judged. One person held responsible for satisfying both audiences, with no acknowledgment from either that the other exists.


![Du Bois named "double consciousness" here — the experience of holding two irreconcilable sets of standards s](images/03-language-identity-and-culture-exploring-employing-embracing-fig-02.png)
*Figure 3.2 — Du Bois named "double consciousness" here — the experience of holding two irreconcilable sets of standards simultaneously. The chapter argues that any writer navigating between a home culture and an institutional one is inside a version of this structure.*

Once you see this structure, you see it everywhere. The Cherokee speaker learning English under federal pressure is inside it. The first-generation college student whose family didn't have English books on the shelves is inside it. The queer teenager in a family church is inside it. The neurodivergent kid in a classroom designed for neurotypical behavior is inside it. The specific cultures vary. The structure — satisfy two audiences who don't share standards, and neither audience sees the work the other requires — is the same.

For writing, this structure appears immediately. Every time you sit down to write for a college class, you are writing into at least two rooms at once: the room of the assignment, with its conventions, and the room of the person who walked in the door carrying everything else. Most writing instruction attends carefully to the first room and barely acknowledges that the second one exists.

## What Standard English actually is

Here is a distinction I want to make precisely, because it is usually stated wrong.

Standard English is the dialect used in formal U.S. publishing, mainstream broadcast journalism, official government documents, and most of academia. It has rules — about syntax, grammar, and usage — that other dialects of English do not share. It is widely treated as *correct* English, with other dialects positioned as deviations from it: errors of ignorance or laziness.

That framing is false, and it is worth understanding exactly why.

Standard English is not the original form. It is not a natural standard that other dialects drifted away from. It is one particular dialect that got institutionally promoted over others because the people who spoke it had the social, economic, and political power to make their version the published one. The other dialects — Southern white, Appalachian, African American Vernacular English, Cajun, Hawaiian Pidgin, and dozens more — are not corrupted Standard English. They are parallel dialects with their own rules, their own internal grammar, their own history.

This is not a political claim. It is a descriptive linguistic fact, the same kind of fact as saying that French and Spanish are both descended from Latin rather than one being a degraded form of the other. The question of which dialect got institutionalized as the standard is a question about power, not a question about linguistic adequacy.

Let me give you a concrete example, because abstract statements about dialects don't land until you see the mechanism.

African American Vernacular English has been studied carefully enough that linguists can write down its rules with precision. One of them: AAVE distinguishes habitual aspect from current aspect in ways Standard English has no clean mechanism for. *He be working* — habitually employed, a stable ongoing condition — is grammatically distinct from *he working* — doing it right now, at this moment. The first describes who someone *is*, occupationally. The second describes what someone is doing. Standard English requires a whole clause to approximate what AAVE does with one word. That is not broken English. It is a richer grammatical apparatus for a specific distinction.

| Distinction | African American Vernacular English | Standard English |
|---|---|---|
| Habitual / ongoing condition | *he be working* (one word marks the aspect) | *he is usually working*, *he works* — both ambiguous; "works" could mean occupation or habit; "is usually working" needs an adverb to clarify |
| Current action, right now | *he working* | *he is working* |
| Single-word habitual marker | Present (*be* + bare verb) | Absent — must be reconstructed by adverb or context |
| Status of the distinction | Grammaticalized — built into the verb system | Lexical — pushed out into surrounding words |

*AAVE encodes a distinction Standard English can only approximate with a full clause. This is a feature, not an error — the central claim of this section.*

AAVE speakers — particularly Black students — have nonetheless been told for decades that their English is wrong. Told by teachers who had no training in linguistics, who had simply absorbed the position that their own dialect was the standard and departure from it was error. The "Students' Right to Their Own Language" statement from the Conference on College Composition and Communication, issued in 1974 and expanded in 2020 to address Black linguistic justice specifically, names this harm directly and at length.

I want to be honest about what is genuinely complicated here.

Different contexts have different conventions. A cover letter to most U.S. employers is expected to read in Standard English. So is a grant proposal, a legal brief, most academic journal submissions. The skill of moving between dialects deliberately — code-switching — is a genuine and substantial literacy. Students who grew up speaking a nonstandard dialect at home often have this skill in deeper supply than students who grew up speaking Standard English, because they have *had* to develop it. The student who has only ever spoken Standard English has never had to think carefully about what dialect they are in. That makes them, in one specific sense, less sophisticated about language — not more.

The point is not that Standard English is wrong or that you shouldn't learn it. The point is that knowing only one dialect, in a country with many, is a limitation. It has been dressed up as a credential. That is the dressing I am asking you to see through.

## Writing from the inside

Charles Dickens visited the United States in 1842 and wrote a book about it, *American Notes for General Circulation*. His description of Broadway in New York City is technically accomplished. The pavement polished to a sheen. The red brick walls like kilns in the afternoon sun. The omnibuses and carriages, the vivid clothing of the crowds. The sentences move. The observation is precise.

It is also entirely from the outside.

Every American on the street is a *type* — a Black porter, a Bowery dandy, a young woman in vivid silk. None of them have interiority. None of them are inside their own cultures the way Dickens is inside his. What he produced is Broadway as it would look to a sufficiently observant European tourist: well-rendered, entirely external, and therefore missing everything that mattered to the people who actually lived there.

The opposite of this is what Cathy Park Hong does in *Minor Feelings: An Asian American Reckoning*. She names something specific inside Korean American experience — an object, a phrase, a quality of attention a parent gives a child — and explains it with enough context that a reader who doesn't share the cultural origin can approach it, but without flattening it or translating it into something else. Not a tour guide. Not a translator. Someone writing from inside, with enough care for the outside reader to open a door without taking the door off its hinges.


![Both books involve one culture explaining itself to another. One is written from the outside in; one from th](images/03-language-identity-and-culture-exploring-employing-embracing-fig-03.png)
*Figure 3.3 — Both books involve one culture explaining itself to another. One is written from the outside in; one from the inside out. The section is about the difference — and why only one of them can pass the recognition test.*

Du Bois's *veil* — his metaphor for the color line, an invisible but real barrier between Black and white American experience — works the same way. It is not a literal object. It is a way of seeing that people inside the culture recognize immediately. An outsider cannot produce the meaning by searching. But Du Bois writes it so that an outsider can feel the edges of it, can understand it as something real, even if they cannot inhabit it from the inside.

There is a simple test here. When you are not sure whether you are writing from the inside or from the outside, ask: would someone else from your culture read this and recognize themselves? Or would they recognize a tour guide's version of themselves? The first is the goal. The second is what most first drafts deliver — not because the writer is dishonest, but because the conventions of academic writing push you toward the external view without your noticing.

Here is what the drift looks like in practice. You are writing about a specific object — a piece of jewelry, a food, a song, a gesture — that means something particular inside your culture. The first draft you show me will probably explain the object from the outside: its origins, its general significance, what it signals to people who don't share the culture. It will read like an encyclopedia entry dressed in first person. The thing it will not contain is the *interior* meaning — the thing an insider knows that cannot be looked up, that is transmitted through growing up inside the culture rather than reading about it.

The revision move, almost every time, is to go smaller and more specific. Not "this food is significant at family gatherings" but *exactly* which gathering, exactly whose hands are making it, exactly what the kitchen smells like, and exactly what would be communicated to everyone in the room if someone brought the wrong version of it. The outsider can enter specificity. They cannot enter a summary.

## The language model in the room

There is a problem this particular kind of writing runs into that did not exist ten years ago.

Large language models are trained predominantly on Standard English text. When you ask one to help you write, its defaults pull toward Standard English. If you draft in AAVE, or in a Spanish-English code-switch, or in a Cantonese-inflected English the model has seen relatively little of, and then you ask it to *polish* the draft — the model will tend to translate your prose toward the Standard English center.

This is not malice. It is a consequence of training-distribution statistics. The model has seen more Standard English than any other dialect, by orders of magnitude. When you say *polish*, it moves toward what it has seen most. That is what its objective function has learned to do.


![When you say "polish," the model moves toward the longest bar. If your dialect is near the bottom, "polish" ](images/03-language-identity-and-culture-exploring-employing-embracing-fig-04.png)
*Figure 3.4 — When you say "polish," the model moves toward the longest bar. If your dialect is near the bottom, "polish" and "erase" point in the same direction.*

The implication is direct. If your essay is written in your authentic voice — including dialect, code-switching, or linguistic moves your culture uses and Standard English does not — and you run the draft through an LLM for editing without thinking about this, the model will systematically erase the very thing you put on the page. Not all at once. Gradually. A centimeter at a time, in places small enough that you might miss the drift.

There is no perfect fix for this in 2026. The honest workflow is: use the model lightly on these drafts, and read every suggestion it makes as if it might be doing the thing you told it not to do.

Two practices help.

*Tell the model explicitly what you are doing.* If you are writing a passage in AAVE on purpose, say so before sharing the draft: "I am writing this in African American Vernacular English. Do not standardize the grammar. Suggest edits only at the level of clarity and flow, and leave the dialect intact." Current models will follow that instruction, mostly.

*When the model proposes a revision, ask why.* If it says a sentence should be changed, ask: *is this making the sentence clearer, or is it making it more Standard English?* Those are different objectives. The model will tell you, if you ask. Then you decide whether the revision serves your writing or serves the model's training distribution.

These are not workarounds. They are part of the literacy this course is actually trying to teach — the literacy of working with a tool that has biases you need to understand in order to use it well. A tool you use without understanding its biases is a tool that is using you.

## What would change my mind

If research over the next decade found that students given full permission to write in their own dialects in college composition classes did *worse* in subsequent professional and academic settings than students trained exclusively in Standard English from the start, I would revise the central argument of this chapter.

The current evidence — the CCCC position statement and the linguistic-justice scholarship built on it — points the other way: students do better, not worse, when their authentic linguistic identities are treated as assets to develop alongside Standard English rather than as deficits to replace. But that is a falsifiable claim. I want to be clear that it is falsifiable. The argument is not self-evident and I am not asking you to take it on faith. I am asking you to look at the evidence and reason about it yourself.

---

## Still puzzling

The mechanics of using a language model on a draft written in a low-resource dialect remain genuinely unsolved. Even when you instruct the model explicitly to preserve the dialect, it will sometimes suggest an edit that standardizes the prose in a place small enough to miss. I do not have a clean fix for this.

The deeper question I keep returning to: when a model trained on one dialect's dominant corpus becomes the primary writing tool for people whose home dialect is underrepresented in that corpus, what happens to the dialects over time? Sequoyah's insight was that you need to own the technology to protect your language. But what does it mean to *own* a language model? Who writes the training data? Who decides what counts as the default? That question does not have an answer yet. It is one worth keeping.

## LLM Exercise — Dialect Preservation

Draft two paragraphs about a cultural artifact — an object, expression, symbol, or practice that does specific work inside a culture you belong to — written in your authentic voice. Then submit them to a language model with the following instruction:

> *I am writing this in [name your dialect or register]. Do not standardize the grammar. Suggest edits only at the level of clarity and flow, and leave the dialect intact.*

Copy every suggestion the model makes. For each one, write one sentence identifying whether the suggestion improves clarity or standardizes dialect. You are not obligated to accept either kind. You are obligated to know which kind it is.

## LLM Exercise — The Why Question

Take any single sentence from your draft that a model has suggested changing. Before accepting or rejecting the suggestion, type this into the model:

> *Why are you suggesting this change? Is it for clarity, grammar, or dialect standardization?*

Read the answer. Then decide. The exercise is not about the sentence. It is about developing the habit of asking *whose standards is this tool applying* before you apply them to your own work.
