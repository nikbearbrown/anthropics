---

Shira is nineteen years old, working at an ice-cream stand. The pay is decent for the season, the work is straightforward, and she has just been promoted to a supervisor role — making schedules for the other staff, sitting in on hiring interviews. She likes this part. It surprises her how much. She finds herself paying attention to who shows up well in interviews and why. She notices when a schedule produces stressed staff and when it produces calm ones. She pays attention to small things she didn't notice before — how a manager's tone in a Sunday morning meeting affects the rest of the week.

Shira does not know it yet, but she has just walked into her career. Not into a job called *career*. Into a *career*, in the sense the word actually means.

Several years later she will work in human resources at a health insurance company. A few years after that she will become a recruiting strategist managing other recruiters. A decade later she will be a director of human resources. The thread connecting all of it back to the ice-cream stand is the thing she noticed at nineteen: she was good at, and interested in, the part of work that involves how people work together.

I want to understand how that thread becomes a career. And I want to understand why most students, planning carefully, manage to miss it entirely.

## Job and career are not the same thing

Most career planning is aimed at jobs. Jobs are the wrong target.

A *job* is work you do in exchange for money — a bounded transaction, a specific role at a specific employer for a specific timeframe. You will have many jobs across a working life. A *career* is the long arc in which jobs accumulate into something coherent — a direction, a body of expertise, a network of people you've worked with, a reputation for doing something well. Most working lives include one or two or three careers, not many.

The implication is subtle but important. When students ask *what job should I get after graduation?* they are asking about the shortest possible unit of the problem. The more useful question is *what kind of work, over the next ten or twenty years, would constitute a career I would want to have?* The second question is harder to answer immediately. It produces much better answers in the end.

There is a set of common myths that make this harder, and they are worth going through because most students have absorbed at least two or three of them.

*A college degree means automatic employment and good pay.* The degree is an entry credential; employers also expect demonstrated capability — internships, projects, the ability to put learning to work. *There is one perfect job for me.* There are many jobs a person could plausibly do well and find fulfilling; the search for the single perfect one doesn't terminate. *I can't get a good job with a humanities major.* Many majors don't map directly to careers; what you build around the major — skills, internships, networks, projects — is also part of the package. *My career path should be a straight line.* Almost no one's is. Most careers are sequences of moves, some lateral, some unexpected, that make sense only in retrospect. *I should be passionate about my work.* Passion is a high standard that most jobs don't meet, and few jobs meet at every moment. The more useful standard is competence plus impact plus satisfaction — work you can do well, work that affects something, work that produces a sense of meaning or pride. Passion often follows competence; it rarely precedes it.

The myths are common because they each contain a grain of truth. A degree does help. Fields do vary in pay. Some careers are easier to enter than others. But each grain is wrapped in a generalization that, if accepted, restricts students from work they could actually do well. The discipline is to extract the truth and discard the generalization.

## How Shira's career actually got built

The moves Shira made are traceable. They are not genius. They are a sequence anyone can run.

At nineteen she *noticed* something — she liked the supervisory work, the people patterns, the small adjustments. This was not a plan; it was data. She didn't act on it immediately. She filed it.

As she took courses in college, she *connected* the noticing to what she was learning. Organizational psychology. Management. The courses made her observations sharper; the observations made the courses more interesting. The connection is not automatic — you have to be willing to look for it — but when you find it, both the academic work and the noticing compound.

In her junior year she *tested* the hypothesis by taking an internship in a human resources department at a bank. The internship was an empirical check: does she actually like this work in a more serious context, with more complexity? She did.

After graduating with a psychology degree she took a *career-aligned first job* — a recruiting specialist position. This was not the career; it was the entry point.

Across the next several years she *built*. She worked diligently. She got promoted. She joined the Society for Human Resource Management. She began studying for the SHRM certification exam. She was not stumbling into the career; she was building it deliberately.

A decade in, she was a director.

Notice the sequence: notice, connect, test, enter, build. None of the steps is heroic. Each step generates evidence for the next. The career emerges from the evidence, not from a plan made before any evidence existed. This is the part most students get backwards — they want the plan first and the evidence later. The plan that works comes from evidence, and the evidence comes from running the earlier steps.

## How to generate the evidence

Self-knowledge is the first move, and it is more specific than the phrase usually implies. Not *know yourself* in a vague aspirational sense. Specific knowledge about the things that bear on career fit: what interests you, what you're good at, what you value, how you prefer to work, what trade-offs you can live with.

Formal assessments — the Strong Interest Inventory, the CliftonStrengths, skills inventories offered through career services — are structured instruments that surface patterns you may not have noticed. The output of any single assessment should be treated as input to thinking, not as a verdict. No instrument knows you better than you do. But good assessments can surface patterns worth examining and prompt useful conversations.

Informal assessment is slower and more valuable. Ask yourself questions. What courses have I most enjoyed? What activities make time disappear? What kinds of problems do I find satisfying to solve? When I'm tired and would rather not be working, what kinds of work can I still bring myself to do? What do I value beyond money? These questions are simple. The honest answers usually take time and several rounds of asking.

Enlist others. The people who know you well often see patterns in you that you don't see in yourself. Ask three or four people — close friends, family, professors who know your work — what they see as your strengths, what they think you should consider. The answers will surprise you. Some won't match your self-image. Both kinds are useful.

Self-knowledge is necessary but not sufficient. You also need to know about the world of work, and most students underestimate the variety of careers that exist. They pick from the small set they can name. The exploration step is to expand that set.

The *Occupational Outlook Handbook* published by the Bureau of Labor Statistics lists thousands of careers with descriptions, training requirements, salary ranges, and projected growth. It is free and surprisingly detailed. Start there for any field you're considering.

Informational interviews are one of the highest-yield career-exploration moves available to a student. A typical informational interview is a twenty- to thirty-minute conversation in which you ask a working professional about their work — how they got into it, what a typical week looks like, what they like, what they don't, what advice they'd give someone exploring the field. Most people will do these because they are not hiring decisions. The request can be simple: *I'm a student exploring careers in [field]; I'd love twenty minutes of your time to ask about your work.* Three or four of these conversations will teach you more about a field than a semester of reading about it.

Volunteer work, shadowing, and direct exposure in settings you're considering provide evidence that no amount of reading or assessment can substitute for. The daily reality of being a teacher is different from the description of teaching as a profession. You need to see both to know whether you want it.

## The resources you are probably not using

Most colleges have a career services office. Most students don't engage with it until senior year, by which time the most useful services are too late to use. The office typically offers career assessments, resume review, mock interviews, job and internship listings, career fairs, alumni connections, and one-on-one counseling. The single most useful piece of advice on this front: go in your second year, not your fourth. The work of building a career is more cumulative than students realize.

Faculty in your major and adjacent fields often know working professionals in those fields. A faculty member who knows your work and respects it can introduce you to alumni, write recommendations, and provide references that change what jobs are available to you. This is not about currying favor; it is about doing work that earns notice from people who can help you.

Alumni networks exist specifically for this. Most colleges maintain alumni databases, and many alumni will take informational interviews from current students. The connection point — *I'm a [year] at [your school], in [your program]* — is a real social bond that opens conversations. You have one. Use it.

Internships are the single most important thing a college student can do for their career. An internship provides direct experience, a name on a resume, references, network connections, and often a job offer at the end. The students with the strongest career outcomes typically complete two or three internships across their college years, not one in the final year. Each internship is an empirical test of fit — and each one either confirms the direction or tells you to adjust, both of which are valuable.

Student professional organizations — field-specific student chapters of groups like the Society of Women Engineers, the American Marketing Association, the Society for Human Resource Management — give you access to working professionals, conferences, scholarships, and a credential for your resume. The access is direct and often dramatically better than what you'd get on your own.

## The question under the question

The previous sections are about what career to pursue. There is a deeper question underneath: *why?*

Abraham Maslow proposed, in 1943, a hierarchy of human needs that runs from basic to complex — physiological needs at the foundation, then safety, belonging, esteem, and at the top, self-actualization. The framework is imperfect; needs are not as strictly hierarchical as Maslow suggested, and it doesn't map uniformly across cultures. As a heuristic for thinking about work, though, it is useful.

A job satisfies physiological and safety needs — income for food, shelter, stability. That is necessary and not sufficient. A career satisfies belonging and esteem needs — relationships built over time, recognition for sustained competence, an identity tied to meaningful work. Purpose satisfies the self-actualization layer — the sense that what you do is connected to something that matters beyond your immediate situation.

![Maslow's hierarchy pyramid ](images/12-planning-for-your-future-fig-02.png)
*Figure 12.2 — Maslow's hierarchy pyramid *

Purpose is the *why* underneath the *what*. It is rarely a single sentence; it is a direction. *Help people who are struggling. Build things that work better than what came before. Understand how the world works at a level deeper than most people get to. Reduce some specific kind of suffering. Create beauty. Support a family well.* These are not job titles. They are the things that, if your work serves them, your work feels worthwhile.

The mistake people make is assuming purpose has to be heroic. It does not. The single parent who earns a stable living to support their children is doing purposeful work even if the daily tasks feel mundane. The librarian who connects readers to books that change their lives is doing purposeful work even if outside observers don't see it. Purpose is felt by the person living it. The external visibility is irrelevant.

Purpose also doesn't have to be singular. Most people have several simultaneously. You can be fully committed to being a good therapist, fully committed to being a good sibling, and fully committed to deepening a physical practice. The percentages don't add to one hundred; they add to whatever you have time and energy for. The pressure to find *the one* defining purpose is a pressure that doesn't match how most fulfilling lives actually work. The fulfilling lives are typically multi-stranded — work, family, community, internal development, all contributing to the sense that the time was well spent.

There is a practical implication worth naming. Sometimes the obvious path to a purpose — the front door — doesn't work for you. Maybe you wanted to be a civil rights lawyer but law school doesn't fit. Maybe you wanted to be a teacher but classroom management drains you in ways you couldn't sustain. The purpose remains; the front door is closed.

The side door is the strategy of using skills you already have to serve the purpose through a different route. A talented salesperson with a passion for racial and economic justice can use those skills selling educational technology to underfunded school districts, or low-cost broadband to rural communities. A skilled engineer with a passion for ending hunger can engineer better food-distribution logistics. A skilled writer with a passion for democracy can write materials that help people understand civic life. The skill is the same; the application serves the purpose. Don't lock yourself into a single path to something you care about. The unexpected route is often the most effective one.

## What changes and what doesn't

One more thing is worth saying before this chapter ends.

Regardless of your major, you will embark on work that changes many times. You will change responsibilities, roles, companies, and likely industries. Jobs that exist in your senior year of college may not exist in twenty years. Jobs that exist in twenty years may not exist now. The labor market is not a stable backdrop.

The implication is not despair. It is *adaptive capacity*. The student who builds durable skills, the ability to learn new things, a network across multiple fields, and financial flexibility handles change well. The student who builds expertise in one narrow specialty without those broader capacities handles change poorly. College is the place to build adaptive capacity. Most of the practices in this book — critical thinking, communication, learning how to learn, financial literacy, mental health practices — are adaptive-capacity practices. They serve you across whatever the labor market produces, not just the version of it that exists when you graduate.

This is also why purpose matters more than plan. Plans need to be revisable; the world will produce new information and you will need to update. Purpose is more durable — it tends to survive the revisions to plans because it is the thing you were trying to serve with the plan in the first place. Be loyal to the purpose; be flexible with the plan. People who get this backwards end up either rigid — clinging to the wrong plan because they confused it with a commitment — or rootless, revising the purpose itself every time a plan doesn't work out. The mature pattern is the inverse.

## The single idea

The chapter can be compressed to one claim, and it turns out to be the same claim every chapter in this book has been making from a different angle.

**The architecture of a working life is built from small daily practices, not from heroic one-time decisions.**

Shira's career was built from a series of small decisions — noticing something, connecting it to what she was learning, testing it in a low-cost setting, taking the first aligned job, building credentials and networks deliberately across years. None of the moves was irreversible. Each generated evidence for the next. The career emerged from the evidence.

The student who wants to plan their career from scratch, before any evidence, will produce a plan that fits their prior beliefs rather than the world. The student who generates evidence through exploration, informational interviews, internships, and self-reflection, and who updates their direction based on what they find, will produce a working life that fits them.

The practices are not complicated. Go to career services before your fourth year. Do informational interviews. Take internships in fields you're considering. Notice what energizes you and what drains you. Talk to faculty who know your work. State a current sense of purpose and let it evolve. Take the first aligned job, even if it's imperfect. Then build.

This is the last chapter. The architecture is in place. The building is yours.

## LLM Exercises

**Exercise 12.1 — Decompose your own thread.** Think back across the last three to five years — not just school, but jobs, clubs, projects, hobbies, anything you've spent real time on. Describe to an LLM what you noticed in each context: what you were drawn to, what you were good at, what drained you. Ask it to identify any patterns across the inputs — recurring themes of skill, interest, or value — that might suggest a career direction. Evaluate whether the patterns it identifies match your own sense of what matters to you. Where do you disagree?

**Exercise 12.2 — Test a myth against your situation.** Pick three of the chapter's career myths that you have heard, believed, or told yourself. Describe each one to an LLM and explain how it has shaped your thinking about your career. Ask the LLM to apply the reality check from the chapter to your specific situation. Evaluate whether the check holds — are there ways in which the myth is partly true for your circumstances, and ways in which it is not?

**Exercise 12.3 — Build a four-year plan.** Tell an LLM your current year in school, your major or intended major, a sketch of your current interests, and two or three career directions you're considering. Ask it to design a year-by-year career-development plan for your remaining time in college: which self-knowledge moves to make, which exploration moves, which internships to pursue and when, which campus resources to engage and in what order. Evaluate the plan: is it realistic given your actual constraints? Does it address your specific uncertainties? Revise it with the LLM until it feels executable.

**Exercise 12.4 — Articulate and test a purpose.** Write two or three sentences describing what you currently understand to be your purpose — the underlying *why* of the work you'd want to do. Share it with an LLM. Ask it to propose three different career paths that could plausibly serve that purpose — including at least one *side door* route that uses skills you already have. For each path, ask the LLM to identify where the purpose would be well served and where it would be compromised. Evaluate which path fits best and identify one move you can make in the next semester to test it.

**Exercise 12.5 — The book in twelve sentences.** For each of the twelve chapters of this book, write one sentence that captures the idea that was most useful to you. Share all twelve with an LLM. Ask it to identify the single underlying claim that connects all twelve — the thesis the book has been making from twelve different angles. Compare its answer to your own. Then write your own final sentence: given everything this book has built, what is college for?
