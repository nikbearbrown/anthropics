*What the instructor's eye missed, and why most of what makes a person who they are is invisible.*

---

Here is a scene worth examining. A classroom. Twenty-five students. One instructor. They are reading *Romeo and Juliet*. The instructor pauses and says, with genuine warmth: *in our classroom, everyone is the same.*

He looks around and sees twenty-five young White Midwesterners. The visible markers of difference are, to his eye, uniform. He concludes, easily, that this is a homogeneous group.

He is wrong. The student reading Romeo is wondering what it would be like to play Juliet, the role men originally performed in Shakespeare's day. The student reading Juliet is wondering whether her classmates know she is a lesbian, and whether that knowledge would change how *my only love sprung from my only hate* lands when she reads it. Another student strongly identifies as German American but is reading an Italian. Another has a Black grandmother whose existence none of his classmates suspect. Five distinct lived experiences with the text are operating in that room, none of them visible to the instructor.

*In our classroom, everyone is the same* covered all five without registering any of them.

This is the chapter about the layer of human variation the instructor's eye missed. Not because his eye was broken — none of the relevant differences were visible — but because most of what makes a person who they are is not visible. Identity is interior before it is exterior. The skill of recognizing that, and conducting yourself in a way that respects it, is called *cultural competence*. It is learnable. This chapter is about how.

## Three words that solve for different things

*Diversity*, *equity*, and *inclusion* travel together so often they can blur into a single vague term. They are not the same thing. They solve for different problems.

**Diversity** is the presence of differences in the human experience — race, ethnicity, age, ability, language, religion, sexual orientation, gender identity, socioeconomic status, veteran status, immigration status, body size, geography, and more. A diverse community is one in which a substantial range of these differences is present.

**Equity** is equal access to opportunity and success. The American Sign Language interpreter at a public emergency announcement is equity — the deaf citizen and the hearing citizen receive the same information at the same moment. The wheelchair ramp is equity. Extended testing time for a student with ADHD is equity. None of these is an advantage; each is the removal of a disadvantage the typical environment imposed by default. Equity treats people *as needed* to produce equal access. Equality treats everyone identically. The person in a wheelchair and the person walking past the ramp are not identically situated, and treating them identically — no ramp — doesn't fix that.

**Inclusion** is the active integration of varied voices into the conversation. A college can have a diverse student body and still produce inclusive outcomes only for the most central group, if its curriculum, its activities, its hiring, and its expectations all reference that central group. Inclusion is what turns demographic diversity into substantive participation.

The three are not the same goal achieved differently; they are three distinct goals that compose. Diversity without equity leaves many people nominally present but structurally disadvantaged. Equity without inclusion equalizes access formally while the everyday experience still privileges some voices. Inclusion without diversity has no one to include.

The reason these concepts exist is historical, not abstract. Native children were taken from their families and placed in boarding schools where Indigenous languages and cultural practices were forbidden — many languages were lost. Enslaved people of African descent built much of the early American economy's wealth without compensation. Japanese Americans were interned during World War II on the basis of national origin alone, with no individual evidence of disloyalty, a policy the government later acknowledged as wrong. Women and minority men were excluded from civic participation in forms we now take for granted: the Supreme Court was founded in 1789 and its first woman justice was confirmed in 1981. Major League Baseball was founded in 1869 and its first African American player stepped onto the field in 1947.

These weren't accidents of demographics. They were policies — formal and informal. The terms *equity*, *inclusion*, and *underrepresented* are not marketing language; they are the vocabulary produced by the centuries-long effort to dismantle those policies and replace them with something different. That work is ongoing. The Pew Research Center projects that by 2065 no single racial or ethnic group will be a majority in the United States. The demographic reality of the classroom and workplace you'll spend your career in is already different from the one the instructor surveyed in the opening scene, and it is moving further in that direction.

## What identity actually is

Social psychologists usually distinguish four dimensions of identity, and it helps to hold them separately.

**Personal identity** is what distinguishes you from any other person — the idiosyncrasies, preferences, history, and scars that belong only to you. Even identical twins have distinct personal identities because no two people lead the same life.

**Role identity** is how you operate in different settings — student in a classroom, sibling at home, colleague at work, friend at a party. The same person presenting differently across roles is not being inconsistent; they are expressing one underlying self through the appropriate register for each context.

**Social identity** is your awareness of belonging to groups — Korean American, Methodist, third-generation New Yorker, alumna of a particular school. Social identities shape interactions because they index which other people share something with you.

**Collective identity** is bonds around shared causes or movements — the civil rights generation, the labor movement, particular fandoms or cultural collectives. Partly chosen, partly emergent.

Most people carry several of each at once. The combination is what makes you specifically you.

The important complication is **intersectionality** — a term introduced by legal scholar Kimberlé Crenshaw in 1989. Crenshaw was studying anti-discrimination cases involving Black women who had been excluded from certain employment opportunities. The discrimination affected Black men differently and White women differently, and neither analysis alone explained what Black women experienced. The legal framework, which treated race and gender as separate categories, could not see the specific intersection.

Intersectionality describes how multiple identity categories combine to produce experiences that are not the sum of their parts. A Black queer woman's experience is not Black + queer + woman. It is its own configuration, with patterns of advantage and disadvantage specific to that intersection. The combinations matter. A first-generation immigrant student who is also a religious minority who is also from a low-income background experiences college differently than someone with any one of those identities alone.

The implication: don't reason about identity from any one category in isolation. The Asian American student in your physics class is not first an Asian American and then a student. The two identities interact; the experience is the interaction.

There is also the question of fluidity and ambiguity. Identities are not always stable or clear-cut. They evolve as people develop. They can be ambiguous — someone whose racial heritage is mixed, someone whose gender expression is non-binary, someone whose religious practice is in flux. The categorical frameworks we use to organize the social world often have trouble accommodating ambiguity.

The writer Malcolm Gladwell, with a Jamaican mother and an Irish father, has a fair-skinned appearance that, with short hair, reads as White to many people. With his hair grown into a curly Afro, the same appearance reads differently. He has reported being treated differently in airport security and traffic stops depending on which read his appearance produced at that moment. Same person. Same identity. Different external classifications. Different consequences.

The competence is to let people define themselves. Not because identity is infinitely malleable, but because the people best positioned to know what someone's identity is are the people whose identity it is. The discipline is to ask, listen, accept, and adjust — not to assign categories from the outside.

## What cultural competence looks like in practice

The previous two sections are conceptual. This one is behavioral. Cultural competence shows up in specific practices, and the practices are learnable.

**Avoid making assumptions.** The default human cognitive move is to fill in what you don't know about someone with whatever fits the nearest category. The cost is that the categorical fill-in is often wrong, and when it is wrong, the consequences land on the person whose actual situation was overridden.

Consider a six-person group project. The group plans to meet at the library; someone proposes an off-campus restaurant; most agree quickly. Two students look uncomfortable. One suggests going back to the library. The others are already standing up. The two are left to either accept the change or look unreasonably difficult. Their discomfort might trace to money — the restaurant costs more than they have to spare for casual dining. Or access — it's not reachable without a car. Or safety — it's in a neighborhood where one of them has had a previous bad experience. Or religious observance — the restaurant doesn't serve food they can eat. None of these reasons would the assuming students automatically know. The cost of the assumption is paid by the two students whose situations were overridden.

The practice: ask before assuming, and give people graceful ways to opt out of decisions that don't fit them. The cost to the asker is small. The benefit to the person who gets to participate is substantial.

**Recognize microaggressions.** A microaggression is an everyday act of insensitivity that indexes implicit bias and reinforces barriers. The acts are usually unintentional. The cumulative effect on the receiver is not.

A manager interrupts an employee laughing in the cafeteria: *Ann, this isn't a night at one of your clubs. Quiet down.* The phrase *your clubs* implies a specific kind of social setting and a specific kind of person who frequents them. Ann hears the implication. The manager may not have realized he was making it.

Other examples from the research literature: *you speak so well for someone like you* (implies that people of a certain race or ethnicity are not expected to speak well). *No, where are you really from?* (implies that the obvious answer — I'm from here — is somehow not the real one). *I don't get all this pronoun stuff, so I'm just going to call you what I call you* (diminishes the importance of gender identity to the person whose identity it is). *Are you going to need help with the software?* directed at an older colleague (assumes age maps onto technological unfamiliarity).

The mechanism worth understanding: microaggressions usually have a generous reading in the speaker's mind. The speaker thinks they are being friendly, complimentary, or helpful. The receiver hears the implication that lives one layer down. The mismatch is the harm.

A useful pause: before delivering a backhanded compliment or an othering remark, ask whether the same comment would land the same way if directed at someone in the dominant group. If the answer is *no, I wouldn't say that to a White man / a cisgender straight peer / someone my own age*, the comment is probably a microaggression.

**Acknowledge implicit bias.** Implicit bias is the mechanism underneath many microaggressions and assumptions. Research on this, developed through work including Harvard's Project Implicit program, demonstrates that most people hold unconscious associations that influence their judgments and behavior even when their conscious values reject those associations. Almost everyone has implicit biases, including people who consciously reject the corresponding explicit forms.

Acknowledging this is not the same as confessing to being a bad person. It is acknowledging that the brain's automatic associations include patterns absorbed from a culture that has those patterns. The work is to notice when those associations are running, slow down in moments where they would otherwise drive behavior, and evaluate whether the behavior fits your conscious values. Over time, the noticing reduces the effect.

**Recognize and share privilege.** Privilege, in the structural sense, refers to benefits that accrue from accidents of birth, geography, era, and family rather than from individual effort. A person of average height can reach most shelves, drive most cars, and fit most chairs without modification. A person with a college degree has a credential that screens out many job applications before the first conversation. A White person in the United States is seen as the default in many institutional contexts — the assumptions built into forms, policies, and marketing often assume them.

The point of naming this is not blame. Most privileges are not earned by the people who hold them. The point is awareness, followed by use. Sharing privilege looks like small acts repeated over time: when you have access to opportunities, notice when others don't and find ways to open the access. When you have the social bandwidth to speak up about something, notice when someone else doesn't and use yours. When you can absorb a small social cost — asking the awkward question, naming the microaggression — notice when someone else can't afford to absorb it, and absorb it instead.

**Debate civilly.** The rules of fair debate apply with particular force to conversations about identity and difference, because these conversations often involve people whose identities are directly at stake. The personal costs of the conversation are unequally distributed. The discipline is to raise the bar on civility in such conversations, not lower it.

What civil debate looks like is specific. It means engaging the argument rather than the person — *ad hominem* attacks are not engagement. It means identifying the strongest version of the opposing view rather than the weakest — the practice called steelmanning. It means making premises explicit rather than implicit, because most logical errors are downstream of unstated assumptions. It means acknowledging when the other side has a point, which is not capitulation but accuracy. And it means the goal of the exchange is mutual understanding, not winning.

One additional practice worth naming: when discussing a group, the highest-quality conversations include members of that group rather than only people speculating about them. The campus housing committee benefits from the participation of students from a range of housing situations. The student government decision about accommodations for students with disabilities benefits from the participation of students with disabilities. *Nothing about us without us* is the slogan; the practice is to notice when a conversation is being had about a group whose members are absent, and to treat that absence as a problem.

## The group project as a worked example

Take a five-person group project whose members are: a returning adult learner with two children; a first-year student in a residence hall; a transfer student from a community college; an international student on a student visa; a student athlete with a demanding training schedule.

A culturally incompetent approach: schedule meetings at times that work for the loudest member, distribute work equally regardless of constraints, default to communication channels familiar to the most tech-fluent member, ignore that the international student's visa status might affect availability for certain components.

A culturally competent approach: in the first meeting, ask each member about their constraints — work hours, family obligations, training schedules, language considerations. Schedule meetings at times that work for everyone, which may mean unusual times. Distribute work according to capacity rather than equality — the adult learner with two children may have fewer total hours; the international student may have particular knowledge to contribute. Use communication channels that work for the group, which might mean a shared document and email rather than a chat platform some members are less fluent in.

The competent version takes more setup time. It produces a project all five members can fully participate in. The incompetent version runs more smoothly for the dominant subset and worse for the others — and often fails outright when the others' constraints, predictably ignored, force a crisis near the deadline.

The pattern generalizes. Any time you are working with people across difference — which is most of the time in a heterogeneous environment — the setup investment pays off by making the working time more functional for everyone rather than optimal for some and tolerated by others.

## The single idea

Cultural competence can be compressed to one mechanism.

**Most of what makes a person who they are is not visible, and the practice of cultural competence is the practice of letting people define themselves rather than assigning categories from the outside.**

The history of diversity and equity policy is a history of institutions learning, slowly and usually under pressure, that the categories they assigned to people were both wrong and harmful. The goal of the behaviors in this chapter — ask before assuming, recognize bias, share privilege, debate civilly — is to compress that institutional learning into a personal practice that operates from the first encounter rather than decades later.

The thing to watch for is the gap between good intentions and the practice. Many well-intentioned people produce harm because they have not done the work. Many people who think of themselves as fair participate in structures that produce unequal outcomes without noticing. Cultural competence is a skill, and like any skill, it requires practice rather than good intentions. The eggshells feeling, when it appears, usually traces to anxiety about saying the wrong thing — an anxiety that diminishes with practice and with willingness to recover from small slips. The recovery is part of the practice.

The instructor in the opening scene was not a bad person. He was an undertrained one. The training is what this chapter is for.

## LLM Exercises

**Exercise 9.1 — The invisible room.** Think of a recent room you spent time in — a class, a meeting, a social gathering. Describe it to an LLM in as much detail as you can: who was there, what was discussed, how decisions were made, whose voices dominated. Ask the LLM to identify what kinds of difference were likely present but invisible or unacknowledged — by category (identity, constraint, experience, language). Evaluate whether the categories it identifies match your own sense of what was in the room. Where do you disagree?

**Exercise 9.2 — Diagnose an assumption.** Describe to an LLM a specific recent interaction where you made an assumption about someone, or someone made one about you. Ask the LLM to identify the category of identity at play, the implicit bias likely operating, and the cost the assumption produced. Then ask it to propose what a different version of the interaction would have looked like if the assumption had been replaced with a question. Evaluate whether the alternative feels realistic.

**Exercise 9.3 — Steelman exercise.** Choose a contested issue you have a strong position on — affirmative action, immigration policy, religious accommodation in schools, anything where you know which side you're on. State your position to an LLM. Ask it to produce the strongest possible version of the opposing view — not a weak strawman, but the actual best argument. Then ask it to identify the evidence that would, in principle, change your mind. Write a paragraph reflecting on whether the steelman version changed anything about your own position.

**Exercise 9.4 — Microaggression audit.** Give an LLM a list of five things you have said or heard said in the last week that you suspect might be microaggressions — or that you are uncertain about. Ask it to classify each one: is it a microaggression, and if so, what category and implication does it carry? Ask it to propose a different version of each statement that communicates the same intent without the problematic implication. Evaluate whether the proposed alternatives are realistic.

**Exercise 9.5 — Privilege mapping.** Describe your own background to an LLM in as much honest detail as you can: racial and ethnic identity, socioeconomic background, educational background of your family, language spoken at home, citizenship status, ability, gender, sexual orientation. Ask the LLM to identify categories where you likely have structural privilege and categories where you likely don't. Then ask it to propose one concrete action you could take in the next two weeks that uses a privilege you have to create access for someone who doesn't. Evaluate whether the proposed action is realistic and actually executable.
