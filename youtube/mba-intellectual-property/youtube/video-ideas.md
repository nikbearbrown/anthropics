## Candidate 01 — The Patent That Accelerates the Competition It Blocks
- Source: `mba-intellectual-property/chapters/01-patent-basics.md`
- Topic: INTELLECTUAL PROPERTY
- Hook: Edison's 1880 patent on the incandescent lamp gave him a 17-year monopoly — and immediately triggered the explosion of innovation that replaced it.
- Key case: U.S. Patent No. 223,898 (Edison, 1880); downstream inventions by Tesla, Brush, Stanley documented in 2012 research.
- The Question: If a patent blocks competitors from copying your invention, why does the historical record show that patents produce *more* downstream innovation than secrecy would?
- Core idea: A patent is a forced disclosure — the inventor publishes the blueprint in exchange for temporary exclusivity. That blueprint becomes the raw material for every competitor trying to design around it, producing a denser burst of downstream invention than secrecy would have allowed. The bargain (temporary monopoly for permanent disclosure) is the mechanism that turns one invention into a cascade.
- Visual object: A single patent document radiating downstream invention arrows — each arrow labeled with a follow-on invention (Tesla coil, tungsten filament, vapor deposition), with a 17-year exclusivity band on a timeline below.
- Manim move: accumulate (arrows accumulate from the patent document outward over time, then the exclusivity window closes and all inventions become public domain simultaneously)
- Example seed: Edison files patent 1880. Competitors read the published document the same day. By 1897 (expiry), at least six major downstream innovations have been built around or beyond it — none of which infringed the original. The moment the patent expires, every competitor can now freely practice the original lamp too. The bargain paid out twice: during the term (disclosure fueled design-around innovation) and at expiry (the original invention becomes public domain).
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: c2v (patent document diagram), geo (timeline with exclusivity band)
- Prerequisites: Basic idea of what a patent is (government-issued right)
- Exclusions: Omit patent prosecution details, claim language, international filings; omit the Wright brothers aviation monopoly tangent
- Score: 10/10

## Candidate 02 — 138 Years vs. 20: Why Coca-Cola Never Filed a Patent
- Source: `mba-intellectual-property/chapters/05-trade-secret-basics.md`
- Topic: INTELLECTUAL PROPERTY
- Hook: The most valuable recipe in the world has been protected for 138 years — and would have expired in 1903 if they had patented it.
- Key case: Coca-Cola formula (1886–present); contrast with any pharma patent that expired and triggered generic entry.
- The Question: If patents give you 20 years of legal protection, why would a rational company choose secrecy over the stronger legal right?
- Core idea: A patent is a 20-year monopoly in exchange for permanent public disclosure. After expiry, any competitor can freely practice the invention. A trade secret offers potentially infinite protection — but only if the secret stays secret; reverse engineering and independent invention are full defenses. The rational choice depends on whether the information is detectable in the product and whether commercial value outlasts 20 years.
- Visual object: Two parallel timelines side by side — the "patent path" (strong protection dropping to zero at year 20, then fully public) and the "trade secret path" (conditional protection extending indefinitely, but with a fragile-line aesthetic and "reverse engineering" and "leak" as rupture points).
- Manim move: split (two timelines diverge from a single 1886 decision point; the patent timeline decays to zero at year 20; the trade secret timeline continues horizontally but shows a breakage if a leak event is injected)
- Example seed: 1886: Pemberton develops the formula. Hypothetically patented in 1886 — expires 1903. Competitor can now freely make identical Coca-Cola. In reality: no patent filed. 1886 to 2026: 140 years of trade-secret protection, still intact, because the formula has never been generally disclosed and the company has maintained reasonable secrecy measures continuously.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: geo (dual timeline diagram), c2v (vault visual, secrecy-measure checklist)
- Prerequisites: Familiarity with the concept of a patent term
- Exclusions: Omit DTSA vs. UTSA statutory technicalities; omit employee mobility / inevitable disclosure doctrine; omit the FTC non-compete rule
- Score: 10/10

## Candidate 03 — Genericide: How a Brand Can Be Too Successful
- Source: `mba-intellectual-property/chapters/04-trademark-basics.md`
- Topic: INTELLECTUAL PROPERTY
- Hook: Bayer spent 18 years marketing Aspirin so aggressively that a federal court canceled the trademark — because the marketing worked too well.
- Key case: Bayer v. United Drug Co. (1921, Judge Learned Hand); also escalator (Otis, 1950), thermos (King-Seeley, 1963), zipper (B.F. Goodrich).
- The Question: If a trademark's job is to identify your brand, why can dominating the market destroy your trademark rights?
- Core idea: A trademark exists to identify the *source* of goods, not the *category* of goods. When consumers start using the mark as the name of the category — "aspirin" for any acetylsalicylic acid, not just Bayer's — the mark loses its source-identifying function and legal protection collapses. The paradox: successful marketing that pushes a brand into generic language kills the trademark the marketing was built to protect.
- Visual object: A single funnel — brand name entering the top as a proprietary term, and exiting the bottom as a generic category label — with a "source identification" sensor midway that goes dark when genericness is reached.
- Manim move: morph (the word "Aspirin®" transforms character by character to lowercase "aspirin", losing its distinctiveness indicator; a timeline below tracks the 1903–1921 arc)
- Example seed: 1903: "Aspirin" is Bayer's fanciful coined trademark, strongly protectable. Bayer markets it as *the* pain reliever. By 1921, retailers call any acetylsalicylic acid product "aspirin"; doctors write "aspirin" on prescriptions without intending Bayer. Judge Hand: "aspirin" now names the category, not the source. Trademark canceled. Bayer retains "Bayer" as a house mark but can never recover "aspirin." Modern response: Xerox, Kleenex, Google use only "Xerox® copier" (adjective + generic noun) to keep their marks tethered to source.
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: c2v (funnel diagram), raster (historical Aspirin tin label)
- Prerequisites: Basic understanding that trademarks identify brand source
- Exclusions: Omit likelihood-of-confusion multi-factor test; omit dilution doctrine; omit naked licensing and abandonment paths
- Score: 10/10

## Candidate 04 — Copyright Protects the Words, Not the Idea Behind Them
- Source: `mba-intellectual-property/chapters/03-copyright-basics.md`
- Topic: INTELLECTUAL PROPERTY
- Hook: A monkey took the world's most famous selfie — and owns none of it. And neither does the photographer.
- Key case: Naruto v. Slater (2017, PETA/9th Circuit dismissal); Baker v. Selden (1879) for the idea-expression dichotomy.
- The Question: If copyright protects creative works automatically on creation, why did the photographer who set up the shot, paid for the trip, and owned the camera get nothing?
- Core idea: Copyright protects *original expression fixed in a tangible medium by a human author* — not effort, not investment, not the idea behind the expression. Slater's setup was effort, not authorship of this specific photograph. The macaque pressed the shutter; no human authored that particular image. Simultaneously, copyright's idea-expression dichotomy means even a fully human-authored romantic comedy screenplay cannot copyright the plot structure "boy meets girl, complications ensue, they end up together" — only the specific words and scenes.
- Visual object: A two-layer diagram — an idea layer (unprotected, labeled "free for all") and an expression layer (protected, labeled "author's exclusive") — with specific examples placed in each layer: "plot formula" in the idea layer, "specific script" in the expression layer.
- Manim move: split (the diagram splits vertically; items migrate to their correct layer; the idea layer stays open/accessible, the expression layer closes with a copyright badge)
- Example seed: Naruto presses the shutter → image created → no human author → no copyright. Anyone can publish it, including Wikipedia. Slater's *other* photographs from the same trip (ones he took himself) are fully copyrighted. The mechanism: human authorship is binary. The idea-expression dichotomy: anyone can write a rival romantic comedy using the same plot structure — they just can't copy Slater's specific words or another author's specific scenes.
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: c2v (two-layer idea/expression diagram), raster (the macaque selfie, which is public domain)
- Prerequisites: None — entirely self-contained
- Exclusions: Omit fair use four-factor test; omit DMCA; omit copyright duration details; omit AI authorship controversy
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 05 — A Patent Excludes — It Does Not Authorize
- Source: `mba-intellectual-property/chapters/01-patent-basics.md`
- Topic: INTELLECTUAL PROPERTY
- Hook: You can hold a valid patent on an invention and still be legally blocked from making it yourself.
- Key case: Implied by the stacking-rights structure; Apple v. Samsung (design + utility patent stacking) as the illustrative modern case.
- The Question: If a patent gives you exclusive rights to your invention, why doesn't it give you the right to make or sell it?
- Core idea: A patent is an *exclusionary* right — it lets you stop others from making, using, selling, or importing your invention. It is not an *affirmative* right to practice the invention yourself. If your invention also infringes someone else's prior patent, you still need a license from them before you can build anything. Patents stack and interlock: you can patent an improvement on someone else's invention without being able to practice the improvement.
- Visual object: Two overlapping patent rectangles on a timeline — one older/broader (competitor's foundational patent), one newer/narrower (inventor's improvement patent). The overlap zone is labeled "license required to practice." An arrow from each patent labeled "can exclude" points outward; no arrow labeled "can make" exists inside the overlap.
- Manim move: duplicate (two patent rectangles slide into place; their overlap zone highlights; labels appear showing the exclusion-only nature of each right)
- Example seed: Inventor A patents a new smartphone battery chemistry (improvement on prior art). Valid patent, fully examined. Inventor B holds the foundational patent on the battery architecture that Inventor A's chemistry uses. Inventor A cannot manufacture their battery without a license from B — even though A holds a valid patent. Both can exclude competitors from their respective claims. Neither can act unilaterally in the overlap zone. The solution: cross-license, which is how most complex technology sectors actually function.
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: geo (overlapping patent rectangles diagram), c2v (exclusion vs. authorization labels)
- Prerequisites: Basic understanding that patents are government-granted rights
- Exclusions: Omit patent prosecution, claim drafting, international filing; omit patent trolls / PAE policy debate
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 06 — The eBay Decision: How One Ruling Rewired Patent Litigation
- Source: `mba-intellectual-property/chapters/02-patent-enforcement.md`
- Topic: INTELLECTUAL PROPERTY
- Hook: Before 2006, winning a patent lawsuit almost automatically shut down the infringer. After eBay v. MercExchange, winning a lawsuit might just get you a check.
- Key case: eBay Inc. v. MercExchange, L.L.C. (2006, U.S. Supreme Court); Polaroid v. Kodak (1990) as the pre-eBay baseline.
- The Question: If a court finds that a competitor is infringing your patent, why would the court refuse to make them stop?
- Core idea: Before eBay, courts treated injunctive relief as nearly automatic on a finding of patent infringement. The Supreme Court's 2006 decision changed this to a four-factor equitable test: irreparable harm, inadequacy of damages, balance of hardships, public interest. The practical result: non-practicing entities (companies that hold patents but don't make products) generally cannot show irreparable harm, so they get damages but no injunctions. This fundamentally changed the patent-troll business model — extracting injunction threats became harder, shifting leverage from the patent holder to the accused infringer.
- Visual object: A balance scale — on one side "injunction" (stop everything), on the other "damages" (pay a royalty). Before eBay: the scale tips automatically to "injunction." After eBay: a four-factor weight is added to each side; the outcome varies by who holds the patent.
- Manim move: transform (the scale starts tipped toward injunction; the eBay ruling appears as a weight on the damages side; the scale rebalances; then two separate scenarios branch — practicing entity tips back to injunction; non-practicing entity tips to damages)
- Example seed: MercExchange holds patents on "buy it now" technology but doesn't run an e-commerce site. eBay infringes. MercExchange wins at trial. Pre-eBay logic: permanent injunction — eBay must remove the feature. Supreme Court: not automatic. Apply the four factors. MercExchange cannot show irreparable competitive harm (they don't compete with eBay in the marketplace). Result: damages, no injunction. Post-eBay, every non-practicing entity patent strategy had to be recalculated.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: geo (balance scale before/after diagram), c2v (four-factor checklist)
- Prerequisites: Basic understanding that patents can be enforced in court; awareness that injunctions exist as a court remedy
- Exclusions: Omit PTAB / IPR proceedings; omit claim construction / Markman hearing; omit the full Panduit / Georgia-Pacific damages frameworks
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 07 — The Distinctiveness Spectrum: Why Kodak is a Better Trademark Than QuickPrint
- Source: `mba-intellectual-property/chapters/04-trademark-basics.md`
- Topic: INTELLECTUAL PROPERTY
- Hook: The easiest brand names to understand are the hardest to protect. The hardest names to understand — "Kodak," "Exxon," "Xerox" — are the strongest trademarks ever created.
- Key case: Bayer v. United Drug Co. (1921) for generic failure; Feist v. Rural (parallel principle); the spectrum itself drawn from Lanham Act doctrine.
- The Question: Why do trademark lawyers tell founders to avoid names that describe their product, and instead choose invented nonsense words?
- Core idea: Trademark law sorts terms on a distinctiveness spectrum from generic (no protection) through descriptive (protection only after consumers learn to associate the term with one source), suggestive, arbitrary, and fanciful (strongest protection, available immediately). Descriptive terms fail because competitors need them to describe their own goods. Fanciful invented terms — "Kodak," "Exxon," "Verizon" — have no prior meaning, so any use of the term in commerce can only refer to one source. The paradox: a descriptive name teaches consumers what you do but cannot be protected; a fanciful name tells consumers nothing but can be protected forever.
- Visual object: A horizontal spectrum bar — five labeled zones from Generic (red/no protection) to Fanciful (green/maximum protection), with example marks placed in each zone and arrows showing the investment required to move a descriptive mark up toward protectability through secondary meaning.
- Manim move: spread (example marks spread out along the spectrum axis; protection shields of varying size appear above each zone; a descriptive mark starts unprotected and slowly acquires a smaller shield only after a "secondary meaning" clock runs)
- Example seed: "Phone" for a phone: generic, zero protection. "QuickPrint" for a printer: descriptive, needs years of advertising before protection kicks in. "Greyhound" for a bus: suggestive (implies speed), protected immediately. "Apple" for computers: arbitrary (has a meaning, but unrelated to computers), strongly protected. "Kodak": fanciful — no prior meaning — strongest possible protection the moment Eastman used it.
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: geo (spectrum bar diagram), c2v (example marks with shield-size icons)
- Prerequisites: Basic understanding that trademarks identify brand source
- Exclusions: Omit likelihood-of-confusion multi-factor analysis; omit dilution doctrine; omit Madrid Protocol international filing; omit TTAB proceedings
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 08 — Fair Use Is Not a Safe Harbor — It's a Four-Factor Gamble
- Source: `mba-intellectual-property/chapters/03-copyright-basics.md`
- Topic: INTELLECTUAL PROPERTY
- Hook: There is no list of things that are automatically "fair use." Every unlicensed use of copyrighted material is a lawsuit waiting for a judge to decide.
- Key case: Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music (1994, Supreme Court — 2 Live Crew parody of "Oh, Pretty Woman"); Authors Guild v. Google (2015, 2nd Circuit — Google Books snippet display).
- The Question: If copyright law allows fair use for commentary, education, and research, why do media lawyers still advise clients to get a license for uses that seem obviously fair?
- Core idea: Fair use is a four-factor balancing test, not a defined list of permitted uses. Each factor — purpose and character of use (especially whether transformative), nature of the original, amount used, effect on the market — is independently weighed, and no single factor is dispositive. Courts apply the test holistically to the specific facts of each case. The same use (e.g., reproducing 20 seconds of a song) can be fair use in one context (documentary criticism) and infringement in another (commercial advertisement), and the only way to know is to litigate.
- Visual object: A four-quadrant weighing table — each quadrant one factor, with a "for fair use" / "against fair use" two-sided bar in each quadrant. The overall result is a weighted sum that varies by factual input.
- Manim move: accumulate (the four factor bars fill in sequentially; each bar tips based on the specific fact pattern; the overall balance accumulates at the bottom; then the fact pattern changes and the bars re-tip)
- Example seed: 2 Live Crew records a parody of "Oh, Pretty Woman" for commercial sale. Factor 1 (purpose): commercial use weighs against fair use, but parody as commentary weighs for it — Supreme Court says transformativeness is the key inquiry. Factor 4 (market effect): a parody doesn't substitute for the original in the same market. Result: fair use. Google digitizes millions of books and shows short snippets in search results. Transformative (searching, not reading). Non-substitutable (snippets don't replace the book). Fair use. AI training on copyrighted data: same test, answer not yet settled.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: geo (four-factor weighing diagram), c2v (Campbell and Google Books case comparison)
- Prerequisites: Basic understanding that copyright grants exclusive reproduction rights
- Exclusions: Omit DMCA safe harbors; omit copyright duration; omit international Berne Convention; omit AI-authorship controversy
- Score: 8/10
