## Candidate 01 — Why a Polished Lesson Plan Can Fail Every Student in the Room
- Source: `claude-for-education-a-practitioners-guide/chapters/00-introduction-the-teacher-is-still-the-system.md`
- Topic: AI IN EDUCATION
- Hook: An AI-generated lesson plan can look more coherent than anything a teacher would write in thirty seconds — and still produce zero learning.
- Key case: Mara teaches ninth-grade biology, asks Claude for a lesson on cellular respiration, gets a perfect-looking plan with warm-up, vocabulary, and exit ticket. Uses it Tuesday. Students can recite "adenosine triphosphate" but cannot explain what a cell would do without ATP. The artifact was coherent. The learning did not happen.
- The Question: A well-structured lesson plan should predict student understanding. Here is the case where it didn't. Why?
- Core idea: An artifact is a proposal for conditions under which learning might happen — it is not learning itself; Claude optimizes for artifact quality, not for the cognitive gap between what students know now and what the outcome requires.
- Visual object: A single lesson plan document on screen, glowing with polish — beside it, a student's blank response on an exit ticket.
- Manim move: split
- Example seed: Ms. Park asks Claude for "a lesson on the water cycle." Claude returns a 50-minute plan with a diagram activity, vocabulary matching, and a fill-in-the-blank formative check. Students complete every task. On Friday's exit ticket — "explain why rain falls as liquid not vapor" — 22 of 28 leave it blank. The plan covered the topic. The outcome was never named.

- Still lanes: geo (abstract mechanism, free, default)
- Prerequisites: None — viewer needs only to know what a lesson plan is

## Candidate 02 — Why Telling Claude the Topic Is the Wrong First Step
- Source: `claude-for-education-a-practitioners-guide/chapters/02-learning-outcomes-before-prompts.md`
- Topic: AI IN EDUCATION
- Hook: Two teachers ask Claude for the exact same lesson — one gets generic coverage, the other gets an activity that fixes the specific misconception her students actually carry.
- Key case: James types "Give me a lesson on photosynthesis." Gets a warm-up, reading, discussion questions. Monday: students can say plants use sunlight to make food. Almost none can connect photosynthesis to why they need to eat — the load-bearing link to the next unit. Same topic, wrong target.
- The Question: Two prompts ask for the same lesson on the same topic. One produces aligned activities; one produces coverage without transfer. They differ by 40 words. Why?
- Core idea: Claude cannot know what students should be able to DO — it only knows what you asked for; naming the outcome, prior knowledge, and the specific misconception before writing the prompt transforms Claude from a content generator into a backward-design partner.
- Visual object: Two Claude prompt windows side by side — one short and topical, one anchored with outcome, misconception, and evidence — and the diverging lesson outputs below each.
- Manim move: compare
- Example seed: Prompt A: "Give me a lesson on fractions." Prompt B: "6th-grade lesson on dividing fractions. Students believe dividing always makes things smaller — need one activity that forces this prediction into conflict, one that resolves it with a visual model. Evidence: students can explain in their own words why 6 ÷ ½ = 12." Claude's response to B names the misconception, builds toward it, and produces a prediction exercise. Response to A gives three definitional exercises.

## Candidate 03 — Why Helpful Feedback Can Steal the Student's Own Learning
- Source: `claude-for-education-a-practitioners-guide/chapters/05-feedback-that-teaches.md`
- Topic: AI IN EDUCATION
- Hook: The more complete and precise Claude's feedback is, the more it may be doing the student's revision work — leaving the student better informed but no better at revising.
- Key case: A student submits a history essay with the thesis: "World War I was caused by many complex factors including nationalism, militarism, and the alliance system." Claude drafts a feedback comment that rewrites the thesis as a model of causal argument. The student incorporates it. The next essay has the same listing problem — because the student never did the revision thinking.
- The Question: Better feedback should produce better revision skills. Here is the case where more specific feedback produced a better-looking essay but no improvement in the student's ability. Why?
- Core idea: Feedback teaches when it supports the student's revision thinking, not when it replaces it; directive correction (Type 3) removes the cognitive labor that produces the skill — a coaching question (Type 2) that asks "what relationship between these causes would someone who disagreed have to accept?" forces the thinking without supplying the answer.
- Visual object: A single student essay paragraph on screen — beside it, three feedback variants morphing from directive rewrite to coaching question, with the student's brain activation metaphorically shown only at the coaching question.
- Manim move: morph
- Example seed: Priya generates Claude feedback for a thesis that lists causes without arguing relationships. Claude draft A: "Revise your thesis to argue that nationalism was the primary trigger, with the alliance system as its amplifier." Draft B: "If a reader asked 'so what relationship between these three causes matters most?' — what would you want them to understand?" Priya selects B. The student has to construct the argument. The next draft shows causal structure Priya did not supply.
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: geo (abstract mechanism, free, default)
- Prerequisites: Viewer should know what formative feedback is; no AI background needed
- Exclusions: No grading vs. feedback policy debate; no discussion of AI detection; no Hattie meta-analysis summary; do not enumerate all six quality-check questions

## Candidate 04 — Why AI Makes Assessment Cheat-Proof or Irrelevant — There Is No Middle Ground
- Source: `claude-for-education-a-practitioners-guide/chapters/08-assessment-in-the-age-of-claude.md`
- Topic: AI IN EDUCATION
- Hook: A take-home essay that bans AI and a take-home essay that welcomes it can both fail to measure student learning — for exactly the same structural reason.
- Key case: A sociology professor assigns a five-page take-home essay: "Analyze how social media platforms affect adolescent identity formation. Use five peer-reviewed sources. Due in two weeks." Claude can produce a structurally coherent version in ninety seconds. The professor adds "no AI" to the rubric. A student submits Claude's essay lightly edited. The professor uses an AI detection tool. It flags a different student — an English-language learner — writing in her authentic voice.
- The Question: An AI prohibition should protect assessment validity. Here is the case where it produced a false accusation and let the actual substitution through. Why?
- Core idea: Detection responds to the absence of evidence — redesign produces its own evidence; a process portfolio, a transfer task introduced close to the deadline, and a five-minute oral check create multiple corroborating points that AI cannot substitute for without student engagement.
- Visual object: A vulnerability map — high-risk, moderate-risk, low-risk assessments — with a "redesign lever" showing which design moves shift each assessment down the risk axis.
- Manim move: transform
- Example seed: Professor adds three changes to the sociology essay: (1) an annotated bibliography due one week early requiring the student's own words on each source; (2) a novel case — a specific recent social media study — introduced one week before the deadline; (3) five-minute office-hour check-in where the student explains their most useful source. A student who submitted Claude's essay cannot explain the source at check-in. A student who read and engaged can. The design produced the evidence; the ban did not.
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: geo (abstract mechanism, free, default)
- Prerequisites: Viewer should understand what academic integrity means; no AI background needed
- Exclusions: No discussion of specific AI detection tools by name; no FERPA/legal detail; no full five-pattern enumeration; do not cover equity surveillance section

## Candidate 05 — Why the Brain Learns Less When AI Does the Struggling
- Source: `claude-for-education-a-practitioners-guide/chapters/97-fundamental-themes.md`
- Topic: AI IN EDUCATION
- Hook: Students who used AI freely during math practice scored 48% higher on practice — and 17 percentage points lower on the unassisted exam.
- Key case: The Bastani study: students with unrestricted AI access during practice outperformed on practice problems. On the exam — same material, no AI — they scored dramatically below students who practiced without AI. They felt like they had learned. The neurological events that produce mastery never occurred.
- The Question: More successful practice should predict better exam performance. Here is the study where it predicted worse performance. Why?
- Core idea: Cognitive friction is not the price of learning — it is the biological mechanism; when AI removes the struggle of encountering something you cannot yet do, it removes the dopamine-BDNF-synapse chain that consolidates memory; the student borrowed cognitive capability from the machine instead of building it in themselves.
- Visual object: A learning curve: AI-assisted practice shoots up steeply, then collapses to below the control curve when AI is removed. The curve is the whole film.
- Manim move: trace
- Example seed: Imagine two groups of 20 students learning to solve algebraic word problems over one week. Group A uses Claude to check each step as they go — 85% correct during practice. Group B works unassisted — 54% correct during practice. Exam day, no Claude: Group A scores 61%, Group B scores 78%. Group A practiced the skill of asking Claude. Group B practiced the skill of solving.
- Length band: ~1 min
- Still lanes: geo (abstract mechanism, free, default)
- Prerequisites: None — accessible to any viewer who has ever studied for an exam
- Exclusions: No neuroscience terminology beyond dopamine/synapse; no full Kosmyna EEG study detail; no tier taxonomy; do not discuss BDNF by name

## Candidate 06 — Why "Good" in a Rubric Teaches Students Nothing About Quality
- Source: `claude-for-education-a-practitioners-guide/chapters/07-rubrics-exemplars-and-criteria.md`
- Topic: AI IN EDUCATION
- Hook: A rubric with levels labeled "Excellent," "Good," "Adequate," and "Unsatisfactory" describes quality with the very word it was supposed to define — and students receiving a "Good" learn nothing about what to do next.
- Key case: A student submits an argumentative essay and receives the rubric notation: "Analysis: Good. Demonstrates strong analytical thinking with minor areas for development." The student reads this. Learns nothing specific. Writes the next essay with no clearer sense of what analysis means in this course than before.
- The Question: A more detailed rubric should help students improve their next submission. Here is the case where a four-level rubric with named criteria produced the same confusion as no rubric at all. Why?
- Core idea: A rubric written with quality adjectives ("strong," "developing," "basic") assumes the judgment rather than enabling it — observable criteria describe what you would see in the student's actual work, not how you feel about it; Claude can audit for adjective-chains and propose observable replacements, but only the teacher can decide what quality means in this course.
- Visual object: A single rubric cell morphing from "Demonstrates sophisticated analysis" into four observable sentences — each describing a concrete move a reader could detect in the essay.
- Manim move: morph
- Example seed: A university writing instructor's rubric says "Analysis: Exemplary — Demonstrates sophisticated analysis." Claude audits it and flags "sophisticated" as a quality word. It proposes: "Exemplary: Each body paragraph connects at least one piece of evidence directly to the paragraph's claim and to the essay's central argument. The explanation interprets the evidence — it does not restate it." The instructor checks whether "interprets" itself needs a glossary entry. It does. She adds one sentence. Students can now self-assess before submitting.
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: geo (abstract mechanism, free, default)
- Prerequisites: Viewer should know what a rubric is; no AI background needed
- Exclusions: No full six-question audit enumeration; no analytic vs. single-point rubric comparison; no discussion of AI exemplar disclosure controversy; do not cover rubric bias section

## Candidate 07 — Why Banning AI from Assignments Usually Teaches the Wrong Lesson
- Source: `claude-for-education-a-practitioners-guide/chapters/10-student-use-from-ban-to-boundary.md`
- Topic: AI IN EDUCATION
- Hook: A blanket AI ban in a composition course produced three student appeals, one wrongly accused ESL student, and a policy that taught students to avoid detection — not to think.
- Key case: A community college composition instructor added "no AI tools" to every assignment. By November: AI-detection software flagged an English-language learner writing in her authentic voice. A student who used Claude substantially got through undetected. By December: the instructor had a policy that generated more conflict than it prevented and had not redesigned a single assignment.
- The Question: A no-AI policy should protect student cognitive labor. Here is the case where it misidentified which students were using AI and left the design problem untouched. Why?
- Core idea: Banning the tool does not redesign the work — an assignment that can be completed by AI without student thinking is a design failure, not an access problem; four policy modes (Prohibited, Permitted with Disclosure, Required for Process, Reflective Use) matched to assignment purpose create teachable boundaries that connect the rule to the learning goal.
- Visual object: A spectrum bar — from "Prohibited" through four policy modes to "Reflective Use" — with assignment types sliding to their correct position as their intellectual purpose is revealed.
- Manim move: scan
- Example seed: The composition instructor redesigns her research argument essay. Week 1: 200-word problem statement, timed in-class — Mode 1, no AI. Week 2: annotated source log, may use AI to locate sources but must document it — Mode 2. Week 3: draft with change-log — Mode 2. Week 4: ten-minute instructor oral conference. Week 5: final with revision note. The AI policy is now: "AI is permitted with disclosure at stages 2 and 3. The oral conference and in-class writing are not AI-assisted." Students can follow this. The reasoning is visible.
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: geo (abstract mechanism, free, default)
- Prerequisites: Viewer should know what academic integrity means; no AI background needed

## Candidate 08 — Why AI Produces Lesson Plans Where the Teacher Does All the Thinking
- Source: `claude-for-education-a-practitioners-guide/chapters/04-lesson-planning-and-activity-design.md`
- Topic: AI IN EDUCATION
- Hook: Claude's default lesson plan puts the teacher at the center of every activity — because that is what the lesson-plan genre looks like, and Claude has read millions of them.
- Key case: Deshawn asks Claude for a lesson plan on chemical reactions. He gets: a teacher-led demonstration, a worksheet with ten definitional questions, and a homework problem set. The demonstration keeps all chemical thinking with the teacher. The worksheet questions can be answered by looking up definitions. Three weeks later, on the unit test, he learns what his students did not understand — because the lesson never required them to think.
- The Question: A complete lesson plan with objective, materials, timing, and closure should predict student cognitive engagement. Here is the case where it predicted passive compliance instead. Why?
- Core idea: Claude models the lesson-plan genre — and that genre centers the teacher; the cognitive labor audit asks one question for every activity: who is doing the thinking? Receiving (watching, listening, copying) vs. producing (retrieving, applying, explaining, predicting) — and every "receiving" moment can be redesigned into a "producing" moment.
- Visual object: A lesson plan on screen with each activity labeled "RECEIVING" or "PRODUCING" in red and green — then the "receiving" activities flipping into redesigned versions.
- Manim move: transform
- Example seed: Deshawn's Claude-generated plan: Activity 1 — teacher demonstrates combustion reaction (RECEIVING). Activity 2 — students copy the equation (RECEIVING). Activity 3 — worksheet: "What are the reactants?" (lookup, RECEIVING). Deshawn's audit: Activity 1 redesigned — students predict what products will form before the demo, then compare prediction to observation (PRODUCING). Activity 2 redesigned — students write the equation from their own prediction notes (PRODUCING). Activity 3 redesigned — "Why did we need oxygen for this reaction? What would happen in a sealed container?" (explanation, PRODUCING).
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: geo (abstract mechanism, free, default)
- Prerequisites: None — accessible to anyone who has sat through a lecture
- Exclusions: No backward design theory in full; no Freeman active learning meta-analysis detail; do not enumerate all five audit questions; no discussion of timing adjustments

## Candidate 09 — Why Pasting a Student's Name into Claude Is a Privacy Decision, Not a Prompt
- Source: `claude-for-education-a-practitioners-guide/chapters/09-privacy-student-data-and-institutional-boundaries.md`
- Topic: AI IN EDUCATION
- Hook: A teacher who pastes a student's accommodation letter into Claude to get a support plan has made a legal data-sharing decision — not a pedagogical one.
- Key case: A high school teacher has three struggling students. She pastes their names, grades, IEP documentation, and counselor notes into Claude. Claude produces thoughtful, specific support plans. She saves them to a shared Google Drive. Identifiable student education records — including disability documentation — have now been processed by an external system without documented institutional approval, and live in a cloud folder the institution has not reviewed for this use. She had good intentions. The risk remains.
- The Question: Using AI to help struggling students should protect them. Here is the case where protecting students created a privacy exposure instead. Why?
- Core idea: Data minimization means giving Claude the pattern, not the person — the teacher describes what she is seeing across a cohort ("students struggling with complex texts and time management") and Claude provides tiered scaffolding templates; the teacher adapts them using person-level knowledge that stays in her head, not in the prompt.
- Visual object: Two prompt windows: Version A with name, grade, IEP label, counselor note highlighted in red; Version B with all identifying elements replaced by "[Student A]," "[documented accommodation]," "[last month]" — same task, no regulated data crossing into the external system.
- Manim move: compare
- Example seed: Teacher needs scaffolding strategies for three specific students. Version A: pastes their first names, IEP labels ("dyslexia," "anxiety"), and counselor summary. Version B: "I am working with several high school students struggling with complex texts and long-term project management. Help me draft tiered support strategies — one for reading scaffolds, one for project-planning structure, one for both." Same useful output. No FERPA-adjacent data transmitted. Teacher adapts using her knowledge of the specific students.

- Still lanes: geo (abstract mechanism, free, default)
- Prerequisites: Viewer should know what FERPA is at a basic level, or care about student privacy
