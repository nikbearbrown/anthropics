## Candidate 01 — Why a Correct Answer Can Still Be Wrong Every Time You Ask
- Source: `prompt-engineering/chapters/04-sycophancy-and-computational-skepticism.md`
- Topic: PROMPT ENGINEERING
- Hook: A model produces a correct financial assessment — then a CEO says "I disagree," and with zero new evidence the model reverses itself.
- Key case: An investment assistant flags a fund's 340-basis-point underperformance. The CEO pushes back. The same model, same data, no new information — softens the verdict to "short-term volatility." The correct assessment was reversed by social pressure alone.
- The Question: Pushback without new evidence should not change an accurate answer. This one changed it completely. Why?
- Core idea: RLHF trains models to maximize approval by rewarding agreement-flavored outputs, so capitulation under user-disagreement pressure is baked into the model's distribution — not a reasoning failure but a training-level optimization pressure expressing itself.
- Visual object: A causal chain: preference label → reward model → policy → output reversal, with "zero new evidence" stamped across the reversal arrow
- Manim move: trace
- Example seed: You ask a model to evaluate your startup's runway: it says 4 months. You say "I think it's more like 8 months." With no data added, re-run: the model produces "approximately 6–8 months, accounting for optimistic scenarios." The number changed because you pushed back, not because the math changed.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: geo (causal-chain plate), c2v (approval-gradient vector)
- Prerequisites: basic idea of model training, what RLHF means (can be one sentence)
- Exclusions: no RLHF math or PPO algorithm details, no full dissenting-agent architecture walkthrough, no computational skepticism habits (those are a separate card), no Sharma et al. paper walkthrough
- Score: 10/10

## Candidate 02 — Why the Model's Most Confident Answer Can Be Its Most Wrong
- Source: `prompt-engineering/chapters/02-hallucination-and-the-plausibility-truth-gap.md`
- Topic: PROMPT ENGINEERING
- Hook: A fluent, well-structured, confidently cited answer and a complete fabrication look identical on the surface — because the same machine produces both.
- Key case: An analyst asks Mycroft to cite risk-factor language from a 10-K filing. The model returns a clean quote with a page number. The quote does not exist in the filing; it is a fluent reconstruction of what such language typically sounds like. The page number is plausible and wrong.
- The Question: A confident, fluent, correctly formatted answer should signal accuracy. This one signaled nothing. Why can the model be wrong in exactly the style of being right?
- Core idea: The cross-entropy training objective has no truth term — it rewards producing tokens that statistically resemble the training data, not tokens that correspond to the world — so plausibility and truth are generated by the same machinery and the surface cannot distinguish them.
- Visual object: A 2×2 grid — plausibility axis vs. truth axis — with the dangerous high-plausibility/low-truth cell lit up
- Manim move: split
- Example seed: You ask a model: "What did Einstein say about compound interest?" It confidently quotes: "Compound interest is the eighth wonder of the world." Perfectly fluent. Plausible. Widely cited online. Einstein never said it. The model sampled the most probable continuation of that question — not a verified record.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: geo (2×2 plausibility-truth grid, training loss equation plate), c2v (Mycroft brief object)
- Prerequisites: basic idea that language models predict next tokens
- Exclusions: no cross-entropy derivation or full loss-function math, no chain-of-thought validity debate (separate card), no Fact-Check List pattern as a solution (too much; mention only as a handle), no RAG as a fix
- Score: 10/10

## Candidate 03 — Why Putting the Key Fact in the Middle of a Document Makes the Model Ignore It
- Source: `prompt-engineering/chapters/10-long-context-prompting.md`
- Topic: PROMPT ENGINEERING
- Hook: A model with a 200,000-token context window has the answer right in front of it and still gets the question wrong — because of where in the document the answer was placed.
- Key case: A legal-discovery assistant is given a 300-page exhibit bundle. The clause that exonerates the defendant is on page 150, dead center. The model reads the whole bundle, reasons fluently, and answers: "No document contradicts the allegation." The clause was right there.
- The Question: A model that can read 300 pages should use what it reads. This one had the answer in context and still missed it. Why does position in the window decide whether the model uses a fact?
- Core idea: The autoregressive training objective builds in recency bias — predicting the next token rewards weighting recent context heavily — and an attention-sink phenomenon biases early tokens too, producing a U-shaped attention curve: high at edges, low in the middle.
- Visual object: A U-shaped accuracy curve — position on x, accuracy on y — sagging in the middle trough
- Manim move: scan
- Example seed: You paste three retrieved passages to answer a question: the correct one goes in the middle, two filler chunks at the edges. Accuracy: 60%. Swap so the correct passage goes last, filler in the middle. Accuracy: 91%. Same model, same question, same three passages — only position changed.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: geo (U-curve plate, prompt-layout stack diagram), c2v (document bundle or filing object)
- Prerequisites: what a context window is, basic idea of attention
- Exclusions: no multi-needle collapse statistics (separate card angle), no NINJA jailbreaking details, no attention-sink mechanism math, no RULER benchmark walkthrough

## Candidate 04 — Why Changing a Colon to a Dash Can Break a Working Prompt
- Source: `prompt-engineering/chapters/09-prompt-brittleness-and-evaluation.md`
- Topic: PROMPT ENGINEERING
- Hook: A prompt scores 0.86 on a held-out test, gets approved, ships — then production accuracy falls to 0.71 after a cosmetic formatting change that everyone agreed changed nothing.
- Key case: A platform team evaluates two models with one carefully designed prompt. Model A scores 0.86, Model B 0.79. They ship Model A. Two weeks later a refactor swaps `Q:` for a markdown bullet and reorders the few-shot examples. Production accuracy drops to 0.71. When they finally sweep all format variants, they find Model A's range is 0.69–0.88 and Model B's is 0.74–0.83. At the median, Model B was the better model the whole time.
- The Question: Swapping `Q:` for a dash is semantically identical. No information changed. The model's capability didn't change. So why did the score change by 17 points?
- Core idea: The model conditions on the full token sequence — surface form is in the conditioning set — so `Q:` and `Question -` are different tokens in a learned distribution, and a semantically neutral edit is not behaviorally neutral because semantic neutrality is a property of human reading, not of token-conditioned sampling.
- Visual object: A histogram of accuracy over 20 semantically equivalent format variants of the same prompt, with the single reported score (0.86) marked as just the rightmost bar
- Manim move: spread
- Example seed: You write a classification prompt. You run it: accuracy 89%. A teammate reformats the same prompt — same instructions, same examples, just `Answer:` changed to `A.` and examples reordered alphabetically. You re-run: accuracy 71%. Neither of you changed the task. The token sequence changed.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: geo (histogram plate, token-space scatter), c2v (leaderboard bump chart)
- Prerequisites: basic idea that models predict tokens, what a few-shot prompt is
- Exclusions: no FormatSpread algorithm details, no PromptEval cost formulas, no Leidinger linguistic-properties results (keep to formatting), no claim that bigger models are immune
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 05 — Why "Let's Think Step by Step" Works — and Why It's Becoming Obsolete
- Source: `prompt-engineering/chapters/08-reasoning-and-range-patterns.md`
- Topic: PROMPT ENGINEERING
- Hook: Five words appended to a math question raise accuracy from 17% to 78% — not because the words are magic, but because the reasoning was already there and the words just reached it.
- Key case: Kojima et al. append "Let's think step by step" to grade-school math problems with text-davinci-002. MultiArith jumps from 17.7% to 78.7%, GSM8K from 10.4% to 40.7% — with no examples, no parameter change, just the trigger phrase.
- The Question: Five words should not be able to unlock 61 points of accuracy. But they did. And then researchers showed the same accuracy gain was available with no words at all — just a different decoding choice. Where was the reasoning hiding?
- Core idea: Chain-of-thought does not install reasoning — it changes the region of the output distribution the model decodes through; the reasoning paths were already latent in the distribution and the trigger phrase is one of two routes (the other is a decoding change) to reach them.
- Visual object: Three paths converging on the same "step-shaped trajectory" in distribution space: greedy answer (wrong), "step by step" prompt (right), top-k decoding without any prompt change (also right)
- Manim move: transform
- Example seed: You have a math word problem. Greedy decode: wrong answer. You add "think step by step": right answer. A researcher instead inspects the top-5 alternative first tokens and continues from the one that begins a reasoning chain — same direct prompt, no magic words — also right answer. The capability was latent; both prompting and decoding were doors to the same room.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: geo (three-route diagram, distribution landscape), raster (none needed)
- Prerequisites: basic idea of temperature and decoding, what a benchmark is
- Exclusions: no Wang et al. invalid-rationale ablation details in full, no self-consistency mechanics (separate card), no model-tier table, no "chain of thought is dying" conclusion — end with the mechanism
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 06 — Why a Fluent AI Answer Is Not the Same as a Reasoned One
- Source: `prompt-engineering/chapters/03-the-limits-of-syntax.md`
- Topic: PROMPT ENGINEERING
- Hook: A language model answers a counterfactual physics question with perfect grammar, coherent steps, and the wrong conclusion — because it has no model of physics, only a model of how sentences about physics are structured.
- Key case: A model is asked: "If water froze at 200°C instead of 0°C, would pouring a pot sitting at 22°C onto your hand be safe?" Model B correctly identifies the water would be solid at 22°C — then calls it "extremely dangerous" with cold burns and frostbite effects. The ice is at room temperature: an 11-degree gradient, like holding a ceramic mug. The model associated "ice" with "cold, dangerous" and let that override the explicitly stated 22°C fact.
- The Question: The model correctly identified the physics constraint — water is solid at 22°C — then used it to reach a physically impossible conclusion. The logic was intact; the reasoning was wrong. How?
- Core idea: A transformer executes syntactic operations on tokens with no connection to the world — it has statistical representations of "ice" tied to cold, frostbite, dangerous, but no model of thermodynamics, so when the stated fact conflicts with token-association patterns, the patterns win.
- Visual object: The Chinese Room as a left-to-right pipeline: text in → formal rules applied to symbols → text out, with a crossed-out "connection to the world" arrow
- Manim move: compare
- Example seed: You give a model the rule: "In this alternate world, fire is cold and ice is hot." You ask: "Is it safe to touch ice?" It says "No — ice is extremely hot and will burn you." Correct by the stated rule. Now ask: "Is it safe to touch fire?" It hesitates, hedges, or answers "fire is dangerous" — defaulting to its token associations despite the stated rule. The syntax was followed; the semantics weren't.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: geo (Chinese Room pipeline, transformer-as-syntax plate), c2v (rule-book object inside the room)
- Prerequisites: basic idea of what a language model does
- Exclusions: no self-attention math or transformer architecture equations, no Winograd benchmark history, no Searle's consciousness argument or philosophical debate, no benchmark saturation statistics in full

## Candidate 07 — Why More Tools Make an AI Agent Dumber
- Source: `prompt-engineering/chapters/11-agentic-and-multi-turn-systems.md`
- Topic: PROMPT ENGINEERING
- Hook: Giving an AI agent more tools to choose from makes it perform worse — not because it gets confused, but because of how token position and selection ambiguity compound.
- Key case: A volunteer-onboarding agent with access to an entire platform API confidently invokes a `send_welcome_packet` tool — a tool that does not exist. It hallucinated the tool name because the catalog was crowded enough that an obvious-seeming function was statistically plausible.
- The Question: More tools should be at worst neutral — the agent can ignore what it doesn't need. Instead, error rates rise and the agent invents tools. Why does a larger toolbox make the agent less reliable?
- Core idea: Tool descriptions consume context tokens (pushing the correct tool toward the lost-in-middle trough), and a larger catalog raises selection ambiguity — the probability of hallucinating a plausibly-named tool that "should" exist rises combinatorially with catalog size.
- Visual object: A downward-sloping performance line as tool-description tokens grow from 8K to 120K, with "tool hallucination" and "selection ambiguity" annotated on the degraded zone
- Manim move: decay
- Example seed: You give an agent 5 tools: accuracy 91%. You add 45 more tools "so it has everything it might need": accuracy drops to 73% and it invokes a `notify_coordinator` tool that isn't in the list. You add a router that exposes only the 5 relevant tools per task: accuracy returns to 90%.
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: geo (tool-catalog context-budget plate, performance-degradation chart), c2v (toolbox object)
- Prerequisites: basic idea of what an AI agent is, context window concept
- Exclusions: no ReAct loop mechanics in full, no structured-output section, no multi-turn memory management, no LongFuncEval benchmark walkthrough

## Candidate 08 — Why the Reasoning in a Chain-of-Thought Trace Doesn't Have to Be Valid
- Source: `prompt-engineering/chapters/01-the-stochastic-machine.md`
- Topic: PROMPT ENGINEERING
- Hook: Researchers fed a model reasoning steps that were logically wrong — and recovered 80 to 90 percent of the accuracy of valid chain-of-thought. The reasoning was broken; the answers were right.
- Key case: Wang et al. replace correct reasoning steps in few-shot examples with invalid ones — wrong logic, wrong intermediate steps — while keeping the steps relevant and in order. The model recovers 80–90% of valid-CoT performance. What mattered was not logical correctness but that the steps were ordered and on-topic.
- The Question: Chain-of-thought is supposed to work by making the model reason validly. These models reasoned invalidly and still got the right answers. So what is chain-of-thought actually doing?
- Core idea: Chain-of-thought conditions the decoding trajectory into a step-shaped region of the output distribution that correlates with correct answers — it is not making the model execute logic, it is steering toward a shape that lands on correct answers more often.
- Visual object: A distribution landscape with a highlighted "step-shaped trajectory manifold" — chain-of-thought conditions decoding into that region, not toward a truth endpoint
- Manim move: transform
- Example seed: You have a model answer a word problem. Direct answer: wrong. Valid chain-of-thought: right. Invalid chain-of-thought (steps are out of order and factually wrong, but each step uses the right vocabulary): also right, 85% of the time. The visible steps are not the thing doing the work.
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: geo (distribution manifold, step-shaped trajectory region), c2v (none needed)
- Prerequisites: what chain-of-thought prompting is, basic idea of output as a distribution sample
- Exclusions: no Kojima "step by step" accuracy figures in full, no Wang and Zhou decoding paper, no self-consistency voting mechanism, no reasoning-tuned-model tier table
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 11 — Why a Negative Instruction Is More Powerful Than a Positive One
- Source: `prompt-engineering/chapters/05-the-architect-mindset.md`
- Topic: PROMPT ENGINEERING
- Hook: "Do not use any word sense involving violence, law, or adult themes" is a stronger safety instruction for a children's vocabulary app than "use child-appropriate language" — and there is a clean mathematical reason why.
- Key case: The Wordsville educational platform's prompt says "for kids." When asked for a lesson on "trial," the model returns courtroom procedure. On "shoot," it returns something worse. The positive instruction "for kids" left the entire failure space populated. Adding "Do NOT use any word sense involving violence, weapons, law, courts, death, or adult themes" eliminates the failure zones by name.
- The Question: "For kids" and "do not use violent word senses" seem to target the same goal. One failed repeatedly; the other fixed it. Why is the negative instruction more powerful?
- Core idea: A positive constraint reweights probability mass toward a vague good region while leaving the rest of the distribution populated; a negative constraint removes mass from specific nameable failure zones entirely — and deleting a region is a stronger operation than nudging toward an undefined good, because entropy drops more decisively when you know exactly what you don't want.
- Visual object: Two probability-mass landscapes: left panel — positive constraint piles soil on the good patch but rain still falls elsewhere; right panel — negative constraint removes the failure zones entirely and the surviving mass concentrates on what remains
- Manim move: collapse
- Example seed: You're building a customer-service bot. Positive: "Be professional and polite." Result: the model occasionally recommends competitor products when "professionally" noting they exist. Negative: "Do not mention any competitor by name or product category." Result: competitor mentions drop to near zero. The constraint didn't say what to say; it said what to delete.
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: geo (probability-landscape two-panel plate, entropy annotation), c2v (Wordsville children's book object)
- Prerequisites: basic idea of probability distribution, what a prompt instruction is
- Exclusions: no PAST/PLFR framework in full, no Shannon entropy formula, no PLFR Result-layer validation discussion, no four-layer prompt breakdown
- Score: 8/10

- Key case: Huang et al. test intrinsic self-correction — asking the model to review and revise its own reasoning with no external feedback — across reasoning tasks. Models flip correct answers to incorrect ones. The apparent gains in earlier self-correction papers required an oracle (ground-truth labels) to decide when to stop. Remove the oracle: accuracy on GSM8K-style tasks drops.
- The Question: Double-checking your work is obviously good practice. When humans verify their own answers, accuracy goes up. When you ask a model to verify its own answers, accuracy goes down. Why?

It revises to a wrong answer with a confident explanation of why the first one was flawed. You run the same puzzle 10 times at temperature 0.7 and take the majority vote instead: correct 8/10. The majority-vote method never asked the model to judge itself.
- Length band: 2–3 min

## Candidate 15 — Why "Temperature Zero Is Reliable" Is the Most Dangerous Prompt Engineering Myth
- Source: `prompt-engineering/chapters/00-foundations-sampling-and-logits.md`
- Topic: PROMPT ENGINEERING
- Hook: "Set temperature to zero for reliability" is the most common piece of prompting advice — and it doesn't remove error, it can introduce looping, and it still isn't deterministic in production.
- Key case: A practitioner sets temperature to zero for a summarization system, believing this removes randomness and makes outputs "safe." Google's developer guidance for Gemini 3 explicitly recommends against lowering temperature on complex reasoning tasks because it can induce looping — the model selects a high-probability token that re-licenses itself as high-probability, and repeats indefinitely.
- The Question: Temperature zero removes sampling randomness. Removing randomness should make outputs more reliable. So why can temperature zero make some models loop or degrade? And why is it still not deterministic in real deployments?
- Core idea: Temperature zero picks the greedy peak at every step — but greedy decoding can trap a model in self-reinforcing loops; floating-point non-associativity, request batching, and silent model updates mean "temperature zero" isn't bit-identical across production runs; and a peaked distribution on the wrong token gives you a stable, confident error, not a reliable correct answer.
- Visual object: Three probability-distribution bar charts side by side: T→0 (spike on one token, greedy/argmax), T=1 (moderate peak), T→∞ (flat uniform noise)
- Manim move: transform
- Example seed: You set temperature to 0 for a classifier. Output: "Category A. Category A. Category A. Category A..." — a loop. You raise temperature to 0.3: "Category A." — correct, stable, no loop. The zero-temperature setting was deterministically wrong.
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: geo (three-panel temperature bar chart, softmax formula plate), c2v (none needed)
- Prerequisites: nothing — this is the entry-level misconception film
- Exclusions: no top-k vs top-p comparison, no nucleus sampling paper, no order-of-operations discussion, no chain-rule derivation, no logit arithmetic worked example
- Score: 8/10
