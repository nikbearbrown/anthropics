## Candidate 01 — "Research the Four Subfields of Anthropology with Claude"
- Source: anthropology-plus-one/chapters/introduction-anthropology/01-what-is-anthropology.md (and module files 01-m00001.md through 01-m00008.md)
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: The same academic discipline encompasses a woman excavating a 9,000-year-old hunter's grave in Peru, a team studying smartphone use in Uganda, and a researcher analyzing monkey hormone levels in a Ugandan conservation park. Researching what holds all four subfields together reveals anthropology's defining question — and why its answer is unusual in the social sciences.

For each subfield: describe its primary focus, its main methods, and give one specific named researcher and their contemporary project. What is the holistic principle that unifies the four fields? - Read / check: Cultural = ethnographic fieldwork, participant observation. Biological/physical = primatology, paleoanthropology, human genetics. Archaeological = material culture, excavation. Linguistic = language structure and social function. Holism = the claim that biology, culture, language, and history cannot be understood independently. The department-split controversy should name at least one institutional example (Stanford, Duke) and the argument for and against integration.
- Human supplies: Nothing — fully synthetic. Spot-check at least one named researcher against a university faculty page.
- Output medium: Manim (a four-quadrant diagram fills in; each quadrant shows the subfield name, methods icon, and a researcher name; connecting lines to the center label "holism" animate last to show the integrating principle)
- The change: Ask Claude to find a research question that genuinely requires all four subfields to answer — for example: "How did agriculture change human health and social organization?" — and trace which subfield contributes what.
- Teardown angle: Anthropology's unusual breadth is its claim and its problem. The claim: only a field that holds biology and culture together can answer the deepest questions about what humans are. The problem: no single researcher can be expert in all four.

## Candidate 02 — "Research Participant Observation and Its Limits with Claude"
- Source: anthropology-plus-one/chapters/introduction-anthropology/02-methods-cultural-and-archaeological.md
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: Participant observation is the method that defines cultural anthropology — but it requires the researcher to become partly an insider in a community they are studying as an outsider. Researching the method's strengths, its epistemological limits, and the ethics of fieldwork with Claude reveals why anthropology's methods are always also moral choices.
- The artifact: A sourced 3-part brief: (1) participant observation defined precisely — the researcher's dual role (participant + observer) and what each role contributes to knowledge, (2) the three major critiques (observer effect, researcher positionality, power asymmetry between researcher and subject community), (3) the shift from salvage anthropology to collaborative and decolonial fieldwork — what changed and why.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Explain participant observation as the core method of cultural anthropology: what does the researcher do, how long does fieldwork typically last, and what kind of knowledge does it produce that survey or interview methods cannot? Then identify the three strongest methodological critiques of participant observation — observer effect, researcher positionality, and power asymmetry between researcher and studied community. Finally, describe the shift toward collaborative and decolonial anthropological practice: what changed in who designs the research questions, who owns the data, and who benefits from the findings?"`
- Read / check: Participant observation: researcher lives within the community for an extended period (typically 12–24 months), learns the language, participates in everyday life while taking systematic notes. Observer effect: researcher's presence changes community behavior. Positionality: researcher's own social position (race, gender, class, nationality) shapes what they can see. Power asymmetry: historically, anthropologists studied colonized peoples for the benefit of colonial administrations. Decolonial shift: community IRB, co-authorship, data sovereignty.
- Human supplies: Nothing — fully synthetic.
- Output medium: slate (two-column diagram: "what participant observation sees" vs. "what it cannot see" — each cell filling in from Claude's response; a second panel shows the decolonial shift as a before/after comparison)
- The change: Ask Claude to identify a contemporary case where the decolonial critique is most active — indigenous data sovereignty in genetic anthropology — and what the field's current best practices are.
- Teardown angle: Participant observation produces knowledge that no other method can access. It also embeds the researcher in a power relationship with the community. The method does not resolve this tension — it requires the researcher to hold it consciously throughout fieldwork.
- Exclusions: Specific fieldwork examples in full, quantitative methods in anthropology — tangents.
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 03 — "Research Biological Evolution and Bipedalism with Claude"
- Source: anthropology-plus-one/chapters/introduction-anthropology/04-biological-evolution-and-early-human-evidence.md (and module files 01-m00023.md through 01-m00030.md)
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: Three sets of footprints in volcanic ash, 3.5 million years old. Already walking upright. Already with a striding gait, an arched foot, a big toe aligned — not opposable. Before any stone tool, before any brain enlargement — bipedalism first. Researching why bipedalism evolved before the large brain flips the conventional story.
- The artifact: A sourced 3-part brief: (1) the Laetoli footprints as evidence — what the prints show, what species made them, why the anatomy is unambiguous for bipedalism, (2) three competing hypotheses for why bipedalism evolved first (thermoregulation in open savanna, freed hands for tool use, energy efficiency on flat terrain) — each with the evidence for and against, (3) why brain enlargement follows bipedalism rather than preceding it — what the fossil sequence shows.
- Prompt seed: `claude "The Laetoli footprints in Tanzania are dated to approximately 3.5 million years ago and attributed to Australopithecus afarensis. What do the prints reveal about the gait and foot anatomy of these hominins, and why is the evidence unambiguous for bipedalism rather than knuckle-walking? Then give me three competing hypotheses for why bipedalism evolved before significant brain enlargement: thermoregulation, freed hands, and energy efficiency. For each: state the evidence supporting it and the evidence that complicates it. Finally, describe the fossil sequence that shows brain size expanded after bipedalism was established — what is the timeline?"`
- Read / check: Laetoli prints: heel-toe gait, arched foot, abducted big toe (not opposable like ape) — all indicating obligate bipedalism. Thermoregulation: upright posture exposes less body surface to direct sun, raises head above hot ground-level air. Freed hands: tools appear 1+ million years after Laetoli — weakens this hypothesis as driver. Energy efficiency: bipedalism costs ~75% less energy per kilometer than quadrupedal knuckle-walking on flat terrain. Brain enlargement: H. habilis (~2.4 mya) shows first significant increase from A. afarensis baseline — ~500 cc vs. ~400 cc.
- Human supplies: Nothing — fully synthetic. Spot-check Laetoli date and species attribution against published paleoanthropology.
- Output medium: Manim (a timeline animates from 4 mya to 1 mya: Laetoli footprints appear at 3.5 mya, brain-size marker appears at 2.4 mya, stone tools at 2.6 mya; the sequence shows bipedalism preceding both brain enlargement and tools)
- The change: Ask Claude to apply the same fossil-sequence reasoning to the Homo floresiensis discovery ("hobbit" — small brain, full bipedalism, island isolation) — does the floresiensis case support or complicate the bipedalism-first sequence?
- Teardown angle: The brain-first narrative of human evolution is wrong. The fossil record is unambiguous: feet changed before brains. What changed first shapes what selective pressures came next — and the freed-hands hypothesis only works if bipedalism was already established for other reasons.
- Exclusions: Full hominin phylogeny, dating methods in detail — separate cards.
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 04 — "Research Kinship Terminologies with Claude"
- Source: anthropology-plus-one/chapters/introduction-anthropology/11-forming-family-through-kinship.md
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: English has one word for "cousin" — a category that other languages split into parallel and cross-cousins, maternal and paternal sides, elder and younger. Researching why different societies classify kin differently reveals that kinship terminologies are not descriptions of biology — they are systems for organizing who can marry whom, who inherits what, and who owes whom what obligations.
- The artifact: A sourced 3-system comparison brief (Eskimo/Lineal = English system, Iroquois, Hawaiian) — what each system lumps together, what it distinguishes, and what the social consequences of each classification are (marriage rules, inheritance patterns, obligation networks) — plus a 100-word analysis of why anthropologists say kinship terminologies are performative (they create social reality) rather than purely descriptive.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Compare three kinship terminology systems: Eskimo (Lineal), Iroquois, and Hawaiian. For each system: explain which relatives it groups together under the same term and which it distinguishes, give a concrete example of how the terminology maps relatives in ego's generation and in the parental generation, and explain what social rules and obligations the terminology reflects or creates (marriage rules, inheritance, reciprocal obligations). Why do anthropologists say kinship terminologies are 'performative' — they create social obligations rather than just describing biological relationships?"`
- Read / check: Eskimo/Lineal: distinguishes lineal from collateral kin — only direct ancestors and descendants are "family" in primary sense; all cousins lumped together. Iroquois: distinguishes parallel cousins (father's brother's children, mother's sister's children — merged with siblings) from cross-cousins (father's sister's children, mother's brother's children — marriageable in many systems). Hawaiian: simplest — all relatives of same generation merged by sex only. The performative point: calling someone "brother" creates obligations of mutual aid and prohibitions on sexual relations — the term does the social work.
- Human supplies: Nothing — fully synthetic.
- Output medium: Manim (three kinship diagrams animate side by side — ego at center, relatives shown as nodes, same-category nodes color-coded together; the different coloring patterns across systems shows the different classification logic)
- The change: Ask Claude to find a contemporary kinship controversy — for example, how step-parenting or same-sex parenting is handled in traditional kinship terminology systems — and which systems adapt more easily than others.
- Teardown angle: Kinship is one of anthropology's oldest puzzles and still its most counterintuitive finding: the family is not a biological fact that different cultures describe differently. It is a social construction that different cultures build differently — and the terminology is the construction blueprint.
- Exclusions: Descent theory (matrilineal, patrilineal) in full, specific marriage rules — tangents.
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 05 — "Research Language Endangerment and Preservation with Claude"
- Source: anthropology-plus-one/chapters/introduction-anthropology/06-language-and-communication.md
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: Of the roughly 7,000 languages spoken today, more than half are expected to disappear by 2100. When a language dies, the ethnobiological and cultural knowledge encoded in it — plant names, kinship systems, navigational metaphors, grammatical categories — often dies with it. Researching what is lost and what can be preserved with Claude reveals the limits of documentation.
- The artifact: A sourced 3-part brief: (1) the scale of language loss — how many languages exist, how many are endangered (fewer than 1,000 speakers), how many die per year (estimate: one every two weeks), (2) two specific cases of what is linguistically irreplaceable (Pirahã's absence of recursion and color terms as a challenge to linguistic universals; the Australian Aboriginal Guugu Yimithirr language's absolute spatial orientation system — north/south/east/west instead of left/right), (3) what language documentation can and cannot preserve — why a dictionary and grammar do not replace a living speech community.
- Prompt seed: `claude "How many languages exist today, and how many are endangered? What is the estimated rate of language extinction? Describe two specific languages whose disappearance would represent an irreplaceable intellectual loss: Pirahã (spoken in the Amazon basin) and Guugu Yimithirr (an Australian Aboriginal language with absolute spatial orientation). For each: what is linguistically unusual about the language, and what would be lost if it were no longer spoken? Finally, explain the limits of language documentation: why can a comprehensive dictionary and grammar not fully preserve a language, and what does documentation preserve vs. what it loses?"`
- Read / check: ~7,000 languages exist; ~40% are endangered (fewer than 1,000 speakers); one dies approximately every two weeks. Pirahã: no recursion (Everett's claim), no number terms, no color terms — contested but unresolved. Guugu Yimithirr: speakers orient using absolute compass directions (north/south/east/west) rather than egocentric (left/right) — even for small-scale body reference. Documentation limit: grammar + dictionary captures the system but not the pragmatics, the community of speakers who know when to use which form, the embodied knowledge of ceremony and context.
- Human supplies: Nothing — fully synthetic. Note: Pirahã claims are contested; Claude should flag the academic debate.
- Output medium: slate (two-panel comparison: Guugu Yimithirr spatial orientation diagram vs. English left/right system; a second panel shows the documentation gap — what is in the dictionary vs. what disappears with the last speaker)
- The change: Ask Claude to apply the language documentation framework to a digitally endangered context — what is being lost as text-based communication replaces oral tradition in communities where literacy and orality coexisted?
- Teardown angle: Language documentation is not language preservation. It is autopsy. The living system — the community of speakers who repair each other's usage, pass down pragmatic knowledge, and create new meanings — cannot be archived. What is lost when a language dies is not a code. It is a world.
- Exclusions: Full Sapir-Whorf debate, neurobiology of language learning — tangents.
- Score: 8/10

- The artifact: A sourced 3-case brief (Thailand, India, Hmong-American communities in the US) with: for each case, what medical systems coexist, why patients use multiple systems simultaneously, what each system handles best, and one case where the systems came into conflict rather than complementing each other — plus a 150-word assessment of what structural violence (Farmer) adds to the explanatory model.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Medical pluralism is the norm globally — most people, in most places, use multiple medical systems simultaneously. Give me three cases: traditional medicine and biomedicine in Thailand, Ayurveda and biomedicine in India, and Hmong healing practices and biomedicine among Hmong-American communities in California. For each case: which systems coexist, why do patients use multiple systems, what does each system handle that the other does not, and give one example of a specific conflict between the systems. Then apply Paul Farmer's structural violence framework: how does the unequal distribution of biomedical resources across class and race lines shape which system patients can access?"`
- Read / check: Thailand: biomedical hospitals, traditional Thai massage (nuad thai), spirit healing for soul-related illness — complementary in practice. India: Ayurveda for chronic conditions and lifestyle, biomedicine for acute infections and trauma — recognized by Indian government as AYUSH system. Hmong-American: txiv neeb rituals + biomedicine — the Lia Lee case is the conflict case. Structural violence: the poorest communities have the least access to biomedicine and therefore rely more heavily on traditional systems — but the systems themselves may be their preference rather than a last resort.
- Human supplies: Nothing — fully synthetic.
- Output medium: slate (3-column table filling in with system comparisons; below it, a structural violence diagram showing unequal access to biomedical care across socioeconomic layers)
- The change: Ask Claude how the COVID-19 pandemic tested medical pluralism in each of these three contexts — which traditional systems were used alongside biomedical public health guidance, and what does that reveal about the limits of a purely biomedical response to a pandemic?
- Teardown angle: Pluralism is not confusion or primitive thinking. It is a rational response to the fact that different medical systems are good at different things. Biomedicine is best at acute infectious disease and trauma — and performs worse than many assume at chronic illness, illness meaning, and illness experience.
- Exclusions: Full pharmacological comparison of traditional remedies, specific drug interactions — tangents.

## Candidate 07 — "Research Indigenous Knowledge and Epistemology with Claude"
- Source: anthropology-plus-one/chapters/introduction-anthropology/19-indigenous-anthropology.md
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: Indigenous knowledge systems have been dismissed as "myth" and "folklore" — and then ethnobotanists discovered that indigenous Amazonian communities had identified 40% of the plants that became modern pharmaceuticals, using systematic observation methods that European science did not recognize as scientific. Researching what makes a knowledge system scientific reveals that the question is harder than it sounds.
- The artifact: A sourced 3-case brief: (1) traditional ecological knowledge (TEK) in indigenous Amazon communities and the pharmaceutical record — how many drugs trace to indigenous identification, (2) the Navigator schools of the Marshall Islands — how Polynesian navigators used wave patterns, star positions, and swells to navigate the Pacific without instruments, (3) the concept of "two-eyed seeing" (Etuaptmumk) developed by Mi'kmaw Elder Albert Marshall — weaving indigenous and Western knowledge systems rather than replacing one with the other. Each case evaluated against the question: what makes this knowledge scientific?
- Prompt seed: `claude "Evaluate three indigenous knowledge systems against the question: 'what makes a knowledge system scientific?' — Focus on: systematic observation, hypothesis testing, inter-generational transmission, and practical verification. Case 1: traditional ecological knowledge in Amazonian communities and its relationship to pharmaceutical discovery. Case 2: the navigation system used by Marshall Island navigators — wave piloting and stellar navigation without instruments. Case 3: Mi'kmaw Elder Albert Marshall's concept of 'two-eyed seeing' (Etuaptmumk) — weaving indigenous and Western knowledge. For each: identify which criteria of scientific knowledge it meets and which it does not, and explain why dismissing these systems as 'unscientific' is an epistemological error."`
- Read / check: TEK/pharmaceutical connection: approximately 25–40% of modern pharmaceuticals derived from plants originally identified by indigenous communities (estimates vary — Claude should give a range). Marshall Island navigation: sticks charts encode wave-interference patterns around islands — navigators read swells with their bodies, not just eyes. Two-eyed seeing: not integration but juxtaposition — each knowledge system retains its integrity and is used in appropriate contexts. The epistemological error: conflating scientific method with Western institutional science.
- Human supplies: Nothing — fully synthetic. Note that pharmaceutical derivation statistics vary by study — Claude should give a range and flag the uncertainty.
- Output medium: slate (3-panel case study layout; each panel shows the knowledge system, the criteria it meets/doesn't meet, and the epistemological lesson)
- The change: Ask Claude what intellectual property protections exist for traditional knowledge, and why the current patent system is structurally unable to protect indigenous pharmaceutical knowledge from biopiracy.
- Teardown angle: The question is not whether indigenous knowledge is scientific. It is whether "scientific" is a wide enough category for all the ways humans have built reliable knowledge. The navigator who crosses the Pacific without instruments has built reliable knowledge about the ocean. What category does that belong to?
- Exclusions: Full postcolonial theory, specific legislative frameworks for indigenous IP — tangents.

## Candidate 08 — "Research Cultural Relativism's Limits with Claude"
- Source: anthropology-plus-one/chapters/introduction-anthropology/01-what-is-anthropology.md; anthropology-plus-one/chapters/introduction-anthropology/03-culture-concept-theory-theories-of-cultural-change.md
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: Cultural relativism — the methodological principle that practices must be understood within their own cultural context rather than judged by outsiders — is the foundation of modern anthropology. But does it require withholding moral judgment about practices that cause harm? Researching the distinction between methodological and moral relativism with Claude, then applying it to three cases, reveals where the principle holds and where it breaks.
- The artifact: A sourced 3-case brief (female genital cutting, indigenous child labor practices vs. exploitative industrial child labor, forced marriage) applying the distinction between methodological relativism (understand the practice in context) and moral relativism (never judge) — each case showing what methodological relativism requires and what it does not require of the anthropologist.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Distinguish methodological cultural relativism from moral relativism in anthropology. Methodological relativism says: understand a practice within its cultural context before evaluating it. Moral relativism says: no practice can ever be judged across cultural lines. Anthropologists endorse the first but not necessarily the second. Apply this distinction to three cases: (1) female genital cutting practiced in certain African and Middle Eastern communities, (2) the difference between traditional child labor in agricultural subsistence communities vs. exploitative industrial child labor, and (3) arranged marriage practices in South Asian communities. For each: what does methodological relativism require of the anthropologist, and where does it leave room for moral evaluation?"`
- Read / check: FGC: methodological relativism requires understanding the practice as a rite of passage, a marker of cultural belonging, and a medical procedure in some contexts — before evaluating it. Moral relativism is not required: harm to girls who do not consent is evaluable across cultures. The UN and many African women's organizations oppose FGC on precisely these grounds. Child labor: the distinction between traditional agricultural participation (child learns skills, works alongside family) and exploitative industrial labor (child works instead of school, for others' profit) is culturally legible. Arranged marriage: consent is the morally salient variable; arranged marriage with consent is different from forced marriage.
- Human supplies: Nothing — fully synthetic.
- Output medium: Manim (a two-column table animates: "what methodological relativism requires" and "what it leaves room for" — rows fill in for each case; the distinction between the two columns is the design judgment the viewer takes away)
- The change: Ask Claude to find the strongest argument for full moral relativism — that all cultural practices are equally valid — and then the strongest anthropological counterargument. Who wins?
- Teardown angle: Methodological relativism is a research tool, not an ethical commitment. The anthropologist who suspends judgment during fieldwork is not committed to suspending it forever. Understanding a practice and judging it are sequential, not mutually exclusive.
- Exclusions: Full history of anthropological theory, specific UN human rights conventions — tangents.
- Score: 7/10

## Candidate 09 — "Research Gender as Social Construction with Claude"
- Source: anthropology-plus-one/chapters/introduction-anthropology/12-gender-and-sexuality.md
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: Margaret Mead's 1935 fieldwork in New Guinea found three cultures where gender roles were distributed in ways that reversed or scrambled Euro-American assumptions. The finding has been contested, refined, and re-confirmed across anthropology for 90 years. Researching what "gender as social construction" means precisely, and what it does and does not claim, makes the concept usable without reducing it to a slogan.
- The artifact: A sourced 3-part brief: (1) sex/gender distinction defined precisely (sex = biological characteristics, gender = social roles and identities built on top of them), (2) three cross-cultural cases — Mead's Tchambuli (gender reversal), the Hijra of South Asia (third-gender institutionalized role), and Two-Spirit identities in some Native American communities (gender categories beyond binary) — each showing what "social construction" means concretely, (3) a 100-word synthesis on what "social construction" does not mean: it does not mean "arbitrary" or "unreal."
- Prompt seed: `claude "Explain the sex/gender distinction as used in anthropology: what does 'sex' refer to, what does 'gender' refer to, and why does the distinction matter for cross-cultural comparison? Then give me three cross-cultural cases that illustrate gender as a social construction: Margaret Mead's Tchambuli findings in New Guinea (contested — include the critique), the Hijra of South Asia as an institutionalized third gender, and Two-Spirit identities in some Native American traditions. For each: what does the case show about gender's social organization, and what does it show about what 'social construction' means? Close with 100 words on what social construction does NOT mean — it is not the same as 'arbitrary' or 'unimportant.'"`
- Read / check: Sex/gender distinction: sex = chromosomes, gonads, hormones, anatomy; gender = roles, identities, expectations built socially on top of sex. Mead Tchambuli: men described as decorative and emotionally dependent, women as practical and assertive — reversal of Western norms. Freeman's critique (1983): methodological errors in Mead's fieldwork. Current status: Mead's specific findings contested; the general principle (gender varies cross-culturally) is not. Hijra: recognized third gender in India, Pakistan, Bangladesh — legal recognition in all three countries since 2014–2018. Two-Spirit: pan-tribal term adopted in 1990 by Indigenous activists; not a single category but a family of gender roles across many nations. Social construction ≠ arbitrary: language is socially constructed but still structures thought; money is socially constructed but still has real economic effects.
- Human supplies: Nothing — fully synthetic.
- Output medium: Manim (a world map with three cultural case sites labeled; each case study panel animates — Tchambuli, Hijra, Two-Spirit — with the gender role configuration shown as a diagram; the final frame shows the "social construction ≠ arbitrary" distinction)
- The change: Ask Claude to describe a society where a binary gender system has been legally extended to include additional categories (India's "third gender" legal recognition) and evaluate what the legal recognition does and does not change about the social experience.
- Teardown angle: Gender is one of the most robustly socially constructed features of human life — and one of the most experienced as natural. The cross-cultural record shows that the categories vary; the experience of them as natural and inevitable does not.
- Exclusions: Biological sex differences research, full debates on gender identity — tangents.
- Score: 7/10

## Candidate 10 — "Research Globalization and Cultural Change with Claude"
- Source: anthropology-plus-one/chapters/introduction-anthropology/20-anthropology-on-the-ground.md; anthropology-plus-one/chapters/introduction-anthropology/15-anthropology-of-media.md
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: When Daniel Miller's team studied smartphone use in nine countries across four continents, they found that the same device took on radically different social functions in each place — not because the technology is culturally neutral but because culture absorbs, adapts, and reconfigures what it receives from global flows. Researching cultural globalization through anthropological fieldwork rather than economic theory reveals that homogenization is only half the story.
- The artifact: A sourced 3-case brief (Miller's smartphone research — Irish "personal avatar" use vs. Ugandan community platform use; McDonald's in East Asia as "localization" case; Korean pop music as global reverse cultural flow) — each case showing the specific direction of cultural change (homogenization vs. adaptation vs. reverse flow) — plus a 150-word synthesis on why anthropologists prefer "glocalization" to "globalization" and what the distinction changes.
- Prompt seed: `claude "The anthropological study of globalization emphasizes that cultures do not passively receive global influences — they actively reconfigure them. Give me three cases that illustrate different directions of cultural change under globalization: (1) Daniel Miller's smartphone research showing how Irish and Ugandan communities use the same device for different social functions, (2) McDonald's in East Asia as a case of 'localization' rather than cultural imperialism, (3) Korean pop music (K-pop) as a case of reverse cultural flow from the global periphery to the center. For each case: what direction is the cultural change moving, and what does it challenge about the simple 'globalization = homogenization' narrative? Close with 150 words on the anthropological concept of 'glocalization.'"`
- Read / check: Miller smartphone study: Ireland — smartphones as personal identity expression (avatar); Uganda — smartphones as community resource, shared hardware, family use across generations. McDonald's East Asia: menu adaptations (rice dishes, local proteins), McDonald's as a clean, respectable family dining space (status marker) in some contexts rather than fast food. K-pop: engineered for global export, draws on Western pop structures while encoding Korean aesthetics and fan culture — influences Western pop industry. Glocalization (Robertson 1995): global and local processes are co-constitutive; the global is always localized, the local is always globalized.
- Human supplies: Nothing — fully synthetic. Spot-check Miller's smartphone project findings against "How the World Changed Social Media" (UCL Press, 2016) — open access.
- Output medium: slate (three case study panels with direction-of-flow arrows: → homogenization, → adaptation, ← reverse flow; the "glocalization" concept appears as a two-way arrow at the bottom)
- The change: Ask Claude to apply the glocalization framework to TikTok — which direction is the cultural influence flowing, and is TikTok a case of Chinese cultural export, global platform adaptation, or something else entirely?
- Teardown angle: "Globalization" names the economic process. "Glocalization" names the cultural process. They run simultaneously and produce opposite predictions about where cultural difference goes. The anthropological record so far suggests difference is more resilient than economic theory predicted.
- Exclusions: Full political economy of globalization, specific trade policy — tangents.
- Score: 7/10
