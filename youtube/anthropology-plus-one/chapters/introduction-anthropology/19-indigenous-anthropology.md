# Chapter 19 — Indigenous Anthropology

## Suggested titles

- *The Mountain that Fell into the Lake: Indigenous Anthropology*
- *Sovereignty, Knowledge, and the Decolonization of a Discipline*

## TL;DR

Indigenous peoples have, for five centuries, been the subjects of an anthropology written largely by outsiders for outsiders. The discipline has spent the last sixty years being told this is no longer acceptable — and is changing. The chapter walks through the Indigenous critique of anthropology (Vine Deloria Jr., Linda Tuhiwai Smith, Beatrice Medicine), the apparatus of treaty rights and tribal sovereignty in U.S. and comparable settler-colonial contexts, and the contemporary work of Indigenous scholars writing their own communities. The source chapter is by David Lewis (Confederated Tribes of Grand Ronde); the present version preserves his framing where it belongs.

## 1. The mountain that fell into the lake

Southwestern Oregon, the southern Cascades. A circular lake more than five miles across, bounded by cliffs that rise 1,000 feet from the water's surface. The water is some of the bluest on Earth — almost 600 meters deep, fed only by snowmelt and rain, with no rivers flowing in or out. The U.S. Geological Survey calls it Crater Lake. The Klamath people, whose traditional territory includes the lake, call it Giiwas. *Crater* is wrong. It is a caldera. The mountain that stood there did not crater inward; it blew apart, and the rim of what had been the mountain collapsed into the empty space where the magma chamber had emptied. The lake is what remained.

Geologists working in the 1940s established the date: the eruption happened approximately 7,700 years before the present. Mount Mazama — the volcanic edifice — exploded in one of the largest eruptions of the Holocene. Tephra from the event has been identified as far east as Saskatchewan and as far north as Banff. The eruption took most of a single day. Anyone within several hundred miles would have seen the ash column, heard the explosions, felt the ground shake.

The Klamath have a story. Two mountain spirits — *Llao* of Mount Mazama and *Skell* of Mount Shasta in northern California — fought. Llao threw fire and rocks; Skell threw fire and rocks back. The battle was so violent that Mount Mazama collapsed under itself. The land was left scarred. The story has been told in the Klamath community since some time before there was geology to corroborate it. The 1940s geological surveys did not invent the eruption; they confirmed what the Klamath had been saying for, conservatively, more than 7,000 years.

David Lewis — the anthropologist whose source chapter the present chapter draws from, a member of the Confederated Tribes of the Grand Ronde Community of Oregon, descendant of the Santiam Kalapuya, Takelma, and Chinook peoples — opens his chapter with this case because the case demonstrates what every contemporary chapter on Indigenous anthropology has to demonstrate first. *Indigenous knowledge has standing*. It has been the case all along; the discipline has been slow to recognize it. The Klamath oral history was correct about the geology of Mount Mazama millennia before Western science could verify the eruption.

The Mazama case is not exceptional. Indigenous oral histories across Cascadia preserve the Missoula floods (15,000–18,000 BP), the Cascadia subduction-zone tsunami of January 26, 1700 (corroborated by Japanese tide-gauge records and Cascadia coastal stratigraphy), the formation of the Columbia River Gorge. Aboriginal Australian oral traditions preserve sea-level changes from the late Pleistocene. Polynesian wayfinding traditions encode astronomical and oceanographic knowledge that contemporary navigators have re-validated empirically. The pattern is consistent. Indigenous epistemologies have been doing real work, sustained by oral transmission, for spans of time the written tradition cannot match.

The chapter that follows takes that recognition as starting point, not as conclusion. Indigenous anthropology — the subdiscipline named, increasingly written by Indigenous scholars about Indigenous communities — exists because the rest of anthropology has had to be told that what it has been calling *folklore* and *myth* is also history, also science, also philosophy.

## 2. Concept 1 — Who is Indigenous, and how anthropology has approached the question

### Terms and their politics

**Indigenous peoples** are the original human populations of a land. The single English word covers an enormous range of communities, languages, traditions, sovereignty arrangements, and contemporary situations. The terms a community prefers depend on history and political context.

In the United States, *Native American* and *American Indian* are both in current use, with regional and generational variation. *Indian* carries a problematic origin (Christopher Columbus's mistaken belief that he had reached India) but is also written into hundreds of treaties, the Bureau of Indian Affairs, and the everyday speech of many tribal communities. As the source chapter notes, *the scholarly debate over these words is somewhat separate from the way the terms are used in Native communities*. Many Native communities have no objection to *Indian*; the academic debate is sometimes a distraction from substantive concerns.

In Canada, *First Nations* applies to many communities; *Inuit* and *Métis* are distinct categories with distinct rights under Canadian law. In Australia, *Aboriginal peoples* and *Torres Strait Islander peoples* are the recognized broad categories, with *Indigenous Australians* as the collective term. In New Zealand, *Maori* is the term Maori communities themselves use. In Mexico, *gente indígena* or *los pueblos indígenas* is standard. In Latin America more broadly, terminology varies by country and by the specific community involved (*indígenas*; *originarios*; *nativos*; or specific community names like *Aymara*, *Mapuche*, *Quechua*).

The most respectful contemporary practice is to use the specific community name when known — *the Lakota*, not *Native Americans*; *the Maori*, not *Indigenous people*; *the Chumash*, not *California Indians*. The general term has a use; specificity is better.

### Tribal membership and blood quantum

The contemporary question of *who counts* as a member of a specific tribal community is governed by tribal membership laws, set by each tribe under federal law. **Blood quantum** — the genealogical-percentage standard the U.S. federal government imposed in the 1930s as a citizenship criterion — remains the dominant framework, though tribes have been increasingly modifying it.

The Grand Ronde tribe of Oregon (David Lewis's tribe) requires a 1/16 blood quantum and ancestral presence on the reservation rolls. Many tribes use 1/4. The Navajo Nation, with more than 300,000 members and a continuing pattern of within-community marriage, retains a higher proportion of full-blooded members than most U.S. tribes. Most tribes are facing the demographic pressure of out-marriage — the percentage of full-blooded members declines generation by generation, and tribes whose blood-quantum requirements remain high will face self-termination by the simple arithmetic of who can prove eligibility.

Some scholars (and many Native critics) have read blood quantum as a federal mechanism to engineer tribal extinction. Russell Thornton's 1997 *Tribal Membership Requirements and the Demography of 'Old' and 'New' Native Americans* makes the demographic case directly: tribes with strict blood-quantum standards will lose membership over time without policy change. Many tribes are shifting to *lineal descent* — membership based on documented descent from an enrolled tribal member, regardless of percentage. The shift is closer to many traditional pre-reservation membership practices, which adopted in-marrying spouses and outsiders who took up the culture. The federal-imposed blood-quantum framework is a relatively recent intervention that displaced more flexible community-membership practices.

The politics inside specific tribes are also complex. At Grand Ronde, a 1999 membership rule restricted enrollment to children whose parents were enrolled at the time of birth — a rule that produced split families, in which some siblings are enrolled and others are not, depending on whether their parents had completed enrollment by the time each child was born. The rule has been the subject of ongoing tribal debate. *Membership rules are political; they reflect contested community judgments about identity, resources, and the future of the tribe.*

### Reservation, urban, and the categories in between

In the United States and Canada, tribal communities have, since the 19th century, been concentrated on reservations — lands set aside (typically marginal lands, often far from traditional homelands) under treaties or executive orders. Reservations vary enormously in size, resources, governance capacity, and economic situation. The Navajo Nation reservation covers 71,000 km² across Arizona, New Mexico, and Utah. The Grand Ronde Reservation in Oregon is 38 km². Some reservations have substantial natural-resource bases (the Osage Nation's oil reserves; the Klamath's pre-termination ponderosa-pine forest); most do not.

**Reservation Indian** and **urban Indian** are the two broad demographic categories the chapter's source identifies. The majority of U.S. Native people now live in urban environments. The shift accelerated in the 1950s and 1960s under the federal **termination** policy — a 1953 policy that voided treaty agreements between the federal government and 109 specific tribes, liquidated reservation lands, and pushed the affected populations into wage labor in cities. Most terminated tribes were eventually restored (Klamath in 1986; Grand Ronde in 1983), but the demographic shift to cities was largely permanent.

Portland, Oregon — the chapter's source author's home metropolitan area — has approximately 40,000 people of Native descent, one of the largest urban Native concentrations in the U.S. The community supports tribal offices (Confederated Tribes of Grand Ronde; Confederated Tribes of Siletz Indians), education programs, intertribal cultural events (the Delta Park Powwow), Indigenous-language learning groups, and a range of advocacy organizations. The community is genuinely intertribal — Native people from across the U.S. and Canada, plus Indigenous diaspora from Latin America and the Pacific Islands, share urban infrastructure that did not exist on any of their original reservations.

The phrase *urban Indian* has, in some contexts, taken on negative connotations within Indigenous communities — used by reservation residents to imply that urban Native people have given up cultural connection. The reality is more complex. Many urban Native people maintain reservation ties, attend tribal-government meetings, return for ceremonial events, and treat the urban setting as one of two or more sites of identity rather than as a replacement for reservation life. The reservation-versus-urban dichotomy is real and is also too clean.

### Becoming minorities in their own lands

The framing the source chapter uses — *Indigenous peoples have become minorities in their own lands* — captures one of the central facts of contemporary Indigenous experience. In the U.S., Native Americans constitute approximately 1.7% of the population. In Canada, Indigenous peoples are approximately 5%. In Australia, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples are approximately 3.8%. In New Zealand, Maori are approximately 17% — the highest proportion among comparable settler-colonial states. In Latin America, the proportions vary widely (Bolivia is approximately 40% Indigenous; Brazil less than 1%) but most Indigenous communities sit below the cultural and political weight their numerical presence might suggest.

The minority status produces a particular legal and political situation. Many Indigenous peoples object to being treated as one minority among many; their treaty-based or sovereignty-based status differs from immigrant minority status. The Maori in New Zealand have asked specifically not to be treated as a minority — their relationship to the New Zealand state is governed by the 1840 Treaty of Waitangi, which gives them a particular standing the framework of *minority* does not capture. Similar arguments apply for treaty-bound Native peoples in the U.S. and Canada. *Sovereignty is not the same as ethnic minority status; conflating them produces the wrong policy.*

## 3. Concept 2 — The Indigenous critique of anthropology and the work of decolonization

### Vine Deloria Jr. and the moment the discipline could no longer ignore

In 1969, Vine Deloria Jr., a Sioux scholar and lawyer, published *Custer Died for Your Sins: An Indian Manifesto*. The book was widely read, sharp-tongued, and politically consequential. One chapter — *Anthropologists and Other Friends* — became one of the most-cited critiques of academic anthropology by anyone, ever. Deloria's argument: anthropologists had been visiting Native communities, collecting knowledge, building academic careers on the backs of Indigenous suffering, and giving back essentially nothing. The communities studied received no royalties, no policy advocacy, no visible benefit. The anthropologists received tenure, books, lecture circuits, professional reputation. *The asymmetry was the entire structure of the field*.

Deloria continued the critique across five decades of scholarship. *We Talk, You Listen* (1970). *God Is Red* (1972). *Red Earth, White Lies* (1995), in which Deloria challenged the Western scientific establishment's relationship to Indigenous knowledge — calling Western science *an entrenched state religion* and arguing that its commitments to particular methodologies could blind it to evidence Indigenous traditions had been preserving for millennia.

The critique landed hard. Anthropology in the 1970s and 1980s underwent a sustained internal reckoning. The American Anthropological Association developed ethical guidelines that responded directly to Deloria's challenges. **Native studies** programs were established at U.S. universities — many founded explicitly in response to the Deloria critique. The discipline's research methods began to shift toward collaborative models in which Indigenous communities held more decision-making authority over what was studied, how, and what was done with the resulting knowledge.

Deloria's critique did not end anthropology. It changed the discipline. The contemporary field, taught from contemporary textbooks, takes Deloria's framing as intellectual furniture rather than radical proposal. *That itself is the measure of how successful the critique was*.

### Linda Tuhiwai Smith and decolonizing methodologies

Linda Tuhiwai Smith — Maori, Ngāti Awa and Ngāti Porou — published *Decolonizing Methodologies: Research and Indigenous Peoples* in 1999, with a second edition in 2012 and a third in 2021. The book has become foundational to Indigenous-studies methodology globally. Smith's argument extends Deloria's: research itself, as a Western academic practice, has functioned as an arm of colonialism. The very methods by which Western researchers approached Indigenous communities — extracting information, publishing it in venues the communities could not access, using it to advance frameworks the communities did not endorse — were continuous with the colonial seizure of land and resources.

Smith proposes a different approach. Indigenous researchers, working from Indigenous methodological starting points, conducting research that serves Indigenous community needs, accountable to Indigenous community governance. The framework does not exclude non-Indigenous researchers; it sets conditions under which non-Indigenous research can be ethical. The conditions include Indigenous control over research design, data, and outputs; Indigenous community benefit as a defining criterion of research worth doing; rigorous attention to whose framework is being applied to what.

Smith's epigrammatic line: *Telling our stories from the past, reclaiming the past, giving testimony to the injustices of the past are all strategies which are commonly employed by Indigenous peoples struggling for justice. The need to tell our stories remains the powerful imperative of a powerful form of resistance*. The framework reframes the production of knowledge itself as a sovereignty question.

### Beatrice Medicine and the work of being inside two frameworks at once

Beatrice Medicine (Sihasapa and Minneconjou Lakota, 1923–2005) demonstrated through fifty years of work that the Indigenous scholar could be both anthropologist and Native — *Learning to Be an Anthropologist and Remaining Native*, the title of her 2001 book. Medicine's storytelling, as David Lewis recalls in the source chapter, was discerning and dryly funny. She told the story of nineteenth-century anthropologists collecting "myth texts" from Lakota informants — and of the Lakota men who, realizing they would be paid for more stories, made up new ones on the spot, earned the cash, and afterward laughed at the anthropologists. The made-up stories ended up in published anthropological collections. *Some of what the discipline thinks it knows about Lakota oral tradition may be improvised material from people who were paid by the line.*

Medicine did not present that history as a reason to reject anthropology. She presented it as evidence that the discipline needs to be careful about its sources, humble about its claims, and willing to laugh at itself. Her career — across fieldwork, applied anthropology, language and culture revitalization, gender studies, the construction of an elementary school at Standing Rock in her retirement — modeled the alternative. Anthropology can be done by Indigenous scholars, in service of Indigenous communities, with the discipline's analytical apparatus as instrument rather than master.

### The SWORP project and the return of knowledge

A specific applied case. The **Southwest Oregon Research Project (SWORP)**, established in 1995 under the leadership of George Wasson of the Coquille Indian Tribe, with support from the University of Oregon and the Smithsonian Institution. The project copied and returned to Oregon's tribes more than 200,000 pages of anthropological and federal documents that had been collected from those tribes between approximately 1850 and 1950 — documents pertaining to roughly 60 different Oregon tribal communities. The materials had been sitting in the Smithsonian's National Anthropological Archives and the National Archives and Records Administration. The tribes themselves had no access to them.

SWORP organized three trips to Washington, D.C., to copy the materials. The copies were brought back to Oregon, organized at the University of Oregon Special Collections, and distributed in 1995 and 2001 to seventeen tribes in Oregon and the surrounding region. *Knowledge that anthropologists had taken from tribal communities a century earlier was returned*. The project is one of the clearest applied cases of decolonizing methodology — not abstract theory but the actual physical movement of documents back to the communities they came from.

The returned materials have powered subsequent revitalization. Clackamas Chinook canoe construction has been revived using design notes from anthropologist Philip Drucker that the SWORP project surfaced. The Pacific Northwest's annual Tribal Canoe Journey, which now involves hundreds of communities and thousands of paddlers, has drawn on the recovered ethnographic record. *The knowledge that had been frozen in archive boxes is doing work in communities again*. That is what return does.

## 4. Concept 3 — Sovereignty, treaties, and the contemporary terrain

### The Marshall Court trilogy and the architecture of U.S. tribal law

In the 1820s and 1830s, three U.S. Supreme Court decisions — collectively the **Marshall Court trilogy** — established the legal framework that has governed Native sovereignty in the United States ever since. *Johnson v. M'Intosh* (1823) held that European colonizers had acquired *title* to North American lands by *discovery* and that Native peoples held only a right of *occupancy* that could be extinguished by the federal government. *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia* (1831) classified Native nations as *domestic dependent nations* — sovereign, but sovereign within a federal-government-administered relationship that limits their sovereignty in specific ways. *Worcester v. Georgia* (1832) held that state governments had no jurisdiction over tribal lands; only the federal government did.

The trilogy's effects continue. Native nations are recognized as sovereign in the U.S. legal framework, but the sovereignty operates through a federal-government-mediated relationship that produces continuing friction. Tribal lands are *federal trust lands* — the federal government holds legal title in trust for the tribes, with consequences for any economic development, any resource extraction, any infrastructure project. The 1988 Indian Gaming Regulatory Act required tribes to *compact* with state governments to operate casinos — a requirement Native communities have repeatedly objected to as inconsistent with the sovereignty the federal trust relationship is supposed to provide.

The Marshall Court framework's paternalism is deep. *Domestic dependent nations* is a structural insult, embedded in the country's foundational law, that has produced more than 700 lawsuits over federal mismanagement of tribal money, lands, and services. The Indian Claims Commission (1946–1978) was established specifically to handle the volume. It cleared 546 dockets and awarded approximately $818 million across the period. The amounts were almost always far less than the value of what had been taken. The 1942 *Indians of California* decision awarded California tribes 47 cents per acre for 64 million acres — a total of $29.1 million for land that, had it been valued at then-current prices, would have been worth orders of magnitude more.

### The fishing wars and the Boldt decision

A more specific legal-rights case. From the 1960s through the 1980s, a series of legal battles over fishing rights in the Pacific Northwest — the *fishing wars* — tested whether the federal treaty rights of Northwest Coast tribes were enforceable. The treaties signed in the 1850s with the tribes of Washington and Oregon had reserved to those tribes the right to fish *in all usual and accustomed places*. The state governments, under pressure from non-Native commercial and sport-fishing constituencies, had been ignoring or limiting that right for a century.

In 1969, the **Belloni decision** (*Sohappy v. Smith / United States v. Oregon*) held that the treaty rights were enforceable in Oregon. In 1974, the **Boldt decision** (*United States v. Washington*) went further, ruling that the Washington-state treaty tribes were entitled to *half* of the salmon harvest in the state. Federal Judge George Boldt's reasoning was textual: the treaty language reserving fishing rights *in common with the citizens of the territory* meant equal sharing, which meant 50%. The decision was contentious. It has been repeatedly upheld on appeal.

The Boldt decision is one of the most consequential Native-rights legal victories in U.S. history. It established that treaty fishing rights were not symbolic; they were enforceable. The decision also produced a peculiar consequence: tribes that had been *terminated* in the 1950s (Klamath, Siletz, Grand Ronde) faced political resistance to their *restoration* in the 1970s and 1980s, partly because non-Native fishing interests feared that more restored tribes would mean less salmon for them. Both Siletz and Grand Ronde were forced to give up fishing and hunting rights as a condition of restoration. *The political cost of the Boldt decision was paid by other tribes.*

### Standing Rock and the contemporary apparatus

The 2016–2017 **Standing Rock** protests, led by the Standing Rock Sioux Tribe and joined by representatives from more than 300 Indigenous nations and a large non-Indigenous solidarity contingent, opposed the construction of the Dakota Access Pipeline near the tribe's reservation in North Dakota. The pipeline route had been moved south of Bismarck (a majority-white city) after Bismarck residents objected; the new route placed the pipeline crossing of the Missouri River immediately upstream of the Standing Rock reservation's water intake. The relocation pattern is structural-violence textbook: the risks the political center could not impose on a white community were instead imposed on a Native one.

The protests produced one of the largest Indigenous coalitions in U.S. history. The encampments at Oceti Sakowin and Sacred Stone hosted thousands of water protectors for months. Federal and state law enforcement responded with considerable force — water cannons in subfreezing weather, attack dogs, rubber bullets, mass arrests. The Obama administration suspended the pipeline's final permit in late 2016; the Trump administration reversed that decision in early 2017; the pipeline was completed and put into operation. The standoff did not stop the pipeline. It did mobilize global attention and produced lasting institutional networks among Indigenous communities and their allies.

The Standing Rock movement's slogan — *Water is life* (in Lakota, *Mní wičhóni*) — connects the chapter's apparatus to the One Health framework of Chapter 17 and the multispecies-ethnography apparatus of Chapter 18. Indigenous frameworks have long held that water, land, and nonhuman beings are participants in the human community rather than resources for it. The contemporary climate-and-pipeline controversies are, partly, controversies about whose framework will govern how the land is treated.

## 5. Tool #19 — Sovereignty / decolonization analyzer

The nineteenth tool in your AI-Augmented Anthropology Toolkit takes a presented case involving Indigenous communities, sovereignty, research ethics, or cultural appropriation and analyzes it across the chapter's apparatus.

**Refined prompt template:**

> You are a cultural anthropologist applying the apparatus of Indigenous anthropology to a presented case. The user will paste a description of a research project, a sovereignty dispute, an appropriation controversy, an institutional decision, or a cultural-heritage situation involving Indigenous people.
>
> Walk through the analysis in five steps.
>
> **Step 1 — Whose framework is the case operating inside?** Identify the dominant ethical and analytical framework the case is being framed by. Settler-colonial state law? Anthropological-disciplinary norms? Indigenous self-determination? An applied-anthropology consultation? Multiple frameworks in tension?
>
> **Step 2 — Which Indigenous community or communities are involved?** Identify them by specific name where possible. Recognize the difference between *one community* and *Indigenous peoples in general*. Note which voices are being heard, which are being represented by others, and which are absent.
>
> **Step 3 — Sovereignty and treaty-rights apparatus.** Identify any treaty rights, federal-recognition status, sovereignty claims, or jurisdictional frameworks at play. In U.S. cases, identify the Marshall Court framework's relevance. In comparable cases (Canada, Australia, New Zealand), identify the relevant frameworks (the Royal Proclamation of 1763; native title under *Mabo*; the Treaty of Waitangi).
>
> **Step 4 — Decolonizing-methodologies test.** Apply Smith's framework. Whose research design? Whose knowledge? Whose benefit? Where are the asymmetries?
>
> **Step 5 — Pathway forward.** Identify what an ethically defensible pathway forward would look like, given the chapter's apparatus. What would the case's resolution look like under genuine collaborative methodology, sovereignty recognition, and Indigenous community benefit?
>
> Be specific. Use the chapter's named scholars (Deloria, Smith, Medicine, Lewis, Wasson, Brayboy, Lomawaima, Estes) where their work is genuinely relevant. Flag attribution uncertainty.

Will outputs be co-authored? **Test case 2 — A non-Indigenous fashion designer using Navajo textile patterns in a 2026 ready-to-wear collection.** The patterns are based on traditional Navajo weaving designs the designer encountered at a museum exhibition. The designer has not consulted with any Navajo weavers or tribal authorities. The collection is being produced for retail sale; estimated revenue is $4 million.

The tool should surface: whose framework (commercial-fashion + intellectual-property law, which provides minimal protection for traditional Indigenous designs); the specific community (the Navajo Nation, with its specific cultural-property protocols and Diné textile-weaver community); the sovereignty apparatus (the Navajo Nation's Trademark Office and its specific cultural-resources protections); decolonizing-methodologies test (no community consultation, no benefit, no attribution — fails the test categorically); pathway forward (formal consultation, licensing arrangements, royalty agreements, partnership with Navajo weavers as co-designers, attribution).

**Test case 3 — A state university museum holding 1,200 ancestral remains and burial-associated funerary objects from regional tribal communities, repatriated under NAGPRA but for which the receiving tribes have requested continued institutional storage during their own ceremonial-protocol consultations.** The museum curator wants to confirm an arrangement that respects the tribes' authority over the timing and method of reburial.

The tool should surface: whose framework (NAGPRA-implementation + collaborative storage); the specific communities (named regional tribes); the sovereignty apparatus (NAGPRA, 1990; the tribes' own protocols for handling ancestral remains); decolonizing-methodologies test (tribes have authority over decisions; museum is in a service role; ongoing consultation is appropriate); pathway forward (formal MOU specifying the tribes' decision authority, the museum's storage role, the consultation cadence; budget for tribal travel and ceremonial expenses; staff training in protocols).

**Failure modes (three documented).**

**Failure mode 1 — Treating Indigenous peoples as monolithic.** The tool sometimes generates analysis as if there is one *Indigenous perspective* on a question. There is not. The Hopi and the Navajo, despite being neighbors, have substantially different protocols on cultural property, ancestral remains, and research permissions. The Maori and the Lakota, despite both being Indigenous peoples of settler-colonial states, have entirely different sovereignty frameworks, language situations, and contemporary political contexts. Always require specificity about *which community* the case involves.

**Failure mode 2 — Confusing romantic-Indigenous and contemporary-Indigenous.** The tool sometimes drifts into romanticized framings — Indigenous peoples as universally spiritual, ecologically wise, and uniformly traditional. The actual ethnographic and contemporary record is more complex. Many Indigenous communities have urban, professional, multi-religious, and modern technological lives that do not fit the romantic frame. Honoring sovereignty means respecting communities as they actually are, not as outsiders imagine them.

**Failure mode 3 — Hallucinating Native-scholar attributions.** The tool sometimes attributes specific quotations or theoretical positions to Deloria, Smith, Medicine, Brayboy, or other Native scholars that those scholars did not actually publish. The risk is particularly high for less-Western scholarship that the LLM training data may underrepresent. Always require citations to specific publications and flag uncertain attributions with `[verify]`.

The tool joins your toolkit. Nineteen of twenty.

## 6. Integration — Reading three contemporary cases

The chapter's apparatus on three contemporary cases.

**The 2024 Klamath River dam removals.** In August–November 2024, the four lower Klamath River dams (Iron Gate, Copco 1, Copco 2, J.C. Boyle) were removed in the largest dam-removal project in U.S. history. The dams had blocked salmon migration on the river since the early 20th century, contributing to the catastrophic decline of Klamath River salmon runs. The removal was the result of a 20+ year advocacy campaign led by the Yurok, Karuk, Hoopa Valley, and Klamath tribes — anchored in the treaty-fishing-rights frameworks the Boldt decision had established and in continuing tribal sovereignty claims. The chapter's apparatus reads the removal as a sovereignty victory of historical scale — a settler-colonial infrastructure project undone, after a century of operation, because the tribes had the legal and political standing to undo it. Salmon are returning. The first salmon ascended the lower river in October 2024, less than two months after Iron Gate Dam came out.

**The Land Back movement.** Across U.S., Canadian, Australian, and New Zealand contexts, the *Land Back* movement has, since approximately 2018, framed contemporary Indigenous advocacy as a return-of-stolen-land question. The movement is not primarily a litigation movement; it is a political-and-cultural framing that has shifted what Indigenous demands are understood to mean. Specific cases include the 2020 designation of the Bears Ears National Monument with co-management authority for five tribal nations (rescinded by Trump, restored by Biden); the 2021 transfer of the Esselen Tribe's ancestral lands at the Esselen Tribe of Monterey County's eastern boundary; multiple smaller-scale acquisitions and easements. The chapter's apparatus reads the movement as the contemporary articulation of the sovereignty framework Deloria and Smith have been building toward — a framing in which tribal land is not *minority cultural heritage* but *sovereign territory*, returnable in principle.

**The 2026 NAGPRA implementation revisions.** The Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act, passed in 1990, established legal procedures for the return of ancestral remains and cultural objects from federally funded institutions to lineal descendants and culturally affiliated tribes. Implementation has been uneven; many institutions have moved slowly, and a substantial backlog of unresolved cases persists. The 2024 NAGPRA implementing regulations (effective January 2024) significantly tightened the framework — explicitly recognizing tribal expertise as authoritative, requiring tribal consultation before any display or loan of NAGPRA-eligible items, and shortening the timeline for repatriation determinations. As of 2026, the implementation of the new regulations is still being worked out, with both compliance gains and continuing institutional resistance documented. The chapter's apparatus reads the revision as a victory for the decolonizing-methodologies framework — Indigenous expertise recognized as expert, tribal sovereignty over ancestral materials given operational force.

The chapter's apparatus reads all three. The framework holds.

## 8. Summary and connections

Indigenous anthropology is the subdiscipline that takes Indigenous peoples as more than research subjects — as research authors, knowledge holders, sovereignty bearers, and audiences for the work. The chapter has worked through three layers — the contested terminology and demographic situation of Indigenous communities; the Vine Deloria Jr. and Linda Tuhiwai Smith critique of the discipline and the SWORP-type return-of-knowledge work that has answered it in part; the sovereignty apparatus from the Marshall Court trilogy through the Boldt decision and Standing Rock to the 2024 Klamath dam removals.

The Mazama eruption, recorded in Klamath oral history seven millennia before geology confirmed it, anchors what the chapter is for. *Indigenous knowledge has standing. The knowledge has been there all along. The discipline is catching up.*

The chapter's source author, David Lewis (Confederated Tribes of Grand Ronde), runs the *Quartux Journal* — a public-anthropology blog that brings Pacific Northwest tribal history to a broader public, with more than 450 essays and a substantial subscriber base. The blog is one model of what the next century of anthropology might look like: Indigenous scholars writing their own communities, in accessible venues, in dialogue with both academic and tribal audiences. The discipline's future is partly there.

**Connections forward.** Chapter 20 (Anthropology on the Ground) closes the book. The capstone exercise builds on the work the present chapter has just gestured toward — the applied work in clinical practice, public-health policy, conservation, food-system reform, language revitalization, cultural-heritage management, and the everyday business of making the contemporary world more livable. The Indigenous-anthropology framework is not a sub-specialty of cultural anthropology; it is part of where the entire discipline is going. The chapter that follows shows the apparatus in action across cases the discipline now takes for granted as sites of work. The toolkit reaches its twentieth tool and the capstone is asked of you in earnest.
