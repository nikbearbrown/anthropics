# Chapter 7 — Work, Life, and Value: Economic Anthropology

## Suggested titles

- *The Original Affluent Society and the Long Industrial Hangover*
- *How Humans Have Made a Living*

## TL;DR

Economic anthropology studies how humans get the things they need from their environments and circulate those things among each other. This chapter walks through the four modes of subsistence (gathering-hunting, pastoralism, plant cultivation, industrialism), the four forms of exchange (generalized reciprocity, balanced reciprocity, redistribution, market), and the long historical arc by which European industrialism reshaped the global economy through colonial extraction.

## 1. A man, a bird, and a sentence in a language neither one fully speaks

Northern Tanzania, dry season, an hour after dawn. A Hadza man walks through scrubby savanna with a digging stick and a small bag. He stops, listens, and whistles a particular pattern — a kind of two-note phrase, slightly trilling. Almost immediately, an answering call comes from a low tree to his right: a chattering, bird-shaped *I am here, follow me*.

The bird is a *honeyguide* — a small, sparrow-sized, gray-brown species (*Indicator indicator*) whose taxonomic name is itself a description of what it does. The man and the bird have an arrangement neither one signed. The bird flies ahead, calls back from the next perch, leads the man through scrub and across fallen logs to a particular tree. There is a hum coming from a hole in the trunk. Wild bees.

The man builds a small smoky fire at the base of the tree, climbs up, breaks open the comb, eats some of the honey, lowers the rest into his bag, and — here is the part that matters — leaves the wax for the bird. The bird descends, eats. The arrangement holds. Tomorrow there will be another hunt.

The Hadza honey-hunting partnership with the honeyguide is real. It is not a metaphor. It is also not an exotic curiosity from a distant tribe — it is one of the most thoroughly documented examples of *interspecies cooperation* in the anthropological record, and it is part of how the Hadza, a *gathering-hunting* people of about 1,000 individuals, have made a living in this landscape for thousands of years. Roughly 10 to 20 percent of their caloric intake comes from honey. The bird gets wax it could not retrieve on its own. Both species benefit. Neither one *understands* the bargain in the way a contract lawyer would. The bargain holds anyway.

This chapter is about how humans have made a living. The Hadza and their honey is one answer; you will meet several more before the chapter is done. The discipline that studies these answers is **economic anthropology**. It is not the same as economics. Economics tends toward *universalism* — the assumption that economic processes work roughly the same way everywhere, with rational self-interested actors responding to supply and demand. Economic anthropology starts from the four-field commitments of Chapter 1: diversity, holism, environmentalism, cultural relativism. It assumes that *how people make a living shapes nearly everything else about their society*, and the *how* varies dramatically across history and geography.

## 2. Concept 1 — The four modes of subsistence

A **mode of subsistence** is the way a society gets its basic necessities — food, clothing, shelter, medicine — from the environment. The mode includes the techniques (digging stick, plow, harpoon, factory line), the social organization (band, household, village, corporation), and the values that come with the techniques (sharing, ownership, productivity, growth).

Most societies practice one *primary* mode and dabble in others. A predominantly agricultural village may keep some chickens and hunt occasionally. A predominantly industrial society still has some farms. The classification is about the dominant pattern, not exclusivity.

Four modes. In rough order of historical emergence:

### Gathering-hunting

Roughly 95% of human evolutionary history was spent in this mode. The Hadza of northern Tanzania, the Dobe Ju/'hoansi of the Kalahari, the Martu and Pintupi of Australia, the Inuit of the Arctic — all are or have been gathering-hunting peoples. Note the word order: *gathering* before *hunting*. Older textbooks said *hunting and gathering*; the reordering reflects an empirical correction. *Most calories in most gathering-hunting societies come from gathered plant foods, not hunted meat.* Hunting is dramatic and prestigious. Gathering is the sustenance.

The social form that pairs with gathering-hunting is the **band**. A band is a small, flexible group — typically 25 to 50 people, sometimes more — that moves through a territory in seasonal cycles, settling at one site for a few weeks before moving on. Bands are *egalitarian*: no formal leaders, no hierarchies, no inherited status, no large material possessions. Decisions are made through public discussion until consensus emerges. Resources are shared widely, often according to **generalized reciprocity** — when a hunter brings home meat, the meat is divided among all band members; when gatherers return with fruit, the fruit is distributed.

Band-level egalitarianism is enforced socially. *Stinginess* is the worst social fault. People who refuse to share, accumulate too much, or assert authority are met with gossip, ridicule, and — at the extreme — ostracism. The anthropologist Richard Lee's classic ethnographic essay *Eating Christmas in the Kalahari* describes how the Dobe Ju/'hoansi systematically belittled the Christmas ox he bought for them, calling it scrawny and inadequate, before slaughtering it and eating it eagerly. Lee was puzzled until an elder explained: *if a hunter walks back with a big kill, we mock him to keep his head from getting too big*. The mocking is the egalitarianism mechanism. *Stop someone from elevating themselves and the band stays a band.*

Marshall Sahlins, in a famous 1972 essay, called gathering-hunting *the original affluent society*. Sahlins's data: many gathering-hunting groups work three to four hours a day for food, eat a varied diet rich in plant nutrients, show stronger bone density and lower blood pressure than neighboring farmers, and report widespread satisfaction with their lifeway. The contrast was deliberate — Sahlins wrote in a context where industrial capitalist societies took for granted that their own way of life was the highest achievement of human progress. Sahlins's gathering-hunting societies, by metrics of leisure and health, beat industrial societies on several dimensions.

The contemporary picture is messier. Most surviving gathering-hunting groups have been pushed onto marginal lands, harassed by states attempting to settle them, restricted from their traditional ranges, and impoverished by the loss of their resource base. The Hadza in 2007 had 6,500 square kilometers of their traditional land leased by the Tanzanian government to the royal family of the United Arab Emirates as a *personal safari playground*; protests by the Hadza and a coalition of NGOs eventually got the deal rescinded, and in 2011 the Hadza won title to 57,000 hectares — the first time the Tanzanian government had recognized the land rights of gathering-hunting peoples. Many Hadza now alternate between government-built villages (where they receive food assistance) and traditional ranges (where they continue to gather and hunt). They are not frozen in the deep past. They are contemporary people whose lifeway is under continuous pressure.

### Pastoralism

Around 10,000 to 12,000 years ago, in several parts of the world, hunters of wild herd animals began the long process of *animal domestication* — selectively breeding the herds, controlling their movement, taking milk and blood from live animals rather than only meat from dead ones. **Pastoralism** is the mode of subsistence built around domesticated herd animals.

Pastoralists move their herds in seasonal cycles to find fresh pasture and water. The full pattern is *nomadism* — continuous movement, no permanent settlement. The compromise pattern is *transhumance* — movement between two or more permanent settlements, with herders driving the animals between them and other family members staying put.

The Bedouin of Arabia and northern Africa (numbering roughly 3 million) are the most-studied pastoralist case. Bedouin herd camels, sheep, goats, and cattle, with the species mix tracking environment: camels in the Sahara and Arabian deserts; sheep and goats around Jordan, Syria, and Iraq; cattle in southern Arabia and Sudan. Camp size: 3 to 15 tents, 30 to 100 people, often related extended families.

Pastoralists differ from gathering-hunters in one socially decisive way: *they own their herds*. Herds are family property, passed from one generation to the next (typically father to son in patrilineal societies; sometimes more flexibly). The shift from "share what you find" (gathering-hunting) to "own what you raise" (pastoralism) is one of the deepest transitions in human social organization. Wealth becomes possible. Wealth becomes inheritable. Inheritance becomes the foundation of inequality.

The social form that pairs with pastoralism is typically the *tribe* — a confederation of bands or extended-family groups, sometimes with informal leadership (a *big man* or council of elders), with kinship as the primary organizing principle. Pastoralists trade with neighboring farmers for grains and vegetables that animal products alone cannot provide.

### Plant cultivation: horticulture and intensive agriculture

Around 10,000 to 12,000 years ago — at roughly the same time as animal domestication — humans in several regions independently began *plant domestication*. Wild seeds were collected, sorted, and replanted. Over many generations, the plants changed: bigger seeds, sweeter fruits, easier-to-thresh grains. The Fertile Crescent of the Middle East (wheat, barley, lentils, peas), the Yangtze and Yellow River basins of China (rice, millet), Mesoamerica (maize, beans, squash), the Andes (potatoes, quinoa), New Guinea (taro, sugarcane), West Africa (yams, sorghum), and the eastern North American woodlands all show independent invention.

There are two flavors of plant cultivation, distinguished by intensity.

**Extensive horticulture** is small-scale, sustainable cultivation of multiple crops, with plots rotated through cycles of farming and *fallow* (rest). The classic Mesoamerican *three sisters* — corn, beans, and squash planted together in flat-topped mounds — is a textbook case of *intercropping*: each plant supports the others (corn provides stalks for beans to climb; beans fix nitrogen for corn and squash; squash blocks weeds and shades the soil). Horticultural societies use digging sticks and hoes. They rotate plots when nutrients deplete. *Slash-and-burn* agriculture is a specific extensive-horticulture technique used in tropical regions where soil nutrients are otherwise low — the existing vegetation is cut and burned, the ash tilled in as fertilizer, the plot used for several years, then left fallow for as long as 20 years.

The social form that pairs with horticulture is typically a *village* — a settled community of perhaps a few hundred people, organized around extended-family households, with land typically held in trust by a family head or village leader rather than as private property. The *usufruct* system gives a household the right to use a piece of land but not to own or sell it. Surplus is modest; specialists (potters, smiths, healers) typically still farm part-time.

**Intensive agriculture** is the high-input cousin: deeper plowing, irrigation, terracing, animal traction (oxen, water buffalo), and continuous use of the same land year after year. Intensive agriculture supports much larger populations than horticulture but requires far more labor per acre. It also produces *surplus* — more food than the cultivators themselves need — which can support people who do not farm: craftspeople, religious specialists, full-time soldiers, government officials.

The social form that pairs with intensive agriculture is the *chiefdom* or *state*. With surplus comes inequality, with inequality comes hierarchy, with hierarchy comes formal authority. Chapter 8 (political anthropology) walks through the resulting political forms in detail. For now, the operative claim: *intensive agriculture made cities possible*. The first cities (Uruk, Mohenjo-daro, Teotihuacán) were all built on intensive agricultural surpluses.

### Industrialism

Beginning in eighteenth-century Britain, a fourth mode of subsistence emerged: **industrialism** — the use of wage labor, machines, and chemical processes to mass-produce commodities. The shift was rapid and consequential.

In the early 1700s, British peasants were producing wool from small-scale sheep farming. The wool was exported, mostly to the Netherlands, where it was processed into cloth. British manufacturers — seeing where the profit margins were — wanted to do the processing themselves. They needed three things: more wool, more workers, and more capital. They got all three by violence.

*More wool*: large landholders evicted small-scale peasants from common lands (*enclosure*) and ran their own sheep on the cleared land. Peasants were displaced into cities. *More workers*: those displaced peasants, with no land to farm, were the labor force that filled the new factories. *More capital*: invested in steam engines, water mills, mechanical looms, and the *clock-regulated workday* — a managerial innovation that broke labor into measurable units. The combination was the *Industrial Revolution*. Within a century, England had transformed from a predominantly agricultural society to a predominantly industrial one.

Industrialism produces commodities at scales the older modes could not approach. It also produces conditions of work that the older modes did not impose: shop-floor discipline, repetitive labor, time-monitoring, the separation of *work* from *life* into discrete blocks of the day. The phrase *time is money* is industrial in origin. Time was not money in a Hadza band or a Bedouin camp.

The social form that pairs with industrialism is the modern *state* with extensive bureaucratic infrastructure, formal property law, wage-labor contracts, and the apparatus of mass production and mass consumption.

A note on industrialism's costs. Industrialism produced enormous wealth. It also produced enormous concentrated misery. The 14-hour factory days of nineteenth-century Manchester. The displacement of agricultural communities. The slums of industrial cities. The transatlantic slave trade — an essential supply chain for cotton, sugar, and other raw materials — operated at industrial scale through most of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. Whether industrialism was *progress* depends on which seat in the system you occupied.

## 3. Concept 2 — Forms of exchange: how things move through societies

Production gets goods made. *Exchange* moves the goods around. The two are intertwined — different production systems tend to use different exchange forms — but they are analytically separable. Anthropologists distinguish four main forms of exchange.

### Generalized reciprocity

*Give without expecting an immediate, calculated return.* Generalized reciprocity is the dominant form of exchange in gathering-hunting societies and within close kin groups in any society. When a hunter brings home meat in a Hadza band, he does not negotiate prices with each person who eats from the kill. The meat is shared. When you live with your parents and you take a soft drink from the fridge, you do not pay them for it. When a friend asks to borrow boots, you do not charge a rental fee. The expectation of reciprocity is *eventually*, *over the long run*, *somehow* — but not for any specific transaction.

Generalized reciprocity *looks* like altruism. In the long run it is not. If too many band members consistently take without giving, the imbalance accumulates and is corrected through gossip, criticism, and ostracism. The mechanism is delayed enforcement. The local norm — *share, or be talked about* — is enough.

Generalized reciprocity scales poorly. It works in small face-to-face groups where everyone tracks who has given what to whom. In a city of millions of strangers, no one can keep that ledger.

### Balanced reciprocity

*Give a roughly equivalent gift back, but on your own schedule and without explicit accounting.* The Dobe Ju/'hoansi of the Kalahari practice *hxaro* — a form of long-term gift exchange between trading partners. One person asks another for a particular item (a digging stick, a bowl, a piece of jewelry); after some unspecified time, the receiver gives back something of roughly equivalent value. The cycle continues, sometimes for decades, occasionally for life. The point is not the economic value of the items exchanged. The point is the *relationship* the exchange creates and maintains. *Hxaro* partners can hunt and gather in each other's territories; their families intermarry; they support each other in disputes. Many Dobe Ju/'hoansi maintain ten to twelve such partnerships at any given time.

Balanced reciprocity also organizes the famous Trobriand *kula ring* (Chapter 1), where shell necklaces and bracelets traveled in opposite directions around an arc of islands, conferring status on those who handled them and underwriting trade in less prestigious goods alongside.

A more aggressive form: the *potlatch* of the Pacific Northwest Coast peoples (Haida, Kwakiutl, Tlingit). At a potlatch, the host chief presented massive gifts to the guest chief — blankets, copper plaques, food, animal skins — sometimes destroying valuable items publicly to demonstrate that he could afford the loss. The guest chief was expected to host a counter-potlatch later, presenting *more*. Status was maintained not by accumulating wealth but by *giving it away*. (Scholars have also argued that potlatches functioned as redistribution networks across an ecologically diverse region — getting goods from where they were plentiful to where they were not.) The U.S. and Canadian governments banned potlatches from the late nineteenth century to the mid-twentieth, on the grounds that the practice was *uncivilized*. The bans were one piece of a broader colonial assault on Northwest Coast peoples. The bans were lifted in the 1950s. Potlatches resumed.

### Redistribution

*Goods flow up to a central point, then back down.* Redistribution is the dominant form of exchange in chiefdoms and states. In precolonial Hawaii, commoners paid tribute to local chiefs in the form of food, labor, and craft goods. Local chiefs passed a portion up to regional chiefs, who passed up to the *ali'i nui* (supreme chief). The chief at each level used the accumulated tribute to support priests, warriors, advisors, and bureaucrats; to fund monumental construction (temples, irrigation works); and to redistribute resources back to commoners during shortfalls. The flow was two-way — up as tribute, down as goods, services, and protection.

Modern states use the same exchange form. *Taxation* is redistribution. The state collects from citizens (and businesses), pools the revenue, and redistributes it as roads, schools, courts, police, military, social insurance, scientific research, public health. Whether you regard this as legitimate (a system for funding shared goods) or as predation (an extraction by elites) is partly an ideological question. Anthropologically: it is redistribution. The state apparatus does not, on average, hoard the tribute; it spends it on services that get back to citizens.

### Market exchange

*Goods move between strangers at prices set by supply and demand.* Markets are the dominant form of exchange in industrial and post-industrial societies, and increasingly the dominant form everywhere else.

A market is, at its most concrete, a place — a Ghanaian *makola* market with shrewd women traders, neat stacks of colorful goods, the smell of fried plantain, the haggling. A market in this sense is a sensory-social experience as much as an economic transaction. Buyers and sellers engage in conversation, negotiate over identity and price, repeat transactions over time, and build relationships.

A market is also, increasingly, an abstraction — Western-style grocery stores in Accra where shoppers silently push carts past fixed-price goods; supermarket self-checkout in the U.S. where the human contact has been engineered out; e-commerce sites where the *market* is a database. The trajectory across the past century has been from market-as-place to market-as-information-system. Each shift removes social texture and substitutes computational efficiency. *The trade-off is not free.*

### Money — general purpose and special purpose

A market system requires money. Money is, at its core, a *medium of exchange* (a thing you can trade for other things), a *unit of account* (a way to measure value), and a *store of value* (a way to keep wealth across time). Anthropologists distinguish two types.

**General-purpose money** can be exchanged for nearly anything: U.S. dollars, Indian rupees, Japanese yen. General-purpose money is what most contemporary readers think of when they hear *money*.

**Special-purpose money** can be exchanged only for specific items or in specific contexts. Pre-colonial West African *cowrie shells* were special-purpose money used for some kinds of transaction (bridewealth, fines, particular markets) but not others. The Yap stone money (giant limestone discs of the Pacific island of Yap) is special-purpose: it can be used for major social transactions (marriages, alliances) but not to buy a fish. *Hxaro* gifts are special-purpose money in the same sense — they discharge specific kinds of social obligation, not general consumption needs.

The shift from special-purpose to general-purpose money is one of the major economic transitions of the past few centuries. General-purpose money makes everything *commensurable* — anything can be priced against anything else. That is enormously efficient. It also flattens distinctions that some societies had previously found important — the distinction between a gift and a payment, between something sacred and something for sale, between human relationships and commodities. Some scholars argue this flattening has costs.

## 4. Concept 3 — Industrialism, colonialism, and the global system

### How British industrialism became a global problem

The Industrial Revolution did not stay in Britain. By the mid-1800s, factories were spreading across western Europe and the United States. The factories needed raw materials at scales that domestic supply could not provide.

The solution was *colonialism* — the political domination of another country in order to extract raw materials and labor. Britain, France, the Netherlands, Belgium, Germany, Spain, and Portugal all built colonial empires across the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries; the United States built one of its own in the late nineteenth and twentieth. The colonial machine had a specific economic logic.

Colonies supplied raw materials. India, Egypt, and the U.S. South supplied cotton for British mills. The Caribbean and Brazil supplied sugar. Indonesia supplied spices and tea. The Belgian Congo supplied rubber and ivory. West Africa supplied cocoa, palm oil, and groundnuts. The colonies were configured *as suppliers, not as competitors*. Colonial governments deliberately suppressed local industrial development. African manufacturing was actively curtailed during the colonial period to protect British and French manufacturing.

Colonies also supplied labor. Sometimes through forced labor on plantations and in mines. From the 1500s through the early 1800s, the *transatlantic slave trade* moved an estimated 12 million people from West Africa to the Americas, primarily to grow sugar and cotton — the raw materials of European industry. Slavery was abolished by Britain in 1807 and by the U.S. in 1865, but the colonial labor system continued through forced labor (the Belgian Congo's rubber harvesting), indentured labor (Indians moved to Caribbean and Pacific colonies), and tax-driven labor (colonial governments imposed taxes payable only in colonial currency, forcing villagers into wage labor on European plantations).

The system reorganized the global economy permanently. Most colonies became politically independent in the mid-twentieth century — India in 1947, much of Africa in the late 1950s and early 1960s. Economic independence has been much harder to achieve. *Postcolonial* economies remain shaped by their colonial roles. African economies are still dominated by raw material exports — cocoa from Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana, oil from Nigeria and Angola, copper from Zambia and the DRC. The export prices fluctuate; the postcolonial governments find it hard to budget; the value of raw material exports erodes over time relative to manufactured imports; real economic development remains difficult.

Some postcolonial countries have responded with industrialization programs. India built a substantial industrial base in the second half of the twentieth century. China's industrial expansion since the 1980s has been the largest such project in history. Smaller economies — Ghana, Vietnam, Bangladesh — have pursued sector-specific industrialization (textiles, electronics assembly). The success has been uneven; the obstacles, partly historical and partly structural, are real.

### Modernity and its costs

The sociocultural complex that emerged in the wake of industrialism is what anthropologists call **modernity**. Modernity bundles a number of features:

- *Wage labor* as the dominant form of work, replacing household-based production.
- *Time discipline* — work organized by the clock, not by the season.
- *Mass production* of standardized goods.
- *Mass consumption* — markets generated for the goods through advertising, fashion cycles, and credit.
- *The nuclear family* — smaller household units replacing extended-family networks.
- *Bureaucratic state administration* — formal property law, contracts, taxation, education systems.
- *Secularization* — religious authority replaced or complemented by scientific and bureaucratic authority.

There is no single modernity. Anthropologists who have studied non-Western industrialization speak of *alternative modernities* — versions of the modern complex that retain or modify some elements while industrializing others. Japanese modernity has different features than American modernity. Saudi Arabian modernity differs from both. The notion that *modernity is just one thing, and everyone is moving toward it* is itself a form of universalism that economic anthropology resists.

A specific feature of late modernity that has received growing attention: **precarity**. Precarity is the condition of working without secure or stable income — gig work, contract labor, zero-hours contracts, the freelance economy, the *uberification* of labor. The anthropologist Anne Allison's 2013 *Precarious Japan* documented how postwar Japanese stable employment patterns (*shūshin koyō*, lifetime employment) have given way to widespread precarious labor among younger Japanese workers, with profound effects on marriage rates, family formation, mental health, and social trust. Similar patterns have appeared across postindustrial economies. Precarity is not a side effect; it is increasingly a structural feature.

A related concept: **time-space compression**. The geographer David Harvey coined the term in 1989 to describe the postmodern feeling that distance has shrunk and time has accelerated. Communications happen in milliseconds. Capital flows across borders faster than goods used to flow within a city. A factory in Bangladesh produces shirts that are on shelves in Atlanta within a week. The compression is real. It has cultural consequences — the dissolution of place-based identities, the rise of always-on attention, the global synchronization of fashion and news cycles.

### Commodity fetishism

A final concept from the Marxist tradition that has been productively reworked by economic anthropologists: **commodity fetishism**. Karl Marx used the term to describe how, under capitalism, commodities appear to have value as if it were a property *of the object* — when in fact the value is the product of the human labor that went into the object. The labor is hidden by the commodity form. The shirt at the store seems just to *be* worth $20; the labor of the Bangladeshi garment worker who sewed it for less than a dollar is not visible in the price tag.

Commodity fetishism is a useful diagnostic for contemporary consumer culture. *Brand* is its most visible form: an iPhone, a Nike sneaker, a Tesla — these are objects whose price is largely the price of association with a particular meaning system. The associations are real; the labor that went into the objects is also real; the relation between the two is what the fetishism conceals. Anthropologists who study contemporary advertising, branding, and luxury markets are largely studying commodity fetishism in detail.

## 5. Integration — A holistic look at one contemporary economy

Pull the chapter together by tracing how *all* the concepts apply to a single case. Take Ghana — Hasty's primary fieldwork site and an economy where the chapter's analytical machinery is on direct display.

Ghana, like most West African countries, has a *predominantly agricultural* mode of subsistence. Smallholder farmers — about half the working population — grow cassava, yam, plantain, maize, cocoa, and oil palm. Most agriculture is *extensive* rather than *intensive*: smallholdings, manual labor, limited mechanization, fallow rotation. The rural social form is the village, organized around extended families with land typically held in trust by family heads or chiefs.

Layered on top of the agricultural base is a *colonial cash-crop economy* — cocoa, the dominant export, was introduced under British colonial rule in the late nineteenth century. Today Ghana is the world's second-largest cocoa producer (after Côte d'Ivoire); the cocoa is sold to multinational chocolate companies whose products do not return to Ghana. The cocoa-export structure is a textbook colonial-economy case: raw material out, manufactured goods (and high-value-add) in.

Layered on top of the cash-crop economy is an *urban industrial sector* — Ghana's industrialization is partial. The Tema industrial zone outside Accra hosts assembly, processing, and small manufacturing. Recent government programs (*One District, One Factory*) aim to expand industrial capacity. Industrial labor, where it exists, runs on the wage-labor / time-discipline / shop-floor pattern recognizable from any industrial economy.

Layered on top of *that* is a *vibrant market sector* — the famous markets of Accra (Makola, Kaneshie) and other cities, where women traders dominate retail, transactions are negotiated, prices are flexible, and social relationships shape commerce. This market is a contemporary version of the West African market system that long predates industrialism.

And layered on top of *all of it* is a *postcolonial state* that runs *redistribution* through taxation — funding schools, roads, healthcare, the courts. The state has limits (revenue collection is leaky, infrastructure is uneven, corruption is real) but it does the redistributive work that no other institution can.

A Ghanaian household's economic life draws on multiple modes simultaneously. A family might farm cassava (subsistence + small market sales), receive remittances from a relative working in industrial-sector electronics in Tema, send a child to a state-funded school, buy soap at a Makola stall (market exchange), receive food during a funeral from extended-family members (generalized reciprocity within kin), and contribute *kola* to a chief's installation (balanced reciprocity at the community level). All four modes of subsistence and all four forms of exchange are present in a single household over a single month. *That is what economic anthropology means when it says modes of subsistence and forms of exchange are practiced in flexible combinations.* The textbook taxonomy is a tool for sorting; the actual lived economy is layered.

This is also what *holism* (Chapter 1) looks like in practice. Pull on the cocoa export thread — say, a 30% drop in global cocoa prices — and the effects ripple through the village (less income), the kinship system (delayed marriages, deferred school fees), the urban remittance flow (more pressure on the family member in Tema), the political system (state revenue drops, austerity follows), and the urban market (less consumer spending, more competition among traders). The economy is not separate from kinship is not separate from politics is not separate from religion. They are layers of the same system.

## 7. Chapter summary — what you can now do

You can name and describe the four modes of subsistence, identify the social form each tends to produce, and recognize layered combinations in real societies.

You can identify the four forms of exchange and recognize them in everyday transactions, including the ones — generalized reciprocity within families, balanced reciprocity at celebrations, redistribution through taxation, market exchange in stores — operating in your own life.

You can distinguish *general-purpose* from *special-purpose* money, and you can articulate what is gained and lost when special-purpose forms are replaced by general-purpose forms.

You can place industrialism and colonialism as historically linked rather than as separate phenomena. The factories of Manchester and the cotton plantations of Mississippi are nodes in the same global system. So are the Tema industrial zone and the cocoa farms of rural Ghana. *Modernity is not just one thing.*

You can use *precarity*, *time-space compression*, and *commodity fetishism* as analytical tools to make sense of contemporary economic conditions in any society.

The single idea that matters most: *the way a society makes a living shapes nearly everything else about it.* Pull the subsistence thread and the cloth moves. This is what holism looks like in economic anthropology.

The common mistake to watch for: *treating economic processes as natural and universal*. The economy you live in is the product of specific historical decisions. Industrial labor discipline was invented; colonial extraction was institutionalized; market exchange was made dominant where other forms previously prevailed. None of it had to be this way; some of it could still be otherwise.

If you can take any society — your own, your grandparents', a neighboring country's, a historical case — and trace its mode(s) of subsistence, exchange forms, money systems, and modernity layer, you have absorbed the chapter's analytical apparatus.

## What would change my mind

If a major body of new historical or cross-cultural evidence — say, archaeological findings showing that certain "egalitarian" gathering-hunting bands were in fact more hierarchical than recent ethnographers have claimed, or comparative-economic data showing that the colonial-extraction story under-reads the agency of postcolonial economies — overturned the basic claims of this chapter, the synthesis would need significant revision. The chapter holds the cautious version of each major claim because the cautious version is what the discipline currently endorses.

## Still puzzling

The relationship between *precarity* and the *original affluent society*. If Sahlins was right that gathering-hunters worked few hours and lived comfortably, and the contemporary gig worker is precariously underemployed, what does that say about the long arc of human economic life? Industrialism created massive wealth concentration and massive precarity at the same time. Whether the trajectory is *progress* depends on how we count, and the count is contested. I do not have a clean view of which way the contemporary balance tips.
