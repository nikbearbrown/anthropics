## TL;DR

The genus *Homo* contains at least eight species, one of which still survives. This chapter walks through what made *Homo* recognizable as a separate genus from *Australopithecus* (encephalization, tool use, reduced sexual dimorphism), how the tool record and behavioral traces tracked our ancestors' minds, and how the archaic *Homo* record — Neanderthals, Denisovans, *H. naledi*, the *Homo sapiens* migrations out of Africa — produces a far stranger story than the textbook ladder version it replaced.

## 1. The Old Man of Shanidar

A cave in northern Iraq. Sometime around 45,000 years ago, an older man dies. He is somewhere between 30 and 45 years old — *old* by the standards of his community, which is why the people who excavate the cave 50,000 years later will give him the nickname *the Old Man*. He is a Neanderthal.

Read his bones and a story comes out. He has been blind in one eye since some past trauma. The lower part of his right arm is missing — amputated, the wound healed clean enough that he survived the procedure and lived for some time afterward. The amputation may have left him paralyzed on the right side. He has no teeth, but the teeth he had show wear patterns suggesting his food was being prepared for him by other people — chewed first, then given to him. He cannot have hunted. He cannot have processed his own food. He cannot have defended himself.

He lived for years in this condition. The community kept him alive.

This skeleton — *Shanidar 1*, excavated by Ralph Solecki in the late 1950s — is sitting at the heart of a hundred-year argument about what kind of creature the Neanderthals were. The popular image is the brute: stooped, club-wielding, dragging dinner. The image is not science. It is nineteenth-century cartoon, repeated long enough to feel like fact. Shanidar 1 is the science. So is Shanidar 4, where pollen analysis suggests flowers may have been placed in the grave (though the pollen interpretation is contested). So is the recent finding of Spanish cave paintings from 65,000 years ago, before *Homo sapiens* even arrived in Europe — paintings made by Neanderthal hands.

The Neanderthals were not us. They were not below us either. They were a separate species of the genus *Homo*, with their own bodies, their own tools, their own burials, their own paintings, their own way of being human-adjacent. Some of them interbred with our ancestors. Some of their genes — between 1% and 4% of most non-African genomes — are inside us right now.

This chapter is the story of the genus *Homo*: eight or nine species over roughly two million years, of which we are the sole survivor. It picks up where Chapter 4 left off, with the early hominins — the australopithecines, the *Paranthropus* line, *Ardipithecus* — and walks forward through the tool industries, the brain-expansion record, the migrations, and the strange recent finds (*H. naledi* with its small brain and possibly intentional burials, *H. floresiensis* the three-foot-seven *Hobbit*, *H. longi* the *Dragon Man* whose phylogenetic placement is still being argued).

## 2. Concept 1 — Defining the genus *Homo*

### The boundary problem

In Chapter 4 you met *Australopithecus afarensis* — Lucy's species, walking upright, brain about 400 cc, still capable of tree-climbing. Around two million years ago, in East Africa, something that looked similar to *afarensis* but with a slightly bigger brain and the capacity to make stone tools entered the fossil record. Paleoanthropologists named the new genus *Homo*. The naming was a claim: *this is something different.* The defending of the claim has occupied the field ever since.

What makes a fossil hominin a *Homo* rather than an *Australopithecus*? In a 1964 paper, Mary Leakey, Louis Leakey, and John Napier — working from the fossils of *Homo habilis* they had excavated at Olduvai Gorge in Tanzania — proposed a working definition. *Homo* should mean: brain size over 600 cc; a more rounded, globular skull; evidence of tool use; reduced *prognathism* (the projection of the jaw); smaller teeth and jaws; humanlike postcranial features; feet fully adapted for striding bipedal walking.

The 1964 list was useful. It was also incomplete. Many specimens since have shown some criteria but not others. *Homo naledi*, found in 2013 in South Africa and dated to about 235,000–335,000 years ago, has a brain of just 560 cc — *smaller* than the *Homo* threshold. Yet other features place it in *Homo*. *Homo floresiensis*, the Indonesian *Hobbit*, has a 380 cc brain — barely bigger than a chimpanzee's — yet made tools, used fire, and hunted. The genus is, in practice, defined by a *cluster* of features, no one of which is absolutely necessary.

### Encephalization

The best single quantitative measure of the *Homo* trend is **encephalization** — the progressive increase in brain size relative to body size over evolutionary time. Brain size by itself is not informative; an elephant has a much bigger brain than a human, but the elephant also has a much bigger body. What matters is the ratio: how big is this brain compared to what we'd expect for an organism of this body size?

The **encephalization quotient (EQ)**, formalized by Harry Jerison in 1973, is one way to make this measurement. EQ compares an organism's actual brain mass to the brain mass predicted from its body mass according to a regression formula derived across many species. An EQ of 1 means the brain is exactly what would be expected. An EQ of 2 means twice as much brain as expected.

Modern humans (*Homo sapiens*) have an EQ of about 6.0–7.0 — roughly six times the brain mass we'd expect for our body size. *Homo erectus* has an EQ of about 4.0. The australopithecines: 2.5–3.0. The *Homo* trend is the rising EQ.

EQ is not without critics. Some recent work argues that body size should not be in the formula at all — that what matters for cognitive capacity is the absolute number of cortical neurons, not the brain-to-body ratio. The argument matters because it bears on cases like *H. naledi* and *H. floresiensis*, whose small absolute brains seem to have done sophisticated cognitive work. The *expensive tissue hypothesis* — proposed by Leslie Aiello and Peter Wheeler in 1995 — argues that brain tissue is so metabolically costly that any species supporting a big brain must compensate elsewhere, often by shrinking the digestive system and switching to higher-quality (typically meat-rich) food. The *Homo* lineage shows exactly that pattern: bigger brain, smaller gut, more meat.

### *Homo habilis* — the type specimen

The earliest widely recognized *Homo* species is **Homo habilis**, dated 2.0–1.7 million years ago. The type specimen — *OH 7*, *Olduvai Hominid #7* — was found in 1960 at Olduvai Gorge by Jonathan and Mary Leakey. It is a partial juvenile skull, hand bones, and foot bones. The brain is around 660–700 cc — bigger than any *Australopithecus*, smaller than any later *Homo*. The hands show structural changes that increase grip dexterity, including a repositioned thumb. The teeth are smaller than any australopithecine's.

The species name — *habilis*, *handy* — is a bet. Louis Leakey and his collaborators thought *H. habilis* was the toolmaker. *Homo habilis* coexisted with the robust australopithecines (*Paranthropus boisei*) at Olduvai. Whether the Oldowan tools found at the site were made by *H. habilis*, by *Paranthropus*, or by both, is unsettled. (We now know that at least one australopithecine — *A. garhi*, around 2.5 million years ago — used stone tools before *H. habilis* even existed.)

There is also a sister species, *Homo rudolfensis*, found in 1972 along Lake Turkana in Kenya by a local Kenyan researcher, Bernard Ngeneo (the discovery was later described by Richard Leakey). *H. rudolfensis* has a larger cranium than *H. habilis* but otherwise overlaps. Some paleoanthropologists treat them as one species with sexual dimorphism. Others treat them as two species. The dispute is the kind of thing the species-definitions discussion in Chapter 4 prepared you for: when you can't test interbreeding, the call about whether morphological variation is *intraspecific* (within a species) or *interspecific* (between species) becomes a judgment call about how derived a trait has to be to mark a species boundary.

The dispute also has a name: *the lumping vs. splitting debate*. Lumpers tend to combine specimens into fewer, more variable species. Splitters tend to recognize many species, each with tighter morphological criteria. Both impulses have a long history in the field; both are operating right now in the active research on *Homo*. Neither side is *just wrong*. The data permit either reading on most hard cases.

## 3. Concept 2 — Tools as cognitive evidence

### What stone tools tell you

A well-made stone tool is the closest thing paleoanthropology gets to a fingerprint of the mind that produced it. The tool's shape, the technique that produced the shape, and the way the tool was used together encode a set of cognitive capabilities — fine motor control, mental templates, sequence planning, social transmission of skill — that would otherwise leave no trace in the fossil record.

Three tool industries dominate the *Homo* record. Each maps loosely (with overlap and contest) onto a phase of human cognitive evolution.

**Oldowan**, 2.5–1.5 million years ago. The earliest known stone tool industry. Named for the Olduvai Gorge in Tanzania. Oldowan tools are made by *hard hammer percussion* — striking one stone with another to detach sharp flakes. The flakes themselves are tools (cutting edges); the cores left behind can also be tools. Oldowan tools are *crude* in the sense that they do not show evidence of careful shaping — they are functional, not designed. They were probably made by *Homo habilis* and possibly also by *Paranthropus* and *Australopithecus garhi*. They appear with cut-marked animal bones, which means the tool-makers were processing meat — though whether by hunting or by scavenging is unsettled.

**Acheulean**, 1.6 million to 200,000 years ago. Associated with *Homo ergaster* and *Homo erectus*. Acheulean tools — most famously the *bifacial hand axe* — are *symmetrical*, worked on both faces, requiring a *mental template* held in mind across many percussion strikes to keep the symmetry. They were made using *soft hammer percussion*: striking the stone with a softer material like bone, antler, or wood, which detaches smaller, more controlled flakes. The cleaver, the side scraper, the hand axe — these tools fit the hand, served different functions (cutting, scraping, hunting, woodworking), and remained essentially stable for over a million years across most of Africa, Europe, and the Middle East.

**Mousterian**, roughly 300,000 to 30,000 years ago. Associated with the Neanderthals. Mousterian tools are *flake-based* — the toolmakers prepared a core in such a way that flakes struck off the core had a predetermined shape. This is the *Levallois technique*, and it requires planning multiple steps ahead: shape the core so the flake will come off the right way, then strike the flake. Mousterian assemblages are dominated by smaller, sharper, more specialized tools than the Acheulean.

These three industries are not just *getting fancier*. Each represents a more sophisticated form of cognitive control. Oldowan: chip flakes off rock, use them. Acheulean: hold a symmetrical shape in mind, work the rock toward it. Mousterian: plan a multi-step sequence, prepare the medium, then extract the result. The cognitive load increases at each step.

### *Homo ergaster*, *Homo erectus*, and the Nariokotome Boy

By around 1.6 million years ago, Africa is home to a hominin that looks substantially more like us than anything before. **Homo ergaster** has long legs, a body roughly the proportions of a modern human, and reduced sexual dimorphism — males about 20% larger than females (compared to 50% in australopithecines). Reduced sexual dimorphism, in mammals broadly, correlates with monogamy and higher male parental investment. The shift in *H. ergaster* is suggestive but not conclusive.

The most complete *H. ergaster* specimen is the **Nariokotome Boy** (KNM-WT 15000), found in 1984 by the Kenyan paleontologist Kamoya Kimeu near Lake Turkana. The Boy is dated to about 1.6 million years ago. He is roughly 12 years old at death, 5'4" tall, with an estimated adult height of 5'10" and a cranial capacity of 900 cc. *He looks remarkably modern.* If a modern reconstruction of his face stood next to a modern human in a crowd, you might double-take.

**Homo erectus** is *H. ergaster*'s near-twin. The two are sometimes treated as one species (with *ergaster* as the African form, *erectus* as the form that migrated out of Africa to Asia and Europe), sometimes as separate species. *Homo erectus* — first found in Indonesia in 1891 by Eugène Dubois at Trinil — is the longest-surviving species in the genus *Homo*, with the species lasting from roughly 2 million to perhaps as recent as 50,000 years ago. *H. erectus* is the species that migrated *out of Africa*, the first hominin to do so on a large scale.

Three behavioral milestones cluster around *H. ergaster / H. erectus*.

**Fire.** Evidence for controlled use of fire appears in the *H. erectus* record by 1.7–2.0 million years ago at Zhoukoudian in China and elsewhere. Hearths, charcoal, and charred animal bones suggest cooking, which alters the equation of nutrition: cooked meat is more easily digested, freeing metabolic budget for the brain.

**Endurance running.** *H. ergaster*'s long legs and other anatomical features suggest the genus had become capable of *persistence hunting* — the ability to chase prey over distances long enough to exhaust the prey, which collapses from heat stress while the human keeps walking. (Bramble and Lieberman, 2004, made the case that endurance running is an evolutionary adaptation specific to *Homo*.)

**Smaller teeth and jaws.** *H. erectus* shows continued reduction in tooth and jaw size — consistent with cooked, softer food, which requires less chewing.

The composite picture: by 1.6 million years ago, the genus *Homo* has long legs for endurance running, controlled fire for cooking, smaller jaws to match a softer diet, and a brain that has roughly doubled from australopithecine sizes. The cognitive capacities suggested by Acheulean tools fit this body. *H. erectus* spreads across Africa, Asia, and parts of Europe, persists for nearly two million years, and only finally disappears in Indonesia perhaps as recently as 50,000 years ago.

### The expensive tissue hypothesis, in one sentence

Brains are metabolically expensive. Big-brained hominins must have offset the cost. The *expensive tissue hypothesis* says: by switching to a higher-quality diet (cooked meat especially), the *Homo* lineage could shrink the digestive system, freeing the metabolic budget for brain expansion. Tools made the meat available. Fire made the meat efficient to digest. Brains, tools, fire, and food converged.

## 4. Concept 3 — Archaic *Homo*, modern humans, and the strange recent record

### *Homo heidelbergensis* — the bridge

By around 800,000 years ago, the *Homo* lineage has produced an *archaic Homo* with a brain in the 1,200–1,400 cc range — overlapping the range of modern humans. **Homo heidelbergensis** is the umbrella name for many of these specimens, found across Africa, Europe, and the Middle East. Specimens include the *Mauer mandible* (Germany, 600,000 years ago), the *Petralona cranium* (Greece, between 200,000 and 350,000 years ago), and *Bodo* (Ethiopia, ~600,000 years ago).

*Homo heidelbergensis* used Acheulean hand axes, built modified dwellings (a 700,000-year-old stone-foundation structure in the Czech Republic), controlled fire continuously (a 780,000-year-old continuous hearth site in Israel), and may have invented *hafting* — the attachment of stone points to wooden handles, which made spears possible. Spears found at Schöningen in Germany, dated to roughly 400,000 years ago, are the earliest preserved hafted hunting weapons. *H. heidelbergensis* is also generally considered the common ancestor of both Neanderthals and modern humans.

### Neanderthals — *Homo neanderthalensis*

Found in Europe, the Middle East, and parts of Central Asia. Dated to between roughly 400,000 and 40,000 years ago. Brain larger than modern humans on average — 1,520 cc to our 1,200–1,400 cc — though brain *organization* may have differed (the frontal lobe relatively smaller, the back of the brain relatively larger, suggestive of different cognitive emphases). Distinctive anatomy: occipital bun, large brow ridges, large nasal cavity, robust postcranial skeleton.

What did Neanderthals do?

**Tools.** They invented and refined the Mousterian tool industry, with its prepared-core flake technique.

**Burials.** At Shanidar in Iraq, at La Chapelle-aux-Saints in France, at Kebara in Israel, and at numerous other sites, Neanderthals deliberately buried their dead — bodies placed in shallow pits, sometimes flexed, occasionally with grave goods. The famous *Shanidar 4* "flower burial" (pollen analysis suggesting flowers placed in the grave) is contested; the rodent-burrow alternative explanation cannot be ruled out. But the *intentional burial* claim, as a general fact about Neanderthals, is now well supported.

**Care of the disabled.** Shanidar 1 — the Old Man of the cold open — could not have survived alone. The Shanidar evidence (see also Shanidar 3, who recovered from a rib injury in apparent care of others) suggests that compassion and social responsibility for disabled members of the community were Neanderthal traits.

**Art.** Cave paintings in Spain, dated to ~65,000 years ago — *before* the arrival of *Homo sapiens* in the region — have been attributed to Neanderthals (Pike et al., 2012). The paintings are simple compared to later *H. sapiens* work at Chauvet and Lascaux. They are paintings nonetheless.

**Speech.** Contested. The hyoid bone of the Neanderthal is anatomically modern, suggesting the ability to anchor the tongue. But the position of the larynx (high in the throat, in some reconstructions) may have limited the range of vowel sounds. The *FOXP2* gene — sometimes called *the language gene*, though that label is misleading — is present in Neanderthal genomes. Whether they had language in the modern sense is unsettled.

**Genes in us.** Most non-African modern humans carry 1–4% Neanderthal DNA, the residue of interbreeding events between roughly 125,000 and 50,000 years ago when *Homo sapiens* migrated out of Africa and met Neanderthal populations already living in Eurasia. Some of those genes affect contemporary disease risk (associations with diabetes, certain immune traits, depression, addiction susceptibility). One study suggested some Neanderthal genes confer protection against severe COVID-19; another suggested some increase risk of severe COVID-19. The discipline is sorting through the contradictions; what is clear is that *Neanderthals are not strictly extinct — pieces of their genomes are still operating inside modern human cells*.

### Denisovans

A different population, mostly known from genetic evidence. The fossil record consists of a finger bone, a few teeth, a partial jaw from Tibet, and a few small fragments — most from Denisova Cave in Siberia, where the genus was first identified in 2010. Genetic analysis shows the Denisovans diverged from the *Homo sapiens / H. neanderthalensis* line around 700,000 years ago, then later interbred with both Neanderthals and modern humans. Denny — *Denisova 11*, a young female from the cave — was a first-generation hybrid: Denisovan father, Neanderthal mother.

Modern Melanesians, Indigenous Australians, and some Tibetan populations carry several percent Denisovan DNA. Some of those genes appear to be functionally important — a Denisovan-derived variant of *EPAS1* helps modern Tibetans cope with high-altitude low-oxygen conditions.

### *Homo naledi* — the small-brained late arrival

In 2013, the South African paleoanthropologist Lee Berger and his team excavated a previously unknown cave system, *Rising Star*, in the Cradle of Humankind region of South Africa. The fossil chamber was reachable only through a 7-inch-wide vertical chute, requiring small-bodied excavators (Berger's team famously recruited via social media for "small, slim, lithe" cavers with paleontology experience). Over 1,500 bones from at least 15 individuals came out.

The species is *Homo naledi*. Brain: 560 cc — australopithecine-sized, far below the *Homo* threshold the 1964 Leakey-Tobias-Napier criteria proposed. Date: 235,000–335,000 years ago — *contemporary with Neanderthals and the earliest modern humans*. The mosaic of features (some australopithecine, some *Homo*) and the recent date together violate the simple progressive-ladder model of human evolution.

Did *H. naledi* deliberately deposit its dead in this hard-to-reach cave? Berger's team has argued yes. Critics have argued the bones may have washed in or been deposited by predators. In 2023 Berger's team published claims that *H. naledi* had also engraved cave walls and used fire. These claims are vigorously contested. The case is genuinely live.

What *H. naledi* does — even apart from the burial question — is undermine the assumption that bigger brain always means more recent. A 560 cc brain coexisted with the 1,400 cc brains of *H. sapiens* and *H. neanderthalensis* on the same continent at the same time. Brain size is a useful diagnostic. It is not a complete one.

### *Homo floresiensis*, *Homo luzonensis*, and island dwarfism

In 2003, on the Indonesian island of Flores, excavators found the remains of a hominin standing 3'7" tall, with a brain of 380 cc — barely larger than a chimpanzee's. *Homo floresiensis*, nicknamed *the Hobbit*, dates to 100,000–60,000 years ago. The species made stone tools, used fire, and hunted on the same island. A second small hominin, *H. luzonensis*, has since been found in the Philippines, dated to at least 50,000–67,000 years ago.

How did such small brains support such complex behavior? One hypothesis: *island dwarfism*, in which mainland-derived populations on islands evolve smaller body sizes (often dramatically) under different selection pressures and limited resources. Both the *Hobbit* and the Luzon hominin were probably descendants of *H. erectus* populations stranded on the islands. The brain shrank with the body. The cognitive capabilities, surprisingly, did not collapse proportionally — which suggests that what matters cognitively is not raw brain size but the organization and density of neurons.

### *Homo sapiens* — Out of Africa

Modern humans — *Homo sapiens* — appear in the African fossil record by at least 300,000 years ago (the *Jebel Irhoud* fossils from Morocco, redated in 2017, are among the oldest claimed). By around 100,000 years ago, *H. sapiens* populations begin migrating out of Africa. By 60,000 years ago, the migration is in full swing. By 40,000 years ago, *H. sapiens* is in Europe and Australia; by 20,000 years ago, in the Americas (the timing of the Americas peopling is itself contested).

The genetic evidence supporting *Out of Africa* comes most directly from mitochondrial DNA. Mitochondria — small structures inside our cells with their own DNA — are inherited maternally and (mostly) without recombination. This means mitochondrial DNA mutations accumulate at a known rate, allowing molecular dating of when populations diverged. **Mitochondrial Eve** (more formally *mtMRCA*, *mitochondrial most recent common ancestor*) is the woman from whom every modern human's mitochondrial DNA descends. She lived in Africa roughly 100,000–200,000 years ago. *Eve* is a misleading name — she was not the only woman alive at the time, just the only one whose mitochondrial line has continuously survived to the present. (Many other women alive then have descendants today, but their mitochondrial lines were lost when they had only sons or had no surviving children.)

Mitochondrial DNA is not the whole story. Nuclear DNA (which includes Neanderthal and Denisovan contributions, see above) tells a more complicated tale of repeated migrations, reverse migrations, interbreeding events, and population contractions. The *Out of Africa* model in its strict form — a single migration, replacing all non-African hominins — has given way to a *mostly Out of Africa with significant interbreeding* picture. Contemporary humans are largely African in ancestry, with regionally varying contributions from Neanderthal, Denisovan, and (probably) other archaic populations.

## 5. Integration — The evolutionary mismatch and human variation today

### Are humans still evolving?

Yes. Evolution does not stop at the threshold of *modern*. It works on us now, has been working on us throughout the Neolithic agricultural transition (roughly 10,000–8,000 years ago), and will continue working on us as long as humans reproduce differentially.

Three contemporary cases.

**Lactose tolerance.** Most mammals, including most humans, lose the ability to digest lactose (the sugar in milk) after weaning. Adult lactose tolerance — the persistence of the lactase enzyme — is the result of recent positive selection in populations with long histories of dairy farming (Northern Europe, parts of East Africa, the Arabian Peninsula). The mutation arose multiple times independently in different populations. Today, roughly 65% of adult humans cannot digest fresh cow's milk; about 35% can. The 35% are over-represented in regions where their ancestors herded cattle for thousands of years.

**Skin pigmentation.** Skin color evolved as an adaptation to ultraviolet radiation — darker pigmentation near the equator (UV protection), lighter pigmentation at higher latitudes (vitamin D synthesis). Recent genetic work (Wilde et al. 2014 and others) shows that lighter skin in European populations is *more recent than was assumed* — significant lightening continued during the Neolithic, well into the period when European populations had adopted agriculture. Some 8,500-year-old European hunter-gatherers (sequenced from DNA in Spanish, Luxembourgian, and Hungarian remains) had skin color much darker than their Neolithic descendants 3,000 years later.

**High-altitude adaptation.** Three high-altitude populations — in the Andes, Tibet, and the Ethiopian highlands — have independently adapted to chronic low-oxygen conditions. Andean populations carry higher hemoglobin concentrations. Tibetans take more breaths per minute (and carry the Denisovan-derived *EPAS1* variant). Ethiopian highlanders thrive at high altitude through mechanisms not yet fully understood. Three populations, three different solutions to the same selection pressure. *Convergent adaptation* in real time.

### Evolutionary mismatch

The body you have is the product of two million years of selection in environments that looked very different from the one you are reading this in. Hunter-gatherer bodies in tropical and temperate Africa, Eurasia, and the Americas evolved for a diet of varied wild plants, occasional meat, frequent activity, irregular access to food, and exposure to a particular set of pathogens.

The agricultural transition starting around 10,000 years ago, and the industrial transition starting around 200 years ago, changed the environment faster than evolution can typically respond. The result is what evolutionary biologists call *evolutionary mismatch* — a population whose biology fits a past environment more than the current one.

The contemporary illnesses most associated with mismatch include type 2 diabetes (a starch- and sugar-rich diet), cardiovascular disease (sedentary work, calorie surplus), obesity (calorie surplus, scarcity-adapted metabolism), some autoimmune disorders (a too-clean environment leaves the immune system without its co-evolved targets — the so-called *hygiene hypothesis*), and dental caries (sugar).

The mismatch hypothesis does not say *we should go back to hunter-gathering*. It says: *some of the diseases of modernity are predictable consequences of running an old genome in a new environment*. This insight is part of medical anthropology (Chapter 17).

### What this chapter has done, and what it has not

The chapter has walked through the genus *Homo* as a coherent biological story: brain expansion, tool industries, fire, language, migration, interbreeding, and the strange surviving cousins (*H. naledi*, *H. floresiensis*, the Denisovans) who push back on the simple ladder. By the end of the chapter you should be able to read a museum label or a journal abstract about a *Homo* fossil and place it: which species, which approximate date, which region, what tool industry, what major behavioral capabilities the bones imply.

What the chapter has *not* done — and what no chapter could honestly do at this length — is settle the open questions. The lumping/splitting debates are live. The *H. naledi* burial question is live. The *H. longi / Dragon Man* phylogenetic placement is live. New specimens, new dating techniques, and new genetic methods (especially ancient DNA from older specimens, currently advancing rapidly) will reshape parts of this story over the next decade.

The discipline is comfortable with this. It is, as Chapter 4 said, the field's working condition. *Strong claim, strong evidence; weak evidence, hedged claim.* The story of human origins is being rewritten in real time, and the willingness to revise as new evidence arrives is what keeps the rewriting honest.

## 7. Chapter summary — what you can now do

You can identify the genus *Homo* and articulate why the boundary with *Australopithecus* is not as clean as the textbook ladder version suggests. *H. naledi* alone breaks the simple version.

You can place the major *Homo* species — *habilis*, *ergaster*, *erectus*, *heidelbergensis*, *neanderthalensis*, *naledi*, *floresiensis*, *sapiens* — by approximate date, region, and key features. You can hold them as a *bushy phylogeny* rather than a linear progression — multiple coexisting branches, only one of which (us) survived.

You can read tool industries (Oldowan → Acheulean → Mousterian) as cognitive evidence: each industry implies a specific level of mental templating, sequence planning, and skill transmission.

You can articulate what *Mitochondrial Eve* refers to and what it does not refer to. You can describe the Out of Africa model in its current form (*mostly out of Africa, with regionally variable Neanderthal and Denisovan contributions*).

You can apply the evolutionary mismatch hypothesis to a contemporary health issue, while recognizing that mismatch is a partial explanation, not a complete one.

The single idea that matters most: *we are not the goal of evolution. We are the only surviving member of a genus that contained at least eight species. The other seven were not failed prototypes; they were their own creatures, doing their own evolutionary work, in their own time.* That posture — toward the Neanderthals, toward *H. naledi*, toward the Denisovans — is what separates contemporary paleoanthropology from the nineteenth-century progress narrative the discipline has spent a century outgrowing.

The common mistake to watch for: *treating brain size as a complete proxy for cognitive complexity*. *H. naledi* and *H. floresiensis* both make trouble for that proxy. Brain *organization* and neuron density may matter more than raw size.

If you can pick up a museum label that says *Mousterian assemblage, ~70,000 years ago, Levantine cave site* and tell yourself *Neanderthal toolmakers, prepared-core flake technique, hafted points, possibly intentional burials in the same context*, you have absorbed the chapter's diagnostic apparatus.

## What would change my mind

If a coordinated body of new evidence — ancient DNA from many more *H. naledi* specimens, dating refinements that move the species earlier or later, taphonomic studies that resolve whether the cave deposit was deliberate — converges on a strong verdict that *H. naledi* did *not* deliberately deposit its dead, the chapter's tentative endorsement of the burial claim would need revision. Lee Berger's team has made strong claims; the discipline has not yet ratified them.

## Still puzzling

The relationship between the Denisovans, *Homo longi*, and the various unnamed *ghost populations* that genetic analyses keep detecting in the deep human past. The fossil record is sparse. The genetic record is rich but indirect. The two sources of evidence are not yet aligned, and the picture they together produce is shifting roughly every two years as new specimens and new sequencing methods arrive. I expect the named cast of characters in *Homo* to look different in a decade.
