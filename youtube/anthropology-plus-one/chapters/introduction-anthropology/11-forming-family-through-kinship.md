## TL;DR

Kinship is the cultural rule system by which families are made — not biology, though biology is one of the materials it works with. This chapter walks through kinship as sociocultural construction, the basics of reading a kinship chart, the three classical kinship systems (lineal, bifurcate-merging, generational), descent rules (patrilineal, matrilineal, bilateral, cognatic), and marriage including the contemporary debates about same-sex marriage and serial monogamy.

## 1. Walking marriages on Lugu Lake

A village on the shore of Lugu Lake, on the border between Yunnan and Sichuan provinces in southwestern China. Population: about 50,000 people across a cluster of villages. Language: Mosuo, also called Na, Tibeto-Burman family. Mode of subsistence: agriculture, fishing, increasingly tourism.

A young woman, sometime after puberty, will be given her own bedroom — sometimes called a *flower room* — in her mother's house. Through the small upper window of the room, men from other households may visit her at night. The visits are called *sese*, sometimes glossed as *walking marriages* in English. The woman chooses who visits and for how long. The man returns to his own mother's household before dawn. If the relationship produces a child, the child is raised in the woman's mother's household, by the woman and her siblings. The biological father has no formal role in the child's life. He is part of his own mother's household, where he helps raise his sisters' children.

There is no marriage ceremony. There is no shared residence. There is no joint property. There is no expectation of permanence. There is no expectation of impermanence either. Some *sese* relationships last a few weeks; some last decades. Mosuo women may have children with several different partners over a lifetime. Each partner remains a part of his own mother's household.

Western anthropologists encountering Mosuo society in the early 20th century called it *matriarchal* — a society ruled by women. The Mosuo themselves do not use this language. Mosuo society is *matrilineal* (descent traced through women) and *matrilocal* (residence in the mother's household), but the elders who lead households and make decisions are typically the eldest women's brothers, not the women themselves. Power is widely distributed and consensual rather than concentrated. The pattern is an unusually clean case of one of the central claims of this chapter:

*Family is not biology. Family is what a culture says family is.*

In Mosuo society, the family is a matrilineal household: a grandmother, her daughters and sons, the daughters' children, occasionally the sons' shared roles in raising their sisters' children. Marriage as Western readers understand it — co-resident, contractual, monogamous, productive of a separate household — is absent. *Sese* is what does the work that marriage does in other societies, but it does it differently.

Mosuo society is not unique in upending Western assumptions about family. The Nayar of Kerala in southwestern India practiced a similar system of visiting husbands from the 16th through the early 20th century. The Navajo of the U.S. Southwest, whom you'll meet later in this chapter, organize society through matrilineal clans that determine who may marry whom and where the new couple will live. The Mundurucú of lowland Amazonia traditionally housed men in a separate men's house, with women and children in family huts. The variations are not exotic outliers. They are the data of kinship.

This chapter walks through the categories anthropology uses to make sense of all these variations. By the end, you should be able to look at any society's family system and read it — what kinship relations are recognized, what descent rules apply, what postmarital residence pattern is expected, what kind of marriage is permitted, who counts as a relative, who is fictive kin, who is excluded.

## 2. Concept 1 — Kinship as sociocultural construction

### Family is what culture says it is

The most foundational claim of kinship anthropology, made repeatedly across the discipline's history, is that *kinship is cultural, not biological*. Biology constrains it (humans are mammals; reproduction works the way it works); culture organizes it (which biological relationships count, which fictive relationships count, who has what obligation to whom).

The clearest evidence for the claim is *adoption*. In every society studied, adopted children are *real family*. Their kinship relationships are not provisional. The biological parents may or may not be known; the adopting parents are *the parents*. If kinship were biology, adoption would be a kind of pretend family. It is not. Adopted children inherit; they bury their parents; they are mourned by their parents. The kinship is full.

Other evidence: *fictive kinship*. The *compadrazgo* system in much of Latin America and the Catholic Mediterranean creates ritualized godparent relationships through baptism, confirmation, and marriage. Godparents are real kin in this system — they have obligations to the godchild that approach those of biological parents. The Yoruba *omo iya* (children of the same mother) language extends to people raised together regardless of biological relationship. African American extended kin networks frequently include *play cousins* and *aunties* whose biological connection is incidental to the relationship.

The discipline's working position: biology *contributes* to kinship as one of its raw materials. It does not *determine* kinship. The cultural rules — who counts as related, in what way, with what obligations — are what make kinship kinship.

### A short methodological history

The systematic study of kinship begins with **Lewis Henry Morgan**, a Rochester, New York lawyer who became fascinated with the kinship system of the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) confederacy in upstate New York in the 1840s. Morgan noticed that Haudenosaunee kinship terminology lumped categories that English kept separate (*father* and *father's brother* under one term; *mother* and *mother's sister* under another) and split categories that English lumped (*mother's brother's children* called by different terms than *father's sister's children*).

Morgan suspected this was not a Haudenosaunee oddity. He sent surveys to missionaries and colonial agents around the world asking them to record the kinship terminology of the peoples they were working with. The responses came back over a decade. Morgan's 1871 *Systems of Consanguinity and Affinity of the Human Family* compiled the data and identified three major kinship-terminology systems: what he called *lineal*, *bifurcate-merging*, and *generational* (we will get to all three). The book was the field's founding document.

A generation later, the British anthropologist **W. H. R. Rivers** introduced the *genealogical method* — a structured interview protocol for collecting kinship data systematically. The method asked informants about their parents, their parents' siblings, their grandparents, their cousins, their children, with each named relationship traced through the kinship chart. The method became standard ethnographic practice. Most fieldwork in kinship since Rivers has used some version of his interview protocol.

In the 1920s, **Bronisław Malinowski** (the Trobriand Islands ethnographer you have met repeatedly) and **A. R. Radcliffe-Brown** brought kinship into a wider analytical frame. Malinowski argued that kinship is the institution that connects everything else — it organizes inheritance, education, politics, and economy. Radcliffe-Brown argued that kinship roles produce *structural positions* — a *mother's brother* is not just a relationship; it is a defined role with defined expected behaviors that shape the whole society.

In the 1940s and 1950s, the French anthropologist **Claude Lévi-Strauss** (whom you met in Chapter 3 on structuralism) reframed kinship as *the management of marriageable categories*. Lévi-Strauss argued that the universal *incest taboo* is the foundational social rule — it separates the categories of *sexually accessible* and *sexually forbidden* — and that kinship systems exist primarily to organize the resulting marriage exchanges between groups. Marriage, in this reading, is not primarily about love or reproduction; it is about *alliance*. Groups give and receive spouses. The exchanges create durable bonds. The bonds make society possible.

### Reading a kinship chart

Anthropological kinship charts use a small set of conventions. Learn them once and you can read any chart.

- **Triangles** represent men. **Circles** represent women. A vertical bar through either represents a person who has died.
- **EGO** (the person whose chart is being drawn) is marked with a thicker outline or a label.
- **Vertical lines** represent descent (parent to child).
- **Horizontal lines** represent sibling relations.
- **Single lines** indicate *consanguineal* (blood) relationships.
- **Double horizontal lines** indicate *affinal* (marriage) relationships.
- **A slash through a double line** indicates a divorce or dissolved marriage.
- **A dashed line** indicates a non-standard relationship — adoption, an honorary uncle, a fictive aunt.

Standard abbreviations: FA (father), MO (mother), BR (brother), SI or Z (sister), SO (son), DA (daughter). Compound abbreviations build from there: MoBr (mother's brother — *uncle* in English), FaSi (father's sister — *aunt* in English), MoMoBr (mother's mother's brother — great-uncle). Older texts also use less abstract abbreviations: GrFa (grandfather), GrMo (grandmother), and so on.

EGO's *kindred* is the entire set of EGO's recognized kin — everyone connected by consanguineal or affinal ties. In Western nuclear-family terminology, kindred is comparatively small: parents, siblings, grandparents, aunts and uncles, first cousins, in-laws. In bifurcate-merging or generational systems, kindred is much larger and the rights/obligations among kindred are much denser.

Two kinds of family are usually distinguished on the chart:

- **Family of orientation**: the family EGO was raised in (parents, siblings).
- **Family of procreation**: the family EGO creates by marriage and reproduction (spouse, children).

Most adults belong to both simultaneously. Both inform identity, obligation, and inheritance.

## 3. Concept 2 — The three classical systems and descent rules

### Three terminology systems

Lewis Henry Morgan's three-way distinction holds up. Anthropologists today usually call the three systems by descriptive names; the older names (Eskimo, Iroquois, Hawaiian) are still encountered.

**Lineal (Eskimo).** The system most familiar to Western readers. The kinship terminology highlights the *nuclear family* — *father, mother, brother, sister, son, daughter* — and lumps everyone else into broader categories. *Aunt* covers both *mother's sister* and *father's sister*. *Uncle* covers both *mother's brother* and *father's brother*. *Cousin* covers all four kinds of first cousins. The terminology treats the nuclear family as the analytical unit and treats other relatives as gradations of distance. This system pairs with *bilateral descent* (kinship traced equally through both parents) and small-family social structure.

**Bifurcate-merging (Iroquois).** A larger family of orientation. *Father's brother* is called *father*; *mother's sister* is called *mother*. Their children (your parallel cousins) are called *brother* and *sister*. *Mother's brother* and *father's sister* are kept separate as distinct kin — *MoBr* and *FaSi* — and their children (your *cross cousins*) are called *cousin* (or by some other distinct term). The system treats parents and their same-sex siblings as effectively interchangeable for kinship purposes, and bifurcates (separates) the opposite-sex siblings as different kin. Bifurcate-merging pairs with *unilineal descent* (patrilineal or matrilineal) and tribal-society social structure. *Cross-cousin marriage* — marrying your mother's brother's child or father's sister's child — is encouraged in many bifurcate-merging societies because it keeps wealth and alliances within an extended kin group across generations.

**Generational (Hawaiian).** The simplest terminology, and the one that produces the largest *family* category. Everyone in the same generation is referred to with the same terms — all the men of EGO's parents' generation are *father*; all the women are *mother*; all of EGO's first cousins are *brother* and *sister*. The system pairs with *cognatic descent* (kinship traced through any combination of parents) and chieftaincy social structure. The terminology produces a very large family of orientation and was the basis for the broad Hawaiian extended families that built and maintained the precolonial chiefdoms (Chapter 8).

The three terminology systems are not just linguistic curiosities. They are *behavioral templates*. In a bifurcate-merging society, the obligations a child has to *father's brother* are similar to obligations to *father*; the obligations to *mother's brother* are different. The terminology reflects and reinforces the difference. Change the terminology and the obligations change.

### Descent

**Descent** is the rule by which a person traces their kinship across generations. Descent rules determine *which* kin you are recognized as related to and *which* you are not — even though biologically you share genes with both lines.

**Patrilineal descent.** Kinship traced through fathers only. A child belongs to the father's lineage; daughters' children belong to the daughters' husbands' lineages. Property typically passes father to son. Surnames (in many contemporary societies) reflect this — the husband's surname is taken at marriage and passed to children. The Maasai, the Nuer, much of the Arab world, and most of Han Chinese society are patrilineal.

**Matrilineal descent.** Kinship traced through mothers only. A child belongs to the mother's lineage; sons' children belong to the sons' wives' lineages. Property and political authority typically pass mother's brother to sister's son (since a man's own children belong to a different lineage from his own). The Mosuo, the Navajo, the Trobrianders, the Hopi, the Iroquois Confederacy, and the Akan of Ghana are matrilineal. Note: *matrilineal* is not the same as *matriarchal*. Matrilineal societies trace descent through women; matriarchies (in the sense of women holding political power) are extraordinarily rare. Most matrilineal societies are governed by men, with women's brothers (rather than husbands) holding the male authority over the matriline's children.

**Bilateral descent.** Kinship traced equally through both parents. Most contemporary Western societies. Kindred is defined as *everyone related to me through either parent* — typically extending to the second-cousin level for active recognition.

**Ambilineal descent.** Each family chooses *either* the mother's *or* the father's descent line, and uses that line for inheritance, residence, and identity. Different families in the same society may make different choices. Many Polynesian societies (Samoa, Tonga, Hawaii) practice ambilineal descent.

**Cognatic descent.** A broad category that includes both bilateral and ambilineal descent — any system that traces descent through both genders.

### A worked example — Navajo matrilineal organization

Among the Navajo (Diné), the largest Indigenous nation in the United States with over 325,000 enrolled members, kinship is matrilineal. A Navajo person belongs to four clans: their mother's clan (*the clan you are*), their father's clan (*the clan you are born for*), their maternal grandfather's clan, and their paternal grandfather's clan. When two Navajo people meet for the first time, the customary introduction is to recite their four clans. Marriage is forbidden within any of these four clans — a strict exogamy rule that maintains the four-clan affiliation system.

The matrilineal structure has practical consequences. Land, livestock, and ceremonial responsibilities pass mother to daughter. A married couple traditionally lives in the wife's family's compound (matrilocal residence). The husband's role with his own children is real but secondary; his more important kin role is with his sisters' children, who belong to his clan. Anthropologist Gladys Reichard, working among the Navajo in the 1930s and 1940s, documented how Navajo matrilineal organization produced a society in which women retained substantial economic independence (they owned the herds and the hogans) and political voice (they were consulted on community decisions) while formal political offices were held by men.

The Navajo case is also a case study in how kinship structures interact with colonial pressure. Forced assimilation policies, federal land allotment, and the Navajo livestock reduction of the 1930s (when the U.S. government slaughtered roughly half of the Navajo herds) all damaged the matrilineal economy. The kinship structure itself adapted but did not collapse. Contemporary Navajo society remains matrilineal, with the four-clan introduction still practiced, even as wage labor and federal-state economic structures have transformed daily life.

## 4. Concept 3 — Marriage, residence, and exchange

### Defining marriage

The anthropologist Edmund Leach, in a 1955 paper, listed ten different things marriage does in different societies — establish paternity, transmit property, legitimize sexual relations, create alliances, and so on — and argued that no single definition could cover all of them. The current working definition is approximate: *marriage is the formation of a socially recognized union, typically (but not always) involving sexual access, joint household formation, and rights to children of the union*.

The exceptions are real. The Mosuo do not marry. The Nayar of Kerala (until the early 20th century) did not marry in the standard sense — women had multiple visiting husbands and children belonged to the matriline. Some Native American societies recognized *two-spirit* marriages between gender-variant individuals. Same-sex marriage is now legally recognized in roughly 35 countries as of 2024. *Servinakuy* in Andean cultures begins as cohabitation and only becomes formal marriage after a child is born. The category is more flexible than its Western implementation suggests.

### Forms of marriage

Two big distinctions.

**Monogamy** — one spouse at a time. The dominant form in industrial societies, in foraging societies (where mobility and small-group living make monogamy adaptive), and in most contemporary state policy. *Serial monogamy* — sequential marriages with divorces between them — is a U.S. variant that approximates polygamy across a lifetime while keeping legal monogamy at any given moment.

**Polygamy** — multiple spouses. Two main subtypes.

- **Polygyny** — one man, multiple wives. The most common form of polygamy globally. Found in much of West Africa, parts of the Middle East, parts of South Asia, and historical North America (early Mormon practice). Polygyny is associated with intensive agricultural societies (where additional wives mean additional household labor and more children to inherit land), with substantial inequality (only wealthier men can support multiple wives), and with hierarchical wife-ranking systems. Bao's research on the *lukchin* (Sino-Thai) in Thailand documents how contemporary polygyny operates as an economic-cooperative arrangement — the *mia noi* (minor wife) brings her own labor and skills into the household; polygyny is sustained partly by the economic logic.

- **Polyandry** — one woman, multiple husbands. Rare. The clearest contemporary case is *fraternal polyandry* in Tibet and Nepal, where brothers share a single wife. The practice is associated with environments with extreme land scarcity (the sons of one generation share the family land instead of dividing it across multiple households) and dispersed economic activity (one brother stays home with the wife while others manage distant herds or fields). The institution holds population growth in check — one wife can carry only one pregnancy at a time, regardless of how many husbands.

**Group marriage** — multiple men, multiple women — is documented in a small number of cases (the late-19th-century Oneida community in New York; some 20th-century intentional communities) and is genuinely rare.

### The incest taboo and exogamy / endogamy

Every society has rules about whom you may not marry. The universal rule is the *incest taboo* — a prohibition on sexual relations between parents and children. Beyond the nuclear-family core, the taboo's extension varies.

- *Exogamy* rules require marriage outside a defined group (one's clan, lineage, village). Most matrilineal societies require clan exogamy.
- *Endogamy* rules require marriage within a defined group (one's caste, religion, ethnic community). Caste endogamy in India, religious endogamy in many traditional Jewish and Muslim communities.

Most societies have both — exogamy at one level (you cannot marry within your clan) and endogamy at another (you should marry within your caste).

Lévi-Strauss's argument from Chapter 3 reappears here: the incest taboo *forces* a society to exchange members. The clan that cannot marry within itself must marry outward. The marriages create alliances. The alliances create society. *Marriage is exchange*; the incest taboo is what makes the exchange necessary.

### Postmarital residence

After marriage, where does the new couple live? The choice is structured.

- **Patrilocal residence** — the couple lives with or near the husband's family. Common in patrilineal societies. The wife joins her husband's household.
- **Matrilocal residence** — the couple lives with or near the wife's family. Common in matrilineal societies. The husband joins his wife's household. Navajo traditional residence pattern.
- **Neolocal residence** — the couple establishes a new, separate household. Common in industrial societies and many foraging societies. The dominant U.S. pattern, though contemporary economic conditions have eroded the strict version.
- **Avunculocal residence** — the couple lives with or near the husband's mother's brother. Common in matrilineal societies where the husband's social and economic roles center on his mother's brother's lineage rather than his birth lineage. Trobriand Islanders practice this.
- **Ambilocal residence** — the couple chooses between the husband's and wife's families' households, typically based on which has more resources to offer.

The residence choice has cascading effects. It determines who lives with the new couple's children (paternal grandparents, maternal grandparents, or no grandparents). It determines who provides childcare, who has authority over the children, who inherits when the parents die. It is one of the densest decisions a kinship system makes.

### Marriage exchanges

A marriage typically involves a transfer of wealth between the families. Three main forms.

- **Bride wealth** (sometimes called *bride price*, though many anthropologists prefer *bride wealth* to avoid the implication of purchase). The husband's family gives goods or money to the wife's family at marriage. Common in patrilineal African societies. The transfer compensates the wife's family for the loss of her productive and reproductive labor and stabilizes the marriage (the bride wealth must be returned if the marriage dissolves, which discourages divorce). The Nuer of South Sudan are the textbook case — bride wealth is paid in cattle, with specific cattle linked to specific obligations across the kinship network.

- **Bride service**. Instead of (or alongside) a wealth transfer, the husband works for the wife's family for a defined period — often years. Common in many foraging societies and some horticultural societies. The husband's labor substitutes for the bride wealth a wealthier patrilineal society would have transferred.

- **Dowry**. The bride's family transfers wealth *to* the bride or to the new couple at marriage. Most common in some intensive agricultural societies (parts of South Asia, historical Mediterranean). Dowry is sometimes interpreted as advance inheritance to a daughter who would otherwise not inherit; sometimes as the bride's family securing a place for her in the husband's household; sometimes (in cases like contemporary Indian dowry pressure) as a transfer that leaves the bride and her family vulnerable to extortion.

The three exchange forms are not mutually exclusive — many societies practice combinations.

## 5. Integration — Reading three families through the chapter's apparatus

Three test cases — each a different family — read through the chapter's full vocabulary.

**A Navajo family on the Diné Bikéyah reservation, Arizona, 2024.** The family is matrilineal: descent traced through women, the four-clan system structuring identity. Residence pattern is matrilocal in tradition (the husband joining his wife's family compound), though contemporary economic mobility has produced wide variation. Marriage rules are exogamous at the clan level (you cannot marry into any of your four clans) and endogamous at the broader level (most marriages are still within the Navajo Nation). The kinship terminology is bifurcate-merging in classical structure. The grandmother's brother — *cheii* — has a special structural role as advisor and authority figure to the grandmother's daughter's children. Wage labor and federal economic structures have changed daily life; the kinship structure has adapted but persists.

**A *lukchin* (Sino-Thai) family in Bangkok, 2024.** A husband, his first wife, and his *mia noi* (second wife). Each wife has her own house. The husband alternates residence between them. Children of both unions are recognized members of the family with shared inheritance claims. The marriage is *de facto* polygynous (Thai law does not formally recognize the second wife) but socially recognized within the *lukchin* community. The kinship is patrilineal in descent (children take the father's surname; inheritance flows through the father). The family's economic structure depends on the labor of both wives, who manage interconnected business enterprises. Bao's ethnography documented this pattern as one form of contemporary polygyny.

**An American same-sex couple in Brooklyn, 2024.** Two women legally married since *Obergefell v. Hodges* (2015), with two children — one biological to one wife, one adopted by both. The kinship is bilateral in descent (children trace family through both mothers; both grandparents on both sides are recognized as kin). Residence is neolocal. The terminology is lineal (Eskimo) — the children call both women *mom*, distinguishing between them by first name. The family is recognized as kin under federal law (full inheritance, healthcare proxy, parental rights). The kinship change wrought by *Obergefell* is, in this case, reduction of a former exclusion to inclusion within the existing Western kinship apparatus.

The framework — kinship as sociocultural construction; descent and residence rules; marriage as exchange — works across all three cases. The frameworks do not collapse the differences. They make the differences legible.

## 7. Chapter summary — what you can now do

You can articulate that kinship is sociocultural construction, not biology. You can identify cases (adoption, fictive kin, the Mosuo) that demonstrate the claim.

You can read a kinship chart using the standard conventions and place EGO, family of orientation, and family of procreation on it.

You can identify the three classical kinship terminology systems (lineal/Eskimo, bifurcate-merging/Iroquois, generational/Hawaiian) and explain how each maps to family obligations.

You can identify the major descent rules (patrilineal, matrilineal, bilateral, ambilineal, cognatic) and trace how each shapes inheritance, residence, and identity.

You can define marriage anthropologically and recognize the major forms — monogamy, polygyny, polyandry, fraternal polyandry, walking marriage — with their typical structural conditions.

You can identify the postmarital residence patterns (patrilocal, matrilocal, neolocal, avunculocal, ambilocal) and the marriage exchanges (bride wealth, bride service, dowry).

The single idea that matters most: *family is what culture says family is*. The Mosuo grandmother's house, the Navajo four-clan introduction, the Bedouin patrilineal compound, the Brooklyn same-sex couple — each is a real family, organized by its own rules. The diversity is the data; the rules are the analytical tools.

The common mistake to watch for: *projecting your own kinship system onto societies that operate differently*. Most contemporary readers grow up bilateral, neolocal, monogamous, and lineal-terminology. None of this is universal. The chapter's point is that recognizing your own system as one among many is the precondition for reading any other system at all.

If you can take a family description (from a novel, an ethnography, a news story, a memoir) and identify (a) descent, (b) residence, (c) marriage form, (d) exchange, (e) terminology, you have absorbed the chapter's apparatus.

## What would change my mind

If genetic and historical evidence converged on a finding that the prehistoric default of human kinship was much more uniform than the cross-cultural record currently suggests — that the variations we see across contemporary societies are recent divergences from a deep common pattern — the chapter's strong commitment to kinship-as-cultural-construction would need revision. The current evidence does not support this; the chapter holds the current consensus.

## Still puzzling

The question of *which* kinship features are most easily transformed by economic and political change and *which* are most durable. The Navajo matrilineal system has held under colonial pressure for 500 years. Same-sex marriage in the U.S. moved from criminalized to constitutionally protected in 30 years. What distinguishes the durable rules from the labile ones? The answer is mixed in ways I do not fully understand.
