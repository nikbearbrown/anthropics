# Chapter 2 — Methods: Cultural and Archaeological

## Suggested titles

- *Methods: How Anthropology Knows What It Knows*
- *The Trowel and the Notebook — Anthropology's Two Hands*
- *In the Field, In the Stratum, In the Archive*

## TL;DR

Anthropology is held together by its central narrative; the methods are how the narrative gets evidenced. This chapter walks through the two main hands of the discipline — the archaeologist's trowel digging through stratified time, and the cultural anthropologist's notebook recording lived perspective — and unpacks the ethics that have rebuilt both since the 1960s.

## 1. A trowel against a stone

Dawn over a canyon in northern Peru. The light is the cold blue of high altitude. An archaeologist sets out a grid of taut string over a square meter of earth that, until two days ago, was ordinary hillside. Each square meter has its own number. Each square meter has its own notebook. She is on her knees with a trowel and, for the difficult places, a paintbrush. Today's task is three centimeters of work. By the end of the season she will have cleared, mapped, and removed perhaps half a cubic meter of soil. From it will come maybe two dozen objects worth a label, and from those two dozen objects, when they get to the lab, will come a story about a community that lived in this canyon nine thousand years ago.

Why so slow?

Because the only thing that makes the dirt worth removing is its order. The pottery shard at twenty centimeters depth is not the same kind of evidence as the pottery shard at ten centimeters. The bone next to the spear point tells you something about hunting. The bone three meters away tells you something about disposal. If the archaeologist removes either object before its position is recorded — its *provenience* — the object loses most of what made it worth digging up. A spearpoint without context is a spearpoint. A spearpoint *in situ*, beside an arrangement of other objects in a known stratum, is a sentence in a story that was not yet legible.

Six time zones away, a different anthropologist arrives in a small town. She has spent six months reading about the place. She has IRB clearance from her university and a permit from the local council. She has her notebook, a digital recorder she will not turn on for the first month, and a list of the families she has been told to introduce herself to first. By the end of her first day, she will have eaten three meals she did not plan to eat, met perhaps fifteen people, and recorded in her field journal mostly her own confusion. By the end of her first year, the names will resolve into kinship, the meals into seasonal cycles, the confusion into something she might begin to call understanding. *Participant observation*, the method she is practicing, is to anthropology what excavation is to archaeology: the slow, patient assembly of evidence that depends on context for meaning.

Two methods. Two trowels of different shapes. One discipline.

This chapter unpacks how both work — the archaeologist's grid and superposition, the cultural anthropologist's emic stance and ethical infrastructure — and what the discipline did to itself in the late twentieth century when it noticed that both methods, for most of their history, had been done badly.

**Learning objectives.** After this chapter, you should be able to:

- Explain the *law of superposition* and apply it to a hypothetical excavation.
- Distinguish *relative* dating methods (stratigraphy, seriation, typological sequence) from *absolute* (chronometric) dating methods (radiocarbon, potassium–argon, dendrochronology) and identify which method fits which kind of question.
- Describe *participant observation* and explain why a researcher's first weeks of fieldwork are typically least productive in the conventional sense.
- Distinguish the *emic* perspective (insider's view) from the *etic* perspective (outsider's view) and explain why anthropologists aim for the emic.
- Explain what an Institutional Review Board (IRB) does and why fieldwork-based research often needs additional protocols beyond the IRB.
- Explain *salvage anthropology* — what it was, why it ended, and what its residue is in present-day museum collections.
- Describe the function of NAGPRA (1990) and the National Historic Preservation Act (1966) in the present-day politics of repatriation.

**Prerequisites.** Chapter 1 — the four-field structure, the goal of an insider's point of view, and the ethical problems of representing other people.

**Why this chapter matters.** Every later chapter assumes you know how anthropology *gets* its evidence. A claim about kinship depends on careful interview work. A claim about hominin evolution depends on careful excavation. A claim about ritual depends on participant observation that doesn't impose its observer's frame. Without the methods chapter, the rest of the book reads like assertion. With it, every claim has a method behind it that you can interrogate.

## 2. Concept 1 — Reading time from the ground: archaeology's working machinery

### Twelve thousand years stacked in a meter

Strip a meter of earth from a long-inhabited site and you do not strip a uniform mass. You strip layers. Each layer — each *stratum* — is a slice of accumulated time. At the bottom: the oldest layer, deposited first, when no one was here. Above it: a layer with the residue of the first occupants, perhaps charcoal and bone fragments. Above that: a layer with potsherds, evidence of cooking and storage. Above that: another layer, perhaps with iron, perhaps with the remains of a building. Above all of it: the soil you walked across this morning, with last year's leaf litter mixed in.

This stack is not metaphor. It is what the dirt actually does. Sediment accumulates, year upon year, century upon century, with whatever was happening on the surface getting pressed down into a thin band of evidence. The Danish scientist Nicolas Steno proposed in 1669 that, absent some disturbance — a flood, an earthquake, a burrowing animal, a builder digging a foundation — the deeper layers will be older than the shallower ones. This is the **law of superposition**. It is the foundation of nearly every claim archaeology makes about time.

The corollary, called the **cross-cutting relationship**, says the same thing the other direction: if a feature *cuts across* multiple strata — say, a post-hole dug for a structure, slicing down through three older layers — that feature was created *after* all the layers it cuts. A grave that disturbs older strata is younger than the strata. This is how you read time from a wall of soil.

### What the grid is for

The grid you saw in §1 is the archaeologist's instrument for keeping superposition usable. The site is divided into square units — typically one meter per side — each labeled. Within each unit, every recovered object is recorded with three coordinates: the two horizontal coordinates of the grid square, and the vertical coordinate of the stratum (or, more precisely, the depth below a fixed datum point). Object number, square coordinates, depth, stratum number, photograph reference, and field-note reference all get recorded before the object is lifted. The recording is the science. The lifting is the cleanup.

If an object is removed before its coordinates are recorded, it is in *secondary context*. It can still be interesting — an arrowhead is still an arrowhead — but it can no longer answer most of the questions an archaeologist would want to ask of it. The community at this site, for example, used what kind of pottery in what period? An arrowhead in secondary context cannot help with that question. An arrowhead in primary context, alongside the right strata of pottery and bone and charcoal, can.

This is why the discipline pairs maddeningly slow excavation with obsessive documentation. *Most of the value of what comes out of the ground is determined before it leaves the ground.*

### Reading time more precisely — relative vs. absolute dating

Superposition gets you *order*: this is older than that. It does not get you *date*: 9,000 years old, give or take 50.

For order, archaeologists use **relative dating**. The two main methods.

**Stratigraphic superposition.** What you just learned. A layer below another layer is older. A feature cutting through layers is younger than the layers it cuts. Caveats apply: animal burrows, geological inversions, erosion. The discipline reads the soil profile to flag these and adjusts.

**Typological sequence and seriation.** Within a region, you can often arrange a class of objects — pottery styles, projectile point shapes, button types — into a sequence where each style is replaced gradually by the next. The English archaeologist Flinders Petrie introduced **seriation** in the late nineteenth century to date Egyptian burials that had no stratigraphic context. He sorted pottery styles into a sequence whose order was confirmed when independent evidence — written records — became available. Today, when archaeologists encounter a site with no stratigraphy (or where stratigraphy has been disturbed), they can often place the site in time by comparing its objects to the regional typological sequence.

Relative dating tells you the *order* of things. **Absolute dating**, also called **chronometric dating**, gives you a number.

**Radiocarbon dating** is the workhorse for organic material — bone, wood, charcoal, fiber, anything that was once alive. Living organisms continuously absorb carbon from the atmosphere; that carbon includes a small steady fraction of radioactive carbon-14. When the organism dies, no more carbon-14 is absorbed, and the existing carbon-14 begins decaying at a known rate (a half-life of about 5,730 years). Measure the ratio of carbon-14 to ordinary carbon-12 in a sample, calculate how many half-lives have passed, and you have a date with a reasonable margin of error — typically ±50 years for samples under 50,000 years old. Beyond 50,000 years there is too little carbon-14 left to measure reliably, and the method runs out of light.

**Potassium-argon dating** extends back further by measuring the decay of potassium-40 into argon-40 in volcanic rock. Radiometric dating in the deep past — millions of years — uses this method or its cousin (argon-argon dating) to establish the ages of hominin fossils embedded in volcanic strata.

**Dendrochronology** — tree-ring dating — uses the patterns of wide and narrow rings in tree cross-sections to date wooden artifacts to the year. Trees grown in the same region in the same year have similar ring-width patterns, dictated by the climate of that year. Build a regional pattern from old, datable trees, then match the rings of an undated wood sample to the regional pattern. The Laboratory of Tree-Ring Research at the University of Arizona, founded in 1937, maintains the world's longest dendrochronological sequences. The technique helped fix the dates of the Pueblo Bonita structures in Chaco Canyon — the same Chaco Canyon Chapter 1 mentioned briefly — to between roughly 800 and 1150 CE.

### The trade-off — precision vs. range

Each method optimizes for something specific. Stratigraphy gives you order at any depth, but no number. Radiocarbon gives you a number, but only for organic material and only out to about 50,000 years. Potassium-argon gives you deeper time but only on volcanic material. Dendrochronology gives you the precise year, but only for wood and only within regions where a continuous ring-pattern reference has been built.

A working archaeologist *triangulates*. A site dated by stratigraphic position, by typological sequence, by radiocarbon on the organics, and (if conditions allow) by dendrochronology produces a date that no single method could deliver. The discipline's confidence in a date scales with how many independent methods converge on the same answer. The discipline's distrust of a date scales with how few do.

### Worked example — three centimeters in a Peruvian canyon

Return to the dawn scene from §1. Tom Dillehay's team — the same Peru excavation Chapter 1 mentioned — spent seven years working sites along the northern coast. The team's claim, when they published, was that this region's communities had developed fishing, farming, and herding strategies over fourteen thousand years, and that the people who lived here placed unusual weight on cooperation and harmony with their environment.

That claim depends on every method in this section. The strata at the site preserved fourteen thousand years of accumulating sediment. The relative ordering came from stratigraphy — what was below was older, what cut across was younger. The absolute dates came from radiocarbon analysis of charcoal, bone, and plant material recovered from each layer. The cultural conclusions — about cooperation, about harmony — came from the patterns visible across the fourteen-thousand-year sequence: how houses were arranged, how food was shared, what got buried where. None of those interpretations were possible without the dating, and the dating was not possible without the patient grid, the careful documentation, the obsessive attention to context.

This is the worked example for *every* archaeological claim. The slow work is the science. The fast claim is what the slow work permits.

## 3. Concept 2 — Reading culture from inside it: participant observation and the ethics that grew up around it

### A first month of usefulness

A cultural anthropologist's first month at a field site is, by any conventional measure, unproductive. The notebook is full of confusion. The names of people don't yet line up with kinship roles. The food is unfamiliar. The cadence of conversation is not yet legible. The researcher's questions, when they ask them, are mostly the wrong questions — the questions an outsider asks before they know which questions are the right ones.

This is *not* a failure of the method. It is the method working as designed. **Participant observation**, the central method of cultural anthropology, requires that the researcher first become familiar enough with the rhythms of the community that the relevant questions become askable. You cannot ask sharp questions about a wedding ceremony before you know what is at stake in this community's marriages. You cannot ask sharp questions about a meal before you know who eats with whom, in what order, and what it means when someone declines a dish. The first month is the apprenticeship to *what is going on*.

Bronisław Malinowski set the modern template a century ago by spending nearly two years among the Trobriand peoples — long enough that the kula ring, that ceremonial exchange of shell bracelets and necklaces along an arc of islands, became *legible* to him as a system. Two years was about right. After two years, you stop asking outsider questions and start asking insider ones.

### The emic / etic distinction

Two perspectives are in play, and the discipline has names for them.

The **etic** perspective — from the linguistic term *phonetic*, meaning sounds described from outside any particular language — is the view from outside the culture. An etic description of a wedding ceremony might note the duration, the number of attendees, the sequence of public actions, the cost, the participants' demographic categories. Etic description is what an anthropologist's cousin who never visited could understand from a phone call.

The **emic** perspective — from *phonemic*, meaning sounds as they are heard within a particular language — is the view from inside the culture. An emic description of the same wedding might describe the relationship being made and unmade, the ritual obligation between two lineages, the moment in the ceremony that participants would point to as the moment of becoming. Emic description is what an insider would explain to another insider.

Anthropology's stated goal is the emic perspective. It is also, as Chapter 1 admitted, never fully reachable. The compromise — what Nancy Scheper-Hughes called *good enough ethnography* — is to get as close as long-term, careful, collaborative work allows, while being honest about the limits.

### The ethical scaffolding — IRB, consent, and what came before it

Until the 1960s, much of cultural anthropology was conducted without anything resembling formal ethical review. A White American anthropologist would arrive in a community, ask questions, take notes, return home, publish a book, and rarely return. The community whose perspective the book represented might never see it. They had no veto over how they were depicted. They had no claim on the data. They had no claim on whatever income the book produced for the anthropologist.

This pattern was not, in retrospect, sustainable, and the discipline has spent the last sixty years rebuilding around it.

The first piece of the rebuild is the **Institutional Review Board** (IRB). Every U.S. university maintains one (or several). Before any research involving human subjects can begin, the IRB reviews the planned research and approves it — or sends it back for revision — based on a set of formal criteria. The IRB checks that participants will give *informed consent* (they understand what the research is about and how their data will be used); that risks to participants will be minimized; that participants can withdraw at any time; that participants' identities will be protected unless they explicitly consent otherwise; that the data will be stored or destroyed according to a defined plan. The IRB process was originally built for medical research — it is the residue of post-Nuremberg, post-Tuskegee reforms — and it does not always fit anthropological fieldwork cleanly. Open-ended fieldwork, where the questions emerge over time, fits awkwardly into IRB templates that expect a fixed protocol.

Because the IRB is not always sufficient, fieldwork frequently runs through *additional* review processes. Many Indigenous nations maintain their own research protocols and require permits before any research can occur on their territory. Foreign countries have their own permitting processes. Many anthropologists now work under what amount to community-level review boards in the places they study. A researcher whose IRB clears the project but whose community contacts decline participation cannot do the research. The *triple gate* — institution, host nation, host community — is the contemporary norm.

The second piece of the rebuild is the *long-term* relationship. Where mid-twentieth-century fieldwork might have meant a single trip and a single book, contemporary anthropology often means decades of return. The anthropologist comes back annually. The community knows them, holds them accountable, sometimes corrects them, sometimes co-authors with them. *Vine Deloria Jr.*, the Standing Rock Sioux scholar, leveled a famous criticism in his 1969 book *Custer Died for Your Sins* — that anthropologists came, took, and left, and the people they studied got nothing back. The discipline took the criticism seriously. The long-term, return-based relationship is one piece of its response.

The third piece is the question of *ownership*. Who owns the data — the recordings, the photographs, the field notes, the oral histories? In older anthropology, the answer was simple: the anthropologist owned them, and the institution that funded the work owned them along with the anthropologist. Today the answer is contested and increasingly different. Many anthropologists now treat the people they record as the *owners* of the recording. Some publications now list the community as the formal author, with the anthropologist as compiler — the *Chinuk Wawa* dictionary published by the Confederated Tribes of the Grand Ronde Community, with the linguist Henry Zenk credited as compiler, is one example. Some research data is destroyed at the end of a project so it cannot be repurposed without re-consent.

### The trade-off — depth vs. permission

Participant observation optimizes for *depth of cultural understanding* at the cost of *time, access, and a willingness to follow community-level rules that may have nothing to do with academic norms*. The depth requires that the researcher submit to relationships and institutions that academic culture by itself would not have produced. A researcher unwilling to submit to those relationships will produce thin work, regardless of how many notebooks they fill.

### Worked example — six months before the recorder turns on

A graduate student plans to study how a small Maine fishing community has adapted to declining lobster populations. Her IRB approves the protocol. The local fishing co-op invites her to attend their meetings. She has reading-list-level knowledge of the political economy of New England fisheries. She has a digital recorder.

She does not turn the recorder on. Not for the first six months. She attends meetings, helps with mundane tasks, eats at the local diner, listens. Her notebook fills with names, with the cadence of disagreements at meetings, with the specific words people use for kinds of weather and kinds of fish. After six months she begins to ask focused questions, almost always with the recorder off. After eight months she asks her first formal interview, with consent paperwork and the recorder running, of a co-op member she knows well by then.

The interview is sharper than any interview she could have conducted in month one. The questions are right. The interviewee trusts her. The transcript is short — maybe forty minutes — but every minute is dense. Six months of un-recorded fieldwork was the precondition for forty minutes of usable recording. *That ratio is the discipline.*

## 4. Concept 3 — The discipline reckons with itself: salvage anthropology, museums, and repatriation

### A diorama that hasn't been touched in 100 years

In the foyer of the Auckland Museum in New Zealand stand two large totem poles, collected from the Northwest Coast of North America in the late nineteenth or early twentieth century. They have been in roughly that location, with roughly the same labels, for over a hundred years. The labels were written by a curator, in a single afternoon, in a year when no member of the originating community was consulted. The label reads as if the totem poles speak for themselves. They do not.

Multiply this scene by every major natural-history museum in Europe and North America. By every university anthropology department's storage room. The cumulative result is a global archive of millions of objects, hundreds of thousands of ancestral human remains, and uncounted notebooks of language and song — most of it collected during a fifty-year window between roughly 1870 and 1930, much of it without informed consent in any modern sense, often by people working under the assumption that the cultures they were collecting from were on the verge of extinction.

The discipline has a name for this period. **Salvage anthropology.**

### What salvage anthropology was, and what it left behind

Salvage anthropology was driven by a specific belief, widely held among nineteenth-century European and American scientists, that Indigenous peoples — especially the Native peoples of North America — were dying out and that their cultures would soon be unrecoverable. The belief was partly empirical: Native populations in North America had collapsed by ninety percent or more from disease, war, and forced removal during the colonial and early-national periods. It was partly ideological: many salvage anthropologists assumed that Indigenous cultures could not survive contact with European modernity. The conclusion drawn from these assumptions was that *somebody had to collect everything before it was gone.*

The collecting took specific institutional form. The U.S. Smithsonian's Bureau of American Ethnology (BAE), founded in 1879, hired anthropologists and sent them to reservations to record languages, oral histories, and ceremonial practices. Albert Gatschet, a Swiss-trained linguist who emigrated to the U.S. in 1868, spent decades among the tribes of the Pacific Northwest, the Southeast, and elsewhere, recording vocabularies and ethnographies. His notebooks remain a primary source for languages that have lost most of their fluent speakers. Leonard Frachtenberg, working in Oregon around 1900, recorded Coos, Coquille, Lower Umpqua, and Alsea materials and — unlike many of his peers — used his archival access to help the tribes recover unratified treaties from the 1850s and successfully sue the federal government for unpaid compensation. Frachtenberg was an exception. The norm was extractive: the anthropologist collected, the museum stored, the community received nothing.

The objects went to museums. Many museums were established or expanded specifically to house ethnographic collections. The American Museum of Natural History in New York, the Smithsonian National Museum of Natural History in Washington, the Pitt Rivers Museum in Oxford, the Auckland Museum, the British Museum — all expanded their holdings dramatically during the salvage period. Most of those holdings have been on display, in storage, or both for the entire century since.

The criticism of this period is sharp. The cultures being "salvaged" were not, in fact, going extinct — most are still here, with most of the people salvage anthropologists assumed would soon be gone instead being represented by their direct descendants today. The framing of *extinction* itself was a self-fulfilling rhetoric: it justified urgent collecting while simultaneously rationalizing assimilation policies that aimed to eliminate Indigenous cultures. The objects were collected without consent in any modern sense. The data was rarely shared back with the communities. And the discipline, for the better part of a century, treated the entire arrangement as legitimate scientific practice.

### The legal infrastructure of repatriation

Two laws in the United States restructure what museums must do with their salvage-era collections.

**The National Historic Preservation Act (NHPA), 1966.** Before any federally-funded development project — a road, a building, an airport — federal agencies must identify historic and cultural resources in the affected area and consult with state historic preservation officers (SHPOs) and tribal historic preservation officers (THPOs). A development project that would destroy a known cultural site without mitigation can be blocked or delayed. This shifted the burden: federal agencies, not private parties, must affirmatively look for cultural resources before breaking ground.

**The Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (NAGPRA), 1990.** This act requires federally-funded museums and agencies in possession of Native American human remains, funerary objects, sacred objects, or objects of cultural patrimony to inventory those holdings and, on request from the originating tribe, return them. As of 2010, more than 20,000 sets of remains had been repatriated under NAGPRA; the work is far from complete. Millions of additional artifacts and remains are still in repositories, some without provenance specific enough to identify the originating community. International collections — like the Northwest Coast objects in the British Museum or the Auckland Museum — fall outside NAGPRA entirely.

NAGPRA's gaps are real. Repatriation requires *provenience* — the documented record of where an object came from — and many older collections lack provenience specific enough to identify a tribe rather than a region. A 2010 amendment broadened the law to permit groups of related tribes to repatriate regional objects together, partly addressing the problem.

### Indigenous-led research and the rebuilding

If salvage anthropology took, contemporary Indigenous-led research takes back. The pattern: a tribe with archived materials in a faraway repository negotiates copies, brings them home, builds language-immersion programs, restores dance traditions, sometimes uses the same archival materials to support legal claims that restore federal recognition or land. The Coquille Indian Tribe's 1989 federal restoration was supported by archival materials they retrieved from the Smithsonian. The Southwest Oregon Research Project copied 150,000 pages of documents about Western Oregon tribes from the Smithsonian and the National Archives between 1995 and 2006; those copies now sit in the Knight Library at the University of Oregon and in seventeen tribal collections, used for cultural recovery.

A linguistic anthropologist named Henry Zenk has spent fifty years working with the Confederated Tribes of the Grand Ronde Community of Oregon. He is a Chinuk Wawa speaker; he has helped re-build a fluent-speaker community within Grand Ronde through immersion teaching; he co-authored *My Life, by Louis Kenoyer* (2017) by translating and editing notebooks the early-twentieth-century anthropologist Melville Jacobs had collected from the Kalapuya elder Louis Kenoyer. Zenk's career, in shape and length, is what contemporary anthropology now expects of itself: long-term, embedded, accountable, focused on giving back as much as taking. If salvage anthropology is what the discipline once was, Zenk is something close to what the discipline is trying to become.

### The trade-off named

Salvage anthropology was *fast collecting at scale*, optimized for *building a comprehensive archive of cultures the collectors believed would soon vanish*, at the cost of *consent, community benefit, and the dignity of the people whose remains and objects were taken*. The contemporary alternative — long-term, consent-based, repatriation-aware, community-accountable — is slow, expensive, and often produces less per-researcher publication output. It also produces work that is less likely to be wrong about the people it represents and more likely to be useful to them.

## 5. Integration — Mixed methods, the scientific arc, and what good fieldwork looks like

The two main hands of anthropology — the trowel and the notebook — are usually not used alone. Most contemporary anthropological projects mix methods. An archaeologist working a site interviews local elders about oral traditions concerning the place. A cultural anthropologist working a fishing community pulls historical photographs from archives to compare past and present. A biological anthropologist studying contemporary diet reviews archaeological middens for the deep history of the same population's foodways. **Mixed methods** is the working norm.

The mixing follows the scientific arc. *Question.* What do we want to know? *Hypothesis.* What is the working answer we plan to test? *Data collection.* What evidence — from excavation, from interview, from archive, from genetics, from language — will speak to the question? *Analysis.* What does the evidence say, and what does it not say? *Conclusion.* A claim, hedged appropriately, with the limitations named and the next questions identified.

Anthropology's data is often a blend of *quantitative* (countable: how many burials at this depth, what percentage of households practice this exchange, how many generations of patrilineal descent) and *qualitative* (interpretive: what does this ritual mean to its participants, what is the felt difference between two registers of speech). Most claims worth making require both kinds. *Triangulation* — confirming a finding by multiple independent methods — is the discipline's working defense against the failure modes of any single method.

### What good fieldwork looks like — three properties

Pulling the chapter together: a piece of fieldwork that the discipline would now consider well-conducted has, at minimum, three properties.

**Context preserved.** Whether the data is artifacts in a stratum or a recorded conversation in a kitchen, the context that gives the data meaning is documented before the data is moved or analyzed.

**Consent secured.** The people contributing to the data — whether they are living interviewees or descendants of the people whose objects are being studied — have been informed about what the research will do, have agreed to participate, and have a continuing claim on how their data is used.

**Reciprocity built in.** The research is not extractive. The community contributing to it gets something back: a copy of the published work, an immersion-school curriculum, an archival repatriation, a successful legal claim, or simply a sustained relationship that does not end when the researcher's first book is in print.

A piece of work that scores well on all three is what good fieldwork looks like. A piece that scores well on the first two but poorly on the third is what a generation of anthropologists has been trying to move past. A piece that scores poorly on any is what the discipline is rebuilding away from.

## 7. Chapter summary — what you can now do

You can read time from a wall of dirt. The law of superposition gives you order; relative dating methods (stratigraphy, typology, seriation) refine the order; absolute dating methods (radiocarbon, potassium-argon, dendrochronology) give you numbers. A working archaeologist triangulates by using several methods at once, and the confidence in any date scales with how many independent methods converge.

You can design and run participant observation. The first weeks are an apprenticeship to *what is going on*; the recorded interviews come later, and only because the un-recorded fieldwork made them possible. You aim for the *emic* perspective — the view from inside the culture — knowing it is never fully reachable.

You can navigate the ethical scaffolding that grew up around fieldwork in the second half of the twentieth century. IRB review, informed consent, community-level permitting, long-term commitment, ownership questions, and reciprocity — these are not optional features of the work. They are the work.

You can locate salvage anthropology in the discipline's history and the contemporary rebuilding around repatriation and Indigenous-led research. NAGPRA (1990) and NHPA (1966) are the two U.S. legal infrastructures you should know by name. Henry Zenk's fifty-year career with the Confederated Tribes of Grand Ronde is a model of what contemporary anthropology aspires to.

The single idea that matters most: *the slow work is the science. The fast claim is what the slow work permits.* Whether the slow work is excavation or participant observation or archival recovery, that ratio holds across the discipline.

The common mistake to watch for: assuming that thoroughness can be replaced by speed. It cannot. A faster fieldworker produces shallower data. A faster excavator produces context-stripped artifacts. A discipline that lets itself be hurried by publication pressure produces work that does not hold.

If you can sketch a research protocol that includes context preservation, informed consent, and a reciprocity plan, you have absorbed the methodological core this chapter is about.

## What would change my mind

If repatriation cases over the next decade reveal that Indigenous-led research projects, on average, produce thinner published output than salvage-era anthropology produced — and that the cultural recovery work the new model promised has not in fact materialized — the chapter's strong endorsement of the contemporary model would need revision. The early returns suggest the opposite, but the empirical question is real.

## Still puzzling

The relationship between IRB compliance and ethical research. The IRB is necessary; it is not sufficient. There are projects that pass IRB review and still feel extractive, and there are projects that take liberties with IRB rules and serve their communities better than rule-following projects do. I do not have a clean theory of when the formal rules suffice and when they don't. The chapter's *triple gate* — institutional review, host nation, host community — is closer to a working answer than the IRB alone, but it is not a final one.
