## Candidate 04 — Build a Resolution Budget Calculator for SEM with Claude Code

- Source: electron-microscopy/chapters/02-electron-optics-and-resolution.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: You want 1 nm resolution. Your beam has spherical aberration coefficient Cs = 2.5 mm. There's a specific aperture angle α that minimizes total probe size — too small and diffraction limits you, too large and aberrations dominate. The optimum is computable in one equation.
- The artifact: A Python SEM resolution budget calculator. Inputs: accelerating voltage (kV), spherical aberration coefficient Cs (mm), chromatic aberration coefficient Cc (mm), energy spread ΔE (eV). Outputs: (1) electron wavelength λ at the given voltage, (2) diffraction limit disc radius d_diff = 0.61λ/α as function of aperture α, (3) spherical aberration disc radius d_sph = 0.5*Cs*α³, (4) chromatic aberration disc radius d_chrom = Cc*(ΔE/E)*α, (5) total probe size d_total = √(d_diff² + d_sph² + d_chrom²) as a function of α, (6) optimal aperture α_opt that minimizes d_total, (7) animated three-panel chart: d_diff, d_sph, d_chrom vs. α, then d_total vs. α with the minimum labeled, then a comparison chart of total probe size vs. accelerating voltage.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Build a Python SEM resolution budget calculator. Inputs: V_kV (accelerating voltage), Cs_mm (spherical aberration coefficient), Cc_mm (chromatic aberration coefficient), delta_E_eV (energy spread). Compute: λ = 1.226/sqrt(V_volts) nm; d_diff(α) = 0.61*λ/α; d_sph(α) = 0.5*Cs_mm*1e6*(α*1e-3)^3 nm (convert mm to nm, α in mrad); d_chrom(α) = Cc_mm*1e6*(delta_E_eV/(V_kV*1e3))*(α*1e-3) nm; d_total(α) = sqrt(d_diff^2+d_sph^2+d_chrom^2). Find α_opt by minimizing d_total. Plot animated 3-panel: individual contributions, d_total with minimum, d_total vs kV from 1 to 30."`
- Read / check: Verify the wavelength formula gives ~7.1 pm at 30 kV. Confirm d_sph grows as α³ (cubic dependence). Check d_diff decreases as α increases (inverse relationship). Verify α_opt produces a clear minimum in d_total and that the minimum probe size matches typical SEM specifications (~1-5 nm at 20-30 kV for Cs~2-5 mm).
- Human supplies: Cs and Cc values for a real SEM (e.g., Cs = 2.5 mm, Cc = 3.0 mm for a typical tungsten hairpin SEM column). These are instrument-specific and should be verified against manufacturer specs for the demo instrument. The video can use generic values with a note.
- Output medium: Manim (animated three-panel chart building each aberration contribution as a curve, then d_total curve appearing with minimum highlighted, then kV sweep building)
- The change: Plug in Cs = 0.001 mm (aberration-corrected instrument) and watch d_total collapse to near-diffraction-limited — demonstrating quantitatively what aberration correction achieves.
- Teardown angle: The optimal aperture is where diffraction and aberration are in balance. Closing the aperture improves aberrations but introduces diffraction. Opening it reduces diffraction but worsens aberrations. The calculation makes this trade-off visible and navigable.
- Exclusions: Astigmatism correction, specimen drift contribution to resolution, field-emission vs. thermionic gun energy spread comparison.
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 08 — Build a TEM Diffraction Pattern Indexer with Claude Code

- Source: electron-microscopy/chapters/15-diffraction-in-tem.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: The diffraction pattern tells you the crystal structure — if you can index it. Each spot's position encodes the d-spacing of a specific plane family. Two spots' angle encodes the zone axis. Both together identify the phase. The indexing is a geometry problem you can solve algorithmically.
- The artifact: A Python TEM diffraction pattern indexer. Inputs: camera length L (mm), wavelength λ (pm), list of measured spot distances from center r_measured (mm) and angles between spots (degrees), candidate crystal structure (cubic, FCC, BCC) with lattice parameter a (Å). Outputs: (1) d-spacings from r_measured via d = λL/r, (2) hkl assignment from d-spacings using the Bragg law for cubic: d_hkl = a/√(h²+k²+l²), finding the (hkl) that minimizes |d_calc - d_meas|, (3) zone axis determination from the cross product of two indexed g-vectors, (4) a simulated diffraction pattern (2D plot) showing the indexed spots with their (hkl) labels, (5) goodness-of-fit metric (mean absolute deviation in d-spacing).
- Prompt seed: `claude "Build a Python TEM diffraction pattern indexer for cubic crystal systems. Inputs: camera_length_mm, wavelength_pm, spot_distances_mm (list from center), angles_between_spots_deg (list), lattice_a_angstrom, system ('FCC' or 'BCC'). d-spacing from spot: d = lambda_nm * camera_length_mm / (spot_dist_mm * 1e6). Allowed hkl for FCC: all same parity; BCC: h+k+l even. For each d_measured, find hkl minimizing |d_calc - d_meas| where d_calc = a/sqrt(h^2+k^2+l^2). Zone axis from cross product of two g vectors. Plot simulated diffraction pattern with hkl labels. Print mean absolute d-spacing deviation. Demo: FCC aluminum (a=4.05 Å) at 300 kV."`
- Read / check: Verify the camera equation d = λL/r is correctly implemented (check units carefully — λ in pm, L in mm, r in mm). Confirm FCC allowed reflections (all same parity) are correctly filtered. Check the zone axis cross-product gives a crystallographic direction. Verify the aluminum demo gives recognizable low-index spots (111, 200, 220) at reasonable d-spacings.
- Human supplies: Nothing — fully synthetic for the demo. A real indexing application would require measured spot distances from actual diffraction patterns, which the human would supply. The video can show the algorithm on synthetic inputs and note real-data application.
- Output medium: Manim (animated diffraction pattern building spot by spot with hkl labels appearing, zone axis revealed at the end, MAD metric shown)
- The change: Switch from FCC to BCC (iron, a=2.87 Å) and show how the allowed reflections change (h+k+l even rule), producing a different set of spots — demonstrating that the crystal structure is encoded in which spots are absent, not just which are present (systematic absences).
- Teardown angle: The diffraction pattern is not an image — it's a Fourier transform of the crystal potential. Indexing it requires knowing the selection rules for each Bravais lattice. The absence of forbidden reflections is as informative as the presence of allowed ones.
- Exclusions: Kikuchi lines, convergent beam electron diffraction (CBED), polycrystalline ring pattern indexing.
- Score: 7/10
