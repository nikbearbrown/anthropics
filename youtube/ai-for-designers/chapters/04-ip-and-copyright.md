# Chapter 4 — IP and Copyright: What You Can and Cannot Give a Client

*You cannot assign rights you do not have.*

There is a Latin phrase that has survived in contract law for centuries not because lawyers love Latin — though they do — but because it names something true about the world. *Nemo dat quod non habet.* No one gives what they do not have. The phrase is a reminder that ownership is not a feeling. It is not effort. It is not what you paid for the tool or how long you spent on the prompt. Ownership is a legal relationship between a person and a thing, and that relationship either exists or it doesn't. When you sign a contract promising to transfer rights to a client, you are making a claim about that relationship. If the claim is wrong, the contract does not fix it. The contract just makes the problem more expensive.

This is not an abstract concern. A designer delivers an AI-generated logo. The client pays three thousand dollars. The presentation looked good. The mockups looked real. The designer sends the final files and moves on. Later, the client asks for the source vector file and registration support. The designer has no vector construction. The original prompt does not reproduce the same image. There is no clear record of human modification beyond selecting a favorite output from a generation grid. Now the client asks the question that should have been asked before delivery: *What exactly did we buy?*

That question is what this chapter is about.

## What Copyright Actually Requires

Copyright law in the United States protects human authorship. Not effort. Not skill. Not the payment of a subscription fee. Human authorship — which means that the expressive choices in the work were made by a human being.

This has always been true, but AI forces us to take it seriously in a way that older tools did not. When a photographer clicked a shutter, courts could debate whether the creative choices — framing, exposure, moment of capture — were sufficiently human. When a graphic designer used Adobe Illustrator, the question barely arose, because Illustrator is a tool and the designer controlled every expressive element. AI image generation is different. The model determines what the image actually looks like. The human provides a prompt, and the prompt constrains the output, but the model's internal machinery — trained on billions of images — selects the specific lines, colors, textures, and forms. That is not the same as pulling a handle and getting a result you could have anticipated. It is more like asking a very fast, very strange collaborator to make something for you and then choosing which of the results you like.

The U.S. Copyright Office's 2023 guidance and its 2025 copyrightability report both center this distinction: copyright attaches to human creative contribution, and material generated by AI may need to be disclaimed in registration contexts. In *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, courts affirmed refusal of copyright registration for work identified as autonomously generated by an AI system. The reasoning is not that AI is bad or that its outputs are worthless. The reasoning is that copyright was designed to incentivize human creativity, and extending it to machine output would require a different theory entirely — one Congress has not enacted.

The practical question, then, is not philosophical. It is: *who determined the expressive elements in this specific deliverable?* If the answer is mostly the human — who drew the mark, built the letterforms, selected and arranged elements creatively, edited AI output into something genuinely different — the claim to authorship is defensible. If the answer is mostly the model — prompt only, select one output, export — the claim is much weaker. And weaker does not mean untrue. It means you should not be promising assignment until you have thought carefully about what you actually own.

The test I use: can I describe, specifically, which expressive choices were mine? Not "I chose this one from forty outputs." That is curation, not authorship. Not "I wrote a detailed prompt." A prompt constrains the output space; it does not determine the expressive elements. The question is whether I can point to specific creative decisions — this curve, this weight, this relationship between forms — and say: I made that choice, and I can reproduce it, and here is the source file that shows my work.

If I can, authorship is real. If I cannot, I am promising something I have not established.

## The Platform Layer and Why "Commercial Use" Is Not Enough

Understanding what you own in a work requires understanding what the platform gave you. These are different questions, and conflating them is how designers end up in trouble.

Platform terms govern use rights. Copyright law governs authorship. A platform can grant you broad commercial use rights without granting you copyright — and without those rights constituting copyright, because only copyright protects against third-party claims and enables client assignment.

Consider the range of tools currently in use. Adobe Firefly markets itself around training data sourced from licensed Adobe Stock and public-domain material, and offers indemnification options for some enterprise customers. That is a meaningfully better posture than many alternatives. But it does not dissolve the human authorship question, and enterprise indemnification terms vary — what is covered, for what uses, under what conditions is something you read in the actual agreement, not infer from marketing. Midjourney's terms grant users rights subject to plan and conditions, but "commercial use allowed" and "copyright safely assignable" are not the same sentence. The litigation landscape — Getty Images versus Stability AI is one ongoing example — shows that training-data and output-similarity questions remain active and jurisdiction-dependent.

The hierarchy I work with runs roughly like this: lower risk when using enterprise tools with clear training-data provenance and available indemnification, where the output is rebuilt or heavily modified by human hands and documented. Higher risk when the final client deliverable is generated directly from any AI tool without human reconstruction, regardless of platform, because the authorship question and the assignment promise then rest on the weakest possible foundation. Open or custom workflows using unclear models, unverified LoRAs, or web-trained material sit at the highest risk end, because the license, training-data provenance, and output-similarity issues are all uncertain simultaneously.

The principle cutting across all of this: *"commercial use allowed" means you can use it in your business. It does not mean you can promise copyright ownership to a client.* Those are different legal relationships, and mixing them up turns a platform permission question into a client contract problem.

## What the Contract Actually Has to Do

The standard design agreement assumes you own everything in the final deliverable. It was written in a world where that assumption was almost always true — where the designer built the mark, controlled every expressive element, and could trace ownership clearly from their hands to the client's. AI changes the assumption, which means the contract needs updating.

The mechanism is a rider — a set of clauses added to your existing agreement that address what older agreements left silent. I think of it as five obligations: what you disclose, what tools you use, what you warrant about authorship, how you allocate risk for third-party claims, and how you protect the client's confidential material from improper tool use.

<!-- → [TABLE: 5-Clause AI Rider — columns: clause name, plain-language purpose, what it addresses that older agreements miss] -->

The disclosure clause says when and how AI tools may be used. The tool clause defines which platforms are permitted and, critically, what client material may be uploaded — because uploading a client's confidential brand strategy to a system that trains on user inputs is a confidentiality problem before it is an IP problem. The human-authorship warranty limits what you are actually promising: not that everything is copyrightable, but that the human-authored elements are yours to assign. The third-party claims clause allocates risk if an AI-related claim arises after delivery — who bears the cost of defending it, and under what conditions. The confidentiality and retention clause protects client material from AI systems that retain or repurpose inputs for model improvement.

What this looks like in practice: your old assignment clause promised to transfer "all right, title, and interest in final deliverables." Your new clause promises to transfer all right, title, and interest in the human-authored elements of the final deliverables, and discloses the presence of AI-generated material where it may affect ownership, registration, or public representation. The client knows what they are getting. You have promised only what you can give. The rider narrows the promise to what you can responsibly stand behind.

One thing the rider cannot do: make uncopyrightable material copyrightable. Contract language manages expectations, disclosure, and risk allocation. It does not create legal rights that do not exist. If the logo has no human-authored expressive elements, no contract clause changes that. What the clause does is prevent the client from discovering this after they have paid and tried to register, because you told them in writing, and they agreed.

## What to Do About Past Work

The discovery of this problem often comes after delivery, not before. A designer who has been using AI tools for a year and has a standard assignment clause in every contract will, at some point, have a client ask for registration support and find themselves explaining a situation they were not prepared for.

The right sequence here is document, diagnose, communicate carefully. Not panic. Not conceal. Not volunteer legal conclusions you cannot support.

Documenting means identifying which past deliverables included AI-generated material, checking whether that material appears in final client assets, locating whatever source files and process records exist, and identifying the human modifications made. Diagnosing means reviewing the contract promise against what you now know about what you actually own. Communicating means, where ownership or registration matters, referring the client to counsel rather than making legal determinations yourself.

Most of the time, the practical problem is smaller than the anxiety. Many clients have paid for a logo and used it without ever attempting registration. Many AI-generated assets appear in deliverables alongside substantial human-authored work and the authorship question is actually defensible on examination. The cases where the problem is serious are the ones where the client is actively trying to register or enforce, where the AI-generated material is the whole deliverable with no human modification, and where the contract promise was unconditional assignment. Those cases need counsel. The rest need documentation and honesty.

The lesson of the three thousand dollar logo is not that AI tools cannot be used in client work. They can. The lesson is that using them responsibly means understanding what they do to the ownership question and adjusting your contracts and your process before the client asks. The nemo dat principle does not change with technology. It just reveals new ways to test it.

## LLM Exercises

**Exercise 1 — Generate and Examine**

Prompt an AI image generator with a detailed brief for a logo mark. Then ask the model to describe, in plain language, which expressive choices it made in producing the output. Compare that description to what you could document as your own human contributions. Where does the boundary fall?

**Exercise 2 — Apply to Known Context**

Take your current client agreement's IP assignment clause and paste it into a conversation with an LLM. Ask the model to identify which scenarios, specific to AI-assisted design work, the clause fails to address. Use the output as a diagnostic checklist against the 5-Clause AI Rider framework.

**Exercise 3 — Stress-Test a Specific Claim**

Tell the LLM: "I used Midjourney to generate a logo, selected the best output, made minor color adjustments in Illustrator, and delivered the PNG and a PDF to the client under a standard assignment clause. What is my exposure?" Ask it to argue the client's position. Then ask it to argue yours. Identify where the actual dispute would center.

**Exercise 4 — Draft or Audit a Professional Deliverable**

Using the 5-Clause AI Rider structure from this chapter, draft the human-authorship warranty limit clause and the confidentiality and retention clause for your own practice. Specify which AI tools you currently use, what client material those tools may or may not receive, and what you are and are not warranting about the authorship of your outputs. Flag every sentence that should go to counsel before relying on it.
