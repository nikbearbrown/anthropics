# Chapter 7 — Taste, Accountability, and the Decisions Only You Can Make

*You are not the person who makes the most images. You are the person who owns the right decisions.*

There is a question that arrives quietly, usually when you are alone with the work and the deadline is close.

You are looking at a brand refresh that came partly from prompts, partly from curation, partly from manual edits, partly from your own sense of what was right. It looks good. The client will probably approve it. And then the question surfaces, not dramatically, just there: is this still design?

The instinct is to treat this as a philosophical question — something to do with authenticity, or originality, or the purity of the creative act. It isn't. It is a practical question with a practical answer. Can you explain, defend, revise, and take responsibility for every load-bearing decision in this piece? If yes, this is design. If no, you have been forwarding machine output with your name on it.

The distinction matters because the client isn't buying an image. They are buying your judgment. When something goes wrong — when the mark breaks at small size, when the concept turns out to be generic, when the campaign image creates an unexpected association — the client will come back to you, not to the model. The responsibility was never the model's to begin with. It was always yours. The question is whether you actually did the work that makes that responsibility defensible.

## Addition Is Easy. Subtraction Is the Work.

Generative AI is extraordinarily good at addition. Given a prompt, it produces more: more variations, more styles, more directions, more textures, more options. This is genuinely useful, and the designers who have learned to use it well know how to set up that generative pass to widen the field before they narrow it. The problem is that widening the field is not the hard part of design. The hard part is narrowing it.

Experienced design work depends heavily on subtraction. Fewer elements. Clearer hierarchy. Sharper meaning. Stronger restraint. The decision to remove something is almost always harder than the decision to add it, because adding feels like progress and removing feels like loss. What you are actually doing when you subtract well is clarifying — you are forcing the remaining elements to carry the weight that the removed ones were diffusing.

This is where taste enters, and I want to be precise about what I mean by taste because the word carries baggage. Pierre Bourdieu spent a career showing that what passes for aesthetic taste is often socially inherited — a set of preferences shaped by class, education, and cultural capital that gets mistaken for universal judgment. That warning is real and worth holding. "Good taste" as a personal preference is not a professional standard. But there is a more specific and more defensible version: taste as the trained ability to choose what fits this client, this audience, this medium, and this risk better than the available alternatives. That kind of taste is not inherited. It is developed through practice, failure, feedback, and the discipline of actually having to defend choices to people who paid for them.

Your taste, in this sense, is visible not only in what you generate but in what you remove. The designer who generates forty directions and presents three has exercised taste in the gap between forty and three. The designer who generates forty directions and presents thirty-eight has not. They have outsourced the judgment to the client, which is a different kind of failure than the fluency trap but a related one — it substitutes volume for decision.

## The Four Questions

There is a practical test for whether you have done the work that makes accountability defensible. Four questions. If the answer to any of them is no, the deliverable is not ready — not bad, not unrecoverable, but not ready.

Can you explain the decision? Not "it felt right" and not "the model suggested it." The explanation does not need to be elaborate. It needs to be specific enough that someone else could understand what you were trying to achieve and why you chose this means over the alternatives. "I used a restrained grid and a warmer neutral palette because the client needs to feel established without feeling corporate" is an explanation. "It looked more premium" is not.

Can you defend it against a reasonable objection? A reasonable objection is not a hostile client demanding you justify everything from first principles. It is someone asking: why this, and not that? Why this typeface and not the other one? Why this mark and not the direction with more energy? If you do not have an answer — if the real answer is that the model generated this one and you liked how it looked — then you are not defending a decision. You are defending a preference you cannot ground.

Can you revise it intentionally? This is the test of ownership that goes beyond explanation. A design system you can explain but cannot revise is a system you observed, not one you built. Intentional revision means you can change a specific element in a specific way to achieve a specific effect, and you can predict roughly what the result will look like before you make the change. If the only revision strategy is to re-prompt and hope the output moves in the right direction, the system is not yours.

Can you take responsibility if it fails? This is the hardest question because it requires imagining the failure case before it happens. If the mark becomes associated with something the client didn't intend. If the campaign image creates a legal problem. If the type choice turns out to be illegible in the primary use context. Can you stand behind the process that produced this? If you would need to say "well, the AI generated it and I thought it looked fine," you are not in a position to take professional responsibility. If you can say "I chose this direction because of these reasons, I tested it against these constraints, and I missed this failure mode" — that is a professional account of a professional process, even if the process included a failure.

Donald Schon argued that professional expertise is not the application of known rules to clear cases — it is the ability to think in action, to learn from the situation as it unfolds, and to revise judgment in real time. The creative accountability test is not a checklist you complete after the work is done. It is a discipline you practice while the work is happening. Accountability is not added to design. It is part of design.

## Where Accountability Is Load-Bearing

Not every decision in a design project carries the same weight. The choice between two very similar shades of warm gray is not load-bearing in the same way as the choice of visual metaphor or the interpretation of a revision note. Knowing where accountability is truly load-bearing is part of professional judgment.

The moments that carry weight are the ones where, if you cannot explain and defend the decision, something important breaks. Direction selection: when you choose which of the explored territories to develop, you are making a bet about what the client needs. If that bet is wrong and you cannot account for how you made it, the revision process becomes guesswork on both sides. Type choice: type carries meaning beyond legibility, and a choice you cannot explain is a choice you cannot defend when the client asks why the brand feels different from what they expected. Visual metaphor: when the brand is built around an idea — stability, navigation, warmth, precision — the visual choices either support that idea or they undermine it. If you cannot articulate the idea, you cannot evaluate whether the choices support it.

Client presentation is load-bearing because it is the moment where your account of the work becomes the client's understanding of what they are buying. If your account is vague — "the AI explored this direction and I refined it" — the client learns that you are a refiner, which is a less accountable role than the one you were hired to fill. Revision interpretation is load-bearing because how you understand what the client is asking for when they say "make it feel warmer" or "this doesn't feel right yet" determines whether the next iteration solves the problem or misses it again. Final sign-off and disclosure are load-bearing in ways the preceding chapters have already described.

The general principle is this: the more a decision affects meaning, trust, or risk, the more it must be yours. AI tools can produce options around every one of these moments. They can suggest typeface pairings, generate metaphor directions, summarize revision notes, produce presentation layouts. But the judgment that selects, modifies, and commits to the final answer has to attach to a person with professional accountability. That person is you.

## The Rationale as Proof of Work

There is a practical artifact that tests whether you have done the accountability work: the creative rationale. Not the mood board. Not the presentation deck. Not the prompt history. The spoken or written argument for why the final direction is right for this specific client.

A rationale that actually works has a specific structure. It starts with the client's problem — not the design problem, the business problem. What was the client trying to achieve or avoid? Then it names the strategic choice: what territory did you decide to work in, and why that territory and not another? Then the visual decision: what specific elements express that strategic choice, and why these elements rather than others? Then the AI's role, honestly described: what did generative tools contribute, and at what stage? Then the human revision: what did you change, remove, or rebuild, and why? And finally the expected effect: what should the client experience when they encounter this system in the world?

A rationale built this way does several things simultaneously. It proves that a person made decisions, which is what the client is paying for. It gives the client a framework for evaluating the work beyond "I like it" or "I don't like it." It protects you when revision requests arrive, because both you and the client are working from a shared account of what the direction is trying to achieve. And it prepares you for the accountability questions this chapter has been building toward: if you can say the rationale out loud clearly, you can answer all four accountability questions.

The prompt is not a rationale. A prompt explains what you asked the model for. A rationale explains why the final decision is right. These are different claims, and the difference matters professionally. "I prompted for a warm institutional brand with restrained typography" is an account of a tool interaction. "I chose a restrained grid and a warmer neutral palette because the client needs to feel established without feeling corporate, and the more decorative directions weakened the sense of stability the advisory role depends on" is a professional argument.

The test I use: if you cannot say the rationale out loud without hedging or vagueness, the decision is not ready for the client. Not because the client will necessarily interrogate you — most clients never ask directly. But because the inability to say it is diagnostic. It means the decision is still a selection rather than a judgment, and selections are not the same as design.

## Who the Designer Is Now

The question of professional identity is real, even if it sounds grandiose. When AI tools can produce polished, fluent, aesthetically coherent work at the push of a prompt, what exactly is the designer doing?

The answer that I find most honest — and most professionally useful — is not about tool purity. You are not more of a designer because you refuse AI. The designer who generates nothing and agonizes over every decision is not automatically producing better work than the designer who uses generative tools intelligently. And you are not less of a designer because you use AI heavily. The work is not judged by the cleanliness of the process. It is judged by whether a person with professional accountability made the decisions that matter.

Nigel Cross spent decades arguing that design is a distinct form of knowing — not science (which discovers what exists), not the humanities (which interpret human meaning), but something irreducibly its own: the ability to create what does not yet exist and to evaluate that creation against criteria that are partly given and partly invented in the act of designing. What generative AI has not changed, and cannot change in any near-term version, is the need for a person to hold the criteria. The criteria — what this client needs, what this audience will trust, what this medium can carry, what this risk requires — are not in the training data. They are in the relationship, the conversation, the history, and the judgment of a professional who has developed the ability to read a situation and make a bet on it.

The U.S. Copyright Office's recent guidance on AI-assisted work keeps returning to the question of human creative contribution not because copyright law is the right frame for design practice, but because it is pointing at something real: when rights, accountability, and professional responsibility are at stake, they attach to the person who made the choices, not to the system that generated the options. That is not a legal technicality. It is a description of how professional accountability actually works.

## A Full Pass

Let me show what this looks like in practice by walking through a complete project in the register of the accountability test, not as a case study with a lesson at the end, but as an account of a process with the load-bearing decisions visible.

A long-term client needs a brand refresh. They have grown from a founder-led consultancy into a serious advisory firm. The old identity is warm, founder-coded, slightly informal. They want to feel more established without losing the warmth that differentiates them from the large corporate firms they now compete with.

I use generative tools to explore three territories: editorial authority, warm institutional, and strategic navigation. The model produces polished directions in each. The editorial direction looks beautiful and completely wrong — it reads as publishing, not advisory. The warm institutional direction is credible and generic, indistinguishable from a dozen other professional services firms. Strategic navigation has something, but the generated marks are full of arrows, compass roses, map lines, path icons. Every navigation cliché is present.

The subtractive pass removes all of it. Arrows. Gradients. Map lines. Iconography that points literally at the concept rather than embodying it. What survives is an idea: orientation under uncertainty. Not navigation toward a known destination, which is what the clichés were depicting. The actual service this firm provides is helping clients think clearly when the next step is genuinely unclear. The visual expression of that is different from a compass.

I build a simple modular line system from scratch in vector. It can become a mark, a layout grid, and a presentation device, which means the system has rules rather than just a style. The palette moves from the model's default blue-green — which reads as technology optimism — to charcoal, off-white, and a muted clay accent that reads as calm authority.

Now the accountability test. Can I explain it? Yes: orientation under uncertainty, expressed through a modular structure that has authority without rigidity. Can I defend it? Yes: every rejected direction has a specific reason that connects to what the client needs and what the category doesn't need more of. Can I revise it? Yes: the system is vector-built, rule-based, and documented so that revisions to the mark, the palette, or the grid have a logic to follow. Can I take responsibility if it fails? Yes: the decisions are mine and I can account for each of them, including the failure modes I can identify and the ones I may have missed.

The rationale for the client is not a summary of this analysis. It is a professional argument: "The refresh is built around orientation, not decoration. Your clients come to you when the next decision is unclear. The new system uses a measured line structure to suggest direction under uncertainty. I explored several territories at the beginning, but the final mark, type, palette, and system rules were selected and rebuilt manually. I removed the more literal navigation symbols because they made the brand feel like software. This direction keeps the warmth of the old brand while giving you a more authoritative system for advisory work."

The model helped widen the field. I made the decisions that matter.

## What Would Change My Account

I would revise this chapter if clients, courts, and professional outcomes all converged on a standard where responsibility for AI-assisted creative decisions no longer needed to attach to a human professional — if the field developed workable frameworks for assigning accountability to workflows rather than people. That is not inconceivable. Professional accountability is a social arrangement, not a natural law, and social arrangements change.

I would also revise it if AI systems developed the ability to explain, defend, revise, and accept accountability for design decisions in a way that clients recognized as equivalent to professional practice. The four accountability questions are my current formulation of what professional judgment requires. If a system could genuinely answer all four — not simulate answering them, but actually ground the answers in the client's specific situation and risk — the argument of this chapter would need to be rethought.

## Still-Open Questions

How design education will teach taste in an environment where students can generate polished options before they have developed judgment is a genuine problem that I do not think the field has answered yet. The traditional pedagogy depended on the difficulty of making things: you had to make a lot of bad work before you could make good work, and the making was where the judgment developed. If the generation is easy, the development of judgment has to come from somewhere else — from critique, from failure analysis, from exactly the kind of accountability practice this chapter describes. But whether that substitution actually works at scale is an open question.

The portfolio question remains open too. Mixed AI/human authorship is now so common that "I designed this" covers an enormous range of actual process. The field will eventually develop more precise language, but what that language will be — and whether it will require active disclosure or simply invite it — is not settled.

And the question of which kinds of taste are professional judgment and which are inherited bias is one I want to sit with rather than resolve quickly. Bourdieu's warning is not dissolved by defining taste more narrowly. The trained ability to choose what fits a client, audience, medium, and risk is real, but it is also shaped by whose clients, whose audiences, whose mediums, and whose risk assessments have been considered worthy of training on. That is a live problem in design practice, not just in design theory.

## LLM Exercises

**Exercise 1 — Generate and Examine**

Use an AI tool to generate three visual directions for a real or hypothetical client brief. Then apply the subtractive pass: remove every element that is decorative, generic, or borrowed from category convention. What survives? Write a sentence naming the surviving idea. If nothing survives, name what was missing from the brief or the generation that would have produced something worth keeping.

**Exercise 2 — Apply to Known Context**

Take one AI-assisted deliverable from your own practice and apply the four-question accountability test. Write one honest sentence for each answer. If any answer is no, identify specifically what is missing — is it the explanation, the defense, the revision capability, or the willingness to take responsibility? — and write what you would need to do to move the answer to yes.

**Exercise 3 — Stress-Test a Claim**

The chapter claims that "a prompt is not a rationale." Test this claim on a real project. Write the prompt you used (or would have used) to generate the work. Then write the creative rationale for the final deliverable using the six-part structure. Read both aloud. What does the prompt tell the client that the rationale doesn't? What does the rationale tell the client that the prompt doesn't? Does the gap between them support or complicate the chapter's claim?

**Exercise 4 — Draft or Audit a Professional Deliverable**

Either (a) write a complete creative rationale for one AI-assisted deliverable in your portfolio — under 300 words, six parts, in client-meeting language — and then run it through the four accountability questions to test whether the rationale actually proves what it claims, or (b) identify the three most load-bearing decisions in a current project, write a one-sentence accountability statement for each, and flag any decision where you could not write the statement without hedging. Those are the decisions that need more human work before delivery.
