# Chapter 5 — Client Disclosure: When, How, and What to Say

*Disclosure is not confession. It is professional framing.*

There is a moment that every designer who uses AI tools will eventually face.

The work is going well. The client likes the direction. The mockups landed. The image style is exactly what the brief asked for. And then, in the middle of the review, the client turns to you and asks: "Did you use AI for any of this?"

What happens in the next ten seconds matters more than almost anything else in the engagement. Not because the answer is difficult — the answer is usually straightforward — but because the way you answer reveals whether you understand what disclosure actually is.

Most designers, the first time this happens, experience something like low-grade panic. They say something vague. "Well, everyone uses AI now." Or something evasive: "Not really, I mean, in the sense that..." Or something worse, something that sounds reassuring but isn't: "Yes, but don't worry, it's completely safe." None of these is an answer. They are exits from the conversation, and the client notices.

The panic comes from a misunderstanding about what disclosure requires. Designers treat it as confession — as though naming AI involvement is an admission of having cheated, cut corners, or delivered something lesser. It isn't. Disclosure is professional framing. It is the act of giving your client an accurate account of what happened in your process so they can make informed decisions about the work. That is not humiliating. It is what professionals do.

The client who asks "did you use AI?" is almost never asking you to apologize. They are asking you to tell them what they are buying, what risks they are carrying, and whether they can trust what they are about to approve. Those are reasonable questions. They deserve a real answer.

## The Question Behind the Question

Before you can answer well, you need to understand what the client is actually asking. Because "did you use AI?" is almost never the real question.

The real question, most of the time, is one of three things. Sometimes it is curiosity — the client has been hearing about AI and wants to understand how their designers are working. Sometimes it is concern — they have read something about copyright, or their legal team flagged it, or they work in a regulated field and they need to know whether there is a risk they haven't accounted for. And sometimes, when the question comes late in an engagement, it is something closer to frustration: they feel they should have known sooner, and they are right.

Each of these questions has a different structure, and responding well means responding to the actual question, not the surface one. The curious client needs an explanation. The concerned client needs a risk assessment. The frustrated client needs an acknowledgment and a path forward. All three need specificity. None of them need a philosophical discussion about AI in the creative industries.

What they all share is this: they are asking whether they can trust the account you're giving them of your own process. That is why vague answers are so corrosive. Vagueness doesn't reduce suspicion — it increases it. A client who hears "well, everyone uses AI now" learns only that you are uncomfortable with the question. That is exactly the wrong signal.

## When Disclosure Is Required

Not every use of an AI tool requires a client conversation. A designer who runs spell-check through an AI-enhanced writing assistant, or who uses AI to generate a rough meeting summary for internal notes, is not withholding material information by not disclosing those uses. The client is not affected. The deliverable is not changed. No right, risk, or expectation is altered.

The question that determines whether disclosure is required is not "did I use AI?" It is "does my AI use change what the client is buying, risking, or representing?"

Three sub-questions make this concrete.

The first is the deliverable test: did AI-generated material appear in the final work? Not in exploration, not in a draft you threw out, but in what you are handing over. If the answer is yes, the client needs to know. They may be registering that work, assigning rights to it, or publicly representing it as original. The U.S. Copyright Office now requires applicants to identify AI-generated material in registration contexts — which means hidden AI content in a final deliverable can become a rights problem the client didn't know they were carrying.

The second is the rights and risk test: could the AI use affect ownership, registration, confidentiality, brand safety, or any public claim the client will make? This question catches cases where the deliverable looks clean but the process created exposure. A logo element refined from AI-generated geometry may be in a gray zone for trademark registration. A campaign image that includes an AI face composite may carry likeness risks the client hasn't considered. These are not hypotheticals — they are the kinds of questions clients' legal teams are now asking routinely.

The third is the expectation test: would a reasonable client feel misled if they learned about the AI use later? This is the hardest to apply because it requires genuine judgment about the relationship and the context. But it is also the most important, because it is the one that governs situations the other two don't explicitly cover. A client who hired you for your hand-crafted aesthetic and received an AI-assisted deliverable without knowing it has a legitimate grievance even if no rights were affected. The expectation test catches that.

If any of these three questions points toward yes, disclose. Not because the rules require it in every jurisdiction — they don't, yet — but because disclosure is what protects the relationship.

## The Four Situations

It helps to think about disclosure in terms of four distinct situations, because the right response in each is different.

The first situation is AI as internal support: grammar cleanup, internal brainstorming, summarizing a brief you're working from. The client's work never left your process in an AI-mediated form. The deliverable is wholly yours. In most cases, disclosure here is unnecessary — the AI use is no more material than the fact that you used a word processor. Unless your contract explicitly requires it, or your client works in a field with heightened AI sensitivity, you can treat this the same way you treat other invisible process infrastructure.

The second situation is AI in exploration that didn't make it to the final deliverable. You generated forty moodboard directions to find three worth developing. You tested typeface pairings with an AI layout tool. None of this appears in the final work. Here the answer is: document it internally, and if asked, be honest. You don't need to volunteer it, but you shouldn't conceal it either. The standard is that you could give a clear account of your process if the client asked.

The third situation is AI material in the final deliverable. This is where disclosure shifts from optional to required. An AI-generated image appearing in a campaign. A logo element derived from generated geometry and rebuilt in vector. A synthetic product mockup used in a client presentation as representative photography. When AI output reaches the deliverable — even transformed, even refined — the client needs to know before they approve. Not because the work is necessarily worse, but because they are now carrying whatever rights and risk questions attach to that material, and they can't make an informed decision without knowing what they're carrying.

The fourth situation is the use of confidential client material with an AI tool. This is the most serious. Uploading a client's unreleased strategy document, customer data, proprietary brand guidelines, or confidential brief to a tool that trains on user inputs without disclosure is a potential confidentiality breach, regardless of what the final deliverable looks like. Many client contracts now explicitly prohibit this. The right response here is not disclosure after the fact — it is pre-clearance before the use, or avoiding the use entirely.

## What a Disclosure Statement Actually Looks Like

The designers who handle this badly usually make one of two mistakes. Either they say too little — "I used some AI tools" — which tells the client nothing actionable. Or they say too much — a lengthy preemptive explanation that raises more questions than it answers and signals anxiety rather than control.

A disclosure statement that works has four components. What AI was used for. What AI was not used for. What human decisions shaped the final work. What safeguards were applied.

Those four things, in plain language, in under a hundred words, give the client everything they need. Not a legal brief. Not a philosophical position on generative tools. An account.

Here is what that looks like in practice: *I used AI tools for early visual exploration and presentation layout. The final mark, type system, and palette decisions are mine, built around your specific brief and constraints. I did not use AI to generate the deliverable logo or upload any confidential materials. All exploration was kept in internal drafts.*

That statement is specific enough to answer the rights question, the risk question, and the trust question simultaneously. It does not apologize. It does not hedge. It describes a professional process with professional clarity.

The passive voice is worth avoiding here. "AI was used" conceals responsibility. The client needs to know that you made decisions — that a person with accountability and judgment was directing the process. "I used AI to widen the range, then narrowed and rebuilt the work around your brief" is a different claim than "AI was used in early stages." The first sentence has an agent. That agent is you.

## Having the Conversation

The three client questions arrive in distinct registers, and it is worth thinking through each one before you face it live.

The curious client is the easiest. They have heard about AI, they are not alarmed, and they want to understand how their designers are working. The answer here is generous and informative: yes, here is what it did, here is what I did, here is why the combination served your project. The goal is to leave the client feeling that they have a designer who knows how to use powerful tools intelligently, which is exactly what you want them to think.

The concerned client is asking because something made them nervous — a news story, a legal memo, a conversation with their IP counsel. The answer here is precise and risk-focused. You need to tell them specifically what AI material is in the final deliverable, what AI material was used only in exploration and is not present in the final work, and what the rights status of the final deliverable actually is. If you don't know the answer to one of those questions, say so and offer to find out. Confident vagueness is worse than honest uncertainty.

The frustrated client — the one who feels they should have known earlier — requires a different kind of response. This client is not asking about AI in the abstract. They are telling you that they experienced a trust breach, even if no material harm resulted. The right response acknowledges that directly. Not with excessive apology, but with the specific correction: here is what happened, here is what the process actually was, here is what I will do differently so the boundary is explicit from the start of the next project. Then follow through by actually adding an AI-use clause to your working agreement.

## The Timing Problem

Most disclosure failures are not failures of content — they are failures of timing. The designer knows what to say. They just say it too late.

The principle here is straightforward: if late disclosure would feel like bad news, disclose earlier. And the corollary is that the right time to establish disclosure norms is before the project begins, not in the middle of a client review.

Proactive disclosure — naming your AI workflow at the start of an engagement — does several things at once. It sets accurate expectations. It gives the client the chance to flag any contractual restrictions before they become violations. It positions you as someone with a considered, professional relationship to these tools rather than someone who uses them furtively and hopes no one asks. And it transforms the "did you use AI?" question from a potential crisis into a point of confirmation.

The Federal Trade Commission has been explicit that businesses should not make deceptive or unsubstantiated claims about their products and processes, including AI-assisted ones. For designers, the practical translation is simple: don't let your process representation drift away from what actually happened. Not because the FTC is auditing creative freelancers, but because the same principle applies to any professional relationship built on accurate representation.

Regulators aside, the market is moving toward disclosure as baseline expectation. The clients who are already asking "did you use AI?" are ahead of a question that will soon be standard. The designers who have built clear, professional disclosure practices now will navigate that shift easily. The designers who have been avoiding the conversation will face a harder version of it later.

## What Disclosure Cannot Fix

I want to be honest about the limits here, because overstating what disclosure does would be its own kind of error.

Disclosure is a communication act, not a remediation act. If confidential client materials were uploaded to a tool that trains on user inputs without authorization, telling the client about it after the fact does not undo the breach. If AI-generated material appears in a final deliverable in a way that creates a genuine rights problem, disclosing that fact puts the client in possession of the problem, but it does not solve the problem. Disclosure is what you owe the client so they can make decisions. It is not a substitute for having made the right decisions in the first place.

## What Would Change My Account

I would revise this chapter if a clear, widely adopted design-industry standard emerged that defined AI disclosure thresholds differently from the materiality approach here. The three-part test — deliverable, rights and risk, expectation — is my best current formulation of a principle that isn't yet codified in professional guidelines. If AIGA, the Graphic Artists Guild, or a comparable body issued specific guidance that drew the lines differently, I'd want to revisit where the line sits.

I would also revise if client research showed that proactive disclosure consistently harms trust even when the AI use is bounded, documented, and professionally framed. My current view is that vague concealment harms trust more than clear disclosure does. If the evidence ran the other way — if clients systematically reacted worse to honest AI-use framing than to finding out later — the strategy would need rethinking.

## Still-Open Questions

Some things remain genuinely unresolved.

The most interesting is whether clients will eventually normalize AI process disclosures the way they normalized stock photography. There was a period when using a stock image without disclosure felt like a professional failure. Now it is unremarkable. It is not obvious to me whether AI-assisted design will follow the same trajectory — the rights questions are more complex, and the substitution questions run deeper — but the possibility seems real.

The portfolio question is also open. How much AI use should be disclosed in portfolio case studies? The professional portfolio makes implicit claims about what you can do. If AI tools did a substantial part of a project, what is the accurate way to represent that work as evidence of your capability? The field has not worked this out yet.

And contracts remain fragmented. Some clients now have explicit AI-use clauses; most don't. Whether standard AI-use language will emerge in design contracts — analogous to the way IP assignment clauses became standard — is genuinely unclear. My guess is that it will, and probably faster than anyone expects, driven by the same insurance and legal-review pressures that standardized other IP language. But that is a guess, not a documented trend.

**Exercise 1 — Generate and Examine**

Prompt an AI tool to draft an AI disclosure statement for a hypothetical design project of your choosing (brand identity, campaign, editorial). Examine the output: does it pass the four-part test — what AI did, what AI didn't do, what human decisions shaped the work, what safeguards were used? Identify what the generated statement gets right and what it leaves out or obscures.

**Exercise 2 — Apply to Known Context**

Review your last three AI-assisted deliverables against the materiality threshold's three-part test (deliverable test, rights/risk test, expectation test). For each project, write one sentence per test. Did any of the three projects require disclosure that you did not provide at the time? What would you say now if the client asked?

**Exercise 3 — Stress-Test a Claim**

The chapter claims that "vagueness doesn't reduce suspicion — it increases it." Test this claim by writing two versions of a disclosure response to the same client question: one vague ("I used some AI tools in the process") and one specific (using the four-part template). Show both to a colleague or a client and ask which one they found more reassuring and why. Does the result support or complicate the chapter's claim?

**Exercise 4 — Draft or Audit a Professional Deliverable**

Either (a) write a complete proactive AI disclosure statement for your current practice — under 100 words, four parts, in your own professional voice — and test it against the three client question types in the chapter, or (b) write a short AI-use clause suitable for your standard client agreement, covering: what AI tools may be used for, what they will not be used for, and what disclosure the client will receive before final approval of any deliverable containing AI-generated material.
