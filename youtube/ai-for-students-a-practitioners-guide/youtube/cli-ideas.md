## Candidate 01 — "Measure the Fluency Trap: Predicted vs. Actual Recall After AI-Assisted Study"
- Source: ai-for-students-a-practitioners-guide/chapters/02-the-fluency-trap-why-ai-makes-you-feel-smarter-while-making-you-less-so.md (LLM Exercise)
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: A student spends three nights on a history paper, uses AI to draft two paragraphs, feels like he understands the period better than ever. Two weeks later he cannot name either historian in his own argument. The calibration gap — predicted recall vs. actual recall — is real, measurable, and predictable. The fluency trap always fires in the same direction.
- The artifact: A Manim animated bar chart showing three study conditions (answer visible, AI-drafted text, retrieval-practice without AI) with two bars per condition: predicted recall (what the student felt after studying) and actual recall at a one-week delay. The gap between the bars grows from retrieval-practice (minimal gap) to AI-assisted (large gap). An animated "illusion of competence" callout appears on the AI-assisted bars.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Produce a dataset for a calibration-gap bar chart: three study conditions (answer-visible, AI-assisted, retrieval-practice). For each condition: predicted_recall (%), actual_recall_at_1_week (%). Use research-grounded estimates: Koriat and Bjork (1994) for answer-visible calibration error, Bastani et al. (2024) for AI-assisted condition (students scored 17pp lower after unguarded AI study), Karpicke and Roediger (2008) for retrieval practice (80% retention at one week). Output as JSON: {conditions: [{name, predicted, actual, gap}]}."` then render Manim animated bar chart with gap annotations.
- Read / check: Verify the AI-assisted condition shows the largest gap (predicted > actual by at least 15pp); verify retrieval practice shows the smallest gap; verify the JSON cites the correct studies; verify the Manim chart renders all three conditions with dual bars side by side.
- Human supplies: Nothing — fully synthetic using published research estimates. Manim installed.
- Output medium: Manim (animated) — bars growing per condition, gap callout animating in on AI-assisted bars, "illusion of competence" label appearing.
- The change: Add a fourth condition: AI-assisted PLUS self-testing (close AI, try to recall from scratch). Show the gap shrink compared to pure AI-assisted — demonstrating that the gate placement (when you close the AI) is the variable.
- Teardown angle: The metacognitive system reads fluency, not depth. AI output is the most fluent text the brain has ever encountered. The calibration gap is not a moral failing — it is a feature of a system that was built when fluency correlated with understanding.
- Exclusions: Full neurochemistry of dopamine/reward lecture, spaced repetition algorithms, Dunning-Kruger effect (a different mechanism).
- Score: 10/10

## Candidate 02 — "Build a Phase Gate for a Research Project: Write It Down Before You Start"
- Source: ai-for-students-a-practitioners-guide/chapters/04-the-phase-gate-when-to-open-the-ai-and-when-to-close-it.md (LLM Exercise)
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: "AI handles X. I handle Y. The gate is at moment Z." If you cannot write those three things down for the task in front of you, you do not have a gate. You have a vague intention — and vague intentions lose to the path of least resistance, which is always AI doing everything.
- The artifact: A Claude-generated phase gate specification for a student research project, validated by a Python gate check: (a) the gate sentence contains all three components (AI handles / I handle / gate is at), (b) the "I handle" clause names a cognitive act (not a task like "find sources"), (c) the gate moment is time-stamped or milestone-anchored (not "when I feel ready"). Output: PASS/FAIL per criterion, then the gate printed as a formatted commitment card the viewer can screenshot.
- Prompt seed: `claude "I am working on [project description]. Write a phase gate for my AI use: a single sentence of the form 'AI handles X. I handle Y. The gate is at moment Z.' Constraints: X must be a mechanical task, Y must be a cognitive act that I cannot delegate (analysis, interpretation, argument formation, judgment), Z must be a specific milestone or time constraint — not 'when I feel ready.' Then validate your own gate: does the 'I handle' clause name something I can demonstrate independently? Would a skeptic accept the gate?"` then `python3 phase_gate_check.py gate.txt`
- Read / check: Verify the gate check fails when the "I handle" clause is "I check it" (not demonstrable); verify it fails when the gate moment is "when I feel I understand it"; verify it passes when I handle is "I form my own interpretation of the primary sources" and the gate is "after I have read all three UNHCR reports."
- Human supplies: A real project the viewer is working on. The gate is only useful when built for a real project — synthetic is NOT acceptable; the commitment must be genuine for the gate to have force.
- Output medium: screen-recording mp4 — terminal running the gate prompt, the check script, PASS/FAIL output, then the gate printed as a formatted ASCII commitment card.
- The change: Take a failing gate (vague "I handle" clause) and revise it three times, running the check after each revision, until it passes all three criteria.
- Teardown angle: The gate is not a philosophy. It is a sentence. The student who can write it in under two minutes has already done the hardest part: deciding what part of the work is theirs.
- Exclusions: Full deliberate practice theory, spaced repetition system design, self-regulation literature beyond gate design.
- Score: 10/10

## Candidate 03 — "Run the Four Self-Assessment Protocols on a Research Summary"
- Source: ai-for-students-a-practitioners-guide/chapters/06-how-to-know-if-you-actually-learned-it-the-self-assessment-protocols.md (LLM Exercise)
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: Your felt sense after studying with AI says "I know this." That signal is a retrieval-strength reading — it reflects how easy the material was to process, not how well it is stored. The four protocols are behavioral measurements, not feelings. They tell you whether you have storage strength, which is what the exam actually tests.
- The artifact: A Claude-powered self-assessment session for any study topic, running all four protocols in sequence: (1) blank-page recall (close everything, write for 3 minutes), (2) explain-to-a-skeptic (Claude plays skeptic, student must answer without the AI's help), (3) transfer test (apply the concept to a novel scenario Claude generates), (4) source-level check (name the evidence, not the synthesis). Output: a per-protocol score (0-2) and a storage-strength estimate.
- Prompt seed: Protocol 2: `claude "I am going to explain [topic] to you. Play a skeptic: ask me two follow-up questions I cannot answer by restating the summary — you want to know the specific evidence I am claiming, the methodology behind it, and a case where my claim would NOT hold. Do not accept vague answers. If I say 'studies show,' ask me which study, what sample, and what methodology."` then Protocol 3: `claude "Generate a novel scenario in [topic domain] that requires me to apply [concept]. The scenario should not match any case I have studied. Give me 3 minutes to respond without AI assistance — tell me when to start and stop."` then score each protocol 0-2.
- Read / check: Verify Protocol 2 fires a follow-up question when the student says "studies show"; verify Protocol 3 generates a genuinely novel scenario (not a paraphrase of the studied cases); verify the score output is honest (a vague Protocol 1 recall scores 0, not 1).
- Human supplies: A real study topic the viewer has studied (with AI assistance). The protocols are only meaningful on real material — synthetic is NOT acceptable. The gap between felt knowledge and demonstrated knowledge is the artifact.
- Output medium: screen-recording mp4 — terminal running protocols 2 and 3, student responses visible (typed live), score output at the end.
- The change: Close the AI entirely for Protocol 1; run the blank-page recall; compare to the felt sense from studying. Reveal the gap. Then use Protocol 4 to identify which specific sources the student can name vs. which they only have in synthesis form.
- Teardown angle: The protocols are lab tests. The felt sense is the patient's self-report. When they disagree, the lab tests win. The student who passes all four protocols has storage strength. The student who passes only the felt-sense check has retrieval strength that will decay by exam day.
- Exclusions: Full Bjork two-strength model mathematical derivation, Karpicke experimental methodology, spaced repetition scheduling algorithms.
- Score: 10/10

## Candidate 04 — "Write a Learning Prompt, Not an Output Prompt: The STEM Phase Gate"
- Source: ai-for-students-a-practitioners-guide/chapters/05-prompting-for-learning-not-for-output.md + chapters/07-math-and-stem-with-ai.md (LLM Exercises)
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: "A prompt that produces a good output is not the same as a prompt that produces learning." A STEM student who asks Claude to solve the problem gets a solution. A student who asks Claude to help them get unstuck and then closes the AI gets a skill. The setup is the skill. Everything after it is arithmetic.
- The artifact: A side-by-side comparison of two prompts on the same physics problem: (1) OUTPUT prompt ("solve this problem") — Claude produces the full solution, student learns nothing about the setup, (2) LEARNING prompt ("I am stuck at setting up the force diagram. Ask me what I already know. If I am wrong, redirect me. Do not give me the answer until I have correctly set up the problem.") — Claude runs a Socratic session. The comparison is animated as a Manim flowchart showing which cognitive operations each prompt activates.
- Prompt seed: Learning prompt: `claude "I am working on this physics problem: [problem]. I am going to try to set it up. Do not give me the answer. Ask me what information is given, what I am solving for, and which principle applies. If my setup is wrong, redirect me with a question — do not correct me directly. Give me the answer only after I have successfully set up the problem without AI help."` then compare the learning session to the output session.
- Read / check: Verify the learning prompt causes Claude to ask a redirecting question rather than providing the next step directly; verify the output prompt produces the complete solution in one response; verify the Manim flowchart correctly labels which cognitive operations each prompt activates (setup → execute vs. passive-read → recognize).
- Human supplies: A real physics or math problem the viewer is working through. A real problem produces a genuine Socratic session; synthetic placeholder reduces the session to theater.
- Output medium: Manim (animated) — two-branch flowchart, left branch showing output-prompt cognitive path (read → passive recognition → forget), right branch showing learning-prompt path (recall → apply → check → store). Branches animate simultaneously.
- The change: After the learning session, close the AI and attempt the problem independently; compare the experience to the output-prompt condition; discuss what "the setup is the skill" means for the exam.
- Teardown angle: Chi, Feltovich, and Glaser's 1981 expert study showed that experts classify problems by underlying principle, not surface features. The Socratic session trains the classification. The output prompt trains nothing.
- Exclusions: Full deliberate practice literature, Krashen's input hypothesis (language learning), specific physics problem solving.
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 05 — "Stress-Test Your History Argument with Claude's Skeptic Mode"
- Source: ai-for-students-a-practitioners-guide/chapters/11-history-politics-and-social-sciences-with-ai.md (LLM Exercises)
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: Wineburg's historians read documents by checking the source first — date, author, purpose, institutional affiliation — before the content. Undergraduates read content first. AI gives you the most fluent travel guide to the past that has never been there. The skeptic mode makes it useful.
- The artifact: A Claude skeptic session for a historical argument: the student states their thesis, and Claude plays an expert skeptic — not to refute, but to probe: (1) what is your primary evidence? (2) who wrote this source and why? (3) what would a historian on the other side say? (4) what would it take to falsify your claim? Output: a structured challenge memo with the skeptic's questions and the student's answers scored for specificity.
- Prompt seed: `claude "I am going to state a historical thesis. Play an expert skeptic — not hostile, but rigorous. Ask me: (1) what is my strongest primary evidence? (2) for each source I cite, who produced it, when, and for what audience? (3) what would the strongest opposing historical interpretation say? (4) what evidence would falsify my claim? Do not accept 'historians argue' without asking which historians. Do not accept a date without asking for the source. Score my answers 1-3: 1=vague, 2=specific with source, 3=specific with source AND methodology."` then the student states their thesis and responds to each probe.
- Read / check: Verify Claude asks for source provenance (who, when, for what audience) on the first primary source the student cites; verify Claude does not accept "historians argue" without asking which historians; verify the score rubric is applied consistently; verify the memo is structured as 4 sections.
- Human supplies: A real historical argument the viewer is working on. The skeptic session is only valuable with a real argument and real sources — synthetic is NOT acceptable; the probes must expose actual gaps in the viewer's knowledge.
- Output medium: screen-recording mp4 — terminal running the skeptic session, student responses typed live, score callouts appearing, then the challenge memo output.
- The change: Take the lowest-scored answer (vague response to "what would a historian on the other side say?") and ask Claude to help locate the opposing historiographical tradition; research it independently; improve the score to 3.
- Teardown angle: The past is a foreign country. AI is a fluent travel guide that has never been there. The skeptic mode makes Claude's fluency work against its tendency to synthesize — forcing the student to produce evidence, not summaries.
- Exclusions: Historiographical theory lecture, full Wineburg lateral reading curriculum, source evaluation as a library science topic.
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 06 — "Build Your Learning Stack: Storage Strength vs. Retrieval Strength Dashboard"
- Source: ai-for-students-a-practitioners-guide/chapters/14-building-your-learning-stack-for-the-long-game.md + chapters/06-how-to-know-if-you-actually-learned-it-the-self-assessment-protocols.md
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: The Bastani decrement is a one-semester number — students who used unguarded AI scored 17 percentage points below control on independent tests. The Matthew effect is what happens when you run that number for four years. More will be given to those who have. Less will be taken from those who do not.
- The artifact: A Python script that tracks a student's self-assessment protocol scores across 10 sessions (Protocol 1-4 scores, 0-2 each, per topic). Renders as a Manim animated scatter plot: x = retrieval strength (felt sense after study), y = storage strength (protocol score at delay). Points plotted per session, with a trend line showing the calibration gap over time. A "getting better" trend (points moving toward the y=x line) is the target.
- Prompt seed: `claude "I have 10 study sessions logged: each has a retrieval_strength (1-10, how confident I felt after studying) and a storage_strength (0-8, my total protocol score at a one-week delay). Generate the data for a learning-stack calibration chart. Make retrieval_strength high for AI-assisted sessions and lower for retrieval-practice sessions. Make storage_strength low for AI-assisted sessions and high for retrieval-practice. Tag each session with method: AI-assisted, retrieval-practice, or hybrid." > sessions.json` then render Manim scatter plot.
- Read / check: Verify AI-assisted sessions cluster in high-retrieval, low-storage quadrant; verify retrieval-practice sessions cluster in high-storage quadrant; verify the trend line is computable from the data; verify the Manim scatter renders with correct quadrant labels.
- Human supplies: 10 real study sessions tracked over a semester (or simulate 10 sessions for the demo, labeled by method). Synthetic data is acceptable if clearly labeled as illustrative. Manim installed.
- Output medium: Manim (animated) — scatter plot with points appearing per session, quadrant labels growing in, trend line animating, "y=x calibration line" appearing as reference.
- The change: Add a session where the student used AI-assisted study PLUS a retrieval test; plot it; show it lands closer to the y=x line than pure AI-assisted sessions.
- Teardown angle: The Matthew effect runs on a four-year compounding curve. Students who build storage strength early get easier access to new material. Students who build only retrieval strength find the later courses harder, not easier. The chart makes this visible before the four years are up.
- Exclusions: Full spaced repetition algorithm design (Anki scheduling), neuroscience of memory consolidation, specific study tools beyond the protocol.
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 07 — "Use Claude as a Research Assistant: Source List Without Synthesis"
- Source: ai-for-students-a-practitioners-guide/chapters/04-the-phase-gate-when-to-open-the-ai-and-when-to-close-it.md (Nicholas case study, LLM Exercise)
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: Nicholas typed "summarize the social and economic situation for Syrian refugees in Jordan" and got a beautiful, comprehensive, pre-assembled picture. His argument then matched the AI's synthesis — which meant it was the AI's argument. The second prompt ("give me a list of primary sources — do not synthesize any of them") is the gate-compliant version. It takes the same 30 seconds and produces a completely different outcome.
- The artifact: A two-prompt comparison: (1) synthesis prompt — Claude produces a structured summary; (2) source-list prompt — Claude produces only source titles, authors, dates, URLs, and one-sentence descriptions of methodology. A Python diff script compares what the student knows at the end of each path: after prompt 1 they have the AI's synthesis; after prompt 2 they have a reading list. The diff is shown as an animated Manim two-column chart.
- Prompt seed: Prompt 2 (gate-compliant): `claude "Give me a list of primary sources on [topic]: UNHCR reports, government publications, peer-reviewed papers. For each source: title, author/organization, year, URL if available, one-sentence description of the methodology or data type. Do not synthesize, summarize, or interpret any of them. Do not tell me what they say. Just the sources and their methodological descriptions."` then track: after prompt 1, student has synthesis. After prompt 2, student has a reading list. After reading the sources: student has argument.
- Read / check: Verify Prompt 2 produces NO synthesis sentences (no "the evidence shows," no "in general," no concluding claim); verify each entry has methodology/data-type description; verify the Manim two-column chart correctly labels what the student has at the end of each path.
- Human supplies: A real research topic the viewer is investigating. The source-list prompt only produces useful output when given a real topic — synthetic placeholder produces generic sources that cannot be read.
- Output medium: Manim (animated) — two columns growing: "Synthesis Path" (column fills with summary text, labeled "AI's argument") vs. "Source-List Path" (column fills with source entries, labeled "Your reading list"). Then a third beat: student reads, column 2 transforms into "Your argument."
- The change: Read one of the sources from the list; notice what the AI synthesis had compressed or missed; bring that finding back as a follow-up prompt — "Here is what I found in source 3. Challenge my reading: what would a researcher who disagreed with me say?"
- Teardown angle: The student who gets the synthesis has the AI's argument. The student who gets the source list has the AI's labor. The research project is what happens in between. Nicholas found that the official synthesis said "integrated into the labor market." The primary sources said the opposite.
- Exclusions: Full research methodology course, citation management tools (Zotero etc.), IRB and ethics in human subjects research.
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 08 — "Calibrate AI Use for Pre-Professional Work: Where the Gate Must Hold"
- Source: ai-for-students-a-practitioners-guide/chapters/12-pre-professional-preparation-pre-med-pre-law-engineering.md (LLM Exercises)
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: In every other chapter, borrowed capability costs you a grade. Here, it costs someone else. A pre-med student who AI-generated their clinical reasoning does not lose points. They become the doctor who AI-generates their clinical reasoning.
- The artifact: A phase gate specification for three pre-professional fields (pre-med, pre-law, engineering) showing where AI use is appropriate (literature search, case summarization, draft generation for review) and where it is contraindicated (clinical reasoning, legal judgment, safety-critical calculations). Rendered as an animated D3 decision tree: "is this task AI-delegable in this field?" with branching paths per field.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Build a phase gate specification for [field: pre-med | pre-law | engineering]. For each task category: (1) literature search, (2) case/situation summarization, (3) draft writing, (4) reasoning and judgment, (5) calculation/analysis: classify as AI-APPROPRIATE (AI can do this and error cost is recoverable), AI-SUPERVISED (AI drafts, human verifies with domain expertise), AI-EXCLUDED (AI use creates unacceptable risk — the human must do this, full stop). For AI-EXCLUDED tasks: name the specific consequence of AI substitution in that field." > phase_gate_professional.md`
- Read / check: Verify clinical reasoning in pre-med is classified AI-EXCLUDED with consequence "patient harm from misdiagnosis"; verify legal judgment in pre-law is AI-EXCLUDED with consequence "malpractice and client harm"; verify load calculation in engineering is AI-SUPERVISED not AI-APPROPRIATE (verify first); verify literature search in all three fields is AI-APPROPRIATE.
- Human supplies: A field the viewer is preparing for. The gating exercise is only meaningful when the viewer is actually in that pipeline — synthetic is acceptable for the demo but should be labeled. Real professional-preparation context makes the consequences concrete.
- Output medium: d3 (animated) — three-column decision tree (one per field), task categories on y-axis, AI-APPROPRIATE/SUPERVISED/EXCLUDED labels animating in per cell, consequence text appearing on AI-EXCLUDED cells.
- The change: Ask Claude to generate one case where AI use in a pre-professional context caused harm — stress-test whether the AI-EXCLUDED classification is defensible; discuss the standard.
- Teardown angle: The gate for pre-professional work is different from the gate for a history paper. In every other context, borrowed capability costs the borrower. Here, the cost is externalized. The locus of harm shifts from student to patient, client, or structure.
- Exclusions: Full medical ethics and informed consent, engineering professional liability law, bar exam preparation methods.
- Score: 8/10
