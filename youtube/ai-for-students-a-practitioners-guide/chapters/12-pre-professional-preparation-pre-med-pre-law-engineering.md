# Chapter 12 — Pre-Professional Preparation: Pre-Med, Pre-Law, Engineering

*In every other chapter, borrowed capability costs you a grade. Here, it costs someone else.*

> *"There is one kind of thinking which is right for athletics or chess, and another which is right for the law."* — Karl Llewellyn, *The Bramble Bush*

---

A pre-med student preparing for the MCAT builds a workflow she is proud of. For four months she works clinical cases with an AI tutor open: paste the vignette, ask for the differential, read the answer, move to the next case. Her in-practice accuracy climbs from 60% to 85%. She tracks the number. She feels prepared.

On test day, in a Pearson VUE testing center with no devices and no AI, she scores 8 points below her last in-practice score.

She retakes four months later. She has changed the workflow: every case gets a written commitment — her own differential, her own reasoning — before any AI is consulted. AI then challenges what she wrote. Her in-practice scores rise more slowly the second time. The number on her chart is less impressive. She scores 8 points higher than her first attempt.

The difference was not knowledge. She knew the medicine both times. The difference was schema. The first four months had bypassed the cognitive event that produces clinical reasoning. The second four months restored it. The argument of this chapter is not a new argument — it is the same argument every previous chapter has made, about fluency and generation and schemas that only form when you do the work. But it is the chapter where the argument changes character. In every previous chapter, borrowed capability hurts the student. Here, it hurts someone else.

## What Gets Built in Professional Training

Three fields, three different kinds of schema, one underlying structure.

In medicine, the cognitive backbone is the **illness script**. Geoffrey Norman's program at McMaster, building on Schmidt and Boshuizen's knowledge-encapsulation model, establishes that experienced clinicians carry packaged representations of diseases — bundles that include predisposing conditions, pathophysiology, characteristic presentation, distinguishing features, workup, and expected response to treatment.[^charlin2007] Diagnosis toggles between fast pattern-recognition (script activation) and slow deliberate reasoning (hypothesis testing). Experienced clinicians do both and know when to switch. The scripts are built through one mechanism: commitment to a diagnosis, comparison against the case, revision when the case fails to fit. Without the commitment, the script does not form.

In law, the backbone is the **constructive argument schema** — the ability to read a fact pattern, identify which legal questions it raises, reason from precedent to a position, and defend that position under hostile questioning. Christopher Columbus Langdell built the Harvard case method in 1870 on exactly this principle: students read appellate opinions and came prepared to be interrogated about them. The Socratic cold call was not theater. It was the mechanism by which the constructive moves — issue-spotting, rule synthesis from precedent — became habitual under pressure. Both moves are bypassed by AI that summarizes a case or states a rule directly.

In engineering, the backbone is **design judgment** — Nigel Cross's argument, developed over four decades, that engineering design constitutes a distinct cognitive discipline whose signature moves are framing (deciding what the problem actually is), abductive reasoning (inferring from desired outcomes to plausible mechanisms), and reflection-in-action as the work proceeds.[^cross2006] The visible output of engineering is calculation. The invisible work that makes the calculation correct is the framing. A well-framed problem with a small arithmetic error is salvageable. A badly framed problem solved to high precision is a designed failure mode.

What these three schemas share: they are built through active commitment under conditions of uncertainty, followed by comparison against reality, followed by revision. They are not built by reading correct answers. The generation effect (Slamecka and Graf, 1978 — the chapter every pre-professional student in this book has now encountered three times) applies here as directly as it applies to paired word associates. You own what you generated. You recognize what you read.

The Bastani performance paradox is the direct mechanism in each field. The pre-med student who consults AI before attempting a diagnosis bypasses the script-activation event. The pre-law student who asks AI for the rule from *Palsgraf* bypasses the precedent-synthesis event. The engineering student who asks AI to frame the problem bypasses the design-judgment event. In each case the schema the professional school was designed to build does not form. On the licensing exam — and later, in practice — the schema is not there when it is needed.

## The Scaffold That Never Gets Removed

Vygotsky's zone of proximal development — the gap between what a learner can do alone and what they can do with assistance — is the most-cited framework in educational psychology precisely because it names something real.[^vygotsky1978] Wood, Bruner, and Ross, and later Collins, Brown, and Newman's cognitive apprenticeship framework, drew the developmental implication: scaffolding must fade. Help that is available when the learner cannot yet perform alone is developmental. Help that stays in place after the learner could have performed alone prevents the developmental event.

The pre-professional failure mode is exactly this. AI is convenient. The student feels capable when AI is present. The scaffold never fades. The student arrives at the licensing exam — and eventually at independent practice — having practiced extensively and never having performed without the scaffold in place.

The Bastani GPT Tutor condition is the operationalization of fading scaffold: an AI configured to provide hints without answer reveals, requiring the student to do the final cognitive work, structured so that the help fades as the student converges on the solution. That arm of the trial showed preserved performance on the unassisted exam. The GPT Base arm — unscaffolded help — produced a 17-percentage-point loss relative to control.[^bastani2025] The difference is not the presence of AI. It is whether the scaffold fades.

The MCAT student's first four months is the textbook version of the scaffold-never-removed failure. She was getting right answers because the AI was producing them. The in-practice accuracy of 85% was measuring AI's accuracy, with her accuracy hidden underneath it. The licensing exam measured her accuracy alone. The gap — 8 points — was the size of the scaffold she had been standing on without knowing it.

The Lee et al. 2025 finding lands here with specific force: higher confidence in AI predicts lower critical thinking.[^lee2025] The student who feels ready because AI-assisted practice scores are high is the most vulnerable, not the least. The metacognitive signal — *I understand this* — is reading the AI's fluency, not the student's schema. Chapter 2's fluency trap operating at the scale of a professional career.

## When Borrowed Capability Injures Someone Else

Every chapter before this one has argued self-interestedly: borrowed capability harms *your* learning, *your* exam, *your* career. This chapter adds a second argument.

On March 1, 2023, attorney Steven Schwartz filed a brief in *Mata v. Avianca, Inc.* in the Southern District of New York. Roberto Mata had been injured by a metal serving cart on an Avianca flight and was suing under international aviation law. Avianca moved to dismiss on statute-of-limitations grounds. Schwartz's brief cited six cases in opposition. None of them existed. All six had been fabricated by ChatGPT.[^mata2023]

When Avianca's counsel could not find the cases and the court could not find the cases, Judge P. Kevin Castel ordered Schwartz to produce copies of the opinions. Schwartz returned to ChatGPT and asked whether the cases were real. ChatGPT confirmed they were real. He asked for the text of the opinions. ChatGPT produced text — fabricated text in the style of federal opinions, with citation strings and quoted holdings that did not exist. Schwartz filed these as exhibits.

The sanctions opinion, issued June 22, 2023, required Schwartz, his appearing co-counsel Peter LoDuca, and their firm to pay $5,000 and to write personally to the six judges whose names ChatGPT had attached as authors of the fabricated opinions — explaining what had happened and apologizing.

The cognitive mechanism is exact. Schwartz did not know aviation personal-injury case law well enough to recognize that the citations were nonexistent. His verification was to ask ChatGPT whether the cases were real. The Lee et al. finding in operational form: he had high confidence in the AI and low independent competence to detect the AI's errors — which means no critical thinking happened at all. The fabrications passed through his review undetected because his review was asking the fabricator to confirm its own fabrications.

By late 2025, an unofficial database maintained by legal-tech researcher Damien Charlotin had logged more than 800 such cases of court filings containing AI-fabricated authority — most from 2025 alone, indicating the failure mode is accelerating rather than receding. The Walters and Wilder study established the baseline: 55% of GPT-3.5 citations and 18% of GPT-4 citations were entirely fabricated; of the non-fabricated citations, 43% (GPT-3.5) and 24% (GPT-4) contained substantive errors.[^walters2023] For medical citations specifically, Bhattacharyya and colleagues found that 93% of papers containing ChatGPT-generated medical references had incorrect PMID numbers.[^bhattacharyya2023]

The pre-law student's takeaway is structural: build the calibration first. Read real cases. Develop the ear for what real precedent sounds like — the citation conventions, the typical case-name patterns, the year-jurisdiction-reporter triad. Then use AI to challenge a position you have constructed from real authority. AI is productive for the post-construction challenge phase. AI is dangerous for the source-finding phase if you lack the independent competence to detect fabrication. And the independent competence is exactly what a pre-law student who practiced on AI-generated case summaries does not have.

The medical analog has fewer visible cases because medical errors do not get sanctioned in open court. They get adjudicated in malpractice review years after the event, behind confidentiality protections. The cognitive failure mode is identical. A resident who has practiced clinical reasoning with AI for two years arrives at a case where the AI suggests the wrong diagnosis. The resident's job is to catch the error. That catch requires an illness script that the AI bypass prevented from forming. The patient receives the wrong treatment. There is no Judge Castel in the room. The harm is borne by the patient.

The argument is not ethical. It is consequential. In a profession with licensing and liability, borrowed capability is a professional risk you are accepting on someone else's behalf.

## The Human-Only Zone

The Human-Only Zone for pre-professional preparation is the moment of initial professional judgment. It looks different in each field. The structure is identical: commit in writing before AI is consulted.

Pre-med: read the case vignette and commit to a top-three differential diagnosis with the one distinguishing finding for each — before any AI consultation, before any reference material, before any source check. The script-activation event must happen in you.

Pre-law: read the fact pattern and commit to an issue-spotting pass — which legal questions does this raise? Read the case and commit to a statement of the holding and its reasoning. Draft an initial rule synthesis. The construction must be yours before the challenge is AI's.

Engineering: read the design or analysis problem and commit to the problem framing — what is the problem actually about, what are the governing assumptions, what model class is appropriate, what failure modes should be expected. The framing must precede any AI consultation, because if AI does the framing, the design judgment never forms.

![Three parallel horizontal pathways — pre-med, pre-law, and engineering — each running from undergrad through the no-AI gatekeeping exams to a filled terminal box marking the human-consequence stage of practice.](../images/12-pre-professional-preparation-pre-med-pre-law-engineering-fig-06.png)
![Three licensure pathways. Every gate after preparation is a no-AI environment.](images/12-pre-professional-preparation-pre-med-pre-law-engineering-fig-06.png)
*Figure 12.6 — Three licensure pathways. Every gate after preparation is a no-AI environment.*

After the commitment, AI opens — as challenger, not as advisor. The simulated patient who pushes back on your differential. The opposing counsel who mounts the argument against your analysis. The senior reviewing engineer who stress-tests your framing. The student plays the professional. AI plays the world the professional acts within.

This is the gate structure that distinguishes the MCAT student's second four months from her first. First four months: AI as advisor, commitment skipped, scaffold in place throughout. Second four months: AI as environment, commitment first, scaffold present only after the judgment event.

## The Pre-Collegiate Software Case

The three professions this chapter centers on share a structural feature — licensing exams between preparation and practice, with no AI in the room. Software has no equivalent licensing exam. The gatekeeping happens earlier and more diffusely: technical interviews, take-home projects, portfolio review, on-site coding rounds. The cognitive failure mode and the corrective are structurally identical.

Seth is a high school senior preparing for collegiate work in software engineering and game development, available August 2026. He has shipped five public projects across Godot, Roblox, Next.js, and vanilla JS Canvas. He has co-built two production AI agents documented in this book's appendix. He is also, like every applicant in his cohort, facing a job market where the technical interview is a Zoom call with a shared editor, no Copilot, no Claude, and a forty-five-minute window to demonstrate a working schema for systems he has actually built.

The failure mode for a student in his position is the one this chapter has named twice already. He could prepare for those interviews the way the MCAT student prepared for her first four months — passing LeetCode problems with Claude open, accepting AI's solution when his stalls, watching his in-practice success rate climb while the schema underneath it does not form. On the call, the shared editor measures what is in his head. The 8-point gap, in his domain, is the difference between a return invite and a polite rejection email.

The corrective is the same. For technical-interview preparation, the gate is: read the problem, commit to a written approach — data structure, algorithm, complexity guess, edge cases — before any AI consultation. Implement the first draft cold. Only then does AI open, and only as challenger: *Here is my solution. What is the failure mode I have not considered? Where is my complexity analysis wrong?* For portfolio review and GitHub repo cleanup, AI is genuinely useful at the extraneous side — README polish, commit-history audit, identifying gaps in test coverage. It is dangerous at the germane side — explaining the architectural decision behind a particular system, which only the developer who made it can speak to. For resume targeting — distinguishing roles at game-dev studios (engine experience, shipping discipline, networked multiplayer) from generalist SWE roles (data structures fluency, system design, language breadth) — AI is useful for the first pass and inadequate for the judgment about which specific projects from the portfolio to lead with for which audience. That judgment is Seth's. The cognitive event the August-2026 job hunt is supposed to produce is a developer who can speak with authority about what he has built. AI cannot produce that event. It can, used badly, prevent it from happening.

The structural lesson generalizes past Seth's specific case. Every pre-professional path that ends in a high-stakes unassisted evaluation — bar exam, MCAT, NCEES, technical interview, residency interview, oral defense — has the same gate-placement decision running underneath it. The format of the gatekeeper changes. The cognitive architecture does not.

## The MCAT Student's Second Four Months

She cancels her AI tutor subscription. She buys the official AAMC practice materials — the only fully calibrated MCAT content — and changes the workflow.

Per practice case: read the vignette cold, AI closed. Write the commitment on paper — top-three differential, one-sentence reasoning for each, the one additional finding that would most change the ranking. Ninety seconds to three minutes per case, no skipping.

Then open AI as challenger: *I have committed to [diagnosis]. Without telling me whether I'm correct, identify the one assumption in my reasoning most exposed to a different ranking. Tell me what evidence in the vignette I underweighted.* Read the challenge. Return to the vignette. Revise the commitment or hold it, with a note about why. Check the answer key only after the final commitment is locked.

Weekly: one AAMC Full Length practice exam under official conditions — no AI, no notes, no internet, official timing. Score it. The number from the official simulation is the number she actually has. The 85% she was seeing in AI-assisted practice was not hers.

Her in-practice scores rise more slowly the second time. The number on the chart is less impressive. But the gap between in-practice and unassisted closes, because the schema is forming.

On test day: 8 points above her first attempt. The medicine has not changed. A year later she is in a clinical reasoning workshop in her first year of medical school. The attending presents a case. Most students wait for the attending to work through it. She is one of the first to commit to a differential and name the one finding that would shift it. The attending notices. The schema transfers because it was built.

## Exercises

### Warm-Up — The Bastani Chart on Yourself

Find your most recent AI-assisted practice session for your high-stakes exam. Note the in-practice accuracy. Then sit a parallel section — same exam, same question type, same length — unassisted: no AI, no notes, no internet, official timing. Note the unassisted accuracy.

Plot both numbers. The gap between them is the Bastani decrement showing up on you personally. Write the number down and look at it. That gap is the scaffold. The goal of the next four months is to close it, not by raising the AI-assisted score but by raising the unassisted one.

If the gap is small, your practice has been building the schema. If the gap is large, the months ahead need to look like the MCAT student's second four months.

---

### Application — The Commitment Drill

For one week, every practice case you work in your discipline must be preceded by a written commitment — differential, issue-spotting pass, or problem framing — before AI is consulted. Paper and pen. No skipping.

Keep a log with three columns: cases where your commitment held under AI's challenge (schema confirmed), cases where AI's challenge surfaced a gap you had not seen (schema extended), and cases where you would have skipped the commitment entirely if the chapter weren't telling you not to (schema absent). The third column is the Bastani performance paradox showing up in your own habits. It is the most important column.

### LLM Exercise — AI as Simulated Environment

The signature prompt type for this chapter. The student plays the professional. AI plays the world. AI does not advise; it responds to the student's professional judgment.

```
You are a 45-year-old patient presenting to an outpatient clinic.
Chief complaint: [symptom]. I will ask you history questions one at
a time. Answer as the patient would — honestly, with limited
self-knowledge, occasionally offering unprompted details because they
worry you.

I will commit to a top-three differential diagnosis in writing before
you give me any vital signs or labs. After my commitment, I will ask
for specific data. Give me only what I ask for.

After my final diagnosis, tell me what the case actually was (invent
any plausible diagnosis fitting the symptoms) and identify the one
history finding I failed to elicit that would have most narrowed
my differential.
```

The pre-law version substitutes: *"Act as opposing counsel preparing for moot court. I will write my analysis first. Then identify the three weakest moves in my argument, the strongest counter-argument from a senior litigator, and two precedents I haven't cited that would damage my position. Do not write my response for me."*

The engineering version: *"I have framed the following problem: [framing]. Before I calculate, identify the two assumptions most fragile under realistic operating conditions, the one missing failure mode, and the one alternative model class I should have considered. I will revise the framing before any calculation."*

Recovery move when AI starts advising instead of responding: *"You are the environment, not the advisor. Do not tell me what to do. Respond to what I do."*

The diagnostic: if the session makes you feel good about your reasoning before you have committed to a judgment, the prompt has failed. A working environment-simulation session produces the feeling of being tested — because you are.

---

### Synthesis — The Full Simulation-Environment Session

Run one complete practice session in which AI plays the environment for your field across three consecutive cases — patient, opposing counsel, or stress-test scenario — without breaking the frame to ask for advice. You play the professional throughout.

After the session, ask AI to evaluate in one paragraph where your professional judgment was most consistent and where it was most exposed. Note one specific schema gap to close before the next session.

Then compare: where AI challenged your reasoning hardest is where the schema is thinnest. That is your study target for the following week — not more cases, but deeper work on the specific mechanism or doctrine or design principle that the challenge exposed.

---

### Challenge — The Licensing Simulation

Sit an official practice exam under official conditions for your target credential. AAMC Full Length for pre-med. LSAC PrepTest for pre-law. NCEES practice problem set for engineering. No AI. No notes. No internet. Official timing. Score yourself honestly.

This score is what you have. The number you have been seeing in AI-assisted practice was not your score — it was AI's score with your name on it. The licensing body does not give you a curve for cognitive debt.

If the gap between this score and your AI-assisted score is wide, the months ahead have a specific prescription: the MCAT student's second four months. Commitment before consultation. Unassisted weekly simulations. The number rises slowly and it is yours.

## Prompt Reference

Seven prompts for the seven AI-on moments in pre-professional preparation.

**Prompt 1 — The Simulated Patient.** Full text in LLM Exercise above. Pre-med. AI is the patient responding only to what is asked; student commits to differential before any data; AI reveals diagnosis and missed finding at the end.

**Prompt 2 — The Simulated Opposing Counsel.** Pre-law. Student submits written analysis first. AI identifies the three weakest moves, the strongest counter-argument from a senior litigator, and two precedents the student hasn't cited that would damage their position. No rewrites. No softening.

**Prompt 3 — The Framed-Problem Stress Test.** Engineering. Student submits problem framing, governing assumptions, model class, boundary conditions, expected failure modes. AI identifies the two most fragile assumptions, the one missing failure mode, and the one alternative model class that might better fit — before any calculation begins.

**Prompt 4 — The Licensing Simulation Prompt.** Generate practice questions at official difficulty for the target exam and section. Student answers under official timing, no AI assistance. After submission, AI identifies question types missed and the specific schema gap each miss suggests.

**Prompt 5 — The Fabricated-Citation Calibration.** Pre-law specific but generalizable. AI generates three case citations in a specified area of law. Student verifies each against Westlaw, Lexis, and Google Scholar. AI does not flag which are fabricated. The exercise calibrates the student's ability to detect fabricated legal authority — the Mata v. Avianca failure mode, practiced as a skill.

**Prompt 6 — The Post-Commitment Attending Round.** Pre-med. Student commits to a diagnosis. AI acts as attending physician on rounds: asks the student to explain why this diagnosis was included, why the most likely alternative was ruled out, and what one finding would change their mind. AI does not reveal whether the student is correct. It assesses only whether the reasoning is sound.

**Prompt 7 — The Post-Judgment Regulatory Audit.** Engineering. Student submits completed design. AI acts as senior reviewing engineer: identifies any element that would not meet the specified code, any element that meets code but represents a questionable judgment, and the one element where the student's engineering judgment is most exposed to liability. No redesign — the student revises independently.

## AI Wayback Machine

The ideas in this chapter didn't appear from nowhere. **Sir William Osler (1849–1919)** is the modern source of the proposition that professional schemas are built at the bedside, not in the lecture hall. Born in Bond Head, Ontario, he became the founding Physician-in-Chief at Johns Hopkins Hospital in 1889 and the principal architect of the four-year clinical-clerkship model that every North American medical school still inherits. His maxim — *"see one, do one, teach one"* — names the same three-move arc the second four months of the MCAT student's preparation rehearsed: observe the move, commit to the move under supervision, then own the move well enough to transmit it. The "see" rehearses recognition; the "do" forces the script-activation event the AI-assisted first four months had bypassed; the "teach" is the schema's transfer test, since explaining the move to a junior is the operational definition of having it. Osler's other line — *"He who studies medicine without books sails an uncharted sea, but he who studies medicine without patients does not go to sea at all"* — locates the failure mode this chapter argues against. The patient is the environment. The AI is not. A pre-med who has worked thousands of cases with an AI tutor open has read the book. Whether she has gone to sea is the question her Pearson VUE testing centre answers.
