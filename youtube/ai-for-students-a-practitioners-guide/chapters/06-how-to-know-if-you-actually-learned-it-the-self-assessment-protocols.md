# Chapter 6 — How to Know If You Actually Learned It: The Self-Assessment Protocols

> "The fluency trap feels identical to genuine mastery. These four protocols tell you which one you're experiencing."

---

Nicholas is a high school student from Cape Cod with an interest in political science that runs deeper than the classroom — the kind of interest that produces opinions at the dinner table and arguments about foreign policy with people who would rather not have them. The summer before his senior year, he joins a research project studying the social and economic impacts of Syrian refugees in Jordan. Serious work: a structured program, a graduate student as research partner, weekly check-ins with a professor. The deliverable is a documented case study on how AI tools can be used to analyze a real policy question without becoming a shortcut past the analysis.

He has been working on the project for three weeks when something exposes the problem.

In a weekly check-in, the professor asks what Nicholas thought of the UNHCR report on labor market integration — specifically, what the sample looked like and whether the wage-suppression finding was based on survey or administrative data. Nicholas opens his mouth. He has the shape of the argument: legal restrictions push refugees into informal labor markets, informal employment suppresses wages, host-community absorption is complicated but measurable. But the sample? He does not know. Survey or administrative data? He cannot say.

He has his notes on his laptop in front of him. He cannot make the notes answer questions his notes do not contain.

What Nicholas has is a document full of AI summaries of sources he hasn't fully read — claims he accepted from synthesis rather than formed through engagement with the underlying evidence. He knows the shape of his argument. He does not know which empirical findings are contested, which methodologies are stronger, where the literature actually disagrees. When the professor's question lands outside the shape of the argument, there is nothing underneath it.

The project has two weeks left. He needs to find out, before it ends and before the Columbia Early Decision application goes in, what he actually owns and what he merely has. Which parts of the summer work made it into his head and which parts stayed on the page. Which parts he can speak from memory, with no rescue, and which parts exist only in the artifact.

The four protocols in this chapter are how he finds that out.

## Why You Cannot Trust the Felt Sense

The single most important fact for this chapter is one of the most uncomfortable facts in the cognitive science of learning: your felt sense of "I understand this" is a poor predictor of whether you can retrieve and apply the material under conditions of delay or novelty.

This is not a moral failing. It is a feature of the cognitive system. Your brain produces the feeling of understanding based on signals that are present at the moment of study — fluent processing, recent exposure, recognition memory, source familiarity. None of these reliably predict whether you can produce the material later, in a different context, without the cues that were present during study.

Asher Koriat and Robert Bjork's research on what they called the *foresight bias* established the canonical demonstration: learners systematically over-predict their own future recall, and they do so most severely under exactly the conditions that AI-assisted study creates by default — high fluency, frequent recent exposure, strong recognition memory. While you are studying with AI, the material is present, the cues are everywhere, and your processing is fluent. Your brain reads "fluent processing" as "I know this." Later, without the cues, the material is not retrieved — but the original confidence was formed under conditions that have now vanished.

The student-facing implication is severe: **you cannot use your felt sense to decide whether you have learned something.** It will say yes when the answer is no. It will do this reliably, in the same direction, every time.

What replaces it? Behavioral measurement. Instead of asking "do I think I understand this?" ask "what can I produce, right now, without help, that requires the understanding?" The protocols below are four ways of running that behavioral measurement on yourself, while there is still time to do something about what you find.

Your felt sense is the patient's self-report. The protocols are the lab tests. When they disagree, the lab tests win.

## Storage Strength and Retrieval Strength

Before the protocols, one piece of machinery that makes them make sense.

Robert and Elizabeth Bjork proposed in 1992 that memory has two distinct strengths. *Storage strength* is how durably an item is embedded in long-term memory — how well integrated with prior knowledge, how permanently encoded. It builds slowly. Once built, it decays very slowly or not at all. *Retrieval strength* is how easily the item can be brought to mind right now, given current cues. It spikes immediately after study and falls as the cues fade.

These two strengths are dissociable. Forgetting is not erasure — it is loss of *access* to a representation that is still in storage somewhere. When you can't remember something you once knew, the storage is probably still there; the retrieval path decayed. And the reverse: high retrieval strength right after studying tells you almost nothing about storage strength. The material is easy to access because it's fresh and the cues are everywhere. That accessibility is not a predictor of what you'll have in two weeks.

Here is what this means for AI use. Conditions that maximize retrieval strength right now often do almost nothing for storage strength. Reading an AI summary makes retrieval strength high in the moment — the output is fluent, the cues are everywhere, recognition is easy. None of that is retrieval. You are not dragging anything back from storage; the material is just sitting there in front of you. The cognitive work that would have built storage strength never happened. The artifact improves. The cortex does not.

And here is the thing that completely reverses your intuitions about studying: *retrieval practice builds storage strength.* Dragging a memory back without cues — making the cognitive system actually go get it — increases how durably the memory is stored. Karpicke and Roediger's 2008 *Science* paper put numbers on it: participants who engaged in repeated retrieval retained roughly 80% of material at a one-week delay. Those who restudied the same material for the same time retained roughly 36%. Same material. Same time. Completely different cognitive operation.

The number that matters for your exam, your interview, your Columbia application, your licensing test — the actual delayed-retrieval event — is storage strength. Your felt sense after a study session is a retrieval-strength reading. The protocols below are storage-strength measurements. They are less flattering and more reliable.

## The Four Protocols

Four protocols. Each catches a different failure mode. Each requires AI to be closed — that constraint is not a formality. A protocol run with AI open is like a pressure test run with a valve open. The impulse to check something midway through is not a weakness; it is exactly what the protocol is designed to expose. When you feel the impulse to rescue yourself and you don't, that is the measurement happening.

---

### Protocol 1: The Spoken Feynman Test

Pick one concept — not a unit, not a chapter, one concept. Close everything. Set your phone to voice memo. Explain the concept out loud, as if to a curious 12-year-old who has never taken the course, for 90 seconds. Then listen back.

The protocol is spoken rather than written because speech is real-time. You cannot pause and revise. Fluent-but-empty phrases that pass unnoticed in text — "wage suppression is basically about informal labor market dynamics" — fall apart when you have to say them to someone who will ask what "basically" means. The cognitive system that produces speech demands more committed structure than the one that produces text. Oral exams catch what written exams miss, and this is why.

When you listen back, you are checking for three things.

First: is there a clean one-sentence definition that does not define the concept using the concept itself? Recursive definitions — "the wage-suppression effect is when wages are suppressed due to the wage-suppression dynamic" — are diagnostic of an empty slot where a definition should be. You can recognize the name without having the definition.

Second: is the mechanism explained step by step, without filler? "Basically," "kind of," "essentially," "you know," "the thing is" — these are not verbal tics. They are location markers. They appear exactly where the cognitive system is reaching for the next move in the causal chain and not finding it. Filler is where the schema breaks.

Third: is there a concrete analogy you generated yourself? Not one you remember AI giving you. A borrowed analogy requires only recognition; a generated analogy requires the schema to exist and be general enough to map onto a new domain. These are different cognitive operations, and they feel different when you're speaking.

If all three pass, something is real. If one fails, the failure is specific: not "I don't get this," but "I don't have the mechanism at the third step" or "my analogy is borrowed." The corrective is not to re-read the AI synthesis — you have already demonstrated that has not built the schema. Go back to the source material and generate your own explanation from it. Re-run the protocol within 24 hours.

| Criterion | Pass | Fail | What the fail reveals |
|---|---|---|---|
| One-sentence definition | States what X is, does, and how — without using X to define X | Recursive ("X is when you use X") or hedged ("it's kind of a thing where") | Concept was recognized but never defined |
| Step-by-step mechanism | Causal chain with named steps, no gaps | Filler at transition points; vague on any step | AI summarized this step; student never processed it |
| Self-generated analogy | Novel domain; generates a prediction about the target | Analogy from the AI session; "it's like" with no unpacking | Schema is borrowed; structure not independently owned |

### Protocol 2: The 24-Hour Closed-Book Reconstruction

Immediately after a study session, retrieval strength is at its peak. The material is fresh, the cues are everywhere, the felt sense of mastery is maximum — and maximally misleading. The 24-hour delay is the protocol's key mechanism. Twenty-four hours later, the cues that produced the fluency have faded. What remains in accessible form is what you actually encoded. What AI processed without going through your cognitive system is not there.

After a substantive study session, set a 24-hour reminder and do not touch the material in any form until it fires. No notes. No AI conversation. No related articles. No textbook. Any peek within the 24 hours invalidates the protocol — it converts a storage-strength measurement into a recent-retrieval measurement, which tells you nothing useful. Then: blank page, 25-minute timer, write out what you remember. Structure, argument, causal chain, sequence of steps — whatever the material was. When you get stuck, leave a placeholder and keep going. You are not trying to produce a perfect reconstruction. You are trying to see what the brain produces under retrieval pressure with no rescue available.

After the timer: compare to the original. The gaps fall into three categories. Material in your reconstruction but absent from the original — these are inventions, which reveal where the schema is filling gaps with plausible-sounding material that is wrong. Material in the original but absent from your reconstruction — the missing pieces, the most diagnostically important category. Material in both but structured differently — often a sign of genuine schema, because you reconstructed the logic rather than the verbatim wording.

For each missing piece, ask: was this something I worked through myself, or something AI processed for me? Material that is absent from your reconstruction and was AI-processed during study is the precise signature of capability-borrowing. The cognitive work stayed in the AI output. Your brain never did it, so there is no memory trace to retrieve.

### Protocol 3: The Transfer Test

This is the most diagnostic single protocol for catching capability-borrowing. The reason is that transfer is the operational definition of a real schema. Surface knowledge and genuine schema are indistinguishable in any test that uses the original context — the familiar problem types, the textbook examples, the homework. They come apart only when the surface features change and the underlying structure must be re-recognized.

Pick a concept you believe you understand. Identify the original context: where and how you learned it. Then generate a target context at least two disciplines away, where the surface features are completely different but the underlying structure is the same. Two disciplines is the rule of thumb because one discipline is often too near — Syrian refugees in Jordan to immigrants in another country shares too much surface to be diagnostic.

Then attempt the structural mapping yourself, without AI. What features carry across? What does the concept predict in the new context?

The evaluation criterion: can your mapping generate a prediction about the target context that you could check? Structural mappings produce predictions. Surface analogies notice that two situations rhyme and stop there. If you cannot predict anything about the target from the original concept, the mapping is decorative.

### Protocol 4: The Spacing Test

The other three protocols can be run within days of studying. This one is run 7 to 14 days later, under the same constraint of no intervening review, to measure whether storage strength survives the kind of delay that real high-stakes moments involve.

The mechanism is the spacing effect, established quantitatively by Cepeda and colleagues in a 2006 meta-analysis of 317 experiments: material studied with intervals between sessions is retained better at delayed test than material massed into a single block of the same total time. The practical calibration: optimal inter-study interval is roughly 10–20% of the retention interval. The Spacing Test inverts this — you wait the interval, then measure what survived.

Set the reminder immediately after the study session, before you leave the material. Then do not touch it. At the interval, generate five conceptual questions and answer cold, in writing, 20 minutes, nothing open. Score each answer in three buckets: schema-level (the answer demonstrates understanding of the mechanism), surface-level (correct terminology, no mechanism), absent. The three-bucket scoring is more informative than a percentage, because a correct answer that reveals only surface recognition is not the same result as a correct answer that demonstrates schema.

Run the Spacing Test repeatedly across a semester and you learn something more valuable than whether you know any specific concept: you learn which of your study modes actually builds storage strength. Which prompts from Chapter 5 produce schema-level answers at 14 days. Which gate placements from Chapter 4 produce durable storage versus retrieval-only confidence that decays to near-zero. The Spacing Test, run as ongoing measurement, is a diagnostic of your own learning system.

## Nicholas Runs the Stack

It is two weeks before Nicholas's Columbia Early Decision application goes in. The research project is complete. He has been working with AI in the disciplined mode the project required — source-finding, claim-challenging, mechanics editing — but he has not run any of the four protocols on the material. He does not know which parts of his understanding he built through his own primary-source reading and which parts he accepted from AI synthesis without fully internalizing. He runs the full stack over ten days.

**Day 1 — Spoken Feynman on six concepts he expects to need.**

The labor market restriction argument — the mechanism by which legal exclusion from formal employment pushes refugees into informal markets: passes cleanly. He built this from primary sources. He has a definition, a mechanism, a self-generated analogy (labor market segmentation as involuntary informality, analogous to how undocumented workers in US agriculture cluster in specific sectors regardless of skill level). Causal chain intact.

The wage-suppression dispute — survey data versus administrative data, why they produce different estimates: passes. This is his original contribution. He knows exactly where the studies disagree and why. He can speak the methodology difference without hesitation.

The host-community absorption literature — why Jordan absorbed a large refugee population without the predicted economic collapse: partial fail. He can name the finding but the mechanism is fuzzy. He let AI summarize this section and never read the underlying studies. Filler appears at the third step.

The Jordan-specific legal framework — work permit history and the 2016 Jordan Compact: partial fail. He knows the policy outcome but not the negotiating history. AI-summarized. Shape without substance.

The UNHCR versus ILO employment figure discrepancy: fail. He encountered this once in an AI synthesis and never checked the primary reports. He cannot explain where the numbers diverge or why. The clearest case of AI processing without Nicholas processing.

His own argument — that the standard account overstates wage suppression by relying on survey data that conflates informal employment with unemployment: passes cleanly. He owns this one entirely.

The pattern is exact: every concept he formed from primary sources survived. Every concept he accepted from AI synthesis without primary engagement did not.

**Corrective study, days 1–2.** Primary sources only, no AI, on the three partial fails and the fail. He reads the Jordan Compact negotiating history from World Bank documentation. He pulls the UNHCR and ILO employment reports side by side and reads the methodology sections. He re-runs the Spoken Feynman the next morning. All four pass.

**Day 3 — 24-Hour Reconstruction on the empirical dispute.** Writes the survey-versus-administrative-data argument from memory on blank paper. The reconstruction preserves the core distinction accurately with one minor gap: he writes that the administrative data produces a lower wage-suppression estimate without specifying the reason — that administrative data captures formal employment only and therefore measures a different population than the surveys. He goes back to the source and adds the methodological nuance. The reconstruction identified exactly where his schema was incomplete.

**Day 3 — 24-Hour Reconstruction on his own argument.** Reconstructs thesis, structure, and evidence chain from scratch. The reconstruction improves on the research paper — he leads with the methodological critique more directly than the paper does. Genuine schema: reconstruction that sharpens rather than degrades.

**Day 5 — Transfer Test on the labor market restriction argument.** Generates a target two disciplines away: undocumented agricultural workers in the US — same structural feature of legal exclusion from formal markets creating involuntary informality and employer capture of the wage gap. The structural mapping holds. Passes.

**Day 5 — Transfer Test on the host-community absorption finding.** Target: post-WWII displaced persons in West Germany. The mapping is partial — the labor demand conditions differ enough that the Jordan case cannot simply be read off the German one. The Transfer Test exposes a limit of the original claim Nicholas had not noticed: the absorption finding may be more contingent on specific labor demand conditions than his research had explicitly argued. He adds a sentence to his understanding.

**Day 7 — Spacing Test on all six concepts.** Five cold questions, 20 minutes, no notes. 5 of 5 on the concepts he built from primary sources, at schema level. 2 of 5 on the Jordan Compact and UNHCR/ILO discrepancy — passing but at surface level rather than schema level. Two more days of targeted review.

**The application.** Nicholas submits to Columbia. He can speak every sentence he has written about his research. No artifact is doing the work his head is supposed to do. The schema exists because the inference events happened during the research — in his head, over two and a half weeks of reading primary sources. The protocols told him which parts he built and which parts still needed work, with ten days still to go.

What the protocols did was not produce knowledge he did not have. They produced *information about* what he had — and they told him precisely where to spend the remaining days. That is what they are for.

## Verification Protocol Quick Reference

| Protocol | What it measures | Time required | When to run | The constraint that matters | Common failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Spoken Feynman Test | Whether you can produce a plain-language explanation from memory | 90 seconds + 5 min review | Day after new concept introduced | No notes; no AI; spoken aloud; listen back | Borrowed analogy mistaken for self-generated |
| 24-Hour Reconstruction | Storage strength at one-day delay | 25 min + 15 min comparison | 24 hours after a substantive study session | No intervening review — any peek invalidates it | The partial peek |
| Transfer Test | Schema generality vs. surface knowledge | 20 min + evaluation | 5+ days post-introduction | Two-discipline rule; mapping must generate a prediction | Too-near target; surface analogy mistaken for structural mapping |
| Spacing Test | Storage strength at high-stakes-relevant delay | 30 min per session | 7–14 days post-study; repeat at 10–20% of retention interval | No intervening review; schema-vs-surface scoring | Picking easy questions; pass/fail scoring instead of schema/surface |

## AI Wayback Machine

The protocols in this chapter share a single ancestor: the conviction that the mind can be measured. **Wilhelm Wundt** founded the world's first experimental psychology laboratory at the University of Leipzig in 1879, and with it the discipline that treats mental processes as objects of controlled observation rather than philosophical speculation. Wundt's method — *experimentelle Selbstbeobachtung*, experimental introspection — was a structured protocol: a trained subject in a controlled condition, given a defined stimulus, reporting the immediate contents of consciousness against a clock. The Spoken Feynman Test, the 24-Hour Reconstruction, the Spacing Test all share Wundt's basic move — they refuse to take the felt sense at face value, and they replace it with a procedure that can be run, scored, and re-run.
