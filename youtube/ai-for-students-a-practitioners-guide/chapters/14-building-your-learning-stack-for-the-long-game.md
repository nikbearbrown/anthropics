# Chapter 14 — Building Your Learning Stack for the Long Game

*The Bastani decrement is a one-semester number. The Matthew effect is what happens when you run that number for four years.*

> *"For to those who have, more will be given."* — Matthew 25:29 (the verse from which the educational "Matthew effect" takes its name)

---

It is December of freshman year. Two roommates. Same GPA — a 3.5 each. The transcripts are nearly identical. But the gap is starting to show, and it is worth understanding exactly where it is showing, because it is not where you would expect.

Alex is not lazy. He is a fluent user of AI. Every paper he has written has been drafted with Claude or ChatGPT. Every problem set has been worked with AI assistance. Every reading has been summarized before being engaged. His work product is polished. His grades are fine. His model of college is a stream of assignments to be completed efficiently, and AI helps him complete them efficiently.

Seth's grades are also fine. What differs is that Seth has been using AI the way this book argues it should be used — as a sparring partner, not as a ghostwriter. His papers are drafted by him and challenged by AI. His problem sets are attempted before hints are requested. He runs a simple verification system: he keeps a running file of atomic notes, written in his own words, and he runs a weekly Spoken Feynman Test on whatever he is supposed to understand.

Last week Alex was asked by his economics professor to explain a problem from his most recent problem set. He could not. The problem had been solved with AI assistance. He had the answer. He did not have the understanding. The professor was kind about it. The professor also noted it.

Last week Seth went to his professor's office and brought a one-page reading of a recent paper from the lab's research group — not a summary, a reading, with a question about a specific methodological choice the lab had made. The professor read the page, asked Seth to talk through his reasoning, and offered him a summer research position.

Same college. Same month. Same GPA. The transcript looks identical. What is different is what the year built.

The rest of this chapter is about why the gap grows, and the one system that captures everything the book has argued and runs it forward in time.

## Why Small Differences Compound

K. Anders Ericsson spent his career documenting how expert performance is built across domains — music, chess, medicine, sports. The 1993 paper with Krampe and Tesch-Römer is the canonical reference.[^ericsson1993] The empirical finding: expert-level capability is predicted not by raw hours of activity but by hours of *deliberate practice*. Four features define it: domain-specific structure, targeted correction of weaknesses, high concentration, and immediate specific feedback.

The plain-language version is this. Getting good at hard things over years requires repeated targeted struggle with specific feedback. Not comfortable practice. Not practice with the correct answer available before the attempt. Struggle with feedback.

This is why the Bastani performance paradox, which looked like a single-semester result, is actually a multi-year forecast. A student who uses AI to remove the struggle in every study session has spent years not doing deliberate practice. The capability gap with a deliberate-practice peer at year five is not 17 percentage points. It is qualitative.

Keith Stanovich's 1986 paper on reading added the multiplier.[^stanovich1986] He named it the Matthew effect, after the verse about those who have receiving more. His finding: children who decode well early read more, encounter more vocabulary, become better readers, read more. A reciprocal loop, not raw ability, drives the divergence. Small early advantages produce large late advantages because the early-skill student keeps doing the thing that builds the skill while the early-deficit student does it less.

The mechanism generalizes to any domain where current capability shapes the quality and quantity of future practice — which is almost all of them. Reading, mathematical reasoning, writing, problem-solving, original inquiry. The student who internalizes deliberate practice in the first year of college is doing qualitatively different study by the third year. The student who does not is doing more of the same comfortable work that produced the comfortable GPA.

Here is the compounding in specific form, which is the chapter's most extrapolated argument and the one to hold most loosely. It is direction, not determinism.

| Checkpoint | Capability-Builder | Capability-Borrower |
|---|---|---|
| End of Semester 1 | Can explain last week's problem set on a whiteboard without notes. Office-hours questions are getting sharper. | Can hand in polished work on time. Cannot reconstruct the reasoning behind it under questioning. |
| End of Semester 2 | First atomic-note archive crosses ~140 notes. Schema beginning to form across two courses. | Workflow is dependent on AI assistance for drafting and problem-solving. Comfortable, fluent, not yet alarmed. |
| End of Summer 1 | Independent reading project completed without AI in the drafting loop. One faculty contact established. | Summer is mostly off. No artifact produced. Returns in September at the same capability level as May. |
| End of Semester 4 | Recommended for research. Can write technical prose unassisted at length. Cross-links in archive begin appearing unplanned. | Struggling in courses that require closed-book technical writing. Recommendation letters would be thin. The transcript begins to diverge. |
| End of Summer 2 | First research position. One paper draft in progress. Mentor relationship is real. | Internship in a domain where AI fluency alone is the offering. No domain mentor. |
| End of Senior Year | Two published papers, graduate school admission, recommenders who can speak specifically to capability. | Degree in hand. Thin portfolio of AI-assisted work that cannot be reproduced in interviews. Tepid letters. |
| First Job, Year 1 | Earns the AI+1 wage premium — domain expertise plus AI fluency, with judgment to catch AI errors. | Produces work that looks like AI output because it is AI output. Premium over what clients can do themselves is shrinking. |

*Same GPA at Semester 1. Qualitative divergence by Semester 4. The transcript is the last thing to show it.*

End of semester one: indistinguishable on GPA. Capability-borrower's papers are more polished. Capability-builder's office-hours questions are slightly sharper.

End of semester four: capability-builder is being recommended for research. Capability-borrower is struggling in courses that require unassisted technical writing. The transcripts diverge.

End of senior year: capability-builder has two published papers and graduate school admission. Capability-borrower has a degree, a thin portfolio of AI-assisted work they cannot reproduce in interviews, and tepid recommendation letters.

First job: capability-builder earns the AI+1 wage premium because they have domain expertise the AI cannot supply and the judgment to tell when the AI is wrong. Capability-borrower produces work that looks like AI output because it is AI output. Their premium over what clients can produce themselves is shrinking.

None of this is about raw intelligence. Both students are working. The difference is the system — and the system determines whether the Matthew loop runs in the right direction.

## The Archive That Compounds

There is a physical residue of deliberate practice that is worth understanding, because it is the part of the system most students skip and the part whose value is hardest to see in the short run.

Niklas Luhmann, the German sociologist, produced more than 70 books and 400 articles in his career. He kept a system of approximately 90,000 atomic notes connected by explicit cross-references — what he called a Zettelkasten, a slip-box. Sönke Ahrens translated the method into a book in 2017.[^ahrens2017] The core: one idea per note, written in your own words, linked to where it came from and to related notes already in the system.

The method's pedagogical claim is the generation effect operating over years rather than over sessions. Writing in your own words at the moment of reading produces the memory and the connection that AI-summarized notes do not. The archive grows, the links multiply, and by year three the system contains cross-connections that were not planned when any individual note was written — concepts from a sophomore bioethics seminar linking to concepts from a junior statistics course linking to concepts from summer research, all in the same archive, all accessible when the senior thesis needs them.

The student who starts a Zettelkasten in September of freshman year and writes ten notes a week will have over a thousand notes by the end of sophomore year, with explicit cross-links across every course. When she sits down to write her senior thesis, she is not beginning from scratch. She is reorganizing material she has already processed and connected across two years. Her roommate with no archive faces three months of cold-start synthesis with material she has never deeply engaged.

The caveat: the empirical evidence on Zettelkasten outcomes is thinner than the framework deserves. The underlying generation effect from Slamecka and Graf is well-established. Whether the specific note-taking practice produces the compounding Ahrens claims is practitioner evidence rather than controlled evidence. The chapter recommends it on first-principles plus Luhmann's record. If you want the controlled empirical basis, it is the generation effect. The Zettelkasten is how the generation effect is run every day for four years.

One practical note: the tool does not matter. Obsidian, Logseq, Roam, plain markdown files, index cards. Pick one. Write the first note today. The compounding requires consistency, not software elegance.

## What the Labor Market Is Paying For

The economic argument closes the loop.

The PwC 2025 Global AI Jobs Barometer analyzed approximately one billion job ads. AI-skilled workers commanded a 56% wage premium in 2024 — doubled from 25% the prior year. Jobs requiring AI skills grew 7.5% even as total job postings fell 11.3%. The premium applies across marketing, finance, operations, medicine, law, and teaching.

The critical specification: the premium goes to AI-fluent domain experts who stayed in their domain. Not to career-switchers who abandoned their domain to become generic technologists. Not to people who learned to prompt. To people who kept the domain expertise they were building and added AI fluency on top.

The strongest evidence on the mechanism comes from Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond's study of 5,179 customer-support agents at a Fortune 500 software firm.[^brynjolfsson2025] AI access increased productivity by 15% on average. The gain was larger for less-experienced workers — AI disseminated the best practices of high performers to newer workers, accelerating movement down the experience curve. The implication is that AI is most valuable when it amplifies genuine domain competence, and least valuable — sometimes negative — when substituted for competence that has not been built.

Daron Acemoglu's macroeconomic model offers the longer-term caution.[^acemoglu2024] About 20% of US labor tasks are exposed to AI substitution. Of those, only about 23% can be profitably automated within a decade. AI follows historical capital-skill complementarity patterns, raising productivity of high-skill workers more than low-skill workers. The implication: the divergence is structural, not temporary.

The two papers are in productive tension. Brynjolfsson finds the leveling-up effect within a well-designed AI deployment. Acemoglu's model predicts that without deliberate design, the pattern favors high-skill workers. Both converge on the same student-level implication: build the domain expertise first; add AI fluency on top; the market will buy the combination.

The capability the market is paying 56% more for is precisely the capability this book has argued AI use should build rather than borrow: judgment, original synthesis, expert evaluation of AI outputs, navigating novel situations, building things that did not exist before. These are the Tier 4–7 capabilities in the book's framework. They are what Bastani 2025 found capability-borrowing destroys.

The economic argument and the cognitive argument converge. The 56% premium is the labor market pricing exactly what the Zettelkasten, the deliberate practice, and the phase gate are building.

## The System

Everything the book has argued reduces to one template, filled in before each semester begins.

For each course: what is the Human-Only Zone (what AI must not touch), what is the AI-open zone (what AI may do), when does the gate open (after what independent work), what verification protocol runs (which test and how often).

The learning stack template is not a good intention. It is a calendar artifact — specific protocols on specific days. The student who fills in the template and puts the protocols in their calendar has operationalized every chapter from 2 through 13 across their entire workload. The student who reads the book and does not fill in the template has acquired recognition memory of the argument without the schema.

Seth's stack for one semester, in abbreviated form:

*Organic Chemistry:* Human-Only Zone is mechanism prediction on new substrates. AI opens after written commitment. Verification is whiteboard reconstruction weekly.

*Comparative Politics:* Human-Only Zone is primary source reading and causal argument construction. AI opens after one-paragraph independent interpretation is written. Verification is three-claims test before each paper.

*Linear Algebra:* Human-Only Zone is problem setup and first-attempt derivations. AI opens with step-based hints only after Seth has attempted and can articulate where he is stuck. Verification is unassisted parallel problem set weekly.

*Intro to Cognitive Science — the Human-Only Zone commitment course:* AI does not open during reading, note-taking, or essay drafting. No exceptions. Verification is 24-hour closed-book reconstruction after each unit and Spoken Feynman Test on each major concept.

The commitment course is the chapter's most important prescription. One course per semester where the Human-Only Zone holds without exception. Not all courses — one. Chosen because its foundational skills compound most across the intended path. The skills built in that course are not borrowed; they are owned. The student arrives at year four with real capability in at least one domain they have worked at without a safety net.

The stack is filled in once, audited at mid-semester, and carried forward. The compounding is not in the individual session. It is in the consistency across semesters.

The end-state of this discipline, in its most operationalized form, is an artifact you can hand to another person. Before Seth arrived at college, the same discipline he is now running across his freshman stack had already produced two production AI agents — Walker, a senior Unity refactoring agent, and Zelda, a senior game-design-documentation consultant — both co-built with the author of this book and both deployed publicly at humanitarians.ai/tools. The full system prompts for each are reproduced in `chapters/98-appendix-walker-and-zelda.md`. They are reference implementations of the phase-gate, the Human-Only Zone, and the verification discipline this chapter has been describing — the conducting discipline turned into deployable form. The point of including them is not that every reader will build their own agent. The point is that the discipline this book teaches is concrete enough to be written down as a system prompt, audited line by line, and run against a tired user at 2 a.m. on a deadline. Walker and Zelda are what the learning stack looks like when it stops being a personal habit and becomes an artifact someone else can use. The student who reaches that level of articulation has, by definition, owned the discipline rather than borrowed it.

Seth has articulated the same insight from a different angle in his published Zebonastic criticism. In an architectural reconstruction of *Hollow Knight* — Team Cherry's 2017 game, built by a two-person studio without a traditional design document — he traces how the studio worked: sketchbooks served as the art bible, PlayMaker FSM graphs served as the technical bible, Kickstarter stretch goals served as the roadmap, the directors' day-to-day conversation served as the design log. Each artifact carried what would otherwise have lived in a separate document. The piece's closing line is the insight in its strongest form: *"The design document for Hollow Knight is Hollow Knight."* The discipline became the work; the work became its own documentation. That is the same principle Walker and Zelda embody. The learning stack at its end-state is not a binder of intentions filed somewhere. It is a body of work that contains its own discipline — atomic notes you can re-read, archives you can search, agents you have written down, papers you have drafted by yourself and can defend line by line. The student who has built this is not relying on the stack as a separate document. The stack has merged into the work.

## Exercises

### Warm-Up — The Single-Course Stack Row

Pick one course you are currently taking. In five minutes, fill in that course's row in the learning stack template: Human-Only Zone, AI-Open Zone, Phase Gate Trigger, Verification Protocol, Frequency.

Don't overthink it. Write what you actually do right now, not what you intend to do. If the Human-Only Zone cell is empty, that is the finding — capability-borrowing is likely happening in that course with no guard in place. If the Verification Protocol cell is empty, you have no way of knowing whether the schema is forming.

The exercise is not to design a better system. It is to discover what your current system actually is. Empty cells are information.

---

### Application — The Full Stack Template

Fill in the template for every course in your current or upcoming semester. Be honest — describe what you actually do, then identify what you want to change. Note empty cells. Note courses where the Human-Only Zone and the Verification Protocol columns are both populated versus courses where one or both are empty.

Then use Prompt 1 (the Learning Stack Audit) to identify the two entries most likely to collapse under time pressure. The audit does not redesign the stack — it locates the risks. You decide what to change.

---

### LLM Exercise — The Two-Year Projection

The thought experiment that makes the trajectory vivid. Run it before the next semester begins.

```
Based on my current learning stack design and my current courses,
project two trajectories for me at the end of my college career:

Trajectory A: I follow the stack with discipline. The Human-Only
Zones hold. The verification protocols run on schedule. The archive grows.

Trajectory B: I follow the stack in intent but not in practice.
The Human-Only Zones drift open under time pressure. Verification
slips. The archive is sparse.

For each trajectory, describe me at:
- End of semester 4 (sophomore year)
- End of summer 2
- End of senior year (graduation)

Be specific: what would I be able to do? What would I not be able to
do? What would mentors and recommenders be able to say about me?
What would my first-year work look like to my employer?
```

Recovery move if the projection is too generic: *"Be more specific. Name the courses I am taking. Name the skills I am building. Name the conversations with mentors that would be possible or impossible in each trajectory."*

The diagnostic is not whether the projection is accurate. It is whether the two trajectories are distinguishable. If they are not — if the capability-builder and capability-borrower arrive at the same place — the system is not doing anything. If they are distinguishable, the projection has made the compounding visible. Print both trajectories. Put them where you will see them.

---

### Synthesis — The One-Course Commitment

Use Prompt 6 to identify the one course in your current or upcoming semester where the Human-Only Zone commitment will produce the largest long-term capability gain. Write the commitment down. Specify what AI-off means for that course, what the verification protocol is, and how often it runs.

Then tell someone — a roommate, a study partner, a mentor — about the commitment. The social visibility is not motivational theater. It is the mechanism that distinguishes commitments that hold from commitments that dissolve under time pressure. The person you tell becomes an accountability structure; you lose something real if you dissolve the commitment in their presence.

At the end of the semester, audit: did the commitment hold? What did you build in that course that you could not have built with AI in the workflow? Use the answer as evidence for whether to expand the commitment next semester.

---

### Challenge — Start the Archive This Week

Use Prompt 5 to configure a Zettelkasten in whatever tool you choose. Write ten atomic notes from your current reading or coursework — one idea each, in your own words, linked to source and to any related notes already in the system.

Continue daily for the rest of the semester. At three months, audit: how many notes? How many cross-links? Which connections appeared that you did not plan when you wrote either note? The unplanned cross-links are the system working. They are the Matthew loop running in the right direction.

If the archive is empty at three months, the compounding is not happening. The chapter's argument about year-four capability rests on the archive growing for four years. It has to start somewhere. It starts this week.

## Prompt Reference

Seven prompts for the seven long-game operations.

**Prompt 1 — The Learning Stack Audit.** Use at mid-semester or semester end. Paste the filled-in stack for every course. Ask AI to identify the entry most likely to collapse into capability-borrowing under time pressure, any subject where the Human-Only Zone column is empty or thin, and one verification protocol not running often enough. No redesign — locate the risks.

**Prompt 2 — The Two-Year Projection.** Full text above in the LLM Exercise section.

**Prompt 3 — The Weekly Verification Schedule.** Paste your existing weekly schedule. Ask AI to identify which verification protocol is most diagnostic for each course, recommend a frequency, and suggest specific times that fit the existing schedule. Output is a calendar block you can paste into Google Calendar.

**Prompt 4 — The Prompt Library Development.** For a specific course, ask AI to help build a ten-prompt library: two Socratic, two step-based hint, two devil's advocate, two retrieval practice, two transfer. For each: the template, the use case, what good output looks like, what bad output (sycophancy default) looks like. Refined by use across the semester.

**Prompt 5 — The Zettelkasten Setup.** Specify the tool and ask for: the atomic-note template, link conventions, the permanent-versus-fleeting distinction for your workflow, and a weekly review practice. Then a ten-question diagnostic for each note you write — is it genuinely atomic? Are the connections real?

**Prompt 6 — The One-Course Commitment.** List current courses. Ask AI to identify the one course where the Human-Only Zone commitment will produce the largest long-term capability gain, considering which skills compound most across future courses, which assessments require unassisted capability, and which foundational needs align with your intended path. Commit to that course. Tell someone.

**Prompt 7 — The Mid-Semester Recalibration.** Paste the original stack. Describe what is working and what is not, honestly. Ask for the smallest adjustment that closes the gap between commitment and practice. Not a redesign — a patch.

## AI Wayback Machine

The ideas in this chapter didn't appear from nowhere. **Eric Hoffer (1898–1983)** is the closest thing American thought has produced to a pure model of the long game. Blinded as a child and self-taught after his sight returned, he spent his adult life as a migrant farmworker and then a San Francisco longshoreman, writing on the dock between shifts. He never enrolled in a university. The Berkeley library was his classroom; the public library card was his transcript. He produced ten books — including *The True Believer* — and a National Humanities Medal, on the strength of an archive built one notebook at a time across fifty years. The line worth carrying out of this chapter is his: *"In times of change, learners inherit the earth; while the learned find themselves beautifully equipped to deal with a world that no longer exists."* The capability-builder is a learner. The capability-borrower is acquiring the equipment of a world that is already changing.
