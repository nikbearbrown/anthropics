## Candidate 01 — Build a Perceptual Positioning Map with Claude
- Source: principles-marketing-with-llms/chapters/07-market-segmentation-targeting-and-positioning.md + LLM Exercise
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: Every strategy deck has a 2×2 perceptual map. Almost none of them are built from data. Claude can scaffold a real one from survey inputs — and show you the white space your competitors missed.
- The artifact: a Manim animated scatter plot showing brand positions on two axes (e.g., price vs. quality perception, or traditional vs. innovative), with brand labels appearing one by one, cluster regions shading in, and a white-space region highlighted as a positioning opportunity.
- Prompt seed: `claude "I have ratings data for 8 coffee brands on 6 attributes (price, quality, sustainability, innovation, accessibility, prestige) on a 1-10 scale. Run a simple 2D positioning analysis: compute pairwise correlations to find the two most independent dimensions, project brands onto them, and print coordinates I can plot. Then identify which quadrant has the fewest brands (the white space). Data: [paste ratings table]"`
- Read / check: Verify the two chosen axes are genuinely independent (correlation <0.3 between them); verify that brand coordinates are correctly computed; verify the white-space identification matches the quadrant with the lowest density of brands; check that the analysis reproduces the chapter's ADAMS criteria logic (is the white space accessible, differentiable, actionable, measurable, substantial?).
- Human supplies (Claude can't): **Actual brand rating data is required for authenticity** — either from a real survey (the human must design and run it, or purchase syndicated data) or from published brand perception research. A synthetic ratings table (8 brands, 6 attributes, author-generated) is acceptable for a teaching demo and is labeled as illustrative. The human must decide which two axes to present and why those dimensions matter to the category.
- Output medium: Manim (animated 2D scatter with brand labels appearing, cluster shading, and white-space callout).
- The change: add a third competitor that just launched into the white space and re-run — showing how the map shifts and whether the original positioning recommendation still holds.
- Teardown angle: The map reveals competitive density — not which brand is best, but which position is least contested. The white space is the strategy; the rest is execution. A map drawn without data is a story; a map drawn from data is a constraint.
- Exclusions: conjoint analysis methodology, brand equity measurement, customer lifetime value modeling.
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 02 — Build a Break-Even and Pricing Sensitivity Model with Claude
- Source: principles-marketing-with-llms/chapters/15-pricing-products-and-services.md + LLM Exercise
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: Amazon Prime raised its price three times without losing members — because the value ratio held. A break-even model shows exactly how much room you have to raise price before the math breaks.
- The artifact: a Manim animated break-even diagram (total revenue and total cost curves crossing at the break-even quantity) with an animated price sweep showing how break-even quantity shifts as price increases — and a second panel showing the value ratio V=B/P as a horizontal gauge with B fixed and P sliding.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Build a break-even model for a subscription service: fixed costs $500K/year, variable cost $8/subscriber/month, price $15/month. Compute: break-even subscribers, profit at 50K/75K/100K subscribers. Then run a price sensitivity: for prices from $10 to $25, show how break-even quantity changes. Show at what price a 10% churn increase eliminates all profit at 75K subscribers."`
- Read / check: Verify the break-even formula (fixed costs / (price - variable cost) per period); verify that the break-even point decreases as price increases (sensitivity direction is correct); verify the churn sensitivity calculation multiplies subscriber count by the churn rate and subtracts the lost revenue correctly; check that the profit calculation is (price - variable cost) × subscribers - fixed costs.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Nothing — fully synthetic. The chapter's Amazon Prime example provides the motivating narrative; the model uses illustrative parameters. For a production-grade video, real SaaS unit economics from a public company's S-1 filing would make it more credible, but the human would need to read the filing.
- Output medium: Manim (animated break-even diagram with price sweep animation and value-ratio gauge).
- The change: add a penetration-vs-skimming comparison panel — show the break-even timeline for a $25 skimming price vs. a $10 penetration price, assuming subscriber growth is faster at lower prices (demand elasticity = -1.5). Reveal that skimming breaks even sooner but leaves market share to competitors.
- Teardown angle: Price is the only P that generates revenue, and break-even is the floor that tells you how much room you have. The animation makes visible what the formula hides: as you raise price, the break-even point drops — but only until elasticity kicks in and volume collapses faster than margin grows.
- Exclusions: price discrimination, yield management, dynamic pricing algorithms, legal/antitrust price-fixing issues.
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 03 — Build a Customer Segmentation Profiler with Claude
- Source: principles-marketing-with-llms/chapters/07-market-segmentation-targeting-and-positioning.md + LLM Exercise
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: Mailchimp found segmented campaigns get 14.3% higher open rates. But most segmentation is demographic guesswork — building a real behavioral segment profile with Claude reveals what the demographics miss.
- The artifact: a Manim animated radar/spider chart for 3–4 buyer personas (each a polygon on the radar), with axes for the AIO dimensions (activities, interests, opinions) plus behavioral variables (usage frequency, price sensitivity, benefit sought) — the personas appear one by one, and a final frame overlays them to show the differentiation.
- Prompt seed: `claude "I'm building buyer personas for a fitness app. For each of these four segments — [Health Optimizers: data-driven, 28-45, high income], [Casual Movers: habit-formation seekers, 22-35], [Comeback Athletes: lapsed gym-goers, 35-55], [Senior Wellness: 60+, doctor-referred] — generate: primary AIO profile, top 3 benefit sought, price sensitivity (low/medium/high), content type preference, and the one thing that would make them churn. Format as a comparison table."`
- Read / check: Verify that the four segments are genuinely differentiated (they should not share the same top benefit or churn trigger); verify the AIO profiles are internally consistent (e.g., Health Optimizers should have data-forward interests); check that price sensitivity varies meaningfully across segments (uniform sensitivity means under-segmentation); verify the benefit-sought list maps to real product features rather than aspirational marketing language.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Real survey data or customer interview transcripts make the personas authentic rather than synthetic — the human must conduct or commission primary research to validate Claude's generated profiles. For a teaching demo, the synthetic personas are labeled as illustrative. **The authentic version requires primary research the human must supply.**
- Output medium: Manim (animated radar/spider chart with four persona polygons appearing sequentially, then overlaid).
- The change: run the personas through the ADAMS criteria filter (accessible, differentiable, actionable, measurable, substantial) — Claude rates each segment green/yellow/red on each criterion, and the viewer sees which segments are worth pursuing and which fail the actionability test.
- Teardown angle: Segmentation without criteria is just categorization. ADAMS is the filter that turns interesting customer observations into actual marketing decisions. Most "buyer personas" fail the actionable and measurable tests — the ADAMS analysis reveals why.
- Exclusions: ethnographic research methods, A/B test design, attribution modeling, CRM database segmentation.
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 04 — Research Competitive Ad Spend and Channel Mix with Claude
- Source: principles-marketing-with-llms/chapters/16-integrated-marketing-communications.md + 17 (Promotion Mix) — RESEARCH
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: You can read a competitor's marketing strategy from their public disclosures, trade press, and ad creative — without buying a single market research report.
- The artifact: a synthesized comparison table (4–6 competitors) showing estimated channel mix (digital/TV/print/OOH percentage), messaging themes, and IMC integration signals — structured as a sourced brief with citations, rendered as a Remotion animated comparison table that builds column by column.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Research the integrated marketing communications strategy for [Brand A, Brand B, Brand C] in the [consumer electronics / CPG / SaaS] category. For each brand: (1) identify their dominant advertising channels from public sources (annual reports, trade press, AdAge, Adweek); (2) characterize their core messaging theme from recent campaign headlines; (3) assess their IMC integration — do their digital, social, and traditional channels carry consistent messaging? Cite your sources. Flag any claim you cannot verify from a citable source."`
- Read / check: Verify that each cited source (AdAge, trade press, annual report) is a real publication and that the specific claim it supports exists in that source; verify that channel mix estimates are labeled as estimates when not from official disclosures; check that messaging themes are grounded in actual campaign taglines or copy, not inferred from brand image.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Claude's training data cutoff limits currency — the human must verify that cited campaigns are from the correct time period and are not superseded by more recent campaigns. **Ad spend figures require Kantar/Nielsen data or annual report disclosures; Claude cannot produce verified spend numbers.** The human must cross-check any spend claim against a primary source.
- Output medium: Remotion animated comparison table (columns appearing per competitor, with source footnotes fading in, and a final channel-mix pie chart per brand).
- The change: add a fourth competitor that entered the market within the past 12 months and lacks public data — show Claude reasoning about what channel mix is *implied* by their pricing, distribution, and visible creative, then flag the inference explicitly.
- Teardown angle: IMC integration is visible in public data if you know where to look — channel choices, message consistency, and timing alignment all leave observable traces. Most competitive analysis skips this because it requires synthesis across heterogeneous sources. Claude collapses the synthesis step.
- Exclusions: first-party ad server data, customer-level attribution, A/B test results, media mix modeling outputs.
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 05 — Build a Digital Marketing Funnel Metric Dashboard with Claude
- Source: principles-marketing-with-llms/chapters/19-direct-online-social-media-and-mobile-marketing.md + LLM Exercise
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: Website traffic means nothing without conversion rate. Conversion rate means nothing without cost-per-acquisition. Claude can build the funnel dashboard that connects them — and show you where your money is actually going.
- The artifact: a Manim animated funnel diagram (impressions → clicks → site visits → adds to cart → purchases) with animated numbers populating each stage, conversion rates appearing at each step, and a final cost-per-acquisition calculation with a comparison bar showing how changing one stage's conversion by 10% moves the CPA.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Build a digital marketing funnel model: total impressions 2M/month, CTR 2.1%, landing page conversion 3.8%, add-to-cart rate 22%, checkout completion 68%, AOV $85, ad spend $42K/month. Compute: total purchases, revenue, CPA, ROAS. Then sensitivity: show how CPA changes if I improve (a) CTR by 0.5pp, (b) landing page conversion by 1pp, (c) checkout completion by 5pp. Which improvement gives the best CPA reduction?"`
- Read / check: Verify the funnel math is multiplicative (each stage applies to the output of the prior stage, not to total impressions); verify ROAS = revenue / ad spend (not profit / spend); verify CPA = ad spend / purchases (not per session); check that the sensitivity analysis correctly isolates each variable while holding others constant.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Nothing — fully synthetic with industry-average conversion rates. **For a production video, real Google Analytics or Meta Ads Manager data (with a specific account's metrics) makes the dashboard authentic** — the human must export their own funnel data. The synthetic version uses published industry benchmarks and is labeled as such.
- Output medium: Manim (animated funnel with stage-by-stage number reveals and a sensitivity tornado chart).
- The change: add an email re-targeting loop — a second funnel where abandoned-cart users receive an email with a 15% discount, reenter the funnel at "add to cart," and show how the blended CPA across both funnels changes.
- Teardown angle: Most marketers optimize the cheapest metric to move (CTR) rather than the one with the highest downstream impact (checkout completion). The sensitivity analysis makes the leverage point visible: a 5% checkout improvement beats a 0.5pp CTR improvement by a factor of 3× in CPA reduction.
- Exclusions: multi-touch attribution modeling, LTV-based bidding, programmatic auction mechanics, SEO vs. SEM comparison.
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 06 — Research Customer Journey Mapping with Claude
- Source: principles-marketing-with-llms/chapters/05-consumer-markets-and-purchasing-behavior.md + LLM Exercise — RESEARCH
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: The textbook says customers go through recognition → information search → evaluation → decision → post-purchase. The real customer journey for a $400 software purchase looks nothing like that — and mapping it reveals the gap.
- The artifact: a synthesized customer journey map for a specific product category (SaaS, consumer electronics, or B2B tool) showing touchpoints, emotional state, and decision-influencing factors at each stage — rendered as a Remotion animated horizontal timeline with emotional-state curve and key touchpoint icons appearing.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Research the typical customer journey for purchasing B2B project management software (e.g., Asana, Monday, Notion tier). Map: (1) what triggers the purchase decision (recognition stage), (2) where buyers research options (information search — name specific channels, review sites, peer networks), (3) how they evaluate alternatives (evaluation criteria — name the top 5 from G2/Capterra reviews), (4) what tips the final decision (decision stage — pricing, trial offer, champion?), (5) what drives retention vs. churn post-purchase. Cite specific sources for each stage claim."`
- Read / check: Verify that cited review sites (G2, Capterra) are real and that specific claims about evaluation criteria come from publicly available aggregate review data; verify that the trigger scenarios are plausible for the category (team scaling, failed process, competitor adoption — not generic); check that each stage cites at least one source rather than reflecting generic marketing-textbook language.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): **Customer interviews or first-party behavioral data are required for a validated journey map** — Claude synthesizes from public sources (reviews, case studies, press), which captures articulated preferences but misses observed behavior. The human should flag when Claude is synthesizing inferences vs. reporting stated data.
- Output medium: Remotion animated horizontal timeline with emotional-state arc curve and touchpoint labels appearing stage by stage.
- The change: add a competitor-comparison layer — for two competing products, show where the journey diverges (one relies on champion-led internal advocacy; the other on self-serve trial). Reveal why the same customer buys different products based on how they enter the funnel.
- Teardown angle: The five-stage buying model is a framework, not a map. Real journeys have loops, shortcuts, and detours — and the research reveals which stages are actually load-bearing for the category. Most go-to-market failures happen because someone optimized stage 3 (evaluation) when the real bottleneck was stage 1 (trigger recognition).
- Exclusions: customer lifetime value calculation, NPS/CSAT measurement methodology, retention cohort analysis.
- Score: 7/10

## Candidate 07 — Build a Marketing Research Survey Design Validator with Claude
- Source: principles-marketing-with-llms/chapters/08-marketing-research-and-market-intelligence.md + LLM Exercise
- Lane: BUILD (Claude Code)
- Hook: LEGO surveyed 7,000 parents and children to prove gender stereotypes shape creative play — and the research design is what made the campaign credible. Claude can audit whether your survey design actually answers the question you think it does.
- The artifact: a structured audit report (Remotion animated slide) showing a survey's question-by-question critique against seven research design criteria (research objective alignment, response scale validity, sampling method, question bias, leading language, double-barreled questions, order effects) with green/yellow/red flags per question.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Audit this 10-question brand perception survey against standard marketing research quality criteria. For each question: (1) flag leading language ('Don't you agree that...'); (2) flag double-barreled questions ('Rate both quality AND value'); (3) check that the response scale is appropriate for the question type (Likert for attitudes, not facts); (4) identify order effects (satisfaction before NPS violates standard order). Survey: [paste questions]. Then rate the overall research design on: internal validity, construct validity, sampling clarity."`
- Read / check: Verify that identified leading-language issues are genuine (e.g., "Don't you agree" is definitively leading; "Do you think" is borderline); verify that double-barreled flags correctly identify questions with compound stimuli; check that scale mismatches are correctly identified (e.g., using a frequency scale for an attitude question); verify the validity ratings are grounded in the specific issues identified, not just generic warnings.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): **A real survey instrument is required** — the human must draft or supply the actual survey questions. The video demonstrates Claude auditing a realistic (intentionally flawed) survey the human writes for the purpose, or a real survey from their own marketing work.
- Output medium: Remotion animated audit table (questions appearing in rows, flag colors populating column by column, final validity ratings appearing at bottom).
- The change: show Claude rewriting the three worst questions to remove bias — then compare the pre/post versions side by side and explain what response behavior would change as a result.
- Teardown angle: Most marketing surveys are written by people who want to confirm what they already believe — the methodology validates or invalidates the business case. Claude as an audit tool catches the design errors before the survey ships, when fixing them is free.
- Exclusions: conjoint analysis design, multivariate statistical analysis, ethnographic research protocol.
- Score: 7/10

## Candidate 08 — Research Sustainability Marketing Claims vs. Reality with Claude
- Source: principles-marketing-with-llms/chapters/22-sustainable-marketing-the-new-paradigm.md — RESEARCH
- Lane: RESEARCH (Claude assistant)
- Hook: "100% sustainable" is a claim on packaging. Whether it holds up to scrutiny is a 30-minute research task with Claude — and the gap between claim and evidence is where the brand risk lives.
- The artifact: a sourced comparison brief for 3–4 brands making sustainability claims — with a claim-vs-evidence table (claim stated, evidence cited, third-party certification status, gap assessment) rendered as a Remotion animated reveal table with a final red/yellow/green credibility rating per brand.
- Prompt seed: `claude "Research the sustainability claims of [Brand A, Brand B, Brand C] in the [apparel / food / packaging] category. For each brand: (1) identify their primary sustainability claim from their website or annual report; (2) find third-party certifications (B Corp, FSC, Fair Trade, USDA Organic) that support or contradict the claim; (3) identify any FTC Green Guide or regulatory challenges to the claim; (4) find one independent fact-check (NGO report, journalist investigation, academic paper). Rate each claim as: verified, partially verified, unverified, or greenwashing. Cite all sources."`
- Read / check: Verify cited certifications exist for the brands claimed (B Corp certification is publicly searchable; FSC chain-of-custody can be verified at fsc.org); verify that FTC challenges are real documented cases (the FTC maintains public records of enforcement actions); check that academic/NGO sources are real organizations with the claimed reports; flag any claim rated "verified" that lacks a specific third-party certification.
- Human supplies (Claude can't): Claude's training cutoff may miss recent certification changes or regulatory actions. **The human must verify that current certification status is up to date** (brands gain and lose certifications; a quick check at the certification body's public registry takes 5 minutes per brand). Recent FTC actions require checking the FTC's public enforcement database.
- Output medium: Remotion animated comparison table (claim → evidence → certification → credibility rating appearing column by column per brand).
- The change: show how the same brand's claim looks different depending on the analytical frame (lifecycle analysis vs. carbon offset accounting vs. supply chain transparency) — revealing that "sustainable" is not one thing, and the choice of frame is itself a marketing decision.
- Teardown angle: Sustainability claims live on a spectrum from verified-and-certified to pure greenwashing — and the research methodology to distinguish them is teachable. The credibility rating is not an opinion; it follows from evidence. Claude collapses the multi-source synthesis that most people don't have time to do.
- Exclusions: lifecycle assessment methodology, carbon accounting standards (GHG Protocol detail), ESG investment screening criteria.
- Score: 7/10
