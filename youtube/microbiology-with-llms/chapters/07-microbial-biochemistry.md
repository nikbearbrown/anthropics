# Chapter 07 — Microbial Biochemistry

*The cell is a chemistry experiment that has been refining itself for four billion years.*

## The question before the answer

If you reduce a bacterial cell to its components, you find roughly the following: 70% water, 15% protein, 7% nucleic acid (DNA and RNA), 4% lipid, 3% polysaccharide, 1% small organic molecules, and a trace of inorganic ions. By mass, those proportions are not very different from a human cell, a plant cell, or a single-celled fungus.

The thing that distinguishes a cell from any other concentration of those molecules is *organization*. The carbon atoms in a cell are arranged into specific molecules. The molecules are arranged into specific structures. The structures are arranged in space such that the metabolic reactions happen in the right order, the right place, and at the right rate. None of this is exotic chemistry. Most of it is the chemistry of carbon bonded to hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur, and phosphorus, in patterns that biology has been refining for billions of years.

This chapter is about that chemistry. Not as a list of facts to memorize — there are too many — but as a small set of design principles. Carbohydrates handle energy storage and structure. Lipids handle membranes and longer-term energy storage. Proteins do almost everything else. Nucleic acids store the instructions for making proteins. Each class has a few characteristic features that make it suited to its job. Once you understand the features, the names become memorable.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

1. Identify common functional groups in organic molecules and predict how they affect reactivity.
2. Distinguish monomers from polymers across each major class (carbohydrate, lipid, protein, nucleic acid) and describe how polymers are built and broken down.
3. Describe the structure of a phospholipid bilayer and explain why it forms spontaneously in water.
4. Explain the four levels of protein structure and predict how a single mutation could destroy function.
5. Describe how a clinical microbiology lab uses biochemical tests (carbon source utilization, MALDI-TOF) to identify bacteria.

Prerequisites: Chapters 1–6. Some high-school chemistry helps.

## The elements

Six elements make up about 99% of the dry mass of every cell on Earth: carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur. The acronym CHNOPS captures them, and it captures essentially all of biochemistry.

Carbon is the backbone. A carbon atom forms four covalent bonds, and those bonds can be with hydrogen, with other carbons (forming long chains and rings), or with the other CHNOPS elements (forming the reactive groups that make biology happen). The ability of carbon to form stable chains and rings of essentially unlimited length is what makes organic chemistry possible at all. Silicon, the only other element with comparable bonding flexibility, forms less stable chains and never became the basis for biology on Earth — though science-fiction writers have been speculating about silicon life for a century.

Hydrogen and oxygen come together as water, the solvent in which all the rest of biology happens. They also build the hydroxyl groups, carboxylic acid groups, and various other functional groups that determine how molecules interact.

Nitrogen forms three bonds and is found in every amino acid (and therefore every protein), every nucleotide (and therefore every nucleic acid), and many other key molecules.

Phosphorus is the energy element. ATP, the molecule cells use to store and transfer chemical energy, contains three phosphate groups. The bonds between phosphates store energy that is released when a phosphate is cleaved off. Phosphorus is also in the DNA and RNA backbone.

Sulfur builds the disulfide bonds that lock protein structures in place and is the active center of several important coenzymes.

A handful of other elements (sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, chlorine, iron, and several trace metals) play essential roles as ions or as cofactors in specific enzymes. But the bulk of cellular chemistry is C, H, N, O, P, S.

## Functional groups

When carbon and the other CHNOPS atoms are arranged in particular patterns, the patterns acquire characteristic chemistry. These patterns are called *functional groups*, and you can predict an enormous amount about a molecule from its functional groups.

The handful that matter most:

- **Hydroxyl (–OH)**: makes a molecule polar and water-soluble. Found in alcohols (like ethanol) and sugars.
- **Carbonyl (>C=O)**: the C=O double bond is reactive. In a chain, this is a ketone; at the end, an aldehyde. Sugars contain carbonyls.
- **Carboxyl (–COOH)**: a carbonyl plus a hydroxyl. Acidic — it readily gives up its hydrogen as H⁺. Found in fatty acids, amino acids, and most organic acids.
- **Amino (–NH₂)**: basic — readily accepts H⁺. Found in amino acids, hence the name.
- **Phosphate (–OPO₃²⁻)**: highly charged. The phosphate-phosphate bonds in ATP store cellular energy.
- **Sulfhydryl (–SH)**: forms disulfide bonds (–S–S–) with other sulfhydryls, locking protein structures together.
- **Methyl (–CH₃)**: nonpolar, doesn't do much chemistry on its own, but used widely as a chemical tag (DNA methylation regulates gene expression; protein methylation regulates activity).

Most biochemistry is built from molecules with these groups attached to carbon backbones in particular arrangements. The arrangement is what generates the specific behavior. Two molecules can have the same atoms in different arrangements — *isomers* — and behave completely differently. Glucose and fructose are both C₆H₁₂O₆. Glucose has an aldehyde; fructose has a ketone. They taste different, metabolize differently, and serve different roles. Same atoms, different chemistry.

## Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are roughly *carbon hydrates* — molecules with the empirical formula (CH₂O)ₙ for small *n*. They are the cell's most abundant class of molecules by mass, and they do two main jobs: store energy and build structures.

A **monosaccharide** is a single sugar molecule — glucose, fructose, galactose, ribose, deoxyribose. The most important by far is glucose. Glucose enters every cell on Earth as fuel for glycolysis (Chapter 8) and as a carbon source for nearly every biosynthetic pathway.

A **disaccharide** is two monosaccharides linked by a glycosidic bond. Sucrose (table sugar) is glucose plus fructose. Lactose (milk sugar) is glucose plus galactose. Maltose is two glucoses. Each disaccharide is broken back into its components by a specific enzyme — sucrase, lactase, maltase. People with lactose intolerance lack a functional lactase enzyme; the undigested lactose passes to the colon, where bacteria ferment it, producing gas and the characteristic symptoms.

A **polysaccharide** is many monosaccharides linked together. The job a polysaccharide does depends on which sugar is the monomer and how the monomers are linked.

- **Starch** is many glucose units linked in alpha bonds (the orientation of the glycosidic bond). Plants use starch to store energy. Human enzymes can break alpha bonds, so starch is digestible.
- **Glycogen** is also glucose units in alpha bonds, but more branched. Animals (including humans) store glycogen in liver and muscle as their carbohydrate reserve.
- **Cellulose** is glucose units in *beta* bonds. The orientation is different. Human digestive enzymes cannot break beta bonds. Cellulose is therefore indigestible to humans (dietary fiber) but digestible to organisms with the right enzymes (cows, termites, gut bacteria). Cellulose is the most abundant organic polymer on Earth — it builds the cell walls of plants.
- **Chitin** is N-acetylglucosamine units in beta bonds — like cellulose but with a nitrogen-containing modification. Builds the cell walls of fungi and the exoskeletons of insects and crustaceans.
- **Peptidoglycan** is a polysaccharide cross-linked by short peptide chains. Builds the cell walls of bacteria. The structure is the target of penicillin and other beta-lactam antibiotics.

The same building block (glucose) in slightly different bond geometry produces materials with radically different properties — soft starch granules in a potato, rigid cellulose fibers in a tree trunk. The information is in the bond, not just in the monomer.

## Lipids

Lipids are a heterogeneous group defined by what they do, not by a shared structure. They are hydrophobic — they don't dissolve in water — and they include fats, oils, waxes, steroids, and phospholipids.

### Fatty acids and triglycerides

A **fatty acid** is a long hydrocarbon chain with a carboxyl group at one end. The chain is usually 12 to 24 carbons long. *Saturated* fatty acids have all single bonds in the chain and are straight; they pack tightly together and are solid at room temperature (butter, lard). *Unsaturated* fatty acids have one or more double bonds, which introduce kinks in the chain; they pack less tightly and are liquid at room temperature (olive oil, fish oil).

A **triglyceride** is three fatty acids attached to a glycerol backbone. Triglycerides are the main long-term energy storage form in animals — fat in adipose tissue is mostly triglyceride. One gram of triglyceride stores about 9 kcal of energy, more than twice as much per gram as carbohydrate, because the hydrocarbon chain is highly reduced (lots of carbon-hydrogen bonds, lots of energy to release on oxidation).

### Phospholipids and the bilayer

A **phospholipid** is structurally similar to a triglyceride but with one fatty acid replaced by a phosphate group connected to a small polar head group. The molecule now has two distinct ends: a polar phosphate head (hydrophilic — "water-loving") and two nonpolar fatty acid tails (hydrophobic — "water-fearing").

In water, phospholipids spontaneously arrange themselves into a *bilayer*: two parallel layers with the polar heads facing outward toward the water and the nonpolar tails facing inward, away from the water. This is not a designed structure. It is the thermodynamically favored arrangement. The hydrophobic tails are repelled by water; the polar heads are attracted. The bilayer is the lowest-energy way for the molecules to organize.

The bilayer is selectively permeable: water and small uncharged molecules can cross by diffusion; ions and charged molecules cannot, because the hydrophobic core repels them. This is one of the most consequential pieces of self-assembly chemistry in biology. Life requires a boundary between inside and outside, and the phospholipid bilayer provides one for free, given the right molecules in water. It is also the reason that mixing oil and water demonstrates the basic principle of cell biology in your kitchen.

## Amino acids and proteins

Proteins are the workhorses of the cell. They are enzymes, they are structural components, they are signaling molecules, they are transporters, they are antibodies, they are motors. Almost everything a cell does, at some step, involves a protein.

A protein is a polymer of **amino acids**. There are 20 standard amino acids used by life on Earth. Each has a central carbon (the alpha carbon) bonded to four things: a hydrogen, an amino group, a carboxyl group, and a side chain that varies. The side chain is what distinguishes the amino acids. It can be small (glycine's side chain is just hydrogen), hydrophobic (alanine, valine, leucine, isoleucine), polar (serine, threonine), charged (lysine and arginine are basic; aspartate and glutamate are acidic), aromatic (phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan), or contain sulfur (cysteine, methionine).

Two amino acids link by forming a *peptide bond* between the carboxyl of one and the amino of the next, releasing a water molecule. The result is a dipeptide. Many amino acids linked together is a polypeptide. A protein is a polypeptide (or several polypeptides) folded into a functional shape.

### The four levels of protein structure

**Primary structure**: the linear sequence of amino acids, from the N-terminus (amino end) to the C-terminus (carboxyl end). The sequence is determined by the gene that encodes the protein. A typical protein has 100 to 1000 amino acids.

**Secondary structure**: local patterns of folding within the chain, stabilized by hydrogen bonds between backbone atoms. The two dominant patterns are the **alpha helix** (the chain coils like a spring) and the **beta sheet** (segments of chain lie alongside each other in extended strands). Many proteins are mosaics of helix and sheet regions.

**Tertiary structure**: the overall three-dimensional fold of a single polypeptide. This is what most people mean when they talk about "the structure" of a protein. The fold is stabilized by interactions between side chains — hydrophobic clustering, hydrogen bonds, salt bridges, disulfide bonds. A protein's tertiary structure is largely determined by its primary structure (the famous Anfinsen experiment in 1961 showed that a denatured protein, given the right conditions, will refold spontaneously to its functional structure). [^1] The sequence specifies the fold.

**Quaternary structure**: the assembly of multiple polypeptide chains into a single functional unit. Hemoglobin has four chains (two alpha, two beta). Some viral capsids have hundreds of identical chains. Quaternary structure is how the cell builds complex molecular machines from simpler components.

### Why folding matters

The shape of a protein determines its function. An enzyme's active site is a particular three-dimensional pocket shaped to bind a particular substrate. An antibody's binding site is a particular three-dimensional cleft shaped to grip a particular antigen. A receptor's ligand-binding domain is a particular pocket shaped to recognize a particular signaling molecule.

A single amino acid substitution can destroy the function. Sickle-cell anemia is caused by a single mutation in the beta chain of hemoglobin, where glutamate at position 6 is replaced by valine. The substitution changes the surface chemistry of the hemoglobin molecule in a way that lets molecules stick together under low oxygen, distorting red blood cells. One point mutation, one amino acid change, a disease.

The protein-folding problem — predicting the three-dimensional structure of a protein from its sequence — was an open problem in biology for fifty years. As of 2021, it has been largely solved by AlphaFold and related methods, which use deep learning trained on the database of known protein structures to predict structures for sequences that have not been crystallographically determined. [^2] This is one of the most consequential applications of machine learning to biology and we will encounter it again in chapters on drug design.

## Identifying bacteria by biochemistry

Clinical microbiology labs identify bacteria from clinical samples partly by what the bacteria look like (Gram stain, morphology) and partly by what they can do biochemically. A biochemical battery is a panel of tests that measures whether a bacterium can use specific carbon sources, produce specific enzymes, or carry out specific metabolic conversions.

Classic biochemical tests:

- **Catalase test**: does the organism break down hydrogen peroxide to water and oxygen? Distinguishes *Staphylococcus* (catalase positive) from *Streptococcus* (catalase negative).
- **Oxidase test**: does the organism contain cytochrome c oxidase? Distinguishes *Pseudomonas* and *Neisseria* (positive) from enteric Gram-negative rods like *E. coli* (negative).
- **Coagulase test**: does the organism coagulate plasma? Distinguishes *Staphylococcus aureus* (positive) from other staphylococci (mostly negative).
- **Urease test**: does the organism hydrolyze urea? *Helicobacter pylori* and *Proteus* are strong urease producers.
- **Sugar fermentation tests**: which sugars can the organism ferment? Different species have characteristic fermentation patterns.
- **Indole test**: can the organism cleave tryptophan to produce indole? *E. coli* is indole positive.

Commercial systems like API 20E run a battery of 20 or so tests in parallel and give a numeric code that maps to a species identification. They work well for the bacteria they were designed for.

The newer approach is **MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry**. The lab takes a colony of bacteria, embeds it in a matrix, hits it with a laser to ionize and vaporize the proteins, and measures the mass-to-charge ratio of the ions as they fly through a tube. The result is a *protein mass spectrum* — a fingerprint of the major proteins (mostly ribosomal) in the cell. The fingerprint is compared to a database of known species.

MALDI-TOF can identify most clinically relevant bacteria in about a minute from a colony — compared to a day or more for traditional biochemical batteries. It has revolutionized clinical microbiology since about 2010. The cost per identification dropped from dollars to cents. The time to diagnosis dropped from a workday to a coffee break. The bacteriologist's job has not gone away, but it has been transformed.

## What the chapter is really about

Biochemistry is the level of biological organization where the abstractions of molecular biology (genes, enzymes, signaling pathways) connect to the chemistry of carbon and hydrogen and oxygen. Above this level, you talk about systems. Below it, you talk about chemistry. Biochemistry is where the two languages meet.

For microbiology specifically, biochemistry matters because microbial identification and antimicrobial drug design both happen at this level. Knowing that *Helicobacter pylori* produces urease tells you both how to identify it (urea breath test) and how to imagine attacking it (urease inhibitors). Knowing that fungal membranes contain ergosterol tells you both how to identify fungi and how to design antifungals selectively. The biochemical differences between pathogens and hosts are the entire surface on which selective therapy is built.

I want you to leave this chapter with one habit: when you read about a new pathogen, ask what biochemistry it does that you don't. The answer to that question is where the therapy goes.

## Still puzzling

I do not understand why protein folding produces such consistent native structures from such a high-dimensional search space. A protein with 100 amino acids has roughly 100³⁰⁰ possible conformations. Random search through that space would take longer than the age of the universe. Yet the protein folds to its native structure in milliseconds. The Levinthal paradox — Cyrus Levinthal's 1969 observation — has been the subject of fifty years of theoretical and computational work, and we now have good schematic explanations (folding funnels, hierarchical folding, kinetic pathways), but I am not sure the explanations fully account for the speed. AlphaFold can *predict* the final structure without explaining *how* the protein gets there.

## What would change my mind

The five-decade-old view that primary sequence fully determines tertiary structure has held up well but is not absolute. Some proteins require chaperones to fold correctly; some adopt different stable structures depending on cellular conditions (intrinsically disordered proteins, prions). If a substantial fraction of cellular proteins turned out to have multiple native structures determined by context rather than sequence alone, the Anfinsen-style picture would need updating. `[verify: current literature on intrinsically disordered proteins and conformational ensemble views as of 2026]`
