# Chapter 25 — Circulatory and Lymphatic System Infections

*A pathogen in the blood is a pathogen in every organ at once.*

## The question before the answer

Blood reaches every tissue in your body. The circulatory system delivers oxygen and nutrients, removes waste, transports hormones and immune cells, and maintains temperature. It also distributes pathogens, if any get in. A bacterium in the bloodstream is, within minutes, in your liver, brain, kidneys, joints, and bone marrow.

This is part of why bloodstream infections are so dangerous. A localized skin infection, even a serious one, is contained. A localized pneumonia is contained. A bacterium in the blood is everywhere. The immune response — also distributed through blood — is global, but so is the damage if the response fails.

The lymphatic system runs in parallel to the cardiovascular system, returning interstitial fluid to the bloodstream and filtering it through lymph nodes that serve as sites of immune sampling and response. Many pathogens move through lymph as they spread, and lymphatic involvement (swollen lymph nodes, lymphangitis) is often the first clinical sign of significant infection.

The point of this chapter is to map infections that primarily involve blood and lymph: the systemic bacterial infections that cause sepsis, the vector-borne diseases that take advantage of blood transit, the parasitic infections of red blood cells (malaria, babesiosis), the viruses that cause hemorrhagic fevers, and the HIV/AIDS story that has shaped modern infectious disease medicine.

6. Identify lymphatic system infections, particularly mononucleosis and filariasis.

## Circulatory and lymphatic anatomy

The **circulatory system**: heart, arteries, capillaries, veins. About 5 liters of blood in an adult. The heart pumps roughly 5 liters/minute at rest, so the entire blood volume circulates approximately once per minute.

The **lymphatic system**: lymphatic capillaries collect interstitial fluid; lymph vessels carry it through lymph nodes; the largest lymphatic (thoracic duct) returns lymph to the venous circulation near the subclavian veins. Lymph nodes are filters that contain dense aggregates of immune cells. Lymphoid organs — thymus, spleen, lymph nodes, tonsils, Peyer's patches, bone marrow — are sites of immune development and response.

Why these systems normally lack microbiota: any organism in blood or lymph is exposed to the immune system globally. Complement, phagocytes, antibodies — the body's full immune machinery — has access. Transient bacteremia happens (from tooth brushing, defecation, minor trauma) and is rapidly cleared. Sustained bacteremia requires either ongoing release of bacteria from a source or impaired immune clearance.

## Definitions of sepsis

These have been refined over the years. As of 2016 (Sepsis-3 consensus):

**Bacteremia**: bacteria in the bloodstream. Can be transient (from minor procedures), intermittent (from a fluctuating source), or continuous (endocarditis, indwelling infected catheter).

**Sepsis**: life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. The previous "SIRS" (systemic inflammatory response syndrome) framework has been replaced; the current definition emphasizes organ dysfunction.

**Septic shock**: a subset of sepsis with circulatory and cellular/metabolic abnormalities — particularly persistent hypotension requiring vasopressors despite adequate fluid resuscitation, with elevated lactate.

The progression from infection to septic shock is the immune response losing control. Cytokines drive vasodilation, vascular leak, and coagulation abnormalities; organ perfusion declines; multi-organ failure follows.

Mortality from septic shock remains 30–40%. Early recognition and treatment (the "Surviving Sepsis" bundle: antibiotics within an hour, fluid resuscitation, lactate measurement, blood cultures before antibiotics if possible) substantially improve outcomes.

## Bacterial infections of the circulatory and lymphatic systems

### Bacterial sepsis

Common causes:

- **Gram-negative sepsis**: *E. coli* from urinary tract source, *Klebsiella*, *Pseudomonas* in hospitalized patients, *Enterobacter*. The lipopolysaccharide (endotoxin) drives much of the clinical picture.
- **Gram-positive sepsis**: *Staphylococcus aureus* (from skin/wound or endovascular source), *Streptococcus* (often from pulmonary or urinary source), *Enterococcus* (often nosocomial).
- **Polymicrobial**: often from intra-abdominal sources.

Treatment requires source control (drain abscess, remove infected hardware) plus broad-spectrum empirical antibiotics, narrowed as cultures and susceptibility results return.

### Endocarditis

Infection of heart valves. The damaged or prosthetic valve provides a surface where bacteria can adhere; the resulting biofilm (Chapter 9) is difficult to clear with antibiotics alone.

Two clinical patterns:

**Acute endocarditis**: rapidly progressive, on previously normal valves. Often *S. aureus*. Frequently in injection drug users.

**Subacute endocarditis**: indolent, on previously damaged valves (e.g., from rheumatic heart disease) or prosthetic valves. Often viridans streptococci (oral flora seeded during dental work) or *Enterococcus*.

Clinical features: fever, new heart murmur, embolic phenomena (Janeway lesions on palms, Osler nodes on fingers, splinter hemorrhages under nails, petechiae). Diagnosis by blood culture (multiple sets) and echocardiography.

Treatment: prolonged IV antibiotics (4–6 weeks), often combined with valve replacement surgery in severe cases.

### Rheumatic heart disease

A delayed sequela of group A streptococcal pharyngitis (Chapter 22). Antibodies against streptococcal antigens cross-react with cardiac tissue, causing inflammation of heart valves. Over time, valve scarring leads to mitral stenosis, mitral regurgitation, and other valve disease. Now rare in developed countries due to antibiotic treatment of streptococcal pharyngitis; remains a leading cause of acquired heart disease globally.

### Specific bloodborne bacterial infections

**Tularemia** (*Francisella tularensis*): zoonotic, acquired from rabbits and ticks. Multiple clinical forms (ulceroglandular, oculoglandular, pneumonic, typhoidal). Highly infectious in laboratory settings — a potential bioterror agent.

**Brucellosis** (*Brucella* species): zoonotic from livestock, unpasteurized dairy. Undulant fever, malaise, myalgia, hepatosplenomegaly.

**Anthrax** (*Bacillus anthracis*): cutaneous (Chapter 21), inhalational, gastrointestinal forms. Bioterror agent (2001 letters).

**Plague** (*Yersinia pestis*): the Black Death organism. Three clinical forms:
- **Bubonic plague**: from flea bite. Painful swollen lymph node (bubo).
- **Septicemic plague**: bloodstream infection without bubo.
- **Pneumonic plague**: lung infection, person-to-person spread possible.

Historically caused massive pandemics (Justinian plague, Black Death, third pandemic). Now treated with antibiotics (streptomycin, doxycycline, fluoroquinolones); still occurs sporadically in the American Southwest and parts of Africa and Asia.

### Tick-borne bacterial infections

Ticks transmit several bacterial pathogens through blood:

**Rocky Mountain spotted fever** (*Rickettsia rickettsii*): tick-borne *Rickettsia*. Fever, headache, characteristic rash starting on wrists and ankles, spreading inward. Untreated mortality is significant; doxycycline is effective.

**Lyme disease** (*Borrelia burgdorferi*): the most common tick-borne disease in the US. Three stages:
- **Early localized**: erythema migrans rash (target lesion) at the bite site, fever, malaise.
- **Early disseminated**: weeks to months later. Multiple erythema migrans lesions, facial nerve palsy, meningitis, carditis (AV block), arthralgias.
- **Late**: months to years. Chronic arthritis (often knee), peripheral neuropathy, encephalopathy.

Treatment: doxycycline, amoxicillin, or ceftriaxone depending on stage. The "chronic Lyme disease" claimed by some patients to persist after antibiotic treatment is controversial; the medical consensus is that prolonged antibiotic therapy is not beneficial, but the clinical picture of post-treatment Lyme disease syndrome is recognized.

**Ehrlichiosis and anaplasmosis**: tick-borne intracellular bacteria. Fever, headache, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, elevated liver enzymes. Treat with doxycycline.

**Relapsing fever** (*Borrelia* species): tick-borne or louse-borne. Recurrent episodes of fever as the bacterium switches surface antigens.

## Viral bloodstream and lymphatic infections

### Infectious mononucleosis

Caused by Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), a herpesvirus. Common in adolescents and young adults. The virus infects B cells.

Clinical features: fever, severe pharyngitis, profound fatigue, generalized lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly. Atypical lymphocytes on peripheral blood smear (these are reactive T cells responding to infected B cells). Hepatic involvement is common.

Diagnosis: heterophile antibody test (Monospot), or specific EBV serology.

Complications: splenic rupture (rare but serious — patients should avoid contact sports during recovery), prolonged fatigue, rare cases of EBV-associated lymphoma later in life.

EBV is also associated with Burkitt lymphoma (particularly endemic in African children), nasopharyngeal carcinoma, and post-transplant lymphoproliferative disease.

### Cytomegalovirus (CMV)

A herpesvirus. Most primary infections are asymptomatic. Congenital CMV infection (from a mother newly infected during pregnancy) is a leading cause of congenital infection-related disability — sensorineural hearing loss, neurological problems. In immunocompromised patients, CMV can cause retinitis, esophagitis, hepatitis, pneumonia.

### HIV/AIDS

We have discussed HIV biology in Chapter 6 and 19. Here is the clinical picture in summary:

HIV infects CD4+ T cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells. The infection has three phases:

**Acute HIV** (2–4 weeks after exposure): flu-like illness — fever, lymphadenopathy, rash, sore throat. Often unrecognized. Viral load is extremely high.

**Chronic HIV (clinical latency)** (years): viral replication continues at lower level. CD4 count gradually declines. Patient is asymptomatic.

**AIDS** (CD4 < 200 or AIDS-defining illness): opportunistic infections (Pneumocystis pneumonia, candidiasis, cryptococcal meningitis, cytomegalovirus retinitis, Kaposi sarcoma, tuberculosis, *Mycobacterium avium* complex), wasting, AIDS-related cancers.

Diagnosis: HIV antibody-antigen testing (current generation tests detect both p24 antigen and antibodies, narrowing the window period from infection to detection). HIV viral load (RNA) for monitoring.

Treatment: combination antiretroviral therapy (ART). Currently typically a single combination pill (e.g., Biktarvy: bictegravir + emtricitabine + tenofovir alafenamide). ART suppresses viral replication indefinitely; CD4 count recovers; opportunistic infections are prevented. With consistent therapy, life expectancy approaches normal.

Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP): tenofovir-based regimens taken before exposure dramatically reduce HIV acquisition. Cabotegravir (long-acting injectable) and lenacapavir (very long-acting injectable) are newer options.

The transformation of HIV from a uniformly fatal illness to a manageable chronic condition is one of the great medical achievements of the past 40 years. The trajectory was: virus identified 1983; HAART (highly active antiretroviral therapy) widely deployed mid-1990s; mortality dropped sharply; PrEP introduced 2012; long-acting therapies 2020s. The remaining challenge: ensuring access to therapy globally and addressing populations where HIV continues to spread.

### Viral hemorrhagic fevers

A group of viral infections that can cause severe disease with vascular leak, bleeding, and shock. Caused by various RNA viruses across multiple families:

- **Filoviridae**: Ebola virus, Marburg virus. Severe disease with high mortality. African outbreaks in 1976, 1995, 2014–2016 (West Africa), 2018–2020 (DRC). The 2014–2016 outbreak in Liberia, Sierra Leone, and Guinea killed over 11,000 people. Ervebo vaccine (live attenuated, single-dose) approved 2019 and used in outbreaks since.
- **Arenaviridae**: Lassa fever (West Africa).
- **Bunyaviridae**: Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever, hantavirus pulmonary syndrome.
- **Flaviviridae**: Yellow fever, dengue (severe forms), Kyasanur Forest disease.

Mortality varies by virus and supportive care availability. Treatment is largely supportive (fluid resuscitation, blood products) with experimental antivirals (remdesivir, monoclonal antibodies for Ebola: REGN-EB3, mAb114).

### Other viral infections with blood involvement

- **Dengue**: flavivirus, mosquito-borne. Most infections produce dengue fever (high fever, headache, retroorbital pain, myalgia, rash). A subset, particularly with secondary infection by a different serotype, can develop **severe dengue** with vascular leak and bleeding (dengue hemorrhagic fever / dengue shock syndrome). About 100 million symptomatic infections per year globally.
- **Yellow fever**: flavivirus, mosquito-borne. Found in tropical Africa and South America. Live attenuated vaccine (17D) is highly effective.
- **Zika**: flavivirus, mosquito-borne. Usually mild illness in adults. Major concern: congenital Zika syndrome (microcephaly and other neurological defects) in infants of mothers infected during pregnancy. 2015–2016 outbreak in the Americas.
- **Chikungunya**: alphavirus, mosquito-borne. Fever and severe joint pain that can persist for months.

## Parasitic infections of blood and lymph

### Malaria

We have mentioned malaria several times. Here is the consolidated treatment.

Caused by *Plasmodium* species — five infect humans (*P. falciparum*, *P. vivax*, *P. ovale*, *P. malariae*, *P. knowlesi*). *P. falciparum* causes most severe disease and mortality.

Life cycle (briefly, from Chapter 5): mosquito injects sporozoites → liver cells → merozoites → red blood cells → schizonts → more merozoites → cycle of RBC invasion → some develop into gametocytes → picked up by next mosquito.

Clinical features: cyclic fevers corresponding to synchronized RBC rupture. *P. falciparum* can cause cerebral malaria, severe anemia, multi-organ failure. Mortality is high without treatment.

Diagnosis: thick and thin blood smears (with Giemsa or Wright stain) for direct visualization of parasites in RBCs. Rapid diagnostic tests (immunoassays for parasite antigens) for point-of-care use.

Treatment: artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) for *P. falciparum*. Chloroquine for sensitive *P. vivax* and *P. malariae*. Primaquine for *P. vivax* and *P. ovale* hypnozoites (dormant liver stages that cause relapse).

Prevention: bed nets, insecticide spraying, antimalarials for travelers (mefloquine, doxycycline, atovaquone-proguanil), the RTS,S/AS01 vaccine (modest efficacy, recommended by WHO for children in high-transmission areas), the newer R21/Matrix-M vaccine.

The global burden of malaria is enormous. About 250 million cases and 600,000 deaths per year, mostly children under 5 in sub-Saharan Africa. Malaria has been eliminated in some regions (US, Europe, much of East Asia) and is a target for elimination in others. The history is one of repeated approaches succeeding partially and then losing ground to resistance (chloroquine resistance, then artemisinin resistance now emerging).

### Babesiosis

*Babesia* species. Tick-borne. Parasitizes RBCs similarly to *Plasmodium*. Most common in the northeastern US (*B. microti*). Often coinfection with Lyme disease (same tick vector). Can be severe in splenectomized patients (the spleen normally clears infected RBCs).

### Leishmaniasis

*Leishmania* species. Sandfly-borne. Three clinical syndromes:

- **Cutaneous leishmaniasis**: chronic skin ulcers at bite sites. Common in Middle East, North Africa, Central and South America.
- **Mucocutaneous leishmaniasis**: destructive lesions of mucous membranes (nose, mouth) — particularly *L. braziliensis*.
- **Visceral leishmaniasis (kala-azar)**: systemic infection. Fever, hepatosplenomegaly, pancytopenia, weight loss. Fatal if untreated.

Treatment: amphotericin B (especially liposomal), miltefosine, paromomycin, sodium stibogluconate.

### Trypanosomiasis

**African trypanosomiasis (sleeping sickness)**: *Trypanosoma brucei* (rhodesiense or gambiense), tsetse fly–borne. Hemolymphatic phase (fever, lymphadenopathy) progresses to CNS involvement (sleep disturbance, somnolence, coma, death). Treatment depends on stage and species; CNS-penetrating drugs (eflornithine, melarsoprol, nifurtimox-eflornithine combination, fexinidazole) are required for late-stage disease.

**Chagas disease (American trypanosomiasis)**: *Trypanosoma cruzi*, transmitted by triatomine ("kissing") bugs. Acute infection often mild; chronic infection over decades causes cardiomyopathy, megaesophagus, megacolon. Endemic in Mexico, Central and South America. Substantial Chagas cardiomyopathy mortality. Treatment with benznidazole or nifurtimox; less effective for chronic disease.

### Schistosomiasis

*Schistosoma* species. Adult worms live in venules (different species in different sites: *S. mansoni* and *S. japonicum* in mesenteric venules, *S. haematobium* in bladder venules). Female worms lay eggs that lodge in tissues, triggering chronic inflammation.

Acquired through contact with contaminated freshwater (cercariae penetrate the skin). About 230 million people infected globally.

Long-term complications: portal hypertension and liver fibrosis (intestinal forms), bladder cancer (S. haematobium), pulmonary hypertension, neuroschistosomiasis.

Treatment: praziquantel.

### Lymphatic filariasis

*Wuchereria bancrofti*, *Brugia malayi*, *B. timori*. Mosquito-borne filarial nematodes. Adult worms in lymphatic vessels. Chronic infection causes lymphedema, including the disfiguring elephantiasis of legs and scrotum.

Mass drug administration (annual ivermectin plus albendazole, or DEC plus albendazole) has dramatically reduced lymphatic filariasis transmission in many endemic regions.

## What the chapter is really about

The bloodstream is the body's distribution network. A pathogen that breaches it has access to every organ. The body responds with a coordinated systemic immune effort — fever, cytokine release, complement activation, phagocyte mobilization. When the response is calibrated, the pathogen is cleared. When it is dysregulated, sepsis follows; when sepsis is severe, mortality is high.

For clinical practice, the bloodstream infections demand urgent attention. Time to appropriate antibiotic in septic shock matters in hours, not days. Recognition of endocarditis, of malaria in returning travelers, of dengue in endemic regions, of HIV at any of its stages — these are the diagnoses that change clinical outcomes.

For broader perspective, several of the most consequential infectious disease stories live in this chapter. HIV transformed from death sentence to chronic condition. Malaria continues to kill more children than almost any other infection. Plague shaped medieval European history. Hemorrhagic fevers will continue to emerge from animal reservoirs. The systems that distribute pathogens through blood are the same systems that distribute the immune response. Most of the time it works.

## Still puzzling

I do not fully understand why some patients with the same bacterial bloodstream infection develop severe sepsis and others do not. The pathogen factors (organism, virulence, load) and host factors (immune status, comorbidities, genetic polymorphisms in TLR4 and others) all contribute, but predicting in advance who will progress to severe sepsis remains imprecise. Biomarkers (procalcitonin, lactate, IL-6) are useful but not deterministic. The biology of sepsis severity is one of the more practical open questions in clinical microbiology — answering it could save lives.

## What would change my mind

The current approach to malaria has been dominated by artemisinin-based combination therapy and vector control. If artemisinin resistance — now established in Southeast Asia and emerging in Africa — spreads further, the entire malaria treatment paradigm will need to shift. New antimalarials (tafenoquine for *P. vivax* hypnozoites, several drug candidates in late-stage development) and the improving vaccines may close the gap.
