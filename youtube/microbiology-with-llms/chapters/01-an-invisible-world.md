# Chapter 01 — An Invisible World

*The history of microbiology is the history of being surprised by what is in a drop of water.*

## The question before the answer

Here is something worth thinking about before we go any further.

For thousands of years — all of recorded history, most of human prehistory — people got sick from invisible things. They ate bread that rose because of invisible things. They made wine, beer, cheese, yogurt, soy sauce, kimchi, and kefir because of invisible things. They died in plagues caused by invisible things. A few of them suspected the invisible things were there. None of them could see one.

Then, in 1675, in a textile shop in Delft, a man who inspected fabric for the city looked through a glass bead at a drop of rainwater and saw them moving.

That is an extraordinary thing to sit with. Not that the creatures were there — they had always been there — but that the difference between an invisible world and a visible one was, in the end, the quality of a single piece of ground glass.

What I want to do in this chapter is take you through the story of how we found out what microbes are. Not just the events, but the reasoning — the kinds of evidence people had, what that evidence could and could not prove, and why each new tool forced a revision of the picture. Because microbiology is not a finished subject. It is a discipline that has been wrong in interesting ways, and understanding how it got things wrong is the fastest path to understanding how it gets things right.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

1. Distinguish microorganisms by size, structure, and cellular organization — including which ones aren't cells at all.
2. Place a named organism (bacterium, archaeon, protist, fungus, helminth, virus, prion) in the right domain or off the tree of life entirely, and say why.
3. Trace a single historical claim about disease back to the primary source it comes from.
4. Read a species name like *Escherichia coli* correctly and explain what each part of the name does.
5. Describe how taxonomy changed three times in two hundred years — Linnaeus, Whittaker, Woese — and what new evidence forced each change.

Prerequisites: Curiosity. A willingness to be surprised by a drop of water.

## Living with the invisible

The story starts, as many stories in biology do, with food.

In 7000 BC, in a village called Jiahu on the floodplain of the Yellow River, somebody figured out how to ferment rice, honey, and fruit into something alcoholic. We know this because their pottery jars survived, and three thousand years later archaeologists found residue inside them: tartaric acid from grapes or hawthorn fruit, calcium oxalate from honey, beeswax, rice starch breakdown products — the chemical fingerprints of fermentation, preserved in clay. [^1]

The villagers of Jiahu did not know what fermentation was. They did not know that *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, a single-celled fungus, was doing the work. They knew that if you combined these things in a covered jar and waited, the liquid became something different. Something useful. Something worth repeating.

This is the pattern for thousands of years: humans depending on microorganisms without knowing microorganisms exist. Cheese, wine, beer, bread, yogurt, pickled vegetables, kimchi, sauerkraut, fish sauce, kefir, kombucha — every one of these is microbial work, harnessed by people who had no concept of a microbe. The fermentation gets its name from the Latin *fervēre*, to boil, because the carbon dioxide bubbling out of solution looks like boiling. There is no fire. The "boiling" is metabolism.

The implicit trade-off: use the microbes that help you eat, and die from the ones that kill you. You cannot separate the populations. You can only learn which preparations work — which temperatures, which timings, which vessels — and pass the recipe down.

Some people, over this long stretch of time, suspected they were not alone.

Ötzi the Iceman, a 5,300-year-old corpse pulled from glacial ice on the Austrian-Italian border in 1991, was carrying the eggs of the parasitic whipworm *Trichuris trichiura*, and his DNA showed traces of *Borrelia burgdorferi*, the bacterium that causes Lyme disease. [^2] He was also carrying, in his belongings, the woody bracket fungus *Fomitopsis betulinus* — which has laxative and antibiotic properties. Researchers think he was using it medicinally. [^3] He could not have known what was wrong with him. He almost certainly had some notion that something was wrong, and some notion that something in the fungus could help.

Across the ancient world, this pattern appears: knowing that contagion exists without knowing what it is. The Bible describes quarantine for leprosy. The Indus Valley cities of Mohenjo-daro and Harappa had engineered drainage and fresh-water systems by 2500 BCE. Rome built the *Cloaca Maxima* — still operating today in some form — and aqueducts that separated clean water from waste. These are microbiology without a name.

In Greece, the explanation was *miasma*: bad air, the exhalations of marshes and rotting matter. The word *malaria* preserves this theory — *mal'aria*, bad air. The theory was wrong about the mechanism (the danger in a swamp is mosquitoes, not the air) but right about a correlation: swampy, stagnant places genuinely were dangerous. A wrong theory that generates correct predictions about where not to camp is still a useful theory.

Hippocrates (460–370 BC) refused supernatural explanations and argued that illness had natural causes in the patient or the environment. His near-contemporary Thucydides (460–395 BC) survived the Athenian plague — which killed perhaps a third of the city between 430 and 410 BC — and recorded a detail so important it often gets buried: those who had recovered from the disease did not catch it again, even when nursing the actively sick. [^4]

That sentence is the first written record of acquired immunity. Thucydides had no idea what was happening inside those bodies. He did not know about antibodies or T cells or immunological memory. He had a correlation, observed in his own neighbors, and the discipline to write it down. The mechanism came two and a half millennia later. The observation came first.

Then there is Varro.

Marcus Terentius Varro (116–27 BC) was a Roman polymath whose agricultural manual, *De Re Rustica*, contains a passage that should be famous. Advising on where to build a farmhouse, he writes:

> "Precautions must also be taken in neighborhood swamps... because certain minute creatures [*animalia minuta*] grow there which cannot be seen by the eye, which float in the air and enter the body through the mouth and nose and there cause serious diseases." [^5]

That is 36 BC predicting the germ theory of disease. Wrong in some details — the route of transmission for malaria is mosquito bite, not airborne particles, and his *minuta* were an inference from correlation, not something he had observed. But right in the essential shape of the claim: *there exist creatures too small to see that cause disease*. That claim is 2,000 years old and still true.

What Varro could not do was produce evidence anyone else was obligated to believe. He had an insight. He did not have a proof. And that, more than any single discovery, is what the next phase of microbiology would supply.

## The lens

Return to Delft. 1675.

Antonie van Leeuwenhoek was a linen draper who inspected fabric for the city — counting threads, judging weave. Grinding lenses was a hobby he refused to share with anyone. His microscopes were not the compound instruments that natural philosophers used. They were simpler: a single glass bead, mounted in a brass plate, with a pin to hold the specimen and screws to focus. They look primitive. They magnified up to roughly 270 times — more than any compound microscope of the era, because a small sphere of glass is more curved, and more curvature means more magnification. [^6]

He looked at everything. Plaque from his own teeth. Stool from people with diarrhea. Pond water. Pepper infusions. Blood. Rainwater that had stood in a covered tub on his roof for several days.

Through the bead, in the rainwater: things swimming. He counted them, estimated "more than a million" in a single drop, and wrote letters to the Royal Society in London describing what he called *animalcules* — little animals.

The Royal Society was skeptical. They sent a delegation to verify. The delegation confirmed it. In 1680, Leeuwenhoek was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society without ever leaving Delft.

From his drawings — still in the archives — we know he was looking at bacteria, protists, sperm cells, and yeast. He did not use any of those names. Those names came later, from a science that needed centuries to develop the categories.

What the lens changed was not the existence of microbes. They had always been there. What it changed was the *kind of claim* you could make. Before Leeuwenhoek, "there exist tiny creatures in this water" was an argument. After Leeuwenhoek, it was a thing you could show someone through a glass bead. The argument moved from rhetoric to demonstration. And once you could demonstrate a microbe, you could start asking which microbe — which one is in this patient's blood, which one is absent from healthy controls, which one, when introduced to a healthy animal, produces the same disease.

Between roughly 1857 and 1914, in the period microbiologists call the Golden Age, the answer to those questions arrived.

Louis Pasteur (1822–1895) was a chemist, not a doctor. Many doctors of his era resented him for it. He worked on problems industry cared about: wine going sour, silkworm disease, anthrax in cattle. He showed that fermentation was not a purely chemical process but the work of living microorganisms. He showed that microorganisms could be killed by brief heating — pasteurization. He developed vaccines against chicken cholera, anthrax, and rabies. In 1885, he gave the rabies vaccine to a nine-year-old boy named Joseph Meister, who had been bitten by a rabid dog. Meister survived.

Robert Koch (1843–1910) was a German country doctor who began doing microbiology in a home laboratory paid for by his wife. He was meticulous in a way Pasteur was not. He developed techniques that the field still uses: growing bacteria in pure culture on solid medium (agar, suggested to him by Walther Hesse — whose wife Fanny remembered using it in her grandmother's jam recipes, in what is possibly the most consequential cooking tip in the history of biology); staining specimens with dyes so they could be seen; photographing bacteria through the microscope. In 1876 he identified *Bacillus anthracis* as the cause of anthrax. In 1882, *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* as the cause of tuberculosis. In 1883, *Vibrio cholerae* as the cause of cholera. [^7]

Koch also formulated what are now called Koch's postulates: the four conditions that must hold before you can claim a microbe causes a disease.

1. The organism must be present in every case of the disease.
2. The organism must be isolated from the diseased host and grown in pure culture.
3. The cultured organism must cause the same disease when introduced to a healthy host.
4. The organism must be recoverable from that newly diseased host.

These postulates are not just a checklist. They are a philosophical statement about what it means to claim causation in biology — and they fail in interesting ways that we will come back to in Chapter 15.

There is a subtle trap built into the lens, though. The microscope makes certain kinds of organisms visible and ignores others. Koch's cultures could grow bacteria that thrive on a petri dish; they could not grow the roughly 99% of bacteria in most environments that refuse to culture under laboratory conditions. For a century, those bacteria were invisible to the discipline — not because they were not there, but because the tool could not reach them. Every instrument creates a bias about what is worth looking at, and the bias becomes invisible to the people working inside it.

## Sorting it

Once you can see thousands of different organisms, the next problem is what to call them and how to organize them.

**Linnaeus, 1735.** Carl Linnaeus, a Swedish botanist with a gift for organizing the natural world, published an 11-page booklet called *Systema Naturae*. He sorted all nature into three kingdoms — animal, plant, mineral — and within the living kingdoms, a hierarchy of nested groups: kingdom, class, order, family, genus, species. He also introduced a convention that has outlasted everything else in his system: every species gets a two-word name. The genus, capitalized and italicized. The species, lowercase and italicized. *Homo sapiens*. *Felis catus*. *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*. When you see *E. coli*, that is shorthand for *Escherichia coli* — the first letter of the genus, then the full species name. The convention is followed today by every biology paper in every language, which is the mark of a design that got something exactly right.

Microbes had no place on Linnaeus's tree. They were too small to have been studied systematically when he wrote. His tree had two big branches and could absorb nothing else.

**Haeckel and Whittaker, 1866–1969.** Ernst Haeckel added kingdoms as microbes came to light — Protista for single-celled eukaryotes, Monera for prokaryotes. Robert Whittaker added a fifth kingdom for Fungi and introduced the idea of a higher level to distinguish eukaryotes (cells with nuclei) from prokaryotes (cells without). Whittaker's five-kingdom tree — Animalia, Plantae, Protista, Fungi, Monera — was the standard in textbooks for the next twenty years.

Notice what is missing from all of these trees: viruses. They are not cells, so there is no branch to put them on. We will return to this.

**Woese and Fox, 1977.** In the late 1960s, an American microbiologist named Carl Woese started worrying about a quiet problem: how do you compare two organisms that look nothing alike? Whittaker's tree used visible features. But what feature do you compare between two bacteria that both look like rods?

Woese's answer: stop looking at the outside. Look at a molecule that every cell on Earth must have — the gene encoding the small subunit of ribosomal RNA (16S rRNA in prokaryotes; 18S in eukaryotes). Ribosomes make proteins. Every cell needs ribosomes. The 16S sequence is essential, conserved, and present in everything living. If two cells share more 16S sequence, they share more recent ancestry. If they share less, they diverged earlier. The molecule is a clock, ticking at roughly known rates, going back billions of years.

In 1977, Woese and George Fox published their results. [^8] One group of prokaryotes — the methanogens, the halophiles, the thermophiles — had a 16S sequence so different from other bacteria that they could not be on the same branch. They were a separate domain of life. Woese called them *Archaebacteria*, later shortened to Archaea.

The implication: all life on Earth falls into three deep groups. Bacteria. Archaea. Eukarya. And Archaea, biochemically, look more like Eukarya than like Bacteria — suggesting that the split between those two domains is more recent than the split that produced Bacteria.

Most microbiologists hated this when it came out. They had been calling certain cells "bacteria" for a century. To be told that some of those cells were not bacteria but a separate domain — older, biochemically alien, misclassified for generations — was a major revision. It took years to settle. By the 1990s it was the consensus.

We now believe all life on Earth shares a single common ancestor, called LUCA — the Last Universal Common Ancestor — living perhaps 3.5 to 4 billion years ago. From LUCA, three lineages descend: Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya. Every cell you have ever seen, eaten, or been, is in one of those three domains.

The tree metaphor has a problem, though. A tree assumes inheritance is vertical — genes pass from parent to offspring, down through time. In animals and plants, this is mostly true. In prokaryotes, it is mostly not. Bacteria and archaea can pick up DNA from each other directly: through transformation (taking up free DNA from the environment), transduction (DNA carried by a virus), or conjugation (a direct molecular bridge between two cells). A bacterium's genome is not a faithful record of its ancestry. Bits of it may have arrived from elsewhere, across domain lines. Some biologists now prefer the metaphor of a *web of life*. We will use the tree throughout this book, because it is the tool the literature uses — but I want you to remember that the tree is an idealization, and most of the messiness is at the microbial scale.

## What a microbe actually is

We have spent most of a chapter discussing something we have not defined. Here is the working definition: *a microbe is anything that requires a microscope to see well.* That is not a phylogenetic category. It is a scale category. Microbes are unified not by what they are but by how small they are.

The scale ladder, in metric:

The smallest thing you can see without a microscope is roughly **100 micrometers (μm)** across. One micrometer is a millionth of a meter — one thousandth of a millimeter. The dot on the letter *i* is about 200 μm.

A typical human cell — skin, liver, muscle — is about **10 μm** across.

A typical bacterial cell is about **1 μm** — ten times smaller than a human cell.

A typical virus is about **0.1 μm (100 nanometers)** — ten times smaller than a bacterium.

A prion — a misfolded protein that transmits disease — is about **0.01 μm (10 nanometers)**.

Each rung of that ladder is a factor of ten. From naked-eye visibility to a prion is four orders of magnitude. The reason microbiology needed a microscope was not that microbes were slightly too small to see. They were a thousand times too small.

**Bacteria** are prokaryotic — no nucleus. Cell walls containing peptidoglycan. They come in shapes named with a persistent affection for Latin and Greek: *coccus* (sphere), *bacillus* (rod), *spirillum* (rigid spiral), *spirochete* (flexible spiral), *vibrio* (comma). Most are harmless or helpful. A minority are pathogens.

**Archaea** are also prokaryotic and single-celled, but their cell wall chemistry is different (no peptidoglycan), their membrane lipids are structured differently (ether linkages, isoprenoid chains), and their molecular biology in many ways resembles eukaryotes more than bacteria. They live everywhere — hot springs, hypersaline lakes, deep-sea vents, Antarctic sediment, the human gut. As of this writing, no archaeon has been confirmed as a human pathogen. We do not fully understand why.

**Protists** are eukaryotes that are not animals, plants, or fungi. The category is historical more than phylogenetic — a drawer labeled "things that didn't fit elsewhere." *Plasmodium falciparum*, which causes malaria. *Giardia lamblia*, which causes intestinal disease. Various algae. Some are photosynthetic. Some are predatory. Some are parasites.

**Fungi** are eukaryotes with chitin in their cell walls — the same molecule that makes insect exoskeletons. They decompose, ferment, or parasitize. Single-celled fungi are yeasts; multicellular fungi with visible filamentous colonies are molds. Yeasts make bread rise and beer ferment. *Penicillium* molds produce penicillin.

**Helminths** are parasitic worms — large enough to see, sometimes meters long in the case of tapeworms, and in that sense not microbes at all. They are studied in microbiology because their eggs and larvae are microscopic, and helminth infection is usually diagnosed by examining stool samples under a microscope.

**Viruses** are acellular — not made of cells. Genetic material (either DNA or RNA, never both) wrapped in a protein shell, sometimes with a lipid envelope. Outside a host cell, they are inert. Inside a host cell, they hijack the host's machinery to copy themselves. Whether they are "alive" depends on what you mean by alive — a question that is more philosophical than useful, but worth thinking about once.

**Prions** are stranger still. They are not cells, not viruses — they are misfolded versions of a normal cellular protein. They propagate by causing other copies of the same protein to adopt their misfolded shape. They contain no genetic material whatsoever. Stanley Prusiner proposed in 1982 that scrapie, a sheep brain disease, was caused by a protein with no nucleic acid. The response from the field was approximately: that is impossible. In 1997, he won the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. [^9]

The category "microbe" breaks at its edges. Are viruses alive? Most biologists say no. Are giant viruses — mimivirus, pithovirus, pandoravirus, with genomes larger than some bacteria — really viruses? The question is genuinely unresolved. The category is a working definition, not a natural kind.

## Cora

The OpenStax textbook this chapter accompanies opens with a patient named Cora — a 41-year-old lawyer with severe headaches, high fever, neck stiffness, confusion, and drowsiness. Her doctor suspects meningitis. Meningitis can be caused by bacteria, fungi, or viruses, so three CSF samples go to three labs.

The microbiology lab Gram-stains a sample. Negative — no bacteria visible. Cultures come back in two days. Nothing grew. The doctor rules out bacterial and fungal meningitis. Viral testing is negative too. Then Cora's symptoms worsen in ways that don't fit any infection: muscle stiffness spreading from her neck, involuntary limb jerking, declining cognition. High levels of 14-3-3 protein in her CSF. Abnormal EEG activity.

Her doctor concludes: this is Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease. A prion disease. About 300 cases a year in the United States. No treatment. Cora dies eight months later. The diagnosis is confirmed by brain autopsy.

Every tool in this chapter reached its limit with Cora. The microscope found no bacteria. The culture found nothing that would grow. The viral tests found no pathogen. The thing killing her was a misfolded protein — no genetic material, not on any branch of the tree of life, not within the framework of infectious disease as it existed before 1982.

Microbiology in 1900 could not have diagnosed Cora. The category "infectious protein" did not exist. And the point is not that CJD is common — it isn't. The point is that microbiology has been forced to expand its categories every few decades, by each new instrument and each new anomaly, and there is no principled reason to believe the expansion is finished.

## What the chapter is really about

Feynman, in his lectures on physics, used to make a distinction between knowing the name of something and knowing something. Knowing that the process in Cora's brain is called a prion disease, and knowing what a prion is — a misfolded protein that templates identical misfolds, propagating through tissue without a genome, invisible to every diagnostic tool designed to find things that have DNA — those are different things.

The history of microbiology is the history of finding out that the world contains things for which no category yet exists, and building the categories after the fact. Leeuwenhoek had no word for bacterium. Koch had no framework for viruses. Woese had no evidence that Archaea were a domain until the rRNA sequences made it undeniable. Prusiner had a correct hypothesis about prions and was wrong by the standards of his field for fifteen years.

Each of these cases follows the same shape: an anomaly that doesn't fit the current category, a period of resistance, a new tool or new evidence that forces a revision.

I want you to hold onto this shape, because it will appear again in almost every chapter that follows. The microbes you will learn to name and classify are, at this moment in history, the ones we have tools to see. The history of the field is a reliable predictor of its future: there are things in a drop of water that we still do not have the lens for.

## Still puzzling

I do not fully understand why no archaeon has been confirmed as a human pathogen. Archaea live in the human gut. They are biochemically distinct from bacteria, which means many of our innate immune sensors should not detect them well. They have the genetic flexibility to acquire virulence factors. And yet — to date — none appear to cause disease. It might be that they have, and we lack the tools to detect them. It might be that something about their biochemistry is fundamentally incompatible with human pathogenesis. I have read several explanations and none of them fully satisfies me. If you figure it out, I want to know.

## What would change my mind

The three-domain framework in this chapter would need revision if a fourth domain — deeper than Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya — were confirmed by independent rRNA analysis and accepted by the community. The CPR (Candidate Phyla Radiation) group of bacteria, discovered through metagenomics around 2015, has been argued by some researchers to constitute a major branch with substantially different cell biology.
