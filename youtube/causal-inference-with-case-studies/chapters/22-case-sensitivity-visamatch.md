## 1. Question

A student on F-1 OPT has ninety days left and a job ad in front of them. The decision-relevant question is small and sharp: *should I apply?* The headline-level fact behind that question — *does this company sponsor visas?* — looks like a lookup. It is not. Sponsorship is not a property of a company; it is a property of a company *at a moment in time*. A firm that filed 600 H-1B petitions in 2021 may have closed the program by 2023 without announcing it. The Department of Labor's PERM disclosure files document what employers did; they do not document what employers will do next quarter.

Raghuraman's framing reads the problem as an inferential one rather than a predictive one. There are several biased proxies for the latent state — current sponsorship policy — and each proxy is stale in a different way and selected on a different basis. The causal-inference move is to treat the verdict as a function of those proxies and ask how the verdict shifts when the staleness assumptions shift. That is sensitivity analysis, not effect estimation.

## 2. Causal diagram

The relevant nodes are: the latent current sponsorship policy `P_t`, the historical PERM record `D_hist` (from DOL FY filings), the recent USCIS petition record `D_usc`, the language inside the job description `J`, the system's sponsorship score `ŝ`, and the recruiter response `R` that eventually arrives if the student applies.

The edges are these. `P_t → D_usc` because current policy generates current petitions, with a filing-and-publication delay measured in months. `P_{t−k} → D_hist` because historical PERM filings were generated by *past* policy, and the relation between `P_{t−k}` and `P_t` is not identity — it is whatever drift the company underwent in the intervening years. `P_t → J` because current policy sometimes shows up in the wording of the job ad, but the channel is noisy: the same description gets cloned across postings, ATS templates carry phrases written years earlier, and recruiters paste boilerplate without intent. `D_hist, D_usc, J → ŝ` because the score is a function of the three proxies. `P_t → R` because the recruiter, eventually, reflects the current policy.

The structural feature that matters is that `D_hist` is downstream of `P_{t−k}`, not `P_t`. The arrow from current policy to the historical record does not exist. That gap is the staleness gap. Any verdict drawn from `D_hist` is reading a parent that the latent state of interest no longer has.

## 3. Identification strategy

The system does not estimate a single causal parameter. It constructs a calibrated verdict — `visa_confidence`, clamped to `[0.05, 0.95]` — that combines the three proxies under explicit weights, and the load-bearing claim is that this verdict is honest about its own uncertainty.

The matching architecture sits underneath. Resumes are parsed into a structured profile by a Groq-hosted Llama 3.3 70B call (temperature zero, JSON-object output, ~1.5 s latency), with a rule-based fallback when the API is unavailable. Profiles and job descriptions are embedded into the same 384-dimensional space by `all-MiniLM-L6-v2` and compared by cosine similarity. The match score is `0.50 × semantic_similarity + 0.30 × skill_overlap + 0.10 × role_match + 0.10 × location_match`. For an international candidate the final score is `0.40 × match_score + 0.35 × visa_confidence + 0.25 × recency_score`, with recency a tiered decay from 1.00 at zero to three days old to 0.10 at sixty-plus.

`visa_confidence` itself is the composite under examination. A PERM volume band sets a base score (200+ certified filings → 0.85), a trend adjustment moves it ±0.05–0.15 based on whether filings are increasing or decreasing, a freshness discount penalises old data, and a regex cascade on the job description adds +0.30 for "will sponsor H-1B" or subtracts 0.40 for "no visa sponsorship," with positive-strong patterns prioritised over negative-strong.

The sensitivity protocol — the chapter's central inferential move — runs the verdict under several priors on staleness. Treat `D_hist` as fresh; the score reads one way. Treat it as eighteen months stale; the freshness discount reweights. Treat the JD language as a proxy for current policy; the JD signal carries weight. Treat the JD as a recycled ATS template; the JD signal becomes uninformative and the verdict leans on filings alone. The verdict the system ships is the one that survives the worst plausible prior.

The identification rests on an assumption worth naming. The recruiter response `R` is treated as the ground-truth oracle — the eventual answer the student is trying to predict. Any feedback loop that trains the system toward `R` inherits the bias in `R`: recruiters say no for reasons that are not policy, and they say yes inconsistently across roles within the same firm.

## 4. Results

The SDD and audit describe what the system delivers, not how often it is right. The pipeline is six workstreams: WS1, a daily Greenhouse/Lever/Ashby scraper into a bronze/silver medallion layer with a 14-day active-job window; WS2, a normaliser of DOL PERM (FY2025 Q4 is the most recent file in the audit) and USCIS data into per-company sponsorship profiles; WS3, the Groq Llama 3.3 70B resume parser with a rule-based fallback and a 12,000-character truncation; WS4, the MiniLM-based matcher building a pre-normalised embedding index; WS5, the recency-and-confidence scorer that produces the final ranking; and WS6, a LangGraph wrapper running the agents in sequence on the `Akash_0405` working branch.

What the SDD does not contain is a precision/recall metric on a labelled set of actual sponsorship outcomes — no curve of "system said yes, recruiter said yes" against a held-out cohort. The system ships a calibrated verdict pipeline. Whether the calibration is calibrated has not been measured against `R`.

## 5. Sensitivity and limitations

The audit names this gap directly. Scoring weights — 40/35/25 for the international formula, 50/30/10/10 for the match score — were chosen in conversation, not from data, and are flagged demo-low / production-high risk. The PERM file is frozen with no automatic refresh; the freshness discount applies *within* a file but not *across* the gap to today. The MiniLM similarity is a measure of resume-to-JD textual overlap, not a sponsorship inference. The rule-based resume fallback is mediocre, the audit says explicitly.

The named failure mode is this: when historical sponsorship is high — a company filing 600 PERM cases in 2021 — but current policy has quietly changed and the company stopped sponsoring in 2023, the volume band returns 0.85, the trend adjustment is small, the freshness discount is partial, and the verdict comes back high-confidence-yes. That is the exact failure the SDD opens with as motivation: stale data presented with false confidence, six weeks of OPT spent on a company that was never going to sponsor.

## 6. Theory connection

This case pairs directly with Chapter 11 on sensitivity analysis. The system's central output is not a point estimate of an effect but a verdict whose stability under realistic perturbations of stale data is the actual property of interest. Calibrated confidence under named priors is the sensitivity-analysis frame applied at inference time.

Three questions for your own work. What input to your model is most likely to be stale, and what is the longest plausible gap between the data and the truth you are trying to read off it? And is the flip-point closer to your prior than you would like — close enough that the model is, in effect, telling you what you already believed?

## A note about AI

VisaMatch sensitivity is a high-stakes policy case — visa allocation decisions where the cost of a wrong match is borne by a person. The model is fluent in the policy vocabulary and naive about the personal stakes.

Where the model genuinely helps: producing the structural sensitivity questions — what level of hidden bias would overturn the policy conclusion, what variables are the most plausible hidden confounders.

Where the model does damage: certifying that the policy is robust. Policy robustness requires a substantive defense against specific objections, and the model cannot anticipate the objections of affected populations.

The rule: sensitivity vocabulary from the model; the robustness defense from named analysts and affected communities.
