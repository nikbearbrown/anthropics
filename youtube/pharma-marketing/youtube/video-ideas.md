## Candidate 01 — The Immunity Illusion: Why Doctors Think They Can't Be Bought (But Can)

- Source: `pharma-marketing/pantry/pharma-marketing-evidence-synthesis.md`
- Topic: PHARMA MARKETING
- Hook: Physicians who accept industry meals are statistically proven to prescribe more branded drugs — but those same physicians rank themselves as uniquely immune to influence. The illusion of immunity IS the mechanism.
- Key case: A single sponsored meal under $20 correlates with significantly higher prescribing across four drug classes, with a dose-response pattern. Rosuvastatin OR 1.18, nebivolol OR 1.70, olmesartan OR 1.52, desvenlafaxine OR 2.18 — all from one lunch.
- The Question: If every doctor in a 36-study systematic review believes they are immune, and 89 of 101 analyses show they are not — what is the self-assessment gap doing for the industry?
- Core idea: The third-person effect (I am immune, my colleagues are not) is not a side bug in pharmaceutical detailing — it is the exploited feature. The physician's genuine belief in their own immunity removes self-protective friction, making the persuasion more effective, not less.
- Visual object: A split bar chart — left side shows doctors' self-rated susceptibility (near zero), right side shows measured prescribing change after payments (substantial). A diagonal "gap" arrow labels the exploited zone.
- Manim move: split → the bar divides cleanly to reveal the gap between belief and behavior
- Example seed: A Johns Hopkins Medicare study estimated $1.7B in excess annual spending driven by brand-over-generic requests, patients paying $270M more than necessary. Every $20 lunch in that causal chain had a physician at it who believed it wouldn't change anything. Walk through one lunch, one prescription, one patient's bill.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: raster (meal/physician imagery), c2v (bar chart data)
- Prerequisites: Basic understanding that pharmaceutical companies market to doctors
- Exclusions: Drug approval process, clinical trial design, individual physician names
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 02 — The Sunshine Act Paradox: Transparency That Changes Nothing

- Source: `pharma-marketing/pantry/pharma-marketing-evidence-synthesis.md`
- Topic: PHARMA MARKETING
- Hook: Congress passed a law requiring complete public disclosure of every dollar pharma pays physicians. Studies confirm it has not changed prescribing behavior by a measurable amount. Making corruption visible did not make it stop.
- Key case: The 2010 Physician Payments Sunshine Act mandated disclosure of all pharma payments. Researchers used the data to publish hundreds of studies confirming the payment-prescribing link. Not one study found that high-prescribing physicians targeted by pharma changed their behavior after their payments became public.
- The Question: If knowing a doctor took money doesn't change how patients or institutions treat that doctor, what would actually work?
- Core idea: Disclosure assumes that social accountability will follow transparency — that employers, journal editors, and meeting organizers will act on public payment data. None of them have, systematically. The physicians most valuable to pharma (highest prescribers) appear uniquely resistant to reputational pressure from disclosure precisely because they are valuable enough to be insulated.
- Visual object: A two-step flow diagram — "Payment Made → Disclosed Publicly → [Expected: Behavior Change] → [Actual: No Change]" — with the expected branch shown as dashed/broken.
- Manim move: collapse — the expected consequence path collapses to nothing while the actual path (payments continue) stays solid
- Example seed: Compare the Massachusetts disclosure law (which paired disclosure with access restrictions) to the Sunshine Act (disclosure only). Massachusetts produced measurable prescribing declines. The Sunshine Act alone produced papers, not change. Walk through what access restrictions add that disclosure alone cannot.
- Length band: 2–3 min
- Still lanes: c2v (flow diagram), geo (Capitol building, physician office)
- Prerequisites: Awareness that pharma pays physicians for various activities
- Exclusions: Congressional politics, individual case investigations, insurance billing
- Score: 8/10

## Candidate 03 — The Generic Puzzle: 80% of Prescriptions, 27% of Spending

- Source: `pharma-marketing/pantry/pharma-marketing-evidence-synthesis.md`
- Topic: PHARMA MARKETING
- Hook: Generic drugs are FDA-confirmed bioequivalent to branded drugs in over 2,000 clinical pharmacokinetic trials. They account for 80% of prescriptions dispensed. Yet they represent only 27% of total drug spending — and the gap is largely marketing-driven.
- Key case: Generic use saved the US healthcare system an estimated $1.07 trillion over one decade. The flip side: branded drug preference — much of it marketing-driven — cost the system over a trillion dollars in the same period. 68% of the most-advertised drugs have low added clinical benefit compared with similar medications.
- The Question: If branded and generic drugs are bioequivalent for the vast majority of cases, why does the most heavily advertised drug category consistently have the least clinical differentiation?
- Core idea: Only on-patent, high-margin branded drugs are advertised. The drugs that most need to overcome physician and patient preference (because they offer the least clinical advantage over cheaper alternatives) receive the heaviest marketing investment. The advertising spend is inversely correlated with genuine clinical superiority. This is not an accident — it is the structural logic of patent-expiry economics.
- Visual object: A stacked proportion bar — left side (prescription volume): 80% generic / 20% branded. Right side (spending): 27% generic / 73% branded. Arrows show the inversion.
- Manim move: transform — the proportion bars flip and invert to reveal the spending reversal
- Example seed: Walk through a specific therapeutic class (statins): multiple generics bioequivalent, priced at $4/month at most pharmacies. A branded statin advertised heavily — same mechanism, same outcomes in most patients — at $300+/month. A patient asks for the brand by name after seeing a TV ad. Their co-pay is covered by a coupon. Their insurer pays the rest. Who wins at the pharmacy counter?
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: c2v (proportion bars), raster (pharmacy counter)
- Prerequisites: Basic understanding of how prescription drugs are priced and dispensed
- Exclusions: Drug manufacturing details, FDA approval pathway specifics, formulary policy mechanics
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 04 — The Lunch Table Who's Not There: Pharma's Structural Blind Spot

- Source: `pharma-marketing/pantry/conversation-summary.md`
- Topic: PHARMA MARKETING
- Hook: In every other advertising market, the person seeing the ad is the person paying for the product. In pharmaceutical marketing, the person seeing the ad is the physician, the person paying is the patient — and the patient is never at the table.
- Key case: The pharma sales lunch. The rep is paid to be there. The physician enjoys it. The pharma brand's interests are represented. Who is absent: the patient, the insurer, the employer, the taxpayer — every party who pays. The people funding the lunch have no representation, no knowledge, and no mechanism to object.
- The Question: When the buyer and the decider are different people, what happens to the normal market signals that correct advertising excess?
- Core idea: Standard market correction for misleading advertising requires that the person deceived is also the person who stops buying, triggering financial consequences. In pharma detailing the physician makes the prescribing decision but bears none of the cost. The patient bears all the cost but makes none of the decisions. This structural separation eliminates the self-correcting mechanism that disciplines other advertising markets.
- Visual object: A triangle diagram — Physician (decider) at top, Patient (payer) at bottom-left, Pharma Brand (advertiser) at bottom-right. A "normal market" reference next to it shows those three roles collapsed into one circle. The gap between circles IS the market failure.
- Manim move: split — the single-circle "normal market" splits apart into the three-corner triangle, with the Patient corner falling below the others
- Example seed: Co-pay coupons. Patient pays $0, feels grateful. Physician feels helpful — they prescribed a medicine and the patient can afford it. Insurer absorbs the full branded cost. The coupon eliminates the one moment (pharmacy counter) where a patient might ask about a generic. Walk through what each party sees vs. what they pay.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: raster (lunch setting, pharmacy counter), c2v (triangle diagram)
- Prerequisites: Basic understanding of how prescriptions are filled and paid for
- Exclusions: Insurance company profits, PBM mechanics in detail, individual drug pricing negotiations
- Score: 9/10

## Candidate 05 — Academic Detailing: The Counter-Model That Works and Stays Underfunded

- Source: `pharma-marketing/pantry/pharma-marketing-evidence-synthesis.md`
- Topic: PHARMA MARKETING
- Hook: There exists a proven alternative to pharmaceutical detailing — same in-person format, same physician targeting — that improves prescribing quality instead of distorting it. It is chronically underfunded because the parties who benefit most from it are the least organized to pay for it.
- Key case: A systematic review of 118 randomized and nonrandomized trials found that academic detailing — unbiased educational outreach from non-commercial teams — produced significant improvements in at least one prescribing behavior in 69% of the lowest-bias studies, with a median absolute change of 4.0%. The Veterans Affairs system runs the most successful US program. Most health systems have none.
- The Question: If academic detailing demonstrably works and uses the same delivery infrastructure as commercial detailing, why is it funded at a fraction of commercial detailing's scale?
- Core idea: Academic detailing is funded by payers and governments, who capture the benefit (lower drug costs, better patient outcomes) diffusely and over long time horizons. Commercial detailing is funded by pharma brands, who capture the benefit (increased branded prescriptions) specifically and immediately. The funding asymmetry is not a market failure in the usual sense — it is a collective action problem among the many diffuse parties who would benefit.
- Visual object: A side-by-side funding scale — commercial detailing ($30B annual spend, growing) vs. academic detailing (fragmented, government-funded, small). Same delivery method, opposite paymaster.
- Manim move: accumulate → the commercial detailing stack builds up tallly vs. the academic stack barely registers; then a "who captures the benefit" label appears on each
- Example seed: The VA academic detailing program identified a class of drugs being overprescribed relative to evidence (antipsychotics in elderly patients). After a detailing program targeting VA physicians, prescribing declined measurably and patient outcomes improved. That program has operated for decades. It has never been replicated at the scale of a single large pharma brand's rep force.
- Length band: 3–5 min
- Still lanes: geo (VA facility, academic medical center), c2v (funding comparison), raster (physician-rep interaction)
- Prerequisites: Basic awareness of pharmaceutical sales practices; no technical knowledge required
- Exclusions: Drug approval, insurance formulary policy, individual drug comparisons
- Score: 8/10
