# Chapter 2 — Early Globalization: The Atlantic World, 1492–1650

*A biological event told as a political one.*

In 1571 a Spanish friar named Bartolomé de Las Casas, then in his eighties, completed the final revisions on a manuscript he had been working on for decades. He had first arrived in Hispaniola in 1502 as a colonist. He had received an *encomienda* — a Spanish royal grant of native labor — and had himself enslaved indigenous people for over a decade. Then he changed his mind. In 1515 he renounced his *encomienda*, freed the people held under it, and spent the next half-century writing, lobbying, and arguing against the Spanish treatment of Indigenous Americans. His *Brevísima relación de la destrucción de las Indias*, published in 1552, was specific. He named places. He named numbers. He described Spanish soldiers betting on whether they could cut a man in half with a single sword stroke. He estimated that the population of Hispaniola, which he had seen in 1502, had fallen from three million to two hundred by 1542.

Las Casas was off on the starting number — modern estimates of Hispaniola's pre-contact population range from several hundred thousand to perhaps a million. The ending number was likely closer to correct. What is not contested by any modern scholarship is that something demographically catastrophic happened to the Indigenous population of Hispaniola in the forty years after Columbus's first voyage, and that it happened across the Caribbean and then through the mainland Americas over the following century and a half. The number that historical demographers debate is what fraction of the indigenous population of the Americas died between 1492 and 1650 — most current estimates fall between 80 and 95 percent. The total death toll, depending on the starting estimate, runs from roughly 30 million to perhaps 90 million people.

This is the largest demographic catastrophe in human history, and it happened mostly before any sustained European settlement of the mainland Americas. By the time the Pilgrims landed at Plymouth in 1620, the population of New England had already collapsed; what they took for an empty wilderness was an ecology recently emptied of the human beings who had managed it for thousands of years.

The puzzle of this chapter is how to tell that story honestly. The traditional textbook framing — "the Age of Exploration," "the Columbian Exchange," "the Spanish, Portuguese, English, French, and Dutch competed to colonize" — is not wrong, but the framing centers the wrong actors. The dominant story of the Atlantic world from 1492 to 1650 is not what European monarchs decided to do. It is what microorganisms did to a continent that had no immunity to them, and what that demographic collapse made possible.

## What the Columbian Exchange actually exchanged

The historian Alfred Crosby published *The Columbian Exchange: Biological and Cultural Consequences of 1492* in 1972. The book made an argument that, in 1972, was novel and now sits at the center of how historians think about the period. Crosby's claim was that the most important consequences of 1492 were not political or economic but biological. Plants, animals, and microorganisms crossed the Atlantic in both directions, and the world that resulted was not the world that existed in 1491.

From the Americas to Europe and Africa: maize, potatoes, sweet potatoes, tomatoes, peppers, beans, peanuts, cassava, cacao, tobacco, vanilla, pineapples, quinoa, sunflowers, and turkeys. From Europe and Africa to the Americas: wheat, barley, oats, rice, sugar, coffee, bananas, citrus fruits, grapes, olives, horses, cattle, pigs, sheep, goats, chickens, donkeys, honeybees — and a microbiological cargo that included smallpox, measles, mumps, influenza, typhus, bubonic plague, and several others. In both directions the biological transfers transformed agriculture. The potato, transplanted to northern Europe, ended a recurring pattern of famine and supported the population growth that fueled European industrialization. Maize and cassava became staple crops across sub-Saharan Africa, raising population there. The horse, reintroduced to the Americas (where its ancestors had gone extinct around 10,000 years ago), transformed the cultures of the Great Plains within two centuries of contact.

The microbiological transfer was asymmetrical. The Americas had no equivalent of the Old World's dense, sedentary domesticated-animal economies. The pathogens that Europeans and Africans had developed partial resistance to over millennia — through repeated exposure, through deaths concentrated in childhood, through the slow biological accommodation of populations to endemic disease — were new to the Americas. The first European epidemic to hit the Caribbean is usually identified as a smallpox outbreak in 1518–19. It then jumped to the mainland just in time to be present at the Spanish siege of Tenochtitlán in 1520–21, where it killed something on the order of 40 percent of the city's population during the months Cortés was at the gates. The Aztec emperor who succeeded Moctezuma — his nephew Cuitláhuac, who had organized the resistance that drove the Spanish out of Tenochtitlán in 1520 — died of smallpox in December of that year. Cortés's reconquest in 1521 happened against a city whose population was depopulated by disease before the siege.

This pattern repeats. The Inca civil war of 1527–1532, which the Spaniard Francisco Pizarro exploited to conquer the empire with fewer than two hundred men, was at least partly caused by the death of the Inca emperor Huayna Capac in a smallpox epidemic that had preceded Pizarro's arrival up the Andean coast. The Mississippian cities of southeastern North America were already in decline by the time Hernando de Soto traveled through the region in 1539–1542; archaeological work suggests that disease, possibly transmitted overland from the Caribbean by way of intermittent contact, had begun to depopulate the urban Mississippian world before any Spanish entered it. By the time English colonists arrived in southeastern North America in the seventeenth century, the population of the region had fallen by perhaps 80 percent. The "vacancy" of the land — a phrase the colonists themselves used — was real, and was less than a century old.

The standard textbook framing — that European technology, organization, and military prowess explain the conquest — needs revision in light of the demographic evidence. The European conquerors had real technological and organizational advantages: gunpowder weapons, steel, oceangoing ships, written record-keeping, the wheel for transportation in places that had not used it. They also exploited divisions within indigenous polities, often allying with one faction against another. But the principal force that broke indigenous resistance was epidemic disease, against which no military preparation could have helped. The conquest was a biological event with a military rider, not the other way around.

## The labor problem, and what produced the answer

The Spanish in the Caribbean and then in central Mexico and Peru had, by the 1520s, conquered enormous territories. The territories had previously been agriculturally productive; they no longer had the labor force to remain so. Indigenous populations were dying in numbers the Spanish could not have killed even if they had wanted to. The Spanish had real estate without workers and a metropolitan economy that demanded gold and silver. The combination produced an economic crisis specific to the Spanish empire: how to extract the wealth of the Americas with a labor force that was vanishing.

The first answer was the *encomienda* system, which gave individual Spaniards legal rights to the labor of named indigenous communities. The system worked badly. The communities were dying, and the Spaniards working them generally did so as if the labor force were inexhaustible. Las Casas's *Brevísima relación* described the consequences in graphic detail; the New Laws of 1542, partly inspired by his lobbying, formally abolished new *encomiendas* and tried to restrict the existing ones. The old colonists in Mexico and Peru revolted; the New Laws were largely unenforced. But by mid-century the *encomienda* system had become biologically unsustainable. There were not enough surviving Indigenous laborers to support the imperial economy.

The second answer is the one that defined the next three and a half centuries. The Portuguese, beginning earlier in the fifteenth century, had established sugar plantations on the Atlantic islands of Madeira and the Cape Verdes, worked with enslaved labor purchased on the West African coast. The system was profitable. The Portuguese had built fortified trading posts down the African coast — Elmina Castle in present-day Ghana, completed in 1482, was one of the earliest — that became the European entry points into the West African slave trade. Other West African states, which had their own systems of slavery already, sold captured enemies and debtors into the Portuguese trade for European goods. By the 1520s the Portuguese were transporting enslaved Africans to plantations in the Atlantic islands and, increasingly, to Brazil, where Portuguese sugar production was becoming the most lucrative agricultural enterprise in the Atlantic world.

The Spanish, lacking Portuguese access to the West African coast (a 1494 treaty had split the world's non-Christian zones between the two crowns, giving West Africa to Portugal), purchased enslaved Africans from Portuguese traders and shipped them to the Caribbean and the Spanish mainland. The transition from indigenous labor to African slave labor in the Spanish Americas happened gradually across the second half of the sixteenth century, accelerating in the seventeenth as the indigenous demographic collapse continued. By 1650, the Atlantic system was a system of Black enslaved labor producing tropical commodities — sugar, then tobacco, then in the eighteenth century rice, then cotton — on plantations owned by Europeans, in colonies governed by European powers, with the profits flowing back across the Atlantic to fund European industrial development.

The system that emerged was new. The previous chapter described how slavery, as practiced in West Africa, the Islamic world, and Mediterranean Europe, was a permanent or temporary form of unfreedom that was not racially defined, was sometimes inheritable and sometimes not, and was not chattel in the strict legal sense. The Atlantic system was racially defined, was made hereditary by colonial legal codes (Virginia in 1662, other colonies in the following decades), and was chattel — enslaved people were legally the same kind of property as livestock. The point is worth repeating because it is one of the most-confused claims in popular history. The Atlantic slave system was not a continuation of older practices. It was a new institution, distinguishable in its racial basis, its heredity, its legal classification of human beings as personal property, and its scale.

About 12.5 million Africans were embarked on slave ships across the Atlantic between 1501 and 1866. Roughly 10.7 million survived the Middle Passage. The numbers come from the [Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database](https://www.slavevoyages.org/), an ongoing scholarly project that has catalogued individual voyages with their cargoes, origins, and destinations. Of those 10.7 million survivors, fewer than 400,000 went directly to what is now the United States. The vast majority went to Brazil, the Caribbean, and Spanish South America. This is one of the most-misunderstood numbers in the field. The United States is the country where the consequences of Atlantic slavery have been most loudly contested politically; it received only about 4 percent of the people transported in the trade.

## Religion as engine and pretext

The Catholic-Protestant schism that opened in Europe with Martin Luther's *Ninety-Five Theses* in 1517 and Henry VIII's break with Rome in the 1530s became, by the seventeenth century, one of the organizing dynamics of European colonization. The colonial competition that produced English, French, and Dutch settlements in the Americas was partly religious. Spain and Portugal — the early colonial powers, both Catholic — built empires that organized themselves around the conversion of indigenous people to Christianity. The English and Dutch, after their reformations, framed their colonial projects partly in opposition to Catholic Spain.

Some of this framing was sincere. The Puritans who settled New England in the 1620s and 1630s were religious refugees as well as economic adventurers, and their political-theological commitments shaped colonial life from the start. Some of it was pretextual. The English privateer Francis Drake, who plundered Spanish ships in the Caribbean in the 1570s and 1580s, framed his attacks as religiously justified resistance against Catholic tyranny; he also kept the gold. Queen Elizabeth I gave Drake's expeditions explicit and implicit royal backing partly because the Spanish wealth he stole funded English state finance.

The seventeenth-century English colonies that became the United States — Virginia (1607), Plymouth (1620), Massachusetts Bay (1630), Maryland (1634), Connecticut (1636), Rhode Island (1636), New Hampshire (1638), Pennsylvania (1681), the Carolinas (1663), Georgia (1733) — had varied religious foundations. Virginia was Anglican and primarily commercial. Massachusetts was Congregationalist Puritan. Maryland was founded as a Catholic refuge by Lord Baltimore but became Protestant-majority within a generation. Rhode Island was founded by Roger Williams as a refuge for religious dissenters from Massachusetts. Pennsylvania was Quaker. These religious distinctions mattered for colonial politics, for legal codes, and for what the colonies sought from settlers. They did not change the underlying economic structure of the colonies, which was, with regional variation, the same Atlantic system that produced the slave trade.

## The Black Legend and the framing problem

The English colonization of North America in the seventeenth century was justified, in much of the contemporary English literature, by the so-called *Black Legend* — the idea that the Spanish had been uniquely cruel in their treatment of Indigenous Americans, and that English Protestant colonization would be more humane. The framing was widely circulated. Las Casas's *Brevísima relación*, translated into English in 1583, was used by English propagandists to support the contrast. The first English captain to settle North America permanently, John Smith of Jamestown, wrote pamphlets contrasting English and Spanish methods.

The Black Legend had some real basis. Spanish colonial violence against Indigenous Americans was extensive, documented, and in some periods systematic. But the framing was also self-serving. It distinguished English from Spanish colonial cruelty by ignoring (or denying) the comparable cruelty English colonists practiced against the people whose land they took. The Pequot War in New England in 1636–37 — in which English militia from Massachusetts Bay surrounded a Pequot fort at Mystic, set it on fire, and killed somewhere between 400 and 700 men, women, and children, mostly by burning them alive — happened seventeen years after the Plymouth landing. The Powhatan War in Virginia (1610–1646) included English raids that destroyed entire Powhatan villages and reduced the Powhatan population from perhaps 15,000 in 1607 to a few thousand by mid-century. King Philip's War in New England in 1675–76 killed roughly 40 percent of the New England Native population.

The Black Legend persisted because it served the political-cultural purposes of Protestant European colonization. It survived into nineteenth- and twentieth-century American historiography as a way of explaining why the United States, descended from English colonization, was not implicated in the worst of European colonial violence in the way Spanish-descended Latin American countries were. The framing is wrong on the facts. English and Dutch colonists were as violent toward the Indigenous people they encountered as Spanish or Portuguese colonists; the difference was in style, scale, and what got recorded in surviving documents, not in essential character.

This is the kind of framing problem that runs through the rest of this book, and it is worth naming early. The framings you will encounter — in older textbooks, in popular accounts, in default LLM responses — are not always wrong, but they are often selective in ways that come from the political-cultural purposes the original framings served. Catching them is one of the skills this book asks you to build.
