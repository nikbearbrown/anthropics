# Chapter 15 — The Civil War, 1860–1865

*The deadliest war in American history, fought over what its participants knew it was about.*

In March 1861, three weeks before the firing on Fort Sumter, the vice president of the newly-formed Confederate States of America gave a speech in Savannah, Georgia, that has come to be called the Cornerstone Speech. Alexander H. Stephens — a former U.S. congressman from Georgia who had served in Washington alongside Abraham Lincoln during the late 1840s, and who had been a Unionist as recently as the previous November — explained to his audience what the new Confederate government had been founded to defend.

"Our new government is founded upon exactly the opposite idea" from the United States government, Stephens said. "Its foundations are laid, its cornerstone rests, upon the great truth that the negro is not equal to the white man; that slavery, subordination to the superior race, is his natural and normal condition. This, our new government, is the first, in the history of the world, based upon this great physical, philosophical, and moral truth."

Stephens was not speaking outside Confederate consensus. The secession ordinances of the seceding states had said the same thing in their texts. Mississippi's ordinance: "Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery — the greatest material interest of the world." South Carolina's "Declaration of the Immediate Causes of Secession": "an increasing hostility on the part of the non-slaveholding States to the institution of slavery, has led to a disregard of their obligations." Texas's ordinance: "the African race... were rightfully held and regarded as an inferior and dependent race." The Confederate constitution explicitly protected slavery in ways the U.S. Constitution did not. The Confederate leaders, in the spring of 1861, were unambiguous about what they had seceded for. The slavery framing was, in the words of the men who built the Confederacy in real time, *the cornerstone*.

This is worth setting out at the start because the question of what the Civil War was about has been more contested in subsequent American memory than perhaps any other historical question about the country. The Confederate leaders of 1860–61 said the war was about slavery. The Union political leaders of 1860–65, including Lincoln, said it was about slavery (in conjunction with the preservation of the Union, which Lincoln considered the immediate political question; the slavery question was the underlying substantive question that had made the Union politically untenable). The historians who have studied the period for a century and a half have, overwhelmingly, concluded that it was about slavery. The popular "states' rights" framing of the war's causes — the framing that emerged after the Confederate defeat, was institutionalized in the postwar Lost Cause narrative, and persists in some popular accounts — was not the framing the Confederate leaders themselves used in 1861.

This chapter is about the war as it was actually fought, with attention to the questions where the popular memory most often diverges from the documentary record. The war ran from April 12, 1861 (the firing on Fort Sumter) to May 1865 (the last Confederate surrenders). It killed approximately 750,000 soldiers — recent revised estimates, by historian J. David Hacker using improved census-based methods, have pushed the figure substantially above the long-standard 620,000. The death toll was approximately 2.5 percent of the American population of 1860 — proportionally equivalent to about 8 million American deaths today. The war was the largest single mortality event in American history.

## What the war was about

The "states' rights" framing of the Civil War's causes deserves a specific response, because it is the framing most likely to appear in default LLM outputs on the question.

The framing argues that the seceding states were defending the constitutional principle of states' rights against federal encroachment, and that slavery was incidental — the specific issue on which the more fundamental states-rights principle happened to come to a head. The framing has rhetorical advantages. It allows the Civil War to be remembered as a constitutional dispute rather than as a war over slavery. It elevates the Confederate cause from defending an institution most modern Americans regard as morally indefensible to defending a principle (federalism, local sovereignty) most modern Americans regard as politically respectable. It allows Confederate political and military leaders to be remembered as principled men rather than as defenders of human bondage.

The framing is wrong on the facts. The seceding states' own contemporary documents — the secession ordinances, the speeches of their political leaders, the Confederate Constitution — were explicit that the institution being defended was slavery. The Confederate Constitution did not generally expand states-rights protections beyond the U.S. Constitution; in some respects it narrowed them. Confederate Article I, Section 9, paragraph 4 specifically prohibited Confederate state legislatures from abolishing slavery: "No bill of attainder, ex post facto law, or law denying or impairing the right of property in negro slaves shall be passed." The Confederate government, when it operated, was substantially more centralized in some respects (military conscription, taxation, currency issuance) than the federal government had been in 1860. States-rights ideology was a useful rhetorical resource for defending the institution of slavery against federal political pressure; it was not the substantive commitment that produced secession.

The states-rights framing emerged after the war, not before it. The Lost Cause narrative that consolidated in the post-Reconstruction decades reframed the war retrospectively to make Confederate political memory more defensible. The reframing was deliberate. Confederate veterans' organizations, Southern political and cultural institutions, and sympathetic Northern historians (the Dunning School, as discussed in Chapter 16) collaborated across the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries to produce the framing that survives in some popular accounts today. The framing is, in short, postwar political-cultural work projected backward onto the prewar political situation. It does not describe what the seceding states themselves said they were doing.

This is not a contested historiographic question. The current consensus among professional historians on the Civil War's causes is essentially unanimous: the war was about slavery, in conjunction with the political question of whether a sectional slaveholding minority could continue to control federal policy on slavery's extension. The historiographic debate is over precise mechanisms and contingencies, not over the fundamental fact. Popular accounts and LLM outputs that retain the "states' rights" framing are reflecting older popular memory, not current scholarship.

## The shape of the war

The military narrative of the Civil War is dense, and a comprehensive account is the work of multi-volume books. What follows is a compression to the major turning points, with attention to the political and economic transformations the war produced.

**1861.** The war began with the Confederate bombardment of Fort Sumter in Charleston Harbor on April 12, 1861. The fort surrendered on April 14 without Union casualties. Lincoln responded with a call for 75,000 volunteers; the Northern response was overwhelming. The remaining border-state question (Virginia, Arkansas, Tennessee, North Carolina) was decided when these states seceded in April–June in response to Lincoln's mobilization call. The first major engagement, the First Battle of Bull Run on July 21, 1861, was a Confederate victory that disabused the Union of any expectation of a quick war. The Union began the long process of organizing and training an army (the Army of the Potomac under General George B. McClellan from August 1861) for a sustained conflict.

**1862.** The war's first full year was one of strategic ambiguity for the Union and tactical brilliance for the Confederacy. The Confederate Army of Northern Virginia under General Robert E. Lee (who took command after General Joseph E. Johnston was wounded at Seven Pines in May 1862) defeated successive Union commanders in the Peninsular Campaign, Second Bull Run, and the Maryland Campaign. The Battle of Antietam on September 17, 1862 — the bloodiest single day in American military history, with about 23,000 combined casualties — was a strategic Union victory (Lee was forced to withdraw from Maryland) that gave Lincoln the political conditions to issue the preliminary Emancipation Proclamation five days later. In the Western Theater, Union forces under General Ulysses S. Grant captured Forts Henry and Donelson in Tennessee in February, fought the bloody battle of Shiloh in April (about 23,000 combined casualties), and began the Mississippi River campaign that would culminate in Vicksburg the following year.

**1863.** The war's turning year. The Emancipation Proclamation, effective January 1, 1863, transformed the legal status of the conflict — though, as we will see, not in the way the popular framing usually describes. The Battle of Gettysburg on July 1–3, 1863 (the largest battle ever fought on the North American continent, with about 51,000 combined casualties across three days) ended Lee's second invasion of the North and marked the high-water mark of Confederate military fortunes. Vicksburg fell to Grant on July 4, 1863, giving the Union control of the Mississippi River and splitting the Confederacy in two. The Battle of Chattanooga in November opened the Deep South to Union invasion. The political-military framework of the war shifted in 1863 from a contest the Confederacy might win by inflicting sufficient casualties to break Northern political will to a contest the Confederacy was eventually going to lose.

**1864.** The year of attrition. Grant, promoted to general-in-chief in March 1864, conducted a coordinated multi-front offensive: Grant himself with the Army of the Potomac against Lee in Virginia (the Overland Campaign, May–June 1864; 55,000 Union casualties in six weeks), Sherman's army group against Atlanta (captured September 2, 1864), Philip Sheridan's army in the Shenandoah Valley (which Sheridan deliberately scorched to deny food and shelter to Confederate forces). The Atlanta capture probably secured Lincoln's reelection in November 1864 against Democratic challenger George B. McClellan, who had run on a platform of negotiated peace. Lincoln's reelection committed the United States to the continued prosecution of the war to a Confederate surrender.

**1865.** Sherman's march from Atlanta to Savannah (November–December 1864) and then through the Carolinas (January–April 1865) destroyed Confederate logistical capacity in the Deep South. Grant's siege of Petersburg, lasting from June 1864 through April 1865, slowly destroyed Lee's Army of Northern Virginia. Lee surrendered at Appomattox Court House on April 9, 1865. The remaining Confederate forces surrendered across the following weeks. Lincoln was assassinated by John Wilkes Booth on April 14, 1865, five days after Appomattox and one day before Easter; he died on April 15. The transition from the war to Reconstruction — the subject of Chapter 16 — happened under the leadership of Vice President Andrew Johnson, whose accession would prove disastrous for the political settlement of the war's outcomes.

## What the Emancipation Proclamation actually did

The Emancipation Proclamation is among the most reliably misdescribed documents in American history. The default LLM output on the Proclamation — "Abraham Lincoln freed the slaves" or "the Emancipation Proclamation ended slavery in the United States" — is wrong in specific ways that the Proclamation's text makes immediately clear.

The Proclamation was issued in two stages. The preliminary Proclamation of September 22, 1862 announced that Lincoln, acting under his war powers as commander-in-chief, would declare free all enslaved people in any state still in rebellion against the United States as of January 1, 1863. The final Proclamation of January 1, 1863 implemented this declaration. It declared free the enslaved people in the seceding states.

The Proclamation's specific terms made its reach narrower than the popular framing suggests:

- It applied only to enslaved people in the seceding states — the eleven Confederate states. The slave states that had not seceded (Maryland, Delaware, Kentucky, Missouri) were *exempted*. Slavery in those states remained legal under the Proclamation. The exemption was a political-military calculation: Lincoln needed to keep the border states in the Union, and a proclamation that freed enslaved people in the border states would have pushed them toward the Confederacy.
- Within the seceding states, the Proclamation exempted specific Union-occupied areas that had already returned to federal control (parts of Louisiana, parts of Virginia). The legal status of enslaved people in those areas was deferred.
- The Proclamation rested on Lincoln's war powers as commander-in-chief. It was an executive military order, not a statute. Its constitutional standing in peacetime, after the war's end, would have been uncertain.

What the Proclamation actually accomplished was substantial but specific. It changed the legal status of approximately 3.5 million people in the seceding states from "enslaved" to "free" under federal law — even though, for most of them, federal authority did not actually reach into their territory at the moment of the Proclamation. It committed the Union war effort to emancipation as a war aim. It authorized the recruitment of African-American soldiers into the Union army; the United States Colored Troops eventually numbered approximately 180,000 men, about 10 percent of the Union army's strength, and their military contribution was substantial. It made foreign recognition of the Confederacy by Britain or France politically impossible after January 1, 1863 — neither European power was willing to recognize a government explicitly fighting against emancipation.

The Proclamation did *not* end slavery in the United States. Slavery in the loyal border states continued legally. Slavery in any seceding state that returned to the Union before the war's end could potentially have continued. The Proclamation's emancipation extended in practice to enslaved people whose communities came under Union military control as the war progressed; their actual freedom was substantially the product of their own action (escaping to Union lines, in the hundreds of thousands; the historian Steven Hahn and others have called this "the largest slave rebellion in American history") and of the advancing Union armies.

Slavery in the United States was formally and permanently ended by the Thirteenth Amendment, which was passed by Congress on January 31, 1865 (with Lincoln's strong support; the Spielberg film *Lincoln* dramatizes this congressional maneuvering) and ratified by the required three-fourths of the states on December 6, 1865 — about eight months after Lincoln's death. The Thirteenth Amendment, not the Emancipation Proclamation, ended slavery as a legal institution in the United States.

The framing problem here is durable. The Emancipation Proclamation has accumulated, in subsequent American memory, a moral and symbolic weight that has substantially obscured its specific legal content. The framing matters because the political process of actual emancipation — which involved a constitutional amendment requiring congressional supermajorities and state ratification, plus the war's military prosecution, plus the actions of millions of enslaved people taking their own freedom — was substantially more contested and required substantially more political work than the simplified "Lincoln freed the slaves" framing suggests. The Proclamation is one moment in that longer process, not the resolution of it.

## What the war transformed

Beyond emancipation, the war produced political-economic transformations of the federal government that are worth naming briefly.

**The size and scope of the federal government.** The wartime Congress, with the Southern Democratic bloc absent through secession, passed major legislation that would have been politically impossible in the antebellum decades: the Morrill Tariff Act (1861) raising federal tariffs substantially, the Homestead Act (1862) providing 160-acre federal land grants to settlers, the Pacific Railway Act (1862) authorizing federal land grants and subsidies for the transcontinental railroad, the Morrill Land-Grant Colleges Act (1862) creating state-funded universities, the National Banking Acts (1863, 1864) creating a national banking system and a national currency, the income tax (1861, expanded in 1862) — the first federal income tax in American history — and the establishment of the federal Department of Agriculture and the federal Bureau of Internal Revenue. The wartime federal government became a much larger and more active institution than the antebellum one. Many of these institutions persisted after the war.

**The transformation of the army and the navy.** The Union army, which had numbered about 16,000 regulars at the war's start, grew to approximately one million men under arms at peak strength. The Navy, which had been a small coastal force, became the world's largest by ship count and pioneered ironclad warships and steam-powered fleet operations. The military-industrial coordination the war required — railroads delivering troops and supplies, telegraph networks coordinating multi-front campaigns, mass production of weapons and uniforms — pioneered the organizational forms that would become standard in industrial-era warfare.

**The political destruction of Southern political power.** The pre-war Southern Democratic political coalition that had controlled federal politics for most of the antebellum decades was destroyed by the war. The eleven seceding states' political institutions were dismantled and rebuilt under federal Reconstruction (Chapter 16). The political-economic class that had dominated Southern politics — the planter elite — emerged from the war substantially weakened: their enslaved property (worth $3.5 billion in 1860) was uncompensated, their plantations were partly destroyed, their political institutions were rebuilt under federal supervision. The post-war Southern political system was a different system from the pre-war one in substantial ways, though (as Chapter 16 discusses) the post-war restoration of white-supremacist political control through different institutional mechanisms was substantially complete by the 1880s.

The Civil War, in short, was both a military conflict that the Union won and a constitutional revolution that produced the three Reconstruction Amendments and the legal-political framework for everything that followed. The next chapter takes up that constitutional revolution and its rapid partial reversal.
