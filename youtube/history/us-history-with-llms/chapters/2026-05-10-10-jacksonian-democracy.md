# Chapter 10 — Jacksonian Democracy, 1820–1840

*An expansion of one democracy alongside the violent contraction of two others.*

In the spring of 1838, a U.S. Army general named Winfield Scott arrived in the Cherokee Nation in what is now northern Georgia, southeastern Tennessee, and western North Carolina, with orders to remove the entire Cherokee population to the Indian Territory west of the Mississippi River. The Cherokee Nation had, over the previous half-century, attempted what no other Indigenous nation in the Southeast had attempted at the same scale: a strategy of accommodation to American political culture. They had drafted a written constitution modeled on the U.S. Constitution (1827). They had adopted Sequoyah's syllabary and produced a literate population that published its own newspaper, the *Cherokee Phoenix*. They had built towns, schools, and commercial farms. They had pursued their legal claims through the federal court system. In *Worcester v. Georgia* (1832), the U.S. Supreme Court had ruled in their favor: Georgia could not extend its state laws over Cherokee territory, the federal government's relationship with the Cherokee Nation was a relationship between sovereign nations, and the United States was obligated to respect Cherokee territorial sovereignty.

President Andrew Jackson, on receiving the Supreme Court's decision, is reputed to have said, "John Marshall has made his decision, now let him enforce it." The exact phrasing is unverified, but the operational meaning was clear: the executive branch would not enforce the Supreme Court ruling. Two years later, in 1835, a small faction of Cherokee leaders without authority from the elected Cherokee government signed the Treaty of New Echota, agreeing to removal in exchange for compensation. The U.S. Senate ratified the treaty by one vote in May 1836. The elected Cherokee government rejected it; about 16,000 Cherokee refused to leave; petitions to Congress with thousands of signatures (including a petition signed by over 16,000 white Americans organized by Ralph Waldo Emerson and others) were filed in opposition.

In May 1838, the U.S. Army began rounding up the resistant Cherokee at bayonet-point. About 17,000 people were marched, across the summer of 1838 and the winter of 1838–39, from their homes to the Indian Territory — a journey of roughly 1,000 miles, mostly on foot, through some of the harshest weather of any of the removal years. Approximately 4,000 Cherokee died on the journey or shortly after arrival. The Cherokee called the route *Nunna daul Tsuny* — "the trail where they cried." It is now known in English as the Trail of Tears.

The Trail of Tears was one episode in the systematic forced removal of the so-called Five Civilized Tribes — the Cherokee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Creek, and Seminole — from the southeastern United States to the Indian Territory (present-day Oklahoma) under the Indian Removal Act of 1830. The Removal Act was signed by President Andrew Jackson in May 1830. The removals were carried out across the 1830s by the Jackson and Van Buren administrations. Approximately 60,000 Indigenous people from the southeastern nations were forced west under the Act's authority; estimates of removal-related deaths range from 8,000 to 13,000. The southeastern Indigenous nations had been an independent political force in the region for thousands of years. They were, by 1840, effectively gone.

This is the central paradox of the period this chapter covers. The 1820s and 1830s saw the most rapid expansion of formal political participation in American history to that point — the elimination of property qualifications for voting in most states, the rise of mass political parties with national reach, the development of campaign rallies and partisan newspapers and the political culture that survives in recognizable form to the present. The 1820s and 1830s also saw the violent forced removal of Indigenous nations from the southeastern United States, the consolidation of the racial-slavery system in the South, the disenfranchisement of free Black voters in states that had previously allowed them to vote (Pennsylvania disenfranchised free Black men in its 1838 constitution), and the systematic legal contraction of women's political and economic standing. The democratic expansion and the violent contractions happened simultaneously, were carried out by the same political coalition, and depended on each other in ways that are easy to miss when the two stories are told separately.

The puzzle of this chapter is what "Jacksonian democracy" actually was — not as it was celebrated by Jackson's supporters at the time or by older generations of American historians, but as it operated for the actual populations it included and excluded.

## What a democracy looked like in the 1830s

The institutional expansion of formal democracy in the 1820s and 1830s was real. Across the period, most states eliminated the property qualifications for voting that had been standard in colonial and early-national constitutions. By 1840, near-universal white-male suffrage was the norm in most states — meaning that white men over twenty-one could vote regardless of whether they owned property, paid taxes, or met the older religious qualifications. This was a substantial expansion. Suffrage participation rates — the percentage of eligible voters who actually voted — also rose: from about 27 percent of eligible voters in the 1824 presidential election to 80 percent in the 1840 election. The electorate was both broader and more engaged than it had been a generation earlier.

The institutional infrastructure of mass democratic politics took shape across the same period. The first national political conventions — at which delegates from state party organizations chose presidential candidates — were held in 1831 (the Anti-Masonic Party) and 1832 (the National Republicans and the Democrats). The Democratic National Convention has met every four years since 1832, making it the oldest continuously operating political organization in the United States. Partisan newspapers proliferated. Mass-circulation campaign biographies were printed for the 1828 and 1832 elections. Campaign songs, slogans, parades, and rallies became the standard infrastructure of electoral politics. The 1840 campaign — the Whig "Log Cabin and Hard Cider" campaign that elected William Henry Harrison — was the first that recognizably resembled twentieth-century American electoral politics in its tactics and intensity.

The transformation produced a substantively different relationship between elected officials and the people who elected them. Members of Congress in the 1790s had operated on something like the framers' republican theory: elected for their judgment, expected to deliberate on the merits of legislation, accountable to voters only in the most general way. Members of Congress in the 1830s operated on something closer to the modern theory: tied to party platforms, accountable to their constituents on specific policy positions, organized into disciplined voting blocs. The transformation was uneven and the older deferential pattern persisted in some places (the Senate in particular, which was indirectly elected by state legislatures until 1913, was more insulated from popular pressure than the House). But the trend was toward mass-democratic politics in a form that the framers had explicitly opposed.

## The Nullification Crisis and what slavery had to do with it

The first major political crisis of the Jackson presidency was over the Tariff of 1828, called by its Southern critics the "Tariff of Abominations." The tariff was protectionist: it raised the prices of imported manufactured goods to advantage Northern manufacturers against British competition. It also raised the prices of goods Southern planters bought without raising the price of the cotton they exported. The Southern complaint was that the federal tariff was being used to transfer wealth from Southern agriculture to Northern manufacturing through the price mechanism.

South Carolina, where the cotton economy had been hit hard by the simultaneous decline in cotton prices through the 1820s, responded with the doctrine of *nullification*. The doctrine, articulated most fully by John C. Calhoun (then vice president under Jackson) in his anonymously published "South Carolina Exposition and Protest" (1828), held that the federal Constitution was a compact among sovereign states, and that any state could declare a federal law unconstitutional within its borders and refuse to enforce it. South Carolina formally nullified the federal tariff in November 1832, declared the federal law null and void within the state, and threatened secession if the federal government attempted to enforce it by force.

The crisis was resolved by a combination of force and compromise. Jackson — despite his political alliance with Calhoun and his own Southern political base — denied the constitutional doctrine of nullification, declared in his December 1832 Proclamation Against Nullification that South Carolina's action would "be regarded as treason and you may probably be punished with death," and obtained from Congress in March 1833 the Force Bill authorizing federal military action against South Carolina. Henry Clay simultaneously negotiated a compromise tariff that gradually reduced rates over the next decade. South Carolina rescinded its nullification ordinance but then nullified the Force Bill as a face-saving gesture. The immediate crisis was defused.

The deeper constitutional question — whether a state could nullify federal law it considered unconstitutional, or could secede from the Union if the federal government overrode its objections — was not resolved by the 1832–33 settlement. Calhoun continued to develop the nullification doctrine through the 1830s and 1840s. By 1850 the South Carolina nullification position had broadened into a more general states-rights argument that included the right of secession; by 1860 it had become the constitutional argument the seceding states would use to justify withdrawal from the Union. The Nullification Crisis of 1832–33 is, in retrospect, the first major rehearsal for the constitutional argument the Civil War would settle.

It is worth being explicit about what was always at stake. The nullification doctrine was an instrument. The interest it was an instrument for was, primarily and pervasively, the defense of slavery. Calhoun and the South Carolina nullifiers were defending the right of a slaveholding minority to resist federal policy they perceived as adverse to slavery's interests. The Tariff of 1828 was a specific occasion; the underlying anxiety was that any federal majority that could regulate Southern economic interests in one area (tariffs) could regulate them in others (slavery). The doctrine of nullification was reusable. The slaveholders knew it. So did the politicians on the other side. The nullification fight was about slavery as much as it was about textiles.

## The Bank War

The second major crisis of the Jackson presidency was the Bank War — Jackson's campaign to destroy the Second Bank of the United States, which ran from 1832 to 1836.

The Second Bank of the United States had been chartered in 1816 after the lapse of the First Bank's charter in 1811. It was a quasi-private national bank with the federal government as a major shareholder, branches in the major cities, and the function of stabilizing the national currency by regulating state-bank note issuance. Under the presidency of Nicholas Biddle from 1823, the Second Bank had successfully stabilized the post-1819 financial system and built itself into one of the most powerful financial institutions in the country.

Jackson hated the Bank. The hatred was partly ideological — Jackson was a hard-money man (a believer that only gold and silver should circulate as currency), and the Second Bank's paper note issuance offended him. The hatred was partly populist — the Bank was seen by many Western farmers and Eastern working-class men as an instrument of elite financial privilege. The hatred was partly personal — Jackson had been financially ruined by speculation in the 1790s and 1800s and never forgave the financial system.

In 1832, Senator Henry Clay (then running for president against Jackson on the National Republican ticket) maneuvered Congress into passing an early rechartering of the Second Bank, four years before the existing charter expired. Clay calculated that Jackson, in an election year, would be forced either to sign the rechartering or to alienate the Bank's defenders. Jackson vetoed the rechartering on July 10, 1832, in a veto message (largely drafted by his Secretary of the Treasury Roger Taney) that framed the Bank as an instrument of monied interests against the common citizen. The veto was politically devastating for Clay; Jackson won the 1832 election in a landslide.

Jackson then moved to destroy the Bank during his second term. He ordered the federal government's deposits withdrawn from the Bank and placed instead in selected state banks (called the "pet banks" by Jackson's critics). The Bank's charter expired in 1836 without renewal. Biddle's institution was eventually rechartered as a Pennsylvania state bank but never recovered its national position. The destruction of the Second Bank was substantial.

The economic consequences of the Bank War were severe. With the disappearance of the Second Bank's regulatory function, state banks dramatically expanded their note issuance — supplying the credit for a speculative land boom in the West that produced the second great speculative bubble in American history. Jackson tried to slow the boom with his Specie Circular of 1836, which required payment for federal land in specie rather than in bank notes. The combination of overextended state banking, speculative land bubbles, and the sudden shift to specie payment produced the Panic of 1837 — a banking crisis that produced a depression lasting from 1837 through about 1843. The depression was the worst the United States had experienced to that point and would remain the worst until 1873.

The political-economic legacy of the Bank War is complicated. Jackson's destruction of central banking shaped American financial development for the rest of the nineteenth century. The country would not have a central bank again until the Federal Reserve in 1913 — an 80-year gap during which periodic financial crises (1837, 1857, 1873, 1893, 1907) produced the political demand for a new central institution. The Jacksonian theory that banking was inherently anti-democratic and central banking inherently elite-serving became one of the durable ideological positions in American politics. The Bank War's specific outcome was a chronic financial instability that was not addressed effectively for almost a century.

## What "the people" meant in the Jacksonian formulation

Jackson and his political coalition spoke continuously and confidently of "the people." The phrase did substantial political work. It allowed Jackson to claim popular legitimacy for executive action that his political opponents considered constitutionally improper — the veto of the Bank rechartering, the removal of federal deposits, the refusal to enforce the *Worcester v. Georgia* decision. It allowed his party to claim a democratic mandate for policies (Indian Removal, the expansion of slavery into new territories, the suppression of abolitionist petitions in Congress through the "gag rule" of 1836–44) that benefited specific constituencies at the cost of others.

The "people" the Jacksonian formulation referred to was a substantially narrower category than the modern reader might assume. It was white male citizens, predominantly Protestant, predominantly of British or German descent, increasingly including the urban working class but excluding women, free Black men (who had been disenfranchised in most states by the 1830s), Indigenous people, and enslaved people. The "people" was about 30 percent of the total adult population of the United States in 1830, and the political program advanced under its banner was substantially designed to serve that 30 percent at the expense of the others.

This is the central point. The expansion of white-male political participation in the Jacksonian period was not just a generalized expansion of democracy. It was a specific expansion that was, in important ways, made *possible* by the contraction of political and personal rights for the excluded groups. The land that was redistributed to expanded white-male voters had been seized from Indigenous nations through the Removal Act. The federal authority that was wielded by the new mass-democratic electoral coalition was wielded substantially to defend and expand the institution of slavery against the political pressure of Northern abolitionists. The political-economic transformations of the Jacksonian period were a coordinated package: the expansion of one democracy and the contraction of two others.

The historian Edmund Morgan's argument from Chapter 3 applies again here. The political dynamic that produced expanded white-male political solidarity in the post-Revolutionary Chesapeake — the Bacon's Rebellion lesson, the legal differentiation of white indentured labor from Black hereditary slavery — operates again in the Jacksonian formulation, on a different scale and with different specifics. Free white men of the 1830s were defined as "the people" in ways that required the contemporaneous exclusion of women, free Black men, Indigenous people, and enslaved people. The exclusions were not failures of an otherwise democratic project. They were features of the project.

The next chapter takes up westward expansion across the same period, which is the direct continuation of the territorial-and-political logic the Removal Act set in motion. The chapters after that take up the antebellum reform movements that began to argue against the exclusions — and the political crisis those arguments produced.
