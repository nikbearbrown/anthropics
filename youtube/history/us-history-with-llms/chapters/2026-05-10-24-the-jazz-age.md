# Chapter 24 — The Jazz Age: Redefining the Nation, 1919–1929

*A decade of cultural conflict on top of a financial bubble.*

In the early hours of June 1, 1921, a white mob in Tulsa, Oklahoma, began attacking the predominantly Black Greenwood district — sometimes called Black Wall Street for its prosperous Black-owned businesses, including banks, theaters, hotels, restaurants, professional offices, and several hundred homes. The attack was triggered by a rumor that a young Black shoeshiner named Dick Rowland had assaulted a white elevator operator the previous day; the actual incident, as best historians have been able to reconstruct it, was probably an accidental contact that the elevator operator initially treated as nothing significant. The story was sensationalized by the *Tulsa Tribune* into a sexual assault accusation. A white mob gathered at the courthouse; Black World War I veterans gathered to defend Rowland from being lynched. Shots were fired. The white mob retreated and then returned in much larger numbers — perhaps 10,000 people — armed with rifles, machine guns, and, by some accounts, biplanes that may have dropped incendiary devices on Greenwood from the air.

The attack continued for about sixteen hours. The Greenwood district — about thirty-five square blocks — was substantially destroyed. The estimated death toll, contested at the time and contested in subsequent historical accounts, was somewhere between 75 and 300 dead, almost all of them Black. Approximately 6,000 Black Tulsans were detained in internment camps for periods of days to weeks; 1,256 houses were burned; the commercial district was almost entirely destroyed. The Oklahoma National Guard, which arrived the morning of June 1, primarily detained Black survivors rather than restraining the white attackers. No white person was ever criminally prosecuted for the massacre. The local insurance industry refused to pay claims to Greenwood property owners on the grounds that the destruction had been caused by "riot" (in this framing, by the Black residents themselves).

The Tulsa Race Massacre was substantially erased from American historical memory for most of the twentieth century. The 1921 *Tulsa Tribune* article that had triggered the violence was excised from microfilm archives at some point in the following decades. The local political and cultural institutions of Tulsa did not commemorate or even acknowledge the massacre. The historical investigation that produced the current understanding of the event began in earnest only in the late 1990s, with the 2001 Oklahoma Commission report and subsequent archaeological work that located mass graves. The 100th anniversary commemorations in 2021 were the first major public engagement with the massacre's history.

The puzzle of this chapter is the simultaneity of the 1920s prosperity and the 1920s violence. The decade is conventionally remembered as the Jazz Age — a period of cultural innovation, consumer prosperity, and modernist experimentation. The decade was also the period of the second Klan at its peak (4 to 5 million members), of major lynching campaigns across the South, of the Tulsa massacre and similar incidents in other cities, of the Sacco-Vanzetti trial that ended with two anarchist immigrants executed on weak evidence in 1927, of the National Origins Act of 1924 that drastically restricted immigration on racial lines, of the Scopes Trial that became a national flashpoint on questions of religious-cultural conflict. The Jazz Age and the cultural-political violence were the same decade. Understanding either one requires understanding the other.

## What the 1920s prosperity was

The American economic prosperity of the 1920s was substantial and uneven. Real per-capita income grew by approximately 30 percent across the decade. The automobile industry, which had been small in 1910, became one of the largest industrial sectors by 1929 — Ford produced over 15 million Model T automobiles between 1908 and 1927, and the introduction of consumer credit ("buy now, pay later") substantially expanded the automobile market. Electrical appliances — refrigerators, washing machines, vacuum cleaners, radios — became standard in middle-class urban households across the decade. Radio broadcasting, which had begun commercially with KDKA Pittsburgh in 1920, reached most American households by the end of the decade. The film industry, which had been small in 1910, became one of the largest American cultural industries, with Hollywood substantially supplanting earlier centers (Chicago, New York) by mid-decade. Mass-circulation magazines (the *Saturday Evening Post*, *Ladies' Home Journal*, *Time* magazine, founded 1923) became a major cultural force.

The prosperity was unevenly distributed. Agricultural workers, who still constituted a substantial fraction of the American workforce in 1920, experienced a sustained agricultural depression across most of the decade — crop prices had fallen sharply after the 1920 collapse of the wartime agricultural boom, and farm incomes did not recover. Black Americans, with limited exceptions, did not share in the urban prosperity at the same scale as white Americans. The post-WWI migration into Northern cities had produced expanded Black communities, but those communities remained substantially poorer than comparable white communities, segregated into specific urban districts (Harlem in New York, Bronzeville in Chicago) by racially-restrictive covenants and discriminatory real-estate practices. Immigrant workers — particularly the recent Southern and Eastern European immigrants who had arrived in the pre-1924 period — were substantially confined to specific industrial and service occupations and faced ongoing discrimination.

The prosperity also rested on financial conditions that were substantially unstable. The expansion of consumer credit, the speculative inflation of stock prices across the late 1920s, the international financial conditions (American loans to Germany were funding German reparations payments to Britain and France, which were then being used to repay wartime American loans — a circular flow that depended on continuous American capital export), and the underlying weakness of the agricultural and several major industrial sectors produced an economic system whose stability was substantially dependent on continued growth. The 1929 crash that ended the decade was, in retrospect, a substantial correction of these instabilities.

The decade's prosperity produced the political-economic framework that the Hoover administration would attempt to maintain after 1929 and that the Roosevelt administration would substantially restructure after 1932. The Republican administrations of the 1920s (Warren G. Harding 1921–23, Calvin Coolidge 1923–29, Herbert Hoover 1929–33) operated on the framework that minimal federal intervention in the economy and substantial favorable treatment of business would produce continued prosperity. Coolidge's slogan — "the chief business of the American people is business" — captured the framework. The framework worked while the prosperity continued. When it ended, the framework's deficiencies were substantial.

## The Harlem Renaissance and Black cultural production

The 1920s also produced the most concentrated burst of Black cultural-intellectual production in American history to that point. The *Harlem Renaissance* — the term applied retrospectively to the literary, musical, visual-arts, and intellectual movement centered in Harlem from roughly 1918 through the early 1930s — was a substantial cultural phenomenon whose specific institutions and figures shaped American cultural life through the rest of the twentieth century.

The institutional infrastructure included the *Crisis* magazine (the NAACP's monthly, edited by W.E.B. Du Bois from 1910 onward; substantial Renaissance work appeared in *Crisis* across the 1920s), *Opportunity* magazine (the National Urban League's monthly, edited by Charles S. Johnson; another major Renaissance venue), the Apollo Theater (in Harlem from 1934, but the Harlem theater scene of the 1920s preceded it), the Cotton Club (which paradoxically featured Black performers for white-only audiences across most of its operation), and a substantial network of Harlem-based literary, journalistic, and artistic salons.

The literary figures included Langston Hughes (whose first published poem appeared in *Crisis* in 1921 when he was nineteen; *The Weary Blues*, 1926), Zora Neale Hurston (anthropologist and novelist; *Their Eyes Were Watching God*, 1937), Countee Cullen (poet; *Color*, 1925), Claude McKay (poet and novelist; *Home to Harlem*, 1928), Jean Toomer (*Cane*, 1923, one of the most experimental American literary works of the decade), Nella Larsen (*Quicksand*, 1928; *Passing*, 1929), Wallace Thurman (*The Blacker the Berry*, 1929), and many others. James Weldon Johnson, who had been the executive secretary of the NAACP, was the elder statesman of the movement and the editor of the influential *Book of American Negro Poetry* (1922).

The musical figures included Duke Ellington (whose Cotton Club orchestra performed live network radio broadcasts that made him a national figure from the late 1920s onward), Louis Armstrong (who moved from New Orleans to Chicago to New York across the 1920s and recorded the Hot Five and Hot Seven sessions that substantially defined modern American jazz), Bessie Smith ("the Empress of the Blues"), Fletcher Henderson, Ma Rainey, and dozens of other musicians whose recordings during the 1920s established the canon of American jazz and blues. The recording industry of the period, which produced the "race records" marketed to Black audiences as a distinct category, was a major economic and cultural channel for Black musical production.

The intellectual figures included Du Bois (*Black Reconstruction in America* would not be published until 1935, but Du Bois's *Crisis* editorials across the 1920s shaped the political-intellectual environment), Alain Locke (whose 1925 anthology *The New Negro* gave the movement its initial name), and a substantial cohort of Black sociologists and historians (Carter G. Woodson founded the Association for the Study of Negro Life and History in 1915 and the *Journal of Negro History* in 1916; the institutional framework for academic Black-history scholarship was built across the 1910s and 1920s).

The political organizational infrastructure included the NAACP (which by the late 1920s was a major civil-rights organization with chapters across the country), the National Urban League (founded 1910, focused on Black urban migration issues), the Universal Negro Improvement Association under Marcus Garvey (which at peak claimed millions of members and was the largest Black political organization in American history to that point; Garvey was deported in 1927 after a federal mail-fraud conviction, and the UNIA's American presence declined substantially after that), and the National Council of Negro Women (founded 1935 by Mary McLeod Bethune, but the organizational infrastructure was being built earlier).

The Harlem Renaissance is sometimes treated as a discrete and time-bounded phenomenon. The framing understates the continuity. The institutional and intellectual networks built during the 1920s continued operating across subsequent decades; the figures who emerged during the Renaissance shaped American cultural life through the mid-twentieth century. Langston Hughes's career ran into the 1960s; Duke Ellington's into the 1970s. The cultural production of the 1920s was, in important ways, the foundation for the Civil Rights movement of the 1950s and 1960s — the political-organizational infrastructure (the NAACP, the Urban League, the *Crisis* readership) and the cultural infrastructure (the recognition of Black cultural production as substantively American culture) had been built in the Renaissance period.

## Prohibition, the second Klan, and the cultural conflict

The 1920s simultaneously produced a sustained cultural-political reaction against the changes urban industrial America had been undergoing.

**Prohibition** — the Eighteenth Amendment to the Constitution, ratified January 1919, prohibiting the manufacture, sale, and transportation of intoxicating liquors, and the Volstead Act of October 1919 that defined and enforced it — was the most ambitious federal social-policy experiment in American history to that point. The Amendment was substantially the political achievement of the moral-Progressive coalition that had been organizing across the previous half-century. Its enforcement across the 1920s was substantially ineffective. American per-capita alcohol consumption declined under Prohibition but did not approach zero; illegal alcohol production, distribution, and consumption became one of the major American industries of the decade; organized criminal organizations (Al Capone's Chicago operation being the most famous) substantially built their political and economic power on the alcohol trade. Federal enforcement was systematically underfunded and corrupt. By the late 1920s, even some major Prohibition advocates had come to conclude that the experiment was producing worse social consequences than the alcohol consumption it was meant to suppress. The Twenty-First Amendment of December 1933 repealed Prohibition, the only Amendment to the Constitution that has been repealed.

**The second Ku Klux Klan** was a substantially different organization from the post-Civil-War Klan that had been suppressed in the 1870s. It was founded in 1915 by William J. Simmons in Atlanta, partly inspired by the 1915 release of D.W. Griffith's *The Birth of a Nation* (which celebrated the original Klan). The second Klan's organizational infrastructure was built across 1915–25; its peak membership of approximately 4 to 5 million was reached in 1924–25. The second Klan was substantially different from the first in several respects. Its geographic base was nationwide rather than primarily Southern; the strongest state Klan organizations in the 1920s were in Indiana, Ohio, Pennsylvania, Oregon, and Colorado, not in the former Confederacy. Its targets were broader; in addition to Black Americans, the second Klan was substantially anti-Catholic, anti-Jewish, anti-immigrant, and anti-"modernist" generally. Its organizational structure was substantially more bureaucratic; the second Klan was, in some respects, a fraternal organization that competed with the Elks, the Masons, and the Knights of Columbus for middle-class membership.

The second Klan collapsed almost as rapidly as it had risen. The 1925 conviction of Indiana Klan leader D.C. Stephenson for the rape and murder of a young woman destroyed the Indiana Klan's political position (Stephenson had been one of the most powerful state-level Klan leaders nationally); subsequent scandals in other state Klans reduced membership across the late 1920s. The second Klan was substantially gone by 1930. Its political-cultural legacy — the framework of racial-religious-cultural nationalism, the institutional model of mass-membership white-supremacist organizations — would influence subsequent American politics in ways that continued through the twentieth century.

**The National Origins Act of 1924** (the Johnson-Reed Act) substantially restricted immigration to the United States on explicitly racial-national lines. The Act established immigration quotas based on the national-origins composition of the 1890 U.S. population — a baseline that substantially favored Northern and Western European immigration over Southern and Eastern European immigration. Asian immigration was substantially prohibited (with limited exceptions for the Philippines, which was an American colony). The Act reflected the moral-Progressive coalition's commitment to immigration restriction; the eugenicist intellectual framework that had developed across the previous two decades was substantially embedded in the Act's structure. The 1924 Act was the most restrictive American immigration legislation in the country's history; it would shape American immigration patterns until the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 substantially revised it.

**The Scopes Trial** of July 1925 was the most-publicized cultural-political event of the decade. John T. Scopes, a young high-school teacher in Dayton, Tennessee, was prosecuted under a Tennessee state law (the Butler Act of March 1925) that prohibited the teaching of evolution in public schools. The trial was orchestrated by the American Civil Liberties Union (founded 1920) as a test case. The prosecution was led by William Jennings Bryan, the three-time Democratic presidential candidate who had become a leading anti-evolution Christian fundamentalist in the postwar period. The defense was led by Clarence Darrow, the country's most famous criminal-defense lawyer. The trial was substantially covered by national radio (it was one of the first major American trials to receive live radio coverage) and by national newspapers (H.L. Mencken's coverage for the *Baltimore Sun* was particularly influential).

Scopes was convicted (the conviction was overturned on a procedural technicality on appeal; the Butler Act remained in force until 1967). The cultural-political significance of the trial substantially exceeded its legal outcome. The trial became the symbolic confrontation between modernist urban-secular culture (Darrow, Mencken, the ACLU) and traditional rural-Protestant culture (Bryan, the Butler Act, the Tennessee state government). The framing was somewhat overdrawn — Bryan was politically a Progressive whose anti-evolution position was substantially about his concerns about Social Darwinism rather than about a literal six-day creation, and Mencken's coverage was substantially condescending toward rural Tennessee in ways that subsequent historians have criticized — but the cultural-political confrontation was substantially real. The 1920s' urban-rural cultural divide became one of the structural features of subsequent twentieth-century American politics.

## The 1929 crash

The decade ended with the most consequential American financial event since the Panic of 1893. The stock-market boom of 1928–29 had been driven by a combination of factors: expansion of consumer purchasing power, rapid industrial growth in major sectors, the widespread use of margin trading (in which investors borrowed substantial fractions of their stock purchase prices and were vulnerable to forced sales if prices declined), and substantial international capital inflows. The market had risen from an index of about 100 in 1924 to about 380 in September 1929.

The crash began on October 24, 1929 ("Black Thursday"), continued through October 28 ("Black Monday") and October 29 ("Black Tuesday"). By November 13, 1929, the market had lost approximately 40 percent of its September peak value. Within three years, the market lost approximately 89 percent of the September 1929 peak. The crash itself was not the Great Depression — the depression that followed was a much longer and broader economic collapse — but the crash was the political-cultural turning point that ended the 1920s.

The Hoover administration that responded to the crash will be the subject of Chapter 25.
