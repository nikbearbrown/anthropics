# Chapter 32 — The Challenges of the Twenty-First Century

*Recent history, with the historiography still in formation.*

On the morning of September 11, 2001, four commercial airliners were hijacked from East Coast U.S. airports by nineteen members of the Islamist organization al-Qaeda. Two were flown into the twin towers of the World Trade Center in Manhattan; one was flown into the Pentagon in Arlington, Virginia; the fourth — United Airlines Flight 93 — was retaken by passengers who had learned about the other attacks by mobile phone and was crashed in a field near Shanksville, Pennsylvania, before reaching its target. The total death toll was approximately 2,977 people, of whom 2,753 died in New York. The attacks were the largest single mass-casualty event on American soil since the Civil War battle of Antietam in 1862, and the largest single mass-casualty event on American civilian soil in American history.

The political-cultural response to the 9/11 attacks substantially restructured American political life across the following two decades. The USA PATRIOT Act, passed by Congress in October 2001 with substantial bipartisan support, substantially expanded federal surveillance authority. The Authorization for Use of Military Force (AUMF) of September 18, 2001, authorized the president to use military force against "those nations, organizations, or persons" the president determined had been responsible for the attacks; the AUMF would, across the subsequent two decades, be used by three successive presidential administrations to justify military operations against organizations and in countries that had not existed in 2001. The Department of Homeland Security, established in 2002 from twenty-two existing federal agencies, became one of the substantial new federal institutions of the post-9/11 period. The Transportation Security Administration substantially transformed American air travel. The federal surveillance programs that were subsequently disclosed (the NSA's bulk-collection programs revealed by Edward Snowden in 2013; the broader post-9/11 surveillance framework) substantially restructured the relationship between the American state and the citizens it monitors.

This chapter takes up the post-2000 period — material whose historiographic consensus is still substantially in formation and where the model's training data may handle the framing inconsistently. The puzzle of this chapter is what to make of the recent past while the historical-interpretive frameworks for understanding it are still being constructed.

## The wars in Afghanistan and Iraq

The American military response to the 9/11 attacks substantially produced two major wars across the post-2001 period.

**The war in Afghanistan** began on October 7, 2001, with American and British air strikes against Taliban government forces and al-Qaeda training camps. The initial military operation, in cooperation with the Afghan Northern Alliance, substantially destroyed the Taliban government across October–December 2001 and disrupted the al-Qaeda organizational structure (though Osama bin Laden, the al-Qaeda leader who had organized the 9/11 attacks, escaped capture during the December 2001 Battle of Tora Bora). The subsequent American military commitment in Afghanistan continued for twenty years — through the Bush, Obama, Trump, and Biden administrations — and substantially failed to produce the political-stability outcomes that the operation had been justified by. The final American military withdrawal from Afghanistan in August 2021 produced the substantial collapse of the U.S.-supported Afghan government and the substantial Taliban return to power. American military deaths in Afghanistan across the twenty years totaled approximately 2,400; American military wounded totaled approximately 20,000; the financial cost of the war was approximately $2 trillion. Afghan deaths across the period — combatant and civilian, across all sides — are estimated at approximately 200,000.

**The war in Iraq** began on March 20, 2003, with American and British air strikes followed by a ground invasion. The stated political justification — that Iraq under Saddam Hussein possessed weapons of mass destruction (WMD) and was developing nuclear weapons — was substantially false on the empirical record. The pre-war intelligence that the Bush administration cited had been substantially distorted across 2002–03 in ways that the subsequent congressional investigations (the Senate Intelligence Committee's reports of 2004 and 2008) and the independent Iraq Survey Group's 2004 report substantially documented. The Iraq Survey Group's conclusion was that Iraq had effectively ended its WMD programs by the mid-1990s under the post-Gulf-War sanctions regime, that no significant active WMD program existed at the time of the 2003 invasion, and that the substantial pre-war intelligence claims to the contrary had been substantially wrong. The political-administrative process by which the pre-war intelligence was substantially distorted to support the policy decision the Bush administration had already made has been substantially documented in subsequent historical and journalistic accounts.

The military operation in Iraq substantially succeeded in its initial objective (the overthrow of the Saddam Hussein government within three weeks) and substantially failed in everything that followed. The Iraq Coalition Provisional Authority that operated the country across 2003–04 made substantial policy errors (the de-Baathification of the Iraqi civil service; the dissolution of the Iraqi army) that substantially contributed to the insurgency that emerged across 2003–06. The American military commitment expanded across 2003–07 (the "surge" of 2007 substantially increased American troop levels to approximately 170,000) and was substantially drawn down across 2007–11 under the Bush and Obama administrations. American military deaths in Iraq across 2003–11 totaled approximately 4,500; American military wounded totaled approximately 32,000; the financial cost was approximately $2 trillion. Iraqi civilian deaths from the war and its consequences — including the 2014–17 conflict with the Islamic State of Iraq and Syria (ISIS) that the Iraq War's political-military aftermath substantially produced — are estimated at between 200,000 and 1 million; the range reflects the substantial methodological disagreements about how to count war-related civilian mortality.

The substantive consequences of the post-9/11 wars are still being assessed. The wars substantially destabilized the Middle East in ways that produced the Arab Spring of 2011, the Syrian civil war of 2011–present, the substantial migration crisis affecting Europe across the mid-2010s, the rise of ISIS across 2014–17, and the substantial reorganization of regional alliances. The American political-cultural confidence in military intervention as a foreign-policy tool was substantially damaged by the wars' outcomes — what some commentators have called the "Iraq syndrome" parallel to the earlier Vietnam syndrome. The financial cost of the post-9/11 wars (the Brown University Costs of War Project's estimate of approximately $8 trillion in cumulative costs through 2022, when counting the substantial long-term care costs for wounded veterans and the substantial interest costs on war-related federal borrowing) substantially contributed to the federal-fiscal conditions of the post-2008 period.

## The 2008 financial crisis

The 2008 financial crisis was the largest single American economic dislocation since the Great Depression. The crisis substantially originated in the American mortgage market across the mid-2000s — specifically in the substantial expansion of subprime mortgage lending, in the substantial securitization of subprime mortgages through complex financial instruments (mortgage-backed securities, collateralized debt obligations), and in the substantial inadequacy of the federal financial regulatory framework to address the systemic risks the framework had been producing.

**The mortgage bubble.** American housing prices grew substantially across 2000–06 — the Case-Shiller national home-price index approximately doubled across the period. The substantial price increase was supported by substantial expansion of mortgage credit, particularly to subprime borrowers who had not qualified for conventional mortgages. The mortgage products that emerged in the period — adjustable-rate mortgages with substantial initial-payment teaser rates, interest-only mortgages, no-documentation mortgages — substantially expanded the population of borrowers who could nominally afford mortgages but who would be vulnerable to default when interest rates rose or housing prices fell.

**The securitization framework.** The mortgage origination industry substantially packaged subprime mortgages into mortgage-backed securities that were sold to financial institutions globally. The securities were substantially rated by the credit-rating agencies (Moody's, S&P, Fitch) using methodologies that substantially understated the underlying risk. The collateralized debt obligation framework — in which mortgage-backed securities were combined into larger packages and re-rated — substantially obscured the underlying risk further. The credit-default-swap framework — in which financial institutions sold insurance against the default of these securities — substantially produced systemic risk that no individual institution had the information to assess.

**The crisis.** The 2007 collapse of housing prices substantially destroyed the underlying mortgage assets. The 2008 financial crisis substantially escalated across the year: the March 2008 collapse of Bear Stearns; the September 2008 bankruptcy of Lehman Brothers (the largest bankruptcy in American history); the September 2008 federal seizure of Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac (the government-sponsored mortgage enterprises); the substantial federal bailouts of AIG, Citigroup, Bank of America, and the major American auto manufacturers; the Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP) of October 2008 that authorized $700 billion in federal financial-system support; the substantial monetary easing by the Federal Reserve under Chairman Ben Bernanke. The crisis substantially produced the worst American recession since the Great Depression (American unemployment peaked at approximately 10 percent in October 2009; American GDP contracted by approximately 4 percent across 2008–09).

**The political consequences.** The 2008 election produced the substantial Democratic victory of Barack Obama (the first Black American president), substantial Democratic congressional majorities, and the political conditions for the Obama administration's substantial legislative response. The Recovery Act of February 2009 ($831 billion in federal economic stimulus), the Affordable Care Act of March 2010 (the most substantial federal health-care legislation since Medicare and Medicaid), the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform Act of July 2010 (substantial restructuring of the federal financial regulatory framework), and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (created under Dodd-Frank in 2010) substantially extended the federal-government framework that the New Deal-Great Society legislation had built.

The 2008 crisis also produced substantial political-cultural reactions. The Tea Party movement of 2009–10 substantially mobilized conservative opposition to the Obama administration. The Occupy Wall Street movement of September–November 2011 substantially mobilized progressive opposition to the substantial financial-system bailouts and the absence of substantial criminal prosecution of the financial-industry leaders whose institutions had been substantially responsible for the crisis. The 2010 midterm election produced substantial Republican gains (the Republican Party gained 63 House seats — the largest single-election partisan shift since 1948 — and 6 Senate seats; the substantial Republican gains substantially ended the Obama administration's capacity for major legislative action across the remainder of its tenure).

## The 2016 election and after

The 2016 presidential election was substantially the political-cultural turning point of the contemporary American political period. Donald Trump — a New York real-estate developer and reality-television personality with no prior political experience — won the Republican nomination against a field of sixteen other candidates and won the general election against Democratic candidate Hillary Clinton, despite losing the popular vote by approximately 3 million votes (Clinton won 48.2 percent to Trump's 46.1 percent). The Electoral College outcome (Trump 304, Clinton 227) reflected substantial Trump wins in the industrial Midwest states (Michigan, Pennsylvania, Wisconsin) that had been substantially Democratic in the previous elections.

The 2016 election outcome substantially reflected several long-developing trends. The substantial deindustrialization of the American Midwest across the post-1970 period had produced substantial economic dislocation that the standard Democratic-Party framework had inadequately addressed. The substantial cultural-political conflicts that had developed across the post-1968 period — over immigration, over racial-cultural questions, over the substantial demographic transformation that the post-1965 immigration framework had produced — had substantially destabilized the post-1960s political coalitions. The substantial post-1970s growth of inequality (the top 1 percent's share of American national income approximately doubled across 1979–2020) had produced substantial political-cultural resentment that both the Republican and Democratic political establishments had been substantially unable to address. The substantial cultural-political effects of the post-1991 globalization framework — in which substantial American manufacturing employment had been substantially relocated to lower-cost foreign locations — had produced substantial political-cultural backlash among the populations most affected.

The Trump administration's policy framework — substantial restrictions on immigration, substantial increases in tariffs (particularly against Chinese goods), substantial regulatory rollbacks, substantial tax cuts (the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017), substantial conservative judicial appointments (three Supreme Court justices across the single term; substantial federal-court appointments at all levels), and the substantial reorganization of American foreign-policy commitments — substantially extended the post-2008 partisan-political conflicts.

**The COVID-19 pandemic** of 2020–22 substantially shaped the late Trump administration and the political-cultural conditions of the period. The pandemic killed approximately 1.2 million Americans across the period (the largest single American mass-mortality event since the 1918 influenza pandemic and the largest absolute mortality event in American history) and substantially restructured American economic, social, and political life. The federal-government response — the CARES Act of March 2020 ($2.2 trillion in federal economic relief); the subsequent Consolidated Appropriations Act of December 2020 ($900 billion); the American Rescue Plan of March 2021 ($1.9 trillion under the Biden administration) — substantially extended the federal-government framework that the 2008–10 response had assembled. The substantial federal fiscal stimulus across 2020–21 substantially produced the inflation surge of 2021–23 that substantially shaped subsequent American economic-political life.

**The January 6, 2021 attack on the U.S. Capitol** — when supporters of the outgoing Trump administration substantially attacked the Capitol building while Congress was certifying the Electoral College results of the 2020 election — was the substantial political-institutional crisis of the post-2020 period. The attack produced the second Trump impeachment (the House impeached Trump in January 2021; the Senate trial produced acquittal with 57 senators voting to convict, ten short of the required 67), substantial federal criminal prosecutions of approximately 1,400 individuals across 2021–25, and substantial political-historical questions about the durability of American constitutional government that the historiography is still substantially in the process of addressing.

**The 2024 election** produced the substantial return of Donald Trump to the presidency after his 2020 defeat — the first time since Grover Cleveland in 1892 that a defeated president had returned to office. The Trump administration's second term, beginning in January 2025, is operating at the time this chapter is being written.

## What the historiography has not yet settled

The historiography of the post-2000 period is substantially in formation. The major interpretive frameworks that subsequent generations of historians will use to understand the period are still being substantially developed. Several questions are worth naming as substantially unsettled.

**The post-9/11 wars.** The substantive question of whether the post-9/11 military interventions were strategic failures, partial successes, or something more complicated is still being debated. The military-historical accounts are substantially complete; the political-historical accounts are still being constructed; the broader interpretive framework that will eventually situate the post-9/11 wars within American history is still substantially in development.

**The 2008 crisis and its aftermath.** The substantive question of whether the 2008 financial crisis was a substantial inflection point in American political-economic development — comparable to the 1929 crash or the 1973 oil crisis — or whether it was a more limited financial event whose political-cultural consequences have been substantially overstated, is still being debated. The substantial post-2008 political polarization, the substantial growth of populist political movements on both the left (Bernie Sanders's two presidential campaigns) and the right (Trump's three campaigns), and the substantial restructuring of American party coalitions across 2008–24 may turn out to have been substantially produced by the 2008 crisis or may turn out to have been substantially independent of it.

**The Trump phenomenon.** The substantive question of what the Trump political phenomenon represents — a substantial reorganization of American party politics that will produce durable institutional changes; a substantial cultural-political reaction to specific post-1965 developments (immigration, demographic change, cultural transformation) that may substantially recede with generational replacement; a substantial threat to American constitutional government that may produce substantive constitutional crises across the coming decades — is among the most-contested questions in current American political analysis. The historiographic frameworks that will eventually be applied to the Trump phenomenon are still being constructed.

**The American political-institutional crisis.** The broader question of whether American constitutional government is operating sustainably in the early 2020s, or whether the substantial political polarization, the substantial loss of public trust in federal institutions, the substantial weakening of the post-Watergate institutional-reform framework, and the substantial cultural-political conflicts of the period represent the early stages of a substantial constitutional crisis, is still being debated in substantial part. The historiographic distance that would be required to assess these questions substantively does not yet exist.
