# Chapter 27 — Fighting the Good Fight in World War II, 1941–1945

*The war that ended the Depression and built the American century.*

On August 6, 1945, at 8:15 a.m. Hiroshima time, a U.S. Army Air Forces B-29 bomber called the *Enola Gay* dropped a uranium gun-type fission weapon — the bomb the Manhattan Project had named *Little Boy* — on the Japanese city of Hiroshima. The bomb detonated at approximately 1,900 feet above the city with a yield equivalent to about 15 kilotons of TNT. The initial blast and the firestorm that followed killed approximately 70,000 to 80,000 people instantly; deaths from burns, radiation sickness, and related causes over the following weeks and months raised the total death toll to somewhere between 90,000 and 166,000 by the end of 1945. Three days later, on August 9, a plutonium implosion weapon called *Fat Man* was dropped on Nagasaki, killing an additional 60,000 to 80,000 people by the end of 1945. The Japanese government accepted unconditional surrender on August 15. The surrender was formally signed aboard the U.S.S. *Missouri* in Tokyo Bay on September 2, 1945.

The decision to drop the atomic bombs is one of the most-contested questions in American historiography. The standard American framing, articulated by President Truman and his administration in 1945 and substantially repeated in subsequent American historical accounts, was that the bombs were necessary to end the war without an American invasion of the Japanese home islands that would have cost hundreds of thousands of American casualties and millions of Japanese casualties. Subsequent historiography has substantially complicated this framing. The historian Gar Alperovitz's *Atomic Diplomacy* (1965, revised 1995) argued that the bombs were dropped primarily to demonstrate American military power to the Soviet Union as the Cold War was beginning. The historian J. Samuel Walker's *Prompt and Utter Destruction* (1997, revised 2016) — the most-cited synthesis in the current scholarly debate — argues that the decision was made for multiple reasons: the desire to end the war quickly, the wish to avoid additional American casualties, the (overestimated, in retrospect) expectation of Japanese military resistance, the diplomatic considerations regarding the Soviet Union, and the institutional momentum of the Manhattan Project itself. The historian Tsuyoshi Hasegawa's *Racing the Enemy* (2005) argued that the Soviet declaration of war against Japan on August 8, 1945 (between the Hiroshima and Nagasaki bombings) was at least as significant as the bombs in producing the Japanese surrender.

The historiographic debate is unsettled in important ways. The American military and intelligence assessments of the period — including the U.S. Strategic Bombing Survey's 1946 report — concluded that Japan would likely have surrendered before November 1, 1945 (the planned invasion date) even without the atomic bombings, primarily because of the conventional bombing campaign that had already destroyed most major Japanese cities, the naval blockade that had cut off Japanese imports, and the Soviet entry into the Pacific war. The internal Japanese political documentation, which has become available since the 1990s, supports the argument that the Soviet declaration of war was a major factor in the Japanese decision to surrender. The standard American framing of the bombs as militarily necessary is, on the current scholarly evidence, substantially overstated.

This chapter is about the war the bombs ended. The puzzle of this chapter is what the Second World War was, what it did to the United States, and how it accomplished the major economic-political transformation that the New Deal had attempted but not fully accomplished. The war ended the Great Depression more decisively than New Deal policy had. It built the federal-government institutional infrastructure that would dominate the post-war American century. And it produced the atomic-weapons program whose moral and political consequences continue to shape the twenty-first-century world.

## The path to American entry

The Second World War began for the Allied powers in September 1939 with the German invasion of Poland. The United States remained formally neutral for two years and three months. The path from American neutrality in September 1939 to American belligerence in December 1941 ran through specific decisions and events.

The Roosevelt administration's foreign-policy commitments were substantially with the Allied side from the early stages of the war. The *Neutrality Acts* of the 1930s — passed by Congress in 1935, 1937, and 1939 to constrain American involvement in foreign conflicts — limited the administration's ability to provide direct military aid. Roosevelt worked around the Neutrality Acts through several mechanisms. The "cash and carry" provision of the 1939 Neutrality Act allowed belligerents to purchase American war materials if they paid in cash and transported them in their own ships; the provision substantially favored Britain (which had access to the Atlantic shipping lanes) over Germany (which did not). The destroyers-for-bases agreement of September 1940 traded fifty obsolete American destroyers to Britain for ninety-nine-year leases on British naval bases in the Caribbean. The *Lend-Lease Act* of March 1941 authorized the president to provide military equipment to any nation whose defense was considered vital to American security; the Act effectively committed the United States to substantial material support for Britain (and, after June 1941, the Soviet Union) without requiring direct American belligerence.

The Japanese-American confrontation in the Pacific developed in parallel. The Japanese Empire had been at war in China since 1937 and had been substantially expanding its East Asian and Pacific position across the 1930s. The Roosevelt administration responded with progressively more restrictive economic sanctions across 1940–41: an embargo on aviation fuel, then on scrap iron, then (in the most consequential step, in July–August 1941) a freeze on Japanese assets that effectively ended American oil exports to Japan. Japan imported approximately 80 percent of its oil from the United States; the embargo left Japan with about eighteen months of oil reserves before its military operations would become impossible. The Japanese government's strategic options narrowed sharply.

The Japanese decision to attack the U.S. Pacific Fleet at Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941, was a calculated military gamble — that a decisive blow against American naval capacity in the Pacific would force a negotiated settlement before American industrial capacity could be mobilized. The gamble failed in several specific ways. The Pacific Fleet's three aircraft carriers were not at Pearl Harbor on December 7 and survived the attack. The political effect of the attack in the United States was the immediate consolidation of public support for war that the prior debate had not produced. Roosevelt's address to Congress on December 8 produced a near-unanimous war declaration. Germany declared war on the United States on December 11, 1941, fulfilling its Tripartite Pact obligations to Japan and substantially simplifying the Allied strategic question of whether the United States would prioritize the European or Pacific theaters. The "Europe First" strategy that Roosevelt and Churchill had agreed to in their pre-Pearl Harbor consultations could now be implemented within a unified two-theater war.

## The wartime federal-government transformation

The wartime mobilization produced the most rapid federal-government expansion in American history. Federal spending grew from about $9 billion in fiscal year 1940 to about $98 billion in fiscal year 1945, an eleven-fold increase. The federal government employed approximately 1 million civilian workers in 1940; the figure peaked at approximately 3.4 million in 1945. The U.S. armed forces grew from approximately 458,000 active-duty personnel in 1940 to approximately 12.2 million in 1945. The wartime federal-government activity at this scale required institutional infrastructure that had not existed in 1940.

The major wartime federal agencies included the **War Production Board** (which coordinated industrial production and the allocation of strategic materials), the **Office of Price Administration** (which administered wartime price controls and consumer rationing), the **Office of War Information** (which managed wartime propaganda and information control), the **War Manpower Commission** (which coordinated wartime labor allocation), the **Office of Strategic Services** (the wartime intelligence agency, the precursor to the CIA), and dozens of other agencies and programs. The wartime federal government substantially planned the American economy at a scale and detail that the New Deal had not approached.

The wartime federal spending substantially solved the Depression. American unemployment, which had been approximately 14 percent in 1940, fell to approximately 1.2 percent by 1944. American real GDP grew by approximately 75 percent across the war years. The federal deficit grew enormously (the public debt rose from about 50 percent of GDP in 1940 to about 110 percent of GDP in 1945) but the economic activity the spending produced substantially exceeded the costs. The wartime experience would substantially inform the postwar Keynesian economic-policy framework — the idea that federal fiscal policy could be used to manage aggregate demand and avoid depressions — that would dominate American economic policy until the 1970s.

The wartime production output was extraordinary. American factories produced approximately 300,000 military aircraft, 89,000 tanks, 8,800 naval ships, and 16 million firearms across the war years. The Henry Ford-managed Willow Run plant in Michigan, which produced B-24 bombers, achieved a production rate of one complete bomber every 63 minutes at peak output. The Kaiser Shipyards in California built cargo ships ("Liberty ships") at production rates that compressed normal construction times by an order of magnitude. The American industrial mobilization was substantially larger than that of any other Allied or Axis power; American war production by 1944 substantially exceeded the combined production of Germany, Japan, and Italy.

## The wartime home front

The home-front experience of the war years substantially reshaped American social and economic life.

**Women in the wartime labor force.** Approximately 6 million American women entered the labor force across the war years; women's labor-force participation grew from approximately 27 percent in 1940 to approximately 36 percent in 1945. Women worked in industrial occupations (the "Rosie the Riveter" image) that had previously been substantially closed to them; in clerical and administrative positions; in agricultural and service work that had been short-staffed by male enlistment. The post-war reversion to peacetime employment patterns substantially returned women to pre-war occupational distributions, but the wartime experience would substantially shape the women's-employment expansion that would resume in the 1960s.

**African Americans in the wartime labor force and military.** The Second Great Migration accelerated during the war years; approximately 1.5 million Black Americans migrated from the rural South to the urban North across 1940–45, primarily to take wartime industrial jobs in California, the Midwest, and the Northeast. The federal **Fair Employment Practices Commission** (established by executive order in 1941 in response to the threatened March on Washington movement organized by A. Philip Randolph) prohibited racial discrimination in defense industries. Approximately 1.2 million Black Americans served in the wartime military, in segregated units. The Tuskegee Airmen — the segregated 332nd Fighter Group — established a distinguished combat record. The "Double V" campaign (victory abroad against fascism, victory at home against racism), articulated by the *Pittsburgh Courier* in 1942, substantially shaped Black political consciousness across the war years and laid groundwork for the postwar Civil Rights movement.

**Japanese-American internment.** Executive Order 9066, signed by Roosevelt on February 19, 1942, authorized the forced relocation and internment of approximately 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry from the West Coast — two-thirds of whom were American citizens — to ten internment camps in the interior West. The internment was upheld by the Supreme Court in *Korematsu v. United States* (1944), in a decision that has been substantially repudiated by subsequent jurisprudence (the Court explicitly disavowed *Korematsu* in *Trump v. Hawaii*, 2018, though without formally overruling it). The internment was a clear racial-political action — Japanese-Americans were interned while German-Americans and Italian-Americans (the populations of the other major Axis powers) were not subject to mass internment despite specific individual security concerns about some of them. The federal Civil Liberties Act of 1988 formally apologized for the internment and provided $20,000 per surviving internee in reparations.

**Mexican-American wartime experience.** The wartime labor shortage produced the *Bracero Program* — a federal-Mexican-government agreement, beginning in 1942 and continuing through 1964, that brought approximately 4.6 million Mexican agricultural workers to the United States across its operation. The program was simultaneously a major labor-recruitment program and a substantial source of labor exploitation; Bracero workers were subject to wage and working-condition abuses that the U.S. and Mexican governments only partially addressed. The Zoot Suit Riots in Los Angeles in June 1943 — in which white American sailors and civilians attacked Mexican-American youth in their distinctive "zoot suit" clothing across several days of violence — was the most-publicized wartime racial-violence incident affecting Mexican-Americans on the home front.

**Indigenous wartime experience.** Approximately 25,000 Native Americans served in the U.S. military across the war years, the highest per-capita military service of any American ethnic group. The Navajo Code Talkers — Marine Corps signalmen who used the Navajo language as an unbroken battlefield code in the Pacific theater — are the most-remembered specific contribution. Indigenous wartime experience accelerated the urbanization of Indigenous populations (substantial post-war migration to West Coast cities for industrial employment) and would substantially shape the post-war Indigenous-political movements that produced the Indian Reorganization Act revisions of the 1950s and the Indigenous activism of the 1960s.

## The European Theater

The European theater of the war ran from the German invasion of Poland in September 1939 to the German unconditional surrender in May 1945. American military involvement began with substantial naval operations in the North Atlantic in 1941, expanded with North African landings (Operation Torch) in November 1942, included the Italian invasion (Sicily in July 1943, the Italian mainland from September 1943), and culminated in the cross-Channel invasion of France (Operation Overlord, beginning on D-Day, June 6, 1944) and the subsequent Western Front campaigns of 1944–45.

The strategic context that shaped American operations included the dominant Soviet contribution to the war against Germany. The Eastern Front, where the German military's main combat strength was committed, accounted for approximately 75 percent of total German military casualties across the war. The Soviet Union suffered approximately 27 million dead (about half of them civilian; the deaths included substantial Soviet civilian deaths from German genocidal policies against Slavs and Jews in the occupied territories), an order of magnitude more than American war deaths. The Western Allied military contribution to the defeat of Nazi Germany was substantial but was structurally secondary to the Soviet contribution; the historiographic record on this is clear, though the American popular memory of the war has often substantially emphasized the Western Allied role.

The Holocaust — the German systematic murder of approximately 6 million European Jews, plus approximately 5 million additional victims (Roma and Sinti, Soviet prisoners of war, Polish civilians, disabled persons, political prisoners, gay men) — was carried out across the German-occupied territories of Eastern and Central Europe from 1941 through 1945. The Roosevelt administration's response to the Holocaust as it was happening has been substantially criticized in subsequent historiography. The administration knew of the killing operations from substantial early 1942 onward; the *Bermuda Conference* of April 1943 substantially failed to produce Allied refugee-assistance programs; the *War Refugee Board* established by Roosevelt in January 1944 came late in the killing operations and substantially saved lives only in Hungary, where it was able to operate effectively. The American immigration restrictions of the 1924 National Origins Act remained substantially in force across the war years; the United States substantially failed to absorb European Jewish refugees who could have been saved by more accommodating immigration policy. The historian David Wyman's *The Abandonment of the Jews* (1984) is the most-cited synthesis on the American response to the Holocaust.

## The Pacific Theater and the bomb

The Pacific theater ran from December 1941 to August 1945. American military operations included the carrier-based naval campaigns of 1942 (Coral Sea, Midway), the island-hopping ground campaigns of 1943–45 (Guadalcanal, Tarawa, Saipan, Iwo Jima, Okinawa), the long-range strategic bombing campaign against Japanese cities (beginning with the firebombing campaign of February 1945 that killed perhaps 100,000 people in Tokyo alone), and the atomic bombings of August 1945.

The American conventional bombing campaign against Japan was, in human-mortality terms, comparable to the atomic bombings. The March 9–10, 1945, firebombing of Tokyo — the most-destructive single conventional bombing operation of the war, by some measures the most-destructive single military operation in history before the atomic bombs — killed approximately 100,000 people in a single night. Subsequent firebombing operations against Japanese cities through summer 1945 killed approximately 350,000 to 500,000 Japanese civilians by the time of the atomic bombings. The atomic bombs were, in this strategic context, an extension of an existing campaign against Japanese cities rather than a categorical departure from it.

The decision to use the atomic weapons was made by President Truman in consultation with his close advisors across summer 1945. The decision was not extensively debated in any formal advisory process; the assumption, substantially built into the Manhattan Project's institutional development from 1942 onward, was that the weapons would be used if completed before the war ended. The specific decision to drop the bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki rather than to demonstrate the weapons against an uninhabited target — which had been advocated by some scientists involved in the Manhattan Project (the Franck Report of June 1945 articulated this position) — was made by the political leadership without substantial public deliberation. Truman's published reflections on the decision in subsequent decades emphasized the casualty-avoidance framing; the subsequent historiography (Alperovitz, Bernstein, Sherwin, Walker, Hasegawa, and others) has substantially complicated this framing.

The atomic bombings, in any framing of the decision, ended the war. The Japanese government accepted the Potsdam Declaration's surrender terms on August 14, 1945; the formal surrender was signed on September 2, 1945. The war's death toll across all theaters and all parties was approximately 70 to 85 million people, approximately 3 percent of the global population at the time. American military deaths were approximately 405,000; American civilian deaths were minimal (a small number of merchant marine deaths and a handful of attacks on the American mainland that produced trivial casualties). The American war experience was substantially less destructive of American lives and property than the war experience of any other major belligerent — a fact that would shape the post-war American economic and political position substantially.

## The post-war American position

The United States ended the war in a position of unprecedented economic and political dominance. The American economy in 1945 produced approximately 50 percent of world manufacturing output. The American military was, in conventional terms, the most powerful in the world, with global reach through the Pacific and European bases and a substantial naval and air force. The American monopoly on atomic weapons (the Soviet Union would conduct its first atomic test in August 1949) gave the United States a strategic position that no previous global power had occupied.

The institutional infrastructure of the post-war international system was substantially American-designed. The United Nations, founded in San Francisco in June 1945, replaced the League of Nations that the United States had rejected in 1919. The International Monetary Fund and the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (which became the World Bank) — established at the Bretton Woods Conference in July 1944 — established the post-war international financial system. The Marshall Plan (the European Recovery Program of 1948–52, providing approximately $13 billion in American aid to Western European countries) substantially shaped post-war European economic recovery. The North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO, established 1949) committed the United States to permanent military alliance with Western European countries — a commitment that reversed the long-standing American foreign-policy commitment to avoiding "entangling alliances" that had been articulated by Washington's Farewell Address in 1796.

The post-war international system that the United States built has substantially shaped the seventy-five years since. The institutional infrastructure has been substantially modified across the period — the abandonment of the gold-dollar standard in 1971; the expansion of European integration; the post-1991 reconfiguration of the international system after the Soviet collapse — but the basic framework of American-led international institutions has remained in operation. The "American century" that the journalist Henry Luce had named in 1941 was substantially built across the war years and consolidated in the immediate post-war period.
