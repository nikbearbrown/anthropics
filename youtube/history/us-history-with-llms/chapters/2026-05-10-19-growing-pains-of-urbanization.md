# Chapter 19 — The Growing Pains of Urbanization, 1870–1900

*The American city as it took its modern shape.*

In December 1890, a Danish-American journalist and photographer named Jacob Riis published *How the Other Half Lives: Studies Among the Tenements of New York*. The book combined photography (which Riis had taught himself, using the new flash-powder technology that allowed indoor and night photography for the first time) with reporting from his work as a police-beat journalist for the *New York Tribune*. Riis had spent years walking the tenement districts of Manhattan's Lower East Side; *How the Other Half Lives* presented what he had seen to an American middle-class readership that had largely been unaware of how the urban poor lived. The book was widely read, repeatedly reprinted, and substantially shaped American urban-reform sensibility for the following generation.

The conditions Riis documented were not exaggerated. Tenement buildings on Manhattan's Lower East Side typically housed thirty or more families on lots that had previously held a single-family dwelling. Interior rooms had no windows. Toilets were typically shared by entire buildings, often located in unventilated airshafts. Tuberculosis rates in the worst tenement blocks ran several times the citywide average; infant mortality in the tenement districts approached 25 percent in the first year of life. Riis's photographs — the gas-jet-lit faces of immigrant families in their crowded rooms; the bands of street children sleeping in alleys; the sweatshop floors where garment workers labored sixteen hours a day — gave urban poverty a visual reality that the previous quarter-century's industrial growth had largely concealed.

The puzzle of this chapter is what the late-nineteenth-century American city was. By 1900 it was a new kind of social space — denser, more ethnically and racially diverse, more economically stratified, more politically organized, and more institutionally complex than any previous American city had been. The institutions that managed it (or failed to manage it) — political machines, settlement houses, public-health departments, mass-transit systems, public parks, mass-circulation newspapers, department stores, vaudeville theaters — were substantially built across this period. By 1900 the basic infrastructure of twentieth-century American urban life was in place.

## The demographic transformation

The numbers describe a population shift without precedent in American history. In 1860, approximately 20 percent of Americans lived in urban areas (defined by the Census as places of 2,500 or more residents). By 1900 it was 40 percent. By 1920 it would be over half, the first time American urban dwellers outnumbered rural ones. The five-decade transformation moved the American population from being substantially rural-agricultural to being substantially urban-industrial.

The major cities grew dramatically. New York City's population (in its current Five-Borough configuration after the 1898 consolidation) was 3.4 million in 1900 — making it the second-largest city in the world after London. Chicago, which had been a frontier town of 30,000 in 1850, was 1.7 million in 1900. Philadelphia, Boston, Baltimore, St. Louis, Pittsburgh, and Cleveland all crossed substantial population thresholds during the period. Cities of 100,000 or more, of which there had been nine in 1860, numbered thirty-eight in 1900.

The population that filled the growing cities came from three principal sources.

**Internal migration from the American countryside.** The agricultural revolution that mechanized American farming (the McCormick reaper, John Deere's steel plow, the spread of railroad freight networks) had reduced the labor required for agriculture per unit of output. The surplus rural labor moved to cities for industrial work. The pattern was particularly strong from the post-Civil-War South, where economic depression compounded the dislocation of emancipation, and from the Plains states in the late 1880s and 1890s, where drought, debt, and railroad freight rates ruined many small farmers.

**The Great Migration of African Americans** from the rural South to the urban North began across this period and accelerated dramatically in the early twentieth century. Approximately 200,000 Black Americans migrated north between 1870 and 1900; another 1.6 million migrated between 1910 and 1930 (the conventional "Great Migration" period); and another 5 million between 1940 and 1970 (the second Great Migration). The northbound destinations clustered in eight major cities — New York, Chicago, Philadelphia, St. Louis, Detroit, Pittsburgh, Cleveland, and Indianapolis — that received about two-thirds of the total flow. The push factors were post-Reconstruction Southern violence (the Klan, lynching campaigns, racial massacres), the legal disfranchisement of Black voters across the post-Reconstruction Southern states, and the sharecropping system that kept Black agricultural workers in permanent debt; the pull factors were industrial wage labor in Northern cities, less severe (though far from absent) racial restriction, and the existence of Black communities in Northern cities that earlier migrants had built.

**The "new" European immigration.** Between 1880 and 1924, approximately 22 million Europeans immigrated to the United States. The geographic pattern was distinctive. The earlier nineteenth-century immigration (about 8 million people between 1830 and 1880) had come predominantly from Northern and Western Europe — Ireland, Germany, Britain, Scandinavia. The post-1880 immigration came predominantly from Southern and Eastern Europe — Italy, Russia, Poland, the Austro-Hungarian Empire, the Balkans, Greece. The new immigrants were predominantly Catholic and Jewish (the older immigration had been substantially Protestant); spoke languages most Americans did not understand; arrived predominantly through Ellis Island in New York Harbor (opened 1892 to handle the immigrant flow); and settled predominantly in the urban-industrial Northeast and Midwest, where the work was. The distinction between "old" and "new" immigration was, at the time, a way of describing what was actually happening; it was also, in many later accounts, a racial-cultural categorization that was used to justify the National Origins Act of 1924 that drastically restricted Southern and Eastern European immigration. The framing was largely abandoned by professional historians in the second half of the twentieth century, but it persists in some popular accounts.

The combination of these three migrations produced the late-nineteenth-century American city: rapidly growing, ethnically heterogeneous, racially stratified (though the racial mix varied substantially by city; New York and Chicago had small Black populations in 1900 that would grow substantially in the early twentieth century, while Philadelphia and Pittsburgh had larger Black populations from the start), economically polarized.

## What the city was physically

The physical city that emerged across the period was substantially built between 1870 and 1900, and it would remain the physical infrastructure of American urban life for the following century.

**Mass transit** transformed urban geography. Horse-drawn omnibuses and streetcars had served mid-nineteenth-century cities, but the technology was limited in speed and capacity. The cable car (San Francisco, 1873) and the electric streetcar (Frank Sprague's Richmond, Virginia, system, 1888) extended urban transit substantially. By 1900, most major American cities had electric streetcar networks; the streetcar systems made it possible for working-class Americans to live further from the urban core and commute to work, accelerating the development of streetcar suburbs that the early twentieth century would fill in. The first American subway systems were built in Boston (1897) and New York (1904); the Chicago elevated lines (the "El") were built across the 1890s and 1900s. The subway and El systems made the high-density urban form of twentieth-century New York and Chicago possible.

**The skyscraper** emerged as the characteristic American urban building type during the 1880s and 1890s. The Home Insurance Building in Chicago (1885, ten stories) was the first to use a structural-steel skeleton instead of load-bearing masonry walls. By 1900, fifteen-story buildings were common in Chicago, New York, and Philadelphia; the twenty-story buildings that would define the early twentieth-century skyline were being built. The skyscraper was made possible by the convergence of three technologies: structural steel (Chapter 18), the Otis safety elevator (the inventor Elisha Otis demonstrated his safety brake in 1854; safe and reliable passenger elevators became standard in tall buildings in the 1880s), and the electric power supply that lit the upper floors that gas could not reach safely.

**Public infrastructure** — water systems, sewage systems, public-health institutions — was built across the period. American urban water and sewage systems had been minimal at mid-century; by 1900 most major cities had municipal water and sewage systems serving most of the population. The public-health infrastructure that emerged — municipal health departments, sanitary inspection, infectious-disease tracking — substantially reduced urban mortality rates across the late nineteenth century, though the reduction was uneven across class and neighborhood lines. The City Beautiful movement that emerged after the 1893 Chicago World's Columbian Exposition reorganized urban planning around comprehensive public design — boulevards, public parks, civic centers — that would shape twentieth-century American urban planning.

**The tenement** was the housing form that received most of the new immigrant population. The standard New York tenement after the 1879 Tenement House Act was the so-called "dumbbell tenement" — five or six stories tall, with apartments arranged around a narrow central airshaft. The buildings were nominally an improvement on the pre-1879 tenement, which had no airshaft at all; in practice, the airshafts were too narrow to provide adequate light or ventilation, and the buildings remained extremely crowded. The 1901 Tenement House Act, passed in response to *How the Other Half Lives* and other reform pressure, finally required substantial improvements in tenement construction. Older tenements remained, however, as housing for the urban poor until well into the twentieth century.

## Political machines and what they did

The political machines that governed most major American cities in the late nineteenth century are usually described in standard accounts in terms of their corruption. The corruption was real and substantial. The machines also performed functions that no other urban institution performed, and that is part of why they survived.

**Tammany Hall** in New York was the most famous of the political machines. Run by the Democratic Party organization of Manhattan, Tammany dominated New York City politics from the 1860s through the early twentieth century. William M. "Boss" Tweed, who ran Tammany in the late 1860s and early 1870s, was responsible for what is conventionally estimated as $30–200 million in graft from city construction contracts and patronage operations — the Tweed Ring scandal that produced one of the major political scandals of the era. Tweed was eventually convicted and died in prison in 1878. The machine survived him; later Tammany leaders — Richard Croker, Charles Murphy — ran the organization with somewhat less spectacular graft but comparable political dominance.

The machines worked through a system of personal political relationships. Local ward leaders, embedded in their immigrant neighborhoods, provided services that the formal city government did not provide: jobs (machine-controlled patronage positions in the police, sanitation, public works), housing assistance (the ward boss who knew which landlord owed a favor), legal help (the lawyer who could intervene with a judge or a policeman), funeral expenses for the indigent. In exchange, the recipients of these services voted as the ward leader directed and contributed labor and small amounts of money to the machine's operations. The system was personal, transactional, and effective. The machines built durable electoral coalitions in immigrant urban neighborhoods that the Anglo-Protestant reform movements of the period (the Mugwumps in the 1880s, the Progressive reformers later) could not easily displace.

The standard reformist critique of the machines was twofold: they were corrupt (graft from contracts, kickbacks from patronage employees, bribes for political protection), and they substituted personal political relationships for impersonal bureaucratic governance. Both critiques were accurate. What the critiques tended to miss was that the personal political relationships were the mechanism by which the machines provided services. The reformist alternative — impersonal, professionalized, civil-service-staffed urban government — was structurally less responsive to the daily emergencies of working-class urban life. The eventual Progressive-era municipal reforms succeeded partly because the political-economic environment changed (industrialization produced a larger middle class with reformist political interests; the federal government took on functions the machines had performed; public-welfare programs replaced the machine's discretionary services) more than because the reformers had defeated the machines politically.

The political machines were not specifically a phenomenon of the immigrant urban Northeast. Cincinnati's George B. Cox ran a substantially similar Republican machine. Chicago had successive machines under different parties. San Francisco's Abe Ruef ran a Republican machine that was exposed by the post-1906 earthquake reform movement. The machine form was a response to the structural problems of late-nineteenth-century urban governance, and it emerged in cities across regional and partisan lines.

## The settlement house movement

Another response to urban poverty emerged in the settlement house movement, which began with Jane Addams's Hull House in Chicago in 1889.

Addams had visited Toynbee Hall in London in 1888 — the first settlement house, founded by Anglican reformers in the Whitechapel district to provide educational and social services to the East End poor. She returned to Chicago and founded Hull House on Halsted Street in 1889 with Ellen Gates Starr. The premise was that middle-class reformers would *settle* in a working-class neighborhood — living in the settlement house, sharing daily life with their neighbors — and provide social services, education, and political organizing that the neighborhood would help to direct.

Hull House grew over the following decades into one of the most influential urban-reform institutions in American history. It offered classes (English language, civics, vocational skills) for immigrant adults, kindergarten and childcare for working mothers, public lectures, art exhibitions, music programs, a meeting space for labor unions. It produced empirical studies (the Hull House Maps and Papers of 1895 was an early sociological survey of immigrant neighborhoods) that contributed to the new academic discipline of sociology. It produced political reformers — Addams herself, Florence Kelley (who went on to lead the National Consumers League), Julia Lathrop (who became the first head of the federal Children's Bureau), Grace Abbott — who were central to the Progressive-era social-reform movements of the early twentieth century. Hull House was the model for about 400 other settlement houses that opened in major American cities across the following two decades.

The settlement house movement was substantially female-led, in ways that mattered for women's political development. Most settlement-house residents were educated unmarried women who could not enter professions (most were excluded from law, medicine, engineering, the clergy) but could organize social-reform institutions. The settlement-house movement gave a generation of college-educated American women a vocational alternative to teaching or domestic life; many of them moved from settlement-house work into Progressive-era reform careers in government, journalism, and academia. Jane Addams herself became, by the early twentieth century, one of the most prominent public figures in American life. She received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1931 for her later anti-war organizing.

The settlement-house movement's relationship to the political machines was complicated. Settlement houses provided some of the same services (English classes, vocational training, emergency assistance), but on different terms — bureaucratically organized, with reformist political agendas, often opposed to the machines that governed the neighborhoods. The two systems coexisted uneasily across the period. The settlement houses were better at producing political reformers; the machines were better at delivering electoral majorities. Both, in their different ways, were attempts to manage the structural problems that late-nineteenth-century industrial urbanization had produced.
