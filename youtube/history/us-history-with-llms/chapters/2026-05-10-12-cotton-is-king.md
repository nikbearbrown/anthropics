# Chapter 12 — Cotton Is King: The Antebellum South, 1800–1860

*An economy built around the financialization of human beings.*

In March 1858, James Henry Hammond of South Carolina rose on the floor of the United States Senate to defend the institution of slavery against Northern political pressure. Hammond was a former governor of South Carolina, a planter who held about three hundred enslaved people on a Savannah River plantation, and one of the most articulate proslavery thinkers of the antebellum decades. His speech ran for several hours. Near the end, he made the claim that the speech is remembered for.

"You dare not make war on cotton," Hammond said. "No power on earth dares to make war upon it. Cotton is king."

The phrase circulated in the Northern and Southern press over the following years and became the slogan it is remembered as. The argument behind it was about geopolitical leverage. Cotton was the most-traded commodity in the global economy of the 1850s. The American South produced about two-thirds of the world's cotton supply. The Lancashire textile industry of Britain — by far the largest manufacturing complex in the world at that point — depended on American cotton for the raw material that fed it. If the United States attempted to suppress Southern slavery, Hammond was arguing, the disruption would be catastrophic for the global economy, the British government would be forced to intervene on the South's side, and the rest of the world would understand that the Southern slave economy was an indispensable part of the modern world system. The South had geopolitical leverage that Northern abolitionists, in Hammond's view, had failed to understand.

The claim was substantially correct as a description of the global cotton economy and substantially wrong as a prediction of how a sectional crisis would play out. The Civil War, when it came, did disrupt the global cotton supply — Lancashire experienced what is called the Cotton Famine of 1861–65, with about a quarter of its textile workforce unemployed at the worst point — but the British government did not intervene. The Confederate strategy of holding cotton off the market to force British recognition (the so-called "cotton diplomacy" of 1861–62) failed because the British found alternative cotton sources (Egyptian, Indian) and because the British public's opposition to slavery made government intervention on the Confederate side politically untenable. King Cotton turned out to have less geopolitical leverage than Hammond believed.

But the underlying economic claim was accurate. The antebellum South was, by 1860, one of the most economically productive agricultural economies in the world, organized around an institution — racial chattel slavery — that had been built and then continuously refined for two and a half centuries. The economic value of enslaved human beings in the United States in 1860 has been estimated by historians at approximately $3.5 billion, equivalent to about $100 billion in modern terms by direct conversion or substantially more by various GDP-share or wealth-share comparisons. The enslaved population of 1860 represented roughly 20 percent of all American wealth — more than all railroads, all manufacturing, and all banking combined.

This chapter is about the economic structure of the antebellum South, the institution of slavery that organized it, and the political consequences of operating an economy of that scale and structure inside a federal system shared with a different and increasingly hostile section.

## What cotton actually was, economically

The cotton economy of the antebellum South was a recent phenomenon. In 1790, the United States produced approximately 1.5 million pounds of cotton — a marginal commodity, grown primarily in the lowcountry of South Carolina and Georgia, contributing little to American export earnings. By 1820, after Eli Whitney's cotton gin (1793) and the spread of cotton planting into the new states of Mississippi, Alabama, and Louisiana, American cotton production had grown to about 160 million pounds. By 1850 it was 980 million pounds. By 1860 it was 2.3 billion pounds. The growth in seventy years was over 1,500-fold.

The cotton gin is conventionally credited with this transformation, and it is worth pausing on why the credit is partly misplaced. Whitney's 1793 mechanical device separated cotton fiber from seed in the species of cotton (short-staple, upland cotton) that could be grown across most of the Southern interior. Before the gin, the labor required to separate seed from fiber by hand had made short-staple cotton uneconomical except as a household craft. The gin made commercial production of upland cotton possible. The gin did not, however, mechanize the cultivation or harvesting of cotton, which remained labor-intensive in ways that the technology of the era could not alter. Cotton fields had to be cleared, plowed, planted, weeded, and harvested by hand. The bolls ripened over several weeks; harvesting required repeated passes through the same field. The labor demand was enormous and continuous. The cotton gin made cotton profitable to grow. Enslaved labor made it profitable to grow at the scale at which the South grew it.

The expansion of cotton production was accompanied by the expansion of slavery. The enslaved population of the United States grew from approximately 700,000 in 1790 to approximately 3.95 million in 1860. The growth was substantially through natural reproduction — the international slave trade had been closed by federal law in 1808, and the smuggling that continued after that date accounted for perhaps 50,000 to 100,000 additional people across the entire half-century. The natural-reproduction growth of the U.S. enslaved population was distinctive. In the Caribbean and Brazil, where the working conditions on sugar plantations were so brutal that enslaved populations did not reproduce themselves naturally, the slave systems depended on continuous African imports. The U.S. South, by contrast, had a self-reproducing enslaved population — and an *internal* slave trade that moved enslaved people from the older plantation regions of Virginia, Maryland, and the Carolinas to the newer cotton frontier of Alabama, Mississippi, Louisiana, Arkansas, and Texas. Approximately 1 million enslaved people were moved through this internal trade across the antebellum decades, in what the historian Walter Johnson has called "soul by soul" — the systematic separation of families and communities through the commercial sale of human beings.

The economic statistics on the antebellum South are worth setting out plainly. By 1860:

- The South produced approximately 60 percent of the world's cotton supply.
- Cotton was the largest American export by value, accounting for about 50 percent of all American export earnings.
- The enslaved population numbered 3.95 million people, approximately 12.6 percent of the total American population of 31.4 million.
- The total value of enslaved human beings, treated as property in the slave-state legal codes, has been estimated at $3.5 billion — more than the combined value of American manufacturing, railroads, and banks.
- The wealthiest concentration of millionaires in the United States, per capita, was in the lower Mississippi River valley.
- Two-thirds of Southern white households owned no enslaved people; about 3 percent of white Southerners owned more than 50.

The last two figures together describe a substantial political problem the planter class had to solve: the institution of slavery generated enormous wealth, but the wealth was concentrated in a small fraction of the white Southern population. Most white Southerners were not slaveholders. The political defense of slavery required mobilizing those non-slaveholders to support an institution that did not directly benefit them.

The mobilization happened through racial ideology. The argument was that slavery's existence preserved white Southerners' racial status — that the alternative to slavery was a free Black population that would compete with white labor, intermarry with white communities, and degrade white political standing. The argument was articulated repeatedly in proslavery writings, in political speeches, and in popular media. The South Carolinian Hammond formulated it as the "mudsill theory" — that every society required a degraded class to perform the necessary degraded work, and that slavery placed Black workers in this role and thereby preserved the dignity of even the poorest white workers. The mudsill argument was crude. It was also politically effective. By 1860 the white Southern political coalition was united in defense of slavery in a way that the economic distribution of slaveholding by itself would not have predicted.

## What slavery was, as an institution

The economic facts of the antebellum slave system are easier to state than the human reality of what enslaved people experienced. The numbers — 3.95 million enslaved people, $3.5 billion in property valuation, two billion pounds of cotton produced — describe an economic structure. They do not describe what it was like to be enslaved, what enslaved people did to survive and resist, or what the institution did to the people held within it.

Some specifics are worth naming.

Labor was the institution's central demand. Enslaved adults on cotton plantations typically worked from before sunrise to after sunset, six days a week, with brief rest periods. The work was physical, repetitive, and supervised by overseers (themselves often poor whites, sometimes enslaved people in supervisory positions). Productivity was enforced through the threat and reality of physical violence — whipping was routine; severe injuries and deaths from beatings were not uncommon. The historian Edward Baptist, in *The Half Has Never Been Told* (2014), has argued that the productivity gains in antebellum cotton agriculture were driven substantially by the systematic intensification of violence applied to enslaved labor — what he called "the calibrated torture" of the cotton field. This argument has been challenged in specific quantitative terms by economic historians like Alan Olmstead and Paul Rhode, who attribute the productivity gains primarily to improved cotton seed varieties; the broader pattern of forced-labor productivity enforcement through violence is, however, well documented in the surviving enslaved-person testimony.

Family separation was a structural feature of the system. The internal slave trade that moved approximately one million enslaved people from the upper South to the cotton frontier across the antebellum decades was conducted through markets in which enslaved people were bought and sold individually — frequently separated from spouses, parents, children, and siblings in the process. The trauma of family separation was the single most frequently cited horror in the surviving testimony of enslaved people. Frederick Douglass, in his 1845 *Narrative*, devotes substantial space to it; the WPA-era interviews conducted with formerly enslaved people in the 1930s return to it repeatedly. The institution made human beings into commodities whose market value could be realized only through transactions that destroyed the relationships that human beings naturally form.

Sexual exploitation was endemic. The *partus sequitur ventrem* doctrine that the Virginia legislature had established in 1662 — that a child's enslaved status followed the mother's — meant that pregnancies that resulted from sexual contact between enslaved women and free white men produced enslaved children who became the property of the slaveholder. The structure removed economic incentive against sexual exploitation; it provided economic incentive for it. The frequency and the systemic character of sexual abuse of enslaved women by male slaveholders was a pattern documented in the surviving sources, in the demographic record (the substantial proportion of enslaved people of mixed-race ancestry by 1860), and in the testimony of free people of color descended from enslaved women and slaveholders. The institution made women's bodies an economic asset that the legal system protected the slaveholder's right to use.

Resistance was continuous and took many forms. Open rebellion was rare — Gabriel's Rebellion (1800, Virginia, suppressed before launch), the Vesey conspiracy (1822, Charleston, suppressed before launch), and the Nat Turner rebellion (1831, southeastern Virginia, suppressed after killing approximately 60 whites and 100–200 enslaved Black people in retaliation) are the three named events of the antebellum period. The retaliation that followed each rebellion produced restrictive legal codes — the Virginia legislative session that followed Nat Turner's rebellion considered, and rejected, gradual emancipation, and instead enacted harsher restrictions on enslaved life, on free Black life, and on white abolitionist activity. Less dramatic forms of resistance were continuous: work slowdowns, tool sabotage, theft of food and other goods, escape attempts (the Underground Railroad helped perhaps 30,000 to 100,000 escape across the antebellum decades, though the higher figure is contested), the maintenance of communities and cultures that the slaveholders did not authorize and could not fully suppress. Enslaved people created and sustained African-American Christianity in the antebellum period — the Black Methodist and Baptist churches that would become institutionally independent after Emancipation grew out of antebellum congregations that frequently included both enslaved and free members. The cultural life of the antebellum enslaved Black community produced spirituals, folk traditions, foodways, and languages (Gullah on the South Carolina and Georgia sea islands; Louisiana Creole) that survived the institution and shaped American culture broadly.

## The political project the cotton economy required

The political defense of slavery became progressively more aggressive across the antebellum decades. In the late eighteenth century, even Southern slaveholders had often described slavery apologetically — as a necessary evil inherited from the colonial period, to be tolerated but not celebrated. By the 1830s the apologetic framing had been replaced by what the historians call the "positive good" defense — the argument that slavery was not merely tolerable but actively beneficial, both to white society and (in proslavery framings) to enslaved people themselves.

The shift was substantially the work of intellectuals. John C. Calhoun, in a series of Senate speeches in the 1830s and 1840s, articulated the positive-good doctrine in its most influential form. George Fitzhugh's *Sociology for the South* (1854) and *Cannibals All!* (1857) extended the argument to a critique of Northern wage labor — wage workers, Fitzhugh argued, were actually worse off than enslaved people because slaveholders were obligated to care for their enslaved workers while Northern industrialists owed wage laborers nothing once the workday ended. Fitzhugh's framing was extreme and not representative of mainstream Southern political opinion, but it gave the proslavery argument a comprehensive ideological structure.

The political consequences of the positive-good shift were substantial. Southern political leaders ceased to compromise on slavery's expansion; what had been a tolerated institution in 1820 had become, by 1850, a positive good that should be defended absolutely. The political space for compromise narrowed across the decade. The slave-state demand was no longer that slavery be permitted where it existed — it was that slavery be permitted to expand into new territories, that the federal government actively protect slavery against state-level abolition, that Northern interference with slavery (through abolitionist organizing, through resistance to fugitive slave recovery, through political opposition to slavery's expansion) be suppressed by federal action.

The aggressive defense of slavery was also expansionist. Southern political leaders supported the Mexican-American War and the acquisition of the Mexican Cession in part because it created new territory potentially open to slavery. Filibuster expeditions — privately organized military adventures aimed at seizing territory from foreign nations — were predominantly Southern projects aimed at adding new slave territory. William Walker's expeditions to Nicaragua in 1855–60 and Narciso López's expeditions to Cuba in 1849–51 were both significantly oriented around the goal of bringing new territory into the United States as slave states. The political theorist George Fitzhugh openly argued for the seizure of Cuba and the Caribbean to expand the American slave empire. The 1854 Ostend Manifesto, drafted by American diplomats in Belgium and intended for Spanish negotiations, declared that the United States should buy Cuba from Spain or, if Spain refused, take it by force. The manifesto was repudiated by the federal government when it leaked, but its content reflected the Southern expansionist program.

By the late 1850s the political logic was clear. Slavery in the United States was a fast-growing economic institution whose defenders had developed an aggressive ideology, a continental-expansion program, and a constitutional theory (nullification, states' rights, secessionist threat) that justified resistance to federal political pressure. The political institutions of the federal Union were no longer adequate to mediate the conflict between this institution and the increasingly anti-slavery political coalition emerging in the Northern states. The constitutional crisis the next several chapters will trace was a crisis that the structure of the antebellum economy had been making more difficult to resolve year by year.
