# Chapter 5 — Imperial Reforms and Colonial Protests, 1763–1774

*A constitutional argument among British subjects.*

In March 1766, Benjamin Franklin sat in front of the British House of Commons and answered 174 questions over four hours about the temper of the American colonies. He was sixty years old, in London as the agent of the Pennsylvania colonial assembly, and the most widely respected colonial figure in the Empire. Parliament was deciding whether to repeal the Stamp Act, which had been passed eleven months earlier and had produced colonial protests on a scale the British government had not anticipated. Franklin's testimony, published throughout the colonies in subsequent months, is one of the clearest contemporary statements of what the colonists believed they were arguing about.

"What was the temper of America towards Great-Britain before the year 1763?" a member of Parliament asked him.

"The best in the world," Franklin replied. "They submitted willingly to the government of the Crown, and paid, in all their courts, obedience to acts of parliament... They had not only a respect, but an affection, for Great-Britain, for its laws, its customs and manners, and even a fondness for its fashions, that greatly increased the commerce."

"And what is their temper now?"

"O, very much altered."

Asked whether the colonies would submit to a modified Stamp Act, Franklin said no. Asked whether the colonies would submit if British troops were sent to enforce it, he said the troops would find no rebellion to suppress — but they would also find no compliance, "unless you mean to make them all unhappy." Asked what would satisfy the colonies, he said: the same status they had held before 1763, which is to say, no Parliamentary taxation, on the principle that the colonists were not represented in Parliament and could only be taxed by their own legislatures.

The Stamp Act was repealed two days after Franklin's testimony. Parliament also passed, on the same day as the repeal, the Declaratory Act of 1766, which asserted Parliament's right to legislate for the colonies "in all cases whatsoever." The repeal looked like a victory and the Declaratory Act looked like a face-saving gesture. Twelve years and four major political crises later, the colonies were independent.

This chapter is about the political argument that produced the American Revolution. The argument was not, as it is sometimes summarized in popular accounts, "no taxation without representation" full stop. It was a specific constitutional argument about who held what authority within the British Empire, an argument that both sides understood themselves to be making from within a shared British constitutional tradition. Both sides cited John Locke. Both sides invoked the Glorious Revolution of 1688–89 as their precedent. Both sides claimed to be defending British liberty. The fight that produced the Revolution was a fight among British subjects about what being a British subject meant.

The puzzle of this chapter is what the colonists were arguing for. The slogan compresses a position; specifying the position is the work.

## What the slogan compressed

"No taxation without representation" is what the colonists chanted in 1765 and after. The slogan is short. The constitutional argument behind it had several distinct moving parts that are worth pulling apart.

The first part is the distinction between an *external* tax (a duty on imported goods) and an *internal* tax (a direct tax on activities or property within the colonies). Most colonists in 1765, including Franklin, accepted Parliament's authority to regulate imperial trade through external duties — the Navigation Acts of the seventeenth century were external taxes in this sense, and the colonists had paid them, evaded them, or both, for a century without raising the constitutional argument. What was new about the Stamp Act was that it was an internal tax: a direct tax on documents, newspapers, playing cards, and other paper goods produced and consumed within the colonies. Internal taxes, the colonists argued, were the exclusive prerogative of legislatures in which the taxed people were represented. The Stamp Act crossed a constitutional line that the Navigation Acts had not.

The second part is the doctrine of *virtual representation*. The British government's response to the colonists' "no taxation without representation" argument was that the colonists *were* represented — by the same form of representation that the city of Manchester was represented, even though Manchester (a major industrial city) had no members of Parliament. The British constitutional position was that members of Parliament represented the entire British people, regardless of where particular MPs were elected from. Manchester's interests were defended by Parliament collectively, not by a Manchester delegation. By the same logic, the colonies' interests were defended by Parliament, even though no colonist sat in it.

The colonists rejected the doctrine of virtual representation. They argued that representation required actual election by the people taxed, and that the constitutional protections of the English Bill of Rights of 1689 included the right to consent to taxation through one's elected representatives. The colonial argument was not a populist demand for democratic representation in the modern sense — colonial legislatures were elected on property-restricted suffrage that excluded most adults — but a Whig constitutional argument about the proper structure of representative government.

The third part is the question of where final sovereign authority within the Empire lay. Some colonists, particularly through the late 1760s, accepted that Parliament had ultimate sovereignty but argued that it was constrained by the same constitutional principles that constrained the crown. Others, by the early 1770s, moved toward a more radical position: that the colonies were legally tied to Britain only through their common allegiance to the king, and that the colonial legislatures, not Parliament, were the proper legislative bodies for the colonies. This position — what the historian Bernard Bailyn called the "Commonwealth theory" — moved the colonies, by 1774, toward an effectively independent constitutional standing within the Empire. It was rejected by every major British political faction. By 1775, the constitutional argument had hardened into a question that admitted no settlement short of either complete colonial submission or complete colonial independence.

The fourth part — and this is the part most easily missed in modern accounts — was the colonists' belief that the British state had become *corrupt* in a specific eighteenth-century sense, and that the colonial assemblies needed to defend the British constitution against its own corrupted government. The English "Real Whig" tradition — the tradition of John Trenchard and Thomas Gordon's *Cato's Letters* (1720–23) and the "Commonwealth men" of mid-eighteenth-century England — held that liberty required vigilance against governmental corruption, that standing armies were threats to civilian government, that ministerial influence over Parliament through patronage destroyed legislative independence, and that the loss of liberty proceeded by small, incremental encroachments that had to be resisted at the start. The colonists read these works extensively. The pattern of grievances they cataloged after 1763 — the standing army in peacetime, the use of Admiralty courts that bypassed jury trial, the diversion of Townshend duty revenue to pay royal officials' salaries — fit the Real Whig template for ministerial corruption. They were not just complaining about taxes. They were articulating a theory of how empires fall into tyranny, and asserting that they could see the pattern beginning at home.

## The sequence of crises

The twelve years between the Treaty of Paris (1763) and the battles at Lexington and Concord (1775) ran through five distinct political crises, each of which raised the stakes. Walking through them in order is worth doing because the sequence shows how a tax dispute became a constitutional revolution.

**Crisis One: The Proclamation Line and the Sugar Act, 1763–1764.** The Royal Proclamation of October 7, 1763 prohibited British colonial settlement west of the Appalachian Mountains, reserving that territory for Indigenous nations and limiting colonial expansion. The proclamation was issued in the wake of Pontiac's Rebellion, a major Indigenous uprising in the Ohio Valley in 1763 that had killed hundreds of colonists. From the British perspective, the proclamation was a peace-keeping measure that reduced the risk of further frontier wars. From the colonial perspective — particularly that of Virginia speculators, who had been buying western land for decades on the assumption it would eventually be sold to settlers — it was a major economic disappointment. George Washington was among those whose investment plans were complicated by the proclamation; he would spend the next decade looking for ways around it. The Sugar Act of April 1764, which reduced the existing duty on molasses but enforced it more rigorously and created Admiralty courts to try smuggling cases without juries, provoked colonial merchants. Neither act produced organized colonial resistance, but they raised the temperature.

**Crisis Two: The Stamp Act, 1765–1766.** The Stamp Act required tax stamps on most printed documents in the colonies. It was the first internal tax Parliament had ever imposed on the colonies and was meant to raise approximately £60,000 annually — about 17 percent of the cost of maintaining the British army in North America. The colonial response was unprecedented. The Stamp Act Congress of October 1765, with delegates from nine colonies, drafted a Declaration of Rights and Grievances that explicitly invoked the no-taxation-without-representation argument. Local crowds, organized in many colonies under the name *Sons of Liberty*, forced stamp distributors to resign before the Act took effect on November 1; in most colonies the Act could not be enforced because there was no one willing to distribute the stamps. Boycotts of British imports — organized through Daughters of Liberty groups that produced homespun cloth as a substitute for English textiles — cut British imports to the colonies by roughly 14 percent in 1765–66. Parliament repealed the Stamp Act in March 1766 — but passed the Declaratory Act the same day asserting Parliament's authority "in all cases whatsoever." The face-saving formula did not commit to actually exercising that authority; the next year, Parliament would.

**Crisis Three: The Townshend Acts, 1767–1770.** Charles Townshend, Chancellor of the Exchequer, designed a new revenue plan that he thought would avoid the Stamp Act's mistakes. The Townshend Acts of 1767 imposed external duties on imported paper, paint, lead, tea, and glass — duties Parliament had always considered itself entitled to impose. The duties were technically external (taxes on imports rather than on internal goods or activities), but the revenue was earmarked to pay the salaries of royal governors and judges in the colonies, removing those officials from financial dependence on colonial assemblies. The colonial response was a second non-importation movement, organized through the Committees of Correspondence that Samuel Adams and others had established in Massachusetts and that spread across colonies through 1768–69. British imports to the colonies fell by about 38 percent. In March 1770, after the Boston Massacre — in which British troops, stationed in Boston since 1768 to enforce the Townshend Acts, fired into a crowd of colonial protesters, killing five — Parliament repealed all the Townshend duties except the one on tea, which was retained as a symbolic assertion of Parliament's authority.

**Crisis Four: The Tea Act and the Destruction of the Tea, 1773.** The Tea Act of May 1773 was, in practical economic terms, a corporate-rescue measure. The British East India Company was facing financial collapse; the Tea Act allowed it to bypass British middlemen and ship tea directly to colonial consignees, lowering the retail price of tea in the colonies even with the surviving Townshend duty paid. The Act would have made East India Company tea the cheapest tea available in the colonies. The colonial response — particularly in Boston, where the Sons of Liberty had organized through the early 1770s — was to refuse to allow the tea to be landed and the duty paid. On the night of December 16, 1773, a group of about 100 colonists, some dressed as Mohawk Indians, boarded three ships in Boston Harbor and dumped 342 chests of East India Company tea (worth approximately £10,000) into the water. This action — what the colonists at the time called *the destruction of the tea* and what later generations would call the *Boston Tea Party* — was a direct, organized, deliberate destruction of British property by Boston colonists. It was a calculated escalation.

**Crisis Five: The Coercive Acts and the First Continental Congress, 1774.** The British government's response to the destruction of the tea was the Coercive Acts (called by the colonists the *Intolerable Acts*) of spring and summer 1774. Four laws specifically targeted Massachusetts. The Boston Port Act closed Boston Harbor to all commerce until the East India Company was compensated for the destroyed tea. The Massachusetts Government Act abrogated the Massachusetts colonial charter of 1691, putting the colonial government under direct royal control. The Administration of Justice Act allowed royal officials charged with crimes to be tried in England rather than in Massachusetts. The Quartering Act required colonists to provide housing for British troops. A fifth law, the Quebec Act, was technically separate but was framed by colonists as part of the same package: it extended Quebec province's boundaries west to the Ohio River, recognized Catholic worship in Quebec, and was read in the colonies as a deliberate offense to Protestant colonial sensibilities.

The Coercive Acts had two effects the British government had not foreseen. First, they radicalized colonial moderates who had not previously supported the Boston Sons of Liberty's tactics. The Massachusetts charter had been abrogated by direct royal action; that, the colonists argued, demonstrated that no colonial charter or assembly was safe from similar treatment. Second, they produced inter-colonial coordination. The First Continental Congress, meeting in Philadelphia from September 5 to October 26, 1774, brought together delegates from twelve colonies (Georgia did not attend) and produced both a Declaration of Rights and Grievances and an Association — a systematic non-importation, non-consumption, and non-exportation agreement that committed every colony to economic resistance against the Coercive Acts.

By October 1774 the framework for the Revolution was in place. Inter-colonial coordination was established. Boycott machinery was operational. The constitutional argument was articulated. The Massachusetts colonial government was operating outside royal authority, with the Suffolk Resolves of September 1774 (drafted by Joseph Warren, endorsed by the Continental Congress) calling for non-payment of taxes to the royal government and for military preparations. What followed in April 1775 was, given the prior twelve years, not a sudden eruption but a continuation of an argument that had been moving toward armed resolution for some time.

## The role of women, and what gets erased when it isn't named

The standard narrative of the imperial crisis foregrounds male political actors — Franklin testifying to Parliament, Samuel Adams organizing the Committees of Correspondence, John Adams writing for the *Boston Gazette*. The political organizing that actually made the boycotts work was substantially the work of women, and the standard narrative tends to elide this.

The non-importation movement depended on the substitution of domestic production for British imports. Colonial households consumed enormous quantities of textiles, ceramics, and other manufactured goods imported from Britain. Cutting these imports required producing substitutes. The substitutes were produced primarily by women, in colonial households, through extensions of women's existing work in spinning, weaving, sewing, and food processing. The *Daughters of Liberty* — a name used in several colonies for women's organizations supporting the boycotts — held public spinning bees, sometimes producing hundreds of yards of cloth in a day, that were as much political performances as economic activities. Boycotts of tea required substituting other beverages (often labeled "Liberty Tea," made from local herbs); the substitution and the household enforcement were performed by women. The non-importation agreements of 1768–70 cut British imports to the colonies by nearly 40 percent. That number is partly the work of women whose names did not enter the standard narrative.

This is the kind of erasure worth noting because it recurs through American history. When the historical actors writing the surviving documents are predominantly elite men, the work of non-elite actors and women tends to disappear from the documentary record, then from the historical narrative, then from the textbook compression of the narrative. Reconstructing the work that has been erased requires looking for it deliberately — in account books, household inventories, women's letters, sermons that address gendered audiences, the back pages of newspapers. The work is harder; the people are harder to find; the framing of the period changes when you find them.
