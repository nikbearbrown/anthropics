# Chapter 1 — The Americas, Europe, and Africa Before 1492

*Three worlds, already complex, on the eve of being yoked together.*

On November 8, 1519, a Spanish soldier named Bernal Díaz del Castillo crossed the Iztapalapa causeway and saw Tenochtitlán for the first time. Decades later, in his old age in Guatemala, he wrote down what he remembered seeing.

"When we saw so many cities and villages built in the water, and other great towns on dry land, we were amazed and said it was like the enchantments... on account of the great towers and *cues* and buildings rising from the water, and all built of masonry. And some of our soldiers even asked whether the things we saw were not a dream. I do not know how to describe it, seeing things as we did that had never been heard of or seen before, not even dreamed about."

The Aztec capital that stunned Díaz was probably the largest city in the Western Hemisphere at the time and likely larger than any city in Europe except Paris. It had perhaps 200,000 inhabitants. It had two stone aqueducts bringing fresh water from springs at Chapultepec. It had a daily market that the conquistador Hernán Cortés estimated drew 60,000 buyers and sellers. It had public sanitation systems and human-waste collection — the city's residents bathed daily, while London at the same date threw chamber pots into the street. It had floating gardens, the *chinampas*, that you can still visit in Xochimilco today. It had been founded, according to Aztec accounts, in 1325 — about a hundred and seventy-five years before Columbus's first voyage.

This chapter is about what existed before 1492 — three continents already populated, already complex, already commercially linked to one another in ways most textbook accounts elide. Tenochtitlán is one entry in a long catalogue of pre-contact sophistication. So is Cahokia, the Mississippian city near present-day St. Louis that at its peak around 1100 CE had perhaps 20,000 inhabitants — more people than London at the same date. So is Timbuktu, the Mali Empire's capital, which by the fourteenth century was a center of Islamic learning, gold-trade taxation, and book commerce. So is Lisbon and Genoa and Bruges in the Atlantic-facing fifteenth-century Europe that produced Columbus and the voyage he sold to the Spanish crown.

The puzzle this chapter sets up is the one that drives the next several: How does a chance encounter between three already-functioning civilizational systems produce the specific catastrophe that followed? The short answer is that the encounter was not balanced — Europeans arrived with diseases the Americas had not seen and with an institution of forced labor that, when transplanted across the Atlantic, became something it had never been before. The longer answer takes the rest of this book to tell.

## What "discovery" hides

Begin with a piece of vocabulary that does damage. The word *discovery*, applied to Columbus's 1492 voyage, contains a quiet assertion: that the place was unknown until a European set foot on it. There were perhaps fifty to a hundred million people already living in the Americas at that moment. They had been there for at least fifteen thousand years. The word *discovery* makes them invisible by definition. It is the kind of word a teacher should mark.

The Americas in 1491 were not a single thing. The Mesoamerican civilizations — Olmec (roughly 1200–400 BCE), Maya (about 2000 BCE through 900 CE, with sequel polities surviving into the contact period), Teotihuacan (peak around 500 CE, population over 100,000), Toltec, and finally Aztec (founded 1325, conquered 1521) — produced cities, mathematics, calendars, writing systems, monumental architecture, long-distance trade, and the only fully developed written language in the pre-Columbian Western Hemisphere. The Andean civilizations — Norte Chico (3500–1800 BCE, contemporaneous with the early Egyptian pyramids), Chavín, Moche, Tiwanaku, Wari, and Inca — built the largest empire in the pre-Columbian world. The Inca state at its peak around 1525 stretched some 2,500 miles along the western coast of South America, governed perhaps ten million people, and was held together by a road network that totaled approximately 24,000 miles. North of the Rio Grande, the Mississippian culture built earthwork cities including Cahokia and traded across half a continent. The Iroquois Confederacy, which Benjamin Franklin would later study and partially borrow from, was a working federal political system by the time European contact began.

Population estimates for the Americas in 1491 vary widely — the upper estimates run to over 100 million, the lower estimates to around 20 million, with most modern scholarship clustering in the 50 to 75 million range. The variation matters because what happened next was a demographic catastrophe whose scale depends on the starting number. The lowest estimate of population decline by 1650 — primarily from smallpox, measles, influenza, and other Old World diseases against which the indigenous populations had no immunity — is around 80 percent. The highest run to over 95 percent. By the time anyone in Europe was paying close attention, the largest die-off in human history had already happened on a continent most Europeans had still never seen.

Europe in 1491 had also just emerged from a demographic catastrophe of its own. The Black Death of 1347–1351 had killed perhaps a third of the population of western Europe — the precise number is debated, but the survivors lived through a hundred years of social and economic restructuring caused by the labor shortage the deaths produced. Serfs gained leverage; wages rose; the feudal manorial system began to dissolve in much of western Europe (though not all of it — eastern Europe's serfdom actually intensified during the same period). By 1450 the European population was recovering, and by 1491 it had nearly regained its pre-plague level. Europeans were on the move again — and Europe in the late fifteenth century was the most violent and politically fragmented part of the Atlantic world. There was no "Europe" in the political sense. There were dozens of competing kingdoms, principalities, free cities, and ecclesiastical states. Spain had been unified by Ferdinand and Isabella only in 1469, and the final Christian conquest of the Muslim emirate of Granada — which is what the word *Reconquista* names — was completed on January 2, 1492, eight months before Columbus sailed. The men Columbus took with him had spent most of their adult lives at war.

West Africa in 1491 was richer than most European accounts admitted. The successive empires of Ghana (roughly 750–1240 CE), Mali (1240–1500), and Songhai (1500–1591) sat astride the trans-Saharan trade in gold, salt, and enslaved people, controlling the flow of West African gold to the Mediterranean economies of North Africa and Europe. When Mansa Musa, ruler of Mali, made his *hajj* to Mecca in 1324, he traveled with a caravan that included thousands of people and gave away so much gold along his route through Cairo and the Hejaz that the value of gold in the Mediterranean economy reportedly fell for a decade afterward. Timbuktu, his capital, was by the fourteenth century a center of Islamic scholarship with libraries, madrasas, and a book trade. Sub-Saharan Africans of the late fifteenth century lived inside political-economic systems as complex as Europe's, with cities, taxation, long-distance trade, written records (in Arabic, in the Islamic states), and a religious institution — Islam — that linked them to the Mediterranean and Indian Ocean worlds.

What is true about West Africa, as about the Americas, is also true about Europe: the term hides as much as it reveals. *European* is a word that flattens the difference between Genoese merchant capitalists with their commodities exchanges, Portuguese mariners with state-funded scientific institutes, Iberian Jews who had just been expelled from Spain in March 1492 (the same year Columbus sailed), and German peasants who would not see a sailing ship in their lifetimes. *Indigenous* flattens five hundred or more distinct linguistic communities in the Americas, ranging from urban civilizations of millions to hunter-gatherer bands of dozens. *African* flattens hundreds of language groups and political systems across a continent larger than Europe and the United States combined.

When this book uses these terms, it is using them as imperfect shorthand for groups that the historical record forces us to refer to collectively. The flattening is a defect of the language, not a feature of the past.

## Slavery before chattel

The institution that becomes the central economic engine of the Atlantic world after 1500 is slavery. The word names something different in 1450 than what it will name in 1750. Tracing the difference is one of the most important pieces of work this chapter does, because the simplification — *slavery has always existed in roughly the same form* — is one of the most common errors in popular histories of the Atlantic world.

Some kind of unfree labor existed in most pre-modern societies. The ancient Mediterranean had slavery; the Romans, the Greeks, and the Persians all enslaved war captives and debtors on a scale that varied across the centuries. Vikings raided Ireland, England, and Slavic Europe for enslaved laborers in the eighth through eleventh centuries; the word *slave* derives from *Slav* because Slavic peoples were the most commonly enslaved population in early medieval Europe. Medieval West Africa had several different practices that the European observers called "slavery": debt bondage, prisoner-of-war captivity, criminal punishment, household domestic service. Some of these involved permanent unfreedom; many were temporary or led to integration into the captor society. Enslaved persons in pre-Atlantic West Africa could often marry, hold property, rise in social position, and have free-born children. The institution was permanent for some, contingent for others, and almost never racial.

Islam, which spread across North Africa beginning in the seventh century, made its peace with slavery in a particular way: a Muslim could not enslave another Muslim, but could enslave non-Muslims taken in war or purchased. The trans-Saharan slave trade that linked sub-Saharan Africa to the Mediterranean world over the eight centuries before 1500 was Islamic-controlled, and brought perhaps eight to ten million people across the Sahara — primarily as domestic servants, soldiers, and concubines in Mediterranean and Middle Eastern households. This Islamic trade was older, longer, and demographically larger than the Atlantic trade that began in the late 1400s and ended in the 1860s.

What changed when slavery crossed the Atlantic was not the existence of forced labor but four specific features that together produced something new. Each deserves naming.

First, slavery in the Americas became *racial*. The legal codes of seventeenth-century English colonies — Virginia's are the most-studied — gradually built race into the definition of slavery. Before about 1660, the legal status of enslaved people in Virginia was contested and inconsistent; some Africans served terms similar to indentured servitude and were freed, some bought their freedom, some held property. After the 1660s, a series of statutes (1662, 1667, 1670, 1691, 1705) made slavery a permanent, hereditary, racially-defined institution in ways that prior systems had not. Race did not exist as a Western legal category in 1500. The Atlantic slave system invented it.

Second, slavery in the Americas became *hereditary*. The 1662 Virginia statute reversed the common-law rule (that a child's legal status followed the father) and instead made a child's status follow the mother. This single change ensured that the children of enslaved women were enslaved at birth, no matter who fathered them — including the slave-owning men who frequently fathered children with women they had enslaved. The institution became self-reproducing across generations in a way that previous slave systems had usually not been.

Third, slavery in the Americas became *chattel*. The legal status of enslaved people became identical to the legal status of livestock or furniture — they could be bought, sold, mortgaged, inherited, and seized for debt without their consent and without any legal recourse. Most prior slave systems had at least nominal limits on what could be done to enslaved persons. Atlantic chattel slavery progressively eliminated these limits.

Fourth, slavery in the Americas became *demographically massive and economically central*. Roughly 12.5 million Africans were embarked on slave ships across the Atlantic between 1501 and 1866; approximately 10.7 million survived the Middle Passage. The labor of those people, and their descendants, produced the sugar, tobacco, rice, cotton, and indigo that financed the rise of European industry and the founding wealth of the United States. The institution was not a side phenomenon. It was the central economic structure of the Atlantic world for three and a half centuries.

The model you may encounter — "slavery has always existed; what the Americas did was just an instance of an ancient practice" — is wrong about the things that matter. What the Americas produced was a new institution, distinguishable from prior forms of slavery by its racial basis, its heredity, its chattel structure, and its scale. The other forms continued to exist alongside it; the Atlantic chattel system was added to the historical inventory of forced labor, not borrowed from it.

## Why 1492 happened then

The encounter between the three continents was not inevitable. It had specific causes traceable to specific decisions in specific cities in the second half of the fifteenth century. Two pieces are worth naming.

The first piece is the closing of the Silk Road. Land trade between Europe and East Asia had been possible — expensive but possible — through the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries under the relative stability of the Mongol Empire and its successors. By the late fourteenth century the route was breaking down: the Mongol successor states had fragmented, the Ottoman Empire was consolidating its control of the eastern Mediterranean, and after the Ottoman conquest of Constantinople in 1453 the overland route to Asia from western Europe became prohibitively expensive and politically fraught. Western European merchants wanted access to the Asian luxury goods — spices, silk, porcelain — that had become central to European elite consumption. They could either pay Ottoman intermediaries or find a way around them. Portugal and then Spain began funding maritime expeditions to find an Atlantic route to Asia.

The second piece is the shipbuilding and navigational technology that made Atlantic voyaging possible. The Portuguese, beginning under Prince Henry the Navigator (1394–1460) and his successors, systematically developed the caravel — a small, maneuverable lateen-rigged ship that could sail closer to the wind than its predecessors and was suited to coastal exploration of unknown shores. They also developed the navigational techniques that allowed sailors to determine latitude using the sun and stars, and refined the use of the magnetic compass. By the 1480s Portuguese mariners had charted the entire west coast of Africa; in 1488 Bartolomeu Dias rounded the Cape of Good Hope; in 1497–98 Vasco da Gama reached India by sea. The Portuguese route to Asia was a working enterprise within a decade of Columbus's first voyage.

Columbus's pitch to the Spanish crown, in 1492, was that there was a shorter route to Asia by sailing west across the Atlantic. He was wrong about the distance — he based his calculation on a small estimate of the Earth's circumference and assumed Asia was about where the Americas turned out to be. The Spanish crown had just finished the Reconquista in January, had Iberian Jews leaving (or being expelled and forcibly converted) in March, had a treasury depleted by the long war, and decided in April to fund Columbus's voyage as a low-cost long-shot. His total expedition cost perhaps one to two million maravedís, modest by royal-financing standards. He returned in March 1493 with the news that he had found land — though not, as he believed, off the coast of Asia.

The decision-making that produced 1492 was therefore neither destined nor cosmic. It was a Spanish crown looking for a return on a small investment, made by people who had just spent eight centuries fighting Muslim political entities on their own peninsula, who had just expelled the Jewish population they had previously lived with, and who saw the Atlantic primarily as a route to Asian gold. The encounter with the Americas was an accident of those specific decisions, taken in those specific months, by those specific people. The next three hundred years' worth of catastrophe is what unfolds from the accident.
