# Chapter 4 — Rule Britannia! The English Empire, 1660–1763

*The first system that thought of itself as global.*

In October 1739, the Anglican itinerant preacher George Whitefield stood on the steps of the Philadelphia courthouse and addressed a crowd that had assembled to hear him. Whitefield was twenty-four years old, English, an associate of John and Charles Wesley, and the most famous public speaker in the eighteenth-century English-speaking world. He preached without notes, in the open air, in a voice that listeners reported could be heard at extraordinary distances. He had begun an itinerant tour of the British North American colonies that would eventually cover thirteen colonies, take seven trips, and continue intermittently for thirty years until his death in 1770. He was, by the contemporary measure, the first colonial celebrity.

Benjamin Franklin happened to be in the Philadelphia crowd that day. He was thirty-three, a printer, a not-yet-famous newspaper editor, skeptical of revivalist religion but curious about the phenomenon Whitefield represented. He noticed something. Whitefield's audiences were said to exceed twenty thousand people. Franklin doubted this. So he conducted an experiment.

"I had the curiosity to learn how far he could be heard, by retiring backwards down the street toward the river; and I found his voice distinct till I came near Front-street, when some noise in that street obscured it. Imagining then a semi-circle, of which my distance should be the radius, and that it were filled with auditors, to each of whom I allowed two square feet, I computed that he might well be heard by more than thirty thousand. This reconciled me to the newspaper accounts of his having preached to twenty-five thousand people in the fields, and to the antient histories of generals haranguing whole armies, of which I had sometimes doubted."

The passage is from Franklin's *Autobiography*. It is doing several things at once. It is testing the audience-size claim against measurable evidence. It is recording the scale of an emergent transcolonial public — twenty-five thousand people listening to one man, in an English-speaking colonial city of less than fifteen thousand permanent residents. It is also, in its method, recording the British Atlantic empire of the mid-eighteenth century. Franklin and Whitefield were both products of the same imperial cultural moment: an Enlightenment-influenced colonial gentry that could measure things, and a transatlantic Protestant revival that could move people across that gentry's heads. The empire that contained both was not just an economic system. It was a cultural and intellectual integration with specific channels — newspapers, sermons, scientific correspondence, consumer goods — that all flowed across the Atlantic.

The puzzle of this chapter is how that integration happened. The British North American colonies in 1660 had been a scattered set of struggling outposts. By 1763 they were the most prosperous and most populous European-settled territory in the Western Hemisphere outside the Spanish silver-mining zones, and they were integrated into a global British imperial system that stretched from Bombay to Boston. The integration was not preordained. It was built. Tracing how is the work of this chapter.

## What "British Empire" was integrating

Begin with the term. *The British Empire* in 1700 was not a coherent political entity in the way nineteenth-century empires would be. It was a loose set of overlapping institutions: a Parliament in London that legislated for the kingdom but only intermittently for the colonies; royal governors appointed by the crown in some colonies and elected officials in others; chartered joint-stock companies (the East India Company, the Royal African Company, the Hudson's Bay Company) that exercised quasi-governmental functions in their respective domains; a Royal Navy that protected trade and projected force; and a body of trade legislation — the Navigation Acts of 1651, 1660, 1663, 1673, 1696 — that aimed to channel imperial commerce through English ports and English ships.

The Navigation Acts were the spine of what the British imperial system was, economically. The principle they enforced was *mercantilism*: the belief that national wealth consisted in the accumulation of bullion, that trade was zero-sum (one nation's gain was another's loss), and that colonies existed to supply raw materials to the metropole and to consume the metropole's manufactured goods. The Acts required that certain colonial products (the "enumerated commodities" — initially sugar, tobacco, indigo, ginger, cotton; later expanded to include rice, naval stores, copper, beaver pelts) be shipped only to England or to other English colonies. They required that European goods bound for the colonies pass through English ports first. They required that the ships carrying this trade be English-built and English-crewed.

The Acts were less effective than they were comprehensive. Colonial merchants smuggled extensively, particularly to and from the Dutch Caribbean and to the French sugar islands. English customs enforcement was lax through most of the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries. The framework existed; the enforcement was selective. But the framework shaped the long-run structure of the British Atlantic economy: enumerated commodities went to England; manufactured goods came from England; the colonial economy specialized in raw-material production and the metropolitan economy in manufacturing.

The political integration of the empire was looser than the commercial one. The English monarchy went through the Glorious Revolution of 1688–89, in which James II — Catholic, absolutist in the model of his cousin Louis XIV — was deposed and replaced by his Protestant daughter Mary and her husband William of Orange. The Glorious Revolution did three things that mattered for the colonies. First, it produced the English Bill of Rights of 1689, which constrained royal power and asserted the supremacy of Parliament — the constitutional doctrine that the colonists would later cite as the foundation for their own rights. Second, it produced colonial revolts in 1689 against the most absolutist royal governors — Sir Edmund Andros was deposed in Boston, Jacob Leisler led a rising against the royal government in New York, the Calverts lost their proprietorship of Maryland — that the colonists framed as parallel to the English Glorious Revolution and as expressing the same political principles. Third, it ended the Royal African Company's monopoly on the slave trade in 1689, throwing the trade open to private merchants and accelerating the rapid expansion of African enslavement in the British Caribbean and the southern mainland colonies after 1700.

The religious integration was more uneven than either. The Anglican Church was the established religion of England, of Virginia, and (after 1692) of Maryland, Carolina, and the parts of New York around Manhattan. The Congregational Church was established in Massachusetts and Connecticut. Pennsylvania had no establishment and tolerated all Christian denominations (including Catholics, which made it distinctive). The Great Awakening of the 1730s and 1740s, which we will get to, ran across these institutional boundaries and was the first social phenomenon that touched all thirteen colonies simultaneously.

The intellectual integration was the most subtle and possibly the most consequential. Newspapers, almanacs, sermons, scientific correspondence, and political pamphlets circulated across the Atlantic in increasing volume through the early eighteenth century. By 1750 the colonial gentry was reading the same books, debating the same political ideas, conducting the same kind of scientific experiments, and writing each other letters that crossed the Atlantic in weeks. Benjamin Franklin's electrical experiments of the 1740s were read in London and Paris. The English political theory of John Locke's *Two Treatises of Government* (1689) — which had been written to justify the Glorious Revolution — became the most-cited political text in the colonies by mid-century. By 1763, the cultural identity of educated colonists was *British*. They thought of themselves as transplanted Englishmen exercising English liberties on the western shore of the Atlantic. The Revolution that followed was, in important ways, an argument among British subjects about what it meant to be British.

## Sugar, and what sugar built

Of all the commodities the British Atlantic empire produced, the most economically important was sugar. By the early eighteenth century, the British West Indian sugar islands — primarily Barbados (settled 1627), Jamaica (captured from Spain in 1655), and the Leeward Islands — generated more revenue for British merchants and the British state than the entire North American mainland did. The Caribbean planter class, headquartered in Bristol and London, had more influence over British policy than any North American colonial interest before the 1760s.

The economics of sugar are worth tracing because they explain a lot. Sugar cane was an extraordinarily labor-intensive crop. Once cut, the cane had to be processed within hours or it began to lose its sugar content. This meant sugar plantations were industrial-agricultural complexes: vast acreages of cane, a boiling-house and a milling operation on every plantation, and a labor force of hundreds of enslaved Africans working in conditions that combined the brutality of field labor with the danger of industrial work. The mortality rate among enslaved Africans on Caribbean sugar plantations was extreme — most plantation owners replaced their enslaved labor force every seven to ten years through new imports rather than through natural reproduction. The Caribbean was, demographically, a place that consumed African lives in order to produce the most-traded commodity of the eighteenth-century Atlantic.

The numbers are worth pausing on. Approximately 4.5 million Africans were transported to the British Caribbean between 1640 and 1808. Approximately 400,000 were transported to British North America (the future United States) over the same period. The Caribbean received roughly eleven times as many enslaved Africans as the mainland did. Yet the Caribbean enslaved population in 1800 was smaller than the mainland enslaved population. The explanation is the differential mortality: the mainland sustained a self-reproducing enslaved population; the Caribbean did not.

The sugar economy financed British industrial development in specific ways. The capital generated by Caribbean sugar production flowed back to British merchants in Bristol and Liverpool, who reinvested it in shipping, manufacturing, and infrastructure. The Caribbean planters themselves were largely absentee — they lived in England and managed their plantations through resident overseers — and their consumption of British luxury goods, country estates, and political offices was a significant pillar of the British political economy. The historian Eric Williams, in *Capitalism and Slavery* (1944), argued that the profits of Caribbean slavery directly funded the Industrial Revolution. The strong form of the Williams thesis has been qualified by subsequent economic historians (Stanley Engerman, David Eltis, others) but the basic point — that Caribbean sugar production was an enormous economic engine whose profits were central to British capital formation in the eighteenth century — is solid.

The mainland colonies were peripheral to the sugar empire but participated in its trade. New England merchants supplied lumber, fish, and provisions to the Caribbean plantations; New England shipping carried the trade. The "triangular trade" of high school textbooks — manufactured goods to Africa, enslaved people to the Caribbean, sugar to England — is a simplification; the actual trade flowed in many more directions than three, and the New England leg (provisions and ships) was as important to the system as the African and Caribbean legs. But the simplification captures the basic structure: the Atlantic was an integrated economic system, and sugar (with tobacco a distant second) was its central commodity.

The Carolinas, settled in the 1660s under royal charter to a group of proprietors, were established as an extension of the Barbados model. The first generation of South Carolina planters were Barbadians who had brought their slave-plantation methods (and their enslaved Africans) to the Carolina lowcountry. South Carolina's economy specialized in rice, which became a major export crop in the early eighteenth century, and in indigo. By 1720 South Carolina had an enslaved majority — the only mainland colony where this would be true for the entire colonial period. The Stono Rebellion in September 1739, in which approximately one hundred enslaved Africans (led by a literate Angolan named Jemmy) marched south through the Carolina lowcountry trying to reach Spanish Florida and freedom, was the largest slave uprising in mainland British North America before the Revolution. The South Carolina militia suppressed the rebellion within a few days, killing most of the rebels and selling the survivors to the West Indies. The colonial assembly responded with the 1740 Negro Act, which sharply restricted the lives of enslaved Africans and the import of new captives.

## The Great Awakening and the Enlightenment, side by side

The two cultural movements that swept the British Atlantic in the second quarter of the eighteenth century looked, on paper, like opposites. The Great Awakening was a Protestant revival that emphasized emotional, personal, immediate religious experience and the immanence of divine judgment. The Enlightenment was a scientific and philosophical movement that emphasized reason, empirical observation, and the regularity of natural law. The Awakening said the world was full of God's wrath and required emotional repentance. The Enlightenment said the world was a system of regular causes that could be measured and understood.

In practice the two movements ran together more than the contrast suggests. The same colonial gentry that read Jonathan Edwards's sermons read John Locke's political philosophy. Benjamin Franklin, who was the leading Enlightenment figure in the colonies and a friend of Whitefield's despite his religious skepticism, conducted experiments on electricity that demonstrated the regularity of natural law and simultaneously hosted Whitefield in his Philadelphia home and printed Whitefield's sermons. Jonathan Edwards, who delivered "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God" in 1741, was also one of the most philosophically sophisticated theologians of his generation and a serious reader of John Locke and Isaac Newton. The picture of an emotional Awakening fighting a rational Enlightenment is wrong about the period; both movements coexisted in the same minds.

What both movements did, structurally, was integrate the British Atlantic culturally. The Awakening produced the first religious movement that crossed every colonial boundary and reached every demographic stratum, from elite urban congregations to enslaved Africans in the southern lowcountry. (The substantial conversion of enslaved Africans to Christianity in British North America dates from the Awakening's reach into the Black community in the 1740s and 1750s.) The Enlightenment produced the first scientific and political community that operated transatlantically, with British colonists and Britons in the metropole exchanging correspondence, papers, and books on shared problems. By the time the political conflict with Britain began in the 1760s, both movements had given the colonists a shared cultural infrastructure for thinking about themselves as a transatlantic community.

## The Seven Years' War

The wars with France for North American empire ran from the late seventeenth century through the mid-eighteenth: King William's War (1689–97), Queen Anne's War (1702–13), King George's War (1744–48), and finally the Seven Years' War (1756–63, called the French and Indian War in the American context). The first three were inconclusive. The fourth was decisive.

The Seven Years' War was the first global war. It was fought in Europe (where it was the War of Austrian Succession's sequel), in North America (where it began in 1754 with a Virginia militia officer named George Washington firing on French troops near present-day Pittsburgh, two years before the official European declaration), in the Caribbean (where Britain captured French sugar islands), in West Africa (where Britain seized French slave-trading stations), in India (where Britain's East India Company defeated French forces and established control of Bengal), and at sea on every ocean. The British victory in 1763 — formalized in the Treaty of Paris — gave Britain control of Canada, of all French territory east of the Mississippi, of Florida (ceded by Spain in exchange for Cuba, which Britain returned), and of dominant positions in India and West Africa. It made Britain the first global power in the modern sense.

It also doubled British national debt — from approximately £75 million in 1755 to approximately £133 million in 1763 — and produced a financial crisis that the British state would attempt to resolve by raising new revenues from the colonies, beginning with the Sugar Act of 1764 and the Stamp Act of 1765. The connection between the financial costs of the Seven Years' War and the political conflicts of the 1760s is direct. Britain had won an empire it could not afford. Its solution was to tax the colonies. The colonies, having spent the war fighting on Britain's side and having shared in its victory, responded that they had won an empire on Britain's behalf and would not be made to pay for it.

That argument is the subject of Chapter 5.
