# Chapter 21 — Leading the Way: The Progressive Movement, 1890–1920

*Several reform movements wearing one label.*

On March 25, 1911, at about 4:40 in the afternoon, a fire broke out on the eighth floor of the Asch Building at the corner of Greene Street and Washington Place in Manhattan, just east of Washington Square Park. The eighth, ninth, and tenth floors of the building housed the Triangle Shirtwaist Factory, where approximately 500 garment workers — most of them young immigrant women, primarily Italian and Jewish, ranging in age from fourteen to about forty — were finishing their Saturday shift. The fire spread rapidly through the cutting tables, where months of accumulated fabric scraps acted as fuel. The factory's exit doors on the eighth and ninth floors had been locked from the outside — a standard practice at the time, intended to prevent theft and to prevent labor organizers from entering the factory. The single fire escape collapsed under the weight of workers trying to descend it. The Fire Department's tallest ladders reached only to the sixth floor. Workers on the ninth and tenth floors who could not escape through the fire-escape or the freight elevators jumped from the windows. The fire killed 146 workers, of whom 123 were women. Most of the dead were buried in the days that followed in a series of public funerals that drew enormous crowds; about 400,000 people lined the streets of New York for one of them.

The Triangle fire was one of the most consequential industrial disasters in American history, not because it was the deadliest (it was not — the Iroquois Theater fire of 1903, the Eastland disaster of 1915, and several mine disasters killed more) but because of what it produced politically. The New York State Factory Investigating Commission, established three months after the fire under the chairmanship of Senator Robert F. Wagner and the vice chairmanship of Assemblyman Alfred E. Smith, conducted what was at that point the most systematic investigation of industrial working conditions in American history. The Commission's recommendations led to the passage of dozens of new state labor laws — fire safety codes, building codes, working-hours limits for women and children, sanitation requirements, factory inspection requirements — across the following four years. Frances Perkins, who had witnessed the fire from the street, would later say that the New Deal was born at Triangle. As Franklin Roosevelt's Secretary of Labor from 1933 to 1945, Perkins drafted much of the federal labor legislation that, two decades after the Triangle fire, extended New York's reforms to the country.

This chapter is about the reform movement that Triangle catalyzed but did not create. The Progressive movement — the loose coalition of reform causes that emerged in the 1890s, consolidated in the 1900s, and largely dissolved into the postwar political settlement of the 1920s — was the most ambitious sustained attempt at political-institutional reform that any American period before the New Deal would produce. It was also, in important ways, internally contradictory. Different Progressives wanted different things, sometimes incompatible things. The movement produced both the Nineteenth Amendment (women's suffrage, 1920) and the Eighteenth Amendment (Prohibition, 1919). It produced both the Federal Trade Commission and the Espionage Act of 1917. It produced both the federal income tax and the most racially restrictive federal immigration legislation in American history. It produced both Theodore Roosevelt's "Square Deal" rhetoric of working-class economic protection and Woodrow Wilson's systematic re-segregation of the federal civil service.

The puzzle of this chapter is what to make of a reform movement whose specific commitments were so often at odds with each other. The compressed answer is that "Progressivism" is a label that gets applied to multiple distinct reform projects whose participants overlapped but whose substantive commitments diverged. Each project has its own logic; the label as a singular thing obscures more than it reveals.

## Three Progressivisms

The cleanest way to make sense of the Progressive Era's contradictions is to distinguish three substantially different reform projects that operated under the broad Progressive label.

**Efficiency Progressivism** was the project of restructuring government and business along the lines of professional managerial expertise. Its core conviction was that the political and economic institutions of late-nineteenth-century America had been inefficient, corrupt, and amateurish, and that reform should make them rational, scientific, and run by trained experts. The efficiency Progressives were responsible for the civil service reforms that replaced patronage with merit-based hiring; for the introduction of city-manager municipal government; for the federal commissions that regulated railroads, banking, and corporations (the Interstate Commerce Commission's expanded authority under the Hepburn Act of 1906; the Federal Reserve Act of 1913; the Federal Trade Commission Act of 1914); and for the Taylorist scientific-management movement in industrial production. Efficiency Progressivism was, in its political instincts, broadly conservative on social and economic questions — its goal was to make capitalism more rational rather than to displace it — and broadly elitist on questions of governance, preferring trained experts to democratic accountability. Walter Lippmann's *Public Opinion* (1922) is the classic articulation of the efficiency Progressive's view that ordinary citizens were too poorly informed to govern themselves well and that policy should be made by professionals.

**Moral Progressivism** was the project of using state power to enforce specific moral commitments that the Progressives identified with civilization. Its core conviction was that certain behaviors (drinking, prostitution, gambling, the consumption of "obscene" materials, certain immigrant cultural practices) were socially destructive and that the state should suppress them. The moral Progressives were responsible for Prohibition (the Eighteenth Amendment, 1919); for the Mann Act of 1910 (criminalizing the interstate transportation of women for "immoral purposes," a vague phrase that was used to prosecute consensual interracial relationships, among other things); for the Comstock Laws restricting the mailing of contraceptive information and other "obscene" materials; for the eugenicist movements that produced forced-sterilization laws in many states; and for the immigration restrictions that culminated in the National Origins Act of 1924, which drastically limited immigration from Southern and Eastern Europe and prohibited most Asian immigration. Moral Progressivism was the most reactionary of the three projects in some respects — it was substantially nativist, often racist, and frequently paternalistic — and it produced some of the most durable American political institutions of the twentieth century (the Federal Bureau of Investigation, founded in 1908, was substantially a product of moral-Progressive concerns; the federal narcotics enforcement system began with the Harrison Narcotics Act of 1914).

**Social-Justice Progressivism** was the project of using government and civil-society organizing to address poverty, working conditions, and social inequality. Its core conviction was that industrial capitalism had produced extreme inequalities and that political action was needed to mitigate them. The social-justice Progressives were responsible for the labor legislation that the Triangle fire catalyzed (the New York State labor reforms of 1912–14 became models for federal legislation in the 1930s); for the federal Children's Bureau (1912); for the workplace safety movements; for the early federal income tax (Sixteenth Amendment, 1913, allowing direct federal income taxation; the federal income tax that followed was structured to fall heavily on the very wealthy); for the women's suffrage movement (Nineteenth Amendment, 1920); and for the early African-American civil-rights organizing that produced the NAACP (1909) and the National Urban League (1910). Social-Justice Progressivism was the most genuinely democratic of the three projects and the most contested in its own time.

The three projects overlapped substantially in personnel. Jane Addams, Florence Kelley, Frances Perkins, and many other reformers were active in all three; the muckraking journalists who exposed industrial-trust abuses (Ida Tarbell, Lincoln Steffens, Upton Sinclair) frequently shared the efficiency Progressives' faith in expert governance while sharing the social-justice Progressives' concerns about working-class welfare. Theodore Roosevelt was simultaneously an efficiency Progressive (his "trustbusting" rhetoric was substantially about making American capitalism more rational rather than less concentrated), a moral Progressive (his racial views included substantial eugenicist content; he was a strong supporter of restrictive immigration policy), and a social-justice Progressive (his "Square Deal" rhetoric and the Hepburn Act). Woodrow Wilson was an efficiency Progressive on economic-regulatory questions (the Federal Reserve, the FTC), a moral Progressive on Prohibition and segregation (his administration systematically re-segregated the federal civil service in ways that reversed Reconstruction-era progress), and an inconsistent social-justice Progressive whose policy positions varied depending on which coalition he was speaking to.

The contradictions of the Progressive movement come into focus when you ask which Progressivism a specific reform belonged to. The Nineteenth Amendment was a social-justice Progressive achievement. The Eighteenth was a moral Progressive achievement. The Federal Reserve was an efficiency Progressive achievement. The National Origins Act of 1924 was a moral Progressive achievement. All four are conventionally described as products of the same "Progressive movement," and they were, in the sense that the same political coalition was substantially responsible for all of them. But the substantive commitments were different, and the people who supported one project did not always support the others.

## The muckrakers and the role of journalism

The Progressive movement's intellectual infrastructure depended substantially on a new style of investigative journalism that emerged across the 1900s. The journalists were called *muckrakers* — a term Theodore Roosevelt applied to them in 1906, deriving from Bunyan's *Pilgrim's Progress* (the man with the muck-rake who looked only downward at filth and never looked up at the heavenly crown). Roosevelt meant the term partly as criticism — he thought some muckrakers had become so focused on exposing wrongdoing that they were undermining public confidence in American institutions broadly — but the journalists adopted it as a description of their work.

The major muckrakers' works are worth naming because they shaped specific political-reform outcomes:

- **Ida Tarbell's *The History of the Standard Oil Company*** (serialized in *McClure's Magazine* 1902–04, published as a book 1904) documented John D. Rockefeller's anticompetitive business practices in extraordinary detail. The book contributed substantially to the political environment that produced the Supreme Court's 1911 decision dissolving Standard Oil into thirty-four separate companies under the Sherman Antitrust Act.
- **Lincoln Steffens's *The Shame of the Cities*** (1904) was a collection of magazine articles exposing the political-machine corruption in major American cities. The series contributed to the municipal-reform movements that displaced political machines in many cities across the 1900s and 1910s.
- **Upton Sinclair's *The Jungle*** (1906) was a novel about the Chicago meatpacking industry; Sinclair had been commissioned by a socialist magazine to expose the conditions of the meatpacking workforce, but the book's most-remembered passages described the unsanitary conditions of meat production rather than the working conditions of the workers. The novel produced an immediate political response: the federal Pure Food and Drug Act and the Meat Inspection Act of 1906 were passed within months of the book's publication. Sinclair famously said: "I aimed at the public's heart and by accident hit its stomach."
- **Jacob Riis's *How the Other Half Lives*** (1890), which I discussed in Chapter 19, was the methodological predecessor to the later muckraking journalism. Riis combined photography with investigative reporting in ways that the next generation of muckrakers extended.
- **Ida B. Wells's anti-lynching journalism**, which began in 1892 with her *Free Speech* newspaper articles in Memphis, produced one of the most sustained investigative-journalistic campaigns of the Progressive era. Wells documented hundreds of specific lynching cases, traced the patterns (most lynchings were not, as the conventional Southern defense claimed, retaliation for sexual assault but were responses to Black economic success or political activity), and built an international anti-lynching movement that operated for several decades. Wells's work was, in its substantive impact, comparable to the better-known white muckrakers' work; it has been less central to the conventional Progressive-Era narrative partly because the racial-justice issues she addressed were systematically marginalized within the Progressive coalition.

The muckrakers' significance was structural as well as substantive. They demonstrated that mass-circulation journalism could produce political reform; they established a journalistic model that subsequent generations of investigative reporters would develop; they created a public-information environment in which the political coalitions for reform could be built. The Progressive movement's intellectual infrastructure was substantially journalistic, and that journalism was substantially commercial — the muckraking magazines (*McClure's*, *Cosmopolitan*, *Collier's*, *Everybody's*, *The American Magazine*) were profitable mass-circulation enterprises that depended on advertising revenue and middle-class readership. The political coalition that read the muckrakers was substantially the urban-middle-class coalition that supported many Progressive reforms.

## The Roosevelt-Taft-Wilson presidencies

The three Progressive-era presidencies — Theodore Roosevelt (1901–09), William Howard Taft (1909–13), and Woodrow Wilson (1913–21) — produced most of the major federal Progressive legislation. The personalities have been heavily covered in popular accounts; the substantive policy outcomes are worth naming.

**Theodore Roosevelt** became president after the assassination of William McKinley in September 1901. He was forty-two, the youngest American president to that point. His major domestic accomplishments included the antitrust prosecutions (most consequentially against the Northern Securities Company in 1904, a major railroad holding company); the Hepburn Act of 1906 (which strengthened the Interstate Commerce Commission's regulatory authority over railroad rates); the Pure Food and Drug Act and Meat Inspection Act of 1906; and the substantial expansion of federal conservation — the national parks system, the national forests, the National Wildlife Refuge system, the Antiquities Act of 1906. Roosevelt's broader political effect was to transform the American presidency into a substantively activist office that the nineteenth-century presidency had not been. His rhetorical tools — the "bully pulpit" was his phrase — and his willingness to use federal authority on questions previous presidents had avoided made the federal government a more visible actor in American life.

**William Howard Taft** succeeded Roosevelt in 1909 and continued much of his Progressive program — Taft's administration prosecuted more antitrust cases than Roosevelt's had, and the Sixteenth and Seventeenth Amendments (income tax and direct election of senators, both passed in 1913) were initiated under Taft. Taft's political relationship with Roosevelt collapsed across his term, however, on questions of conservation policy and on Taft's perceived insufficient commitment to Progressive reform. The 1912 Republican convention split between Taft (the incumbent, with conservative Republican support) and Roosevelt (who had decided to run again and had Progressive Republican support). Roosevelt walked out of the Republican convention and ran on the Progressive Party ticket (the "Bull Moose Party"). The split allowed the Democratic candidate, Woodrow Wilson, to win the 1912 election with 42 percent of the popular vote.

**Woodrow Wilson**, the only Democratic president between 1897 and 1933, ran in 1912 on a platform called the "New Freedom" that emphasized smaller-scale anti-monopoly action and tariff reduction. Wilson's first term produced the Underwood Tariff (substantially reducing tariff rates), the Federal Reserve Act (1913), the Federal Trade Commission Act (1914), and the Clayton Antitrust Act (1914) that strengthened the Sherman Act's labor-union and trust-busting provisions. Wilson's domestic record on racial questions was substantially regressive. His administration systematically re-segregated federal departments (Treasury, the Post Office, the Navy) that had been desegregated since Reconstruction; he supported the showing of D.W. Griffith's *Birth of a Nation* — a film that celebrated the Ku Klux Klan and portrayed Reconstruction as a Black-misrule disaster — at the White House in 1915; his administration's official stance on lynching was indifference. The Wilson administration's racial regression is one of the elements of the Progressive period that the standard accounts most often elide.

Wilson's second term was dominated by World War I (the next chapter takes that up). The wartime political environment substantially shifted the Progressive movement's political conditions. Many social-justice Progressives opposed the war and were marginalized politically during it. Many efficiency Progressives supported the war and saw their managerial-expertise commitments substantially expanded by wartime federal mobilization. Moral Progressives saw both Prohibition (federal wartime alcohol restrictions in 1917 prepared the political ground for the Eighteenth Amendment in 1919) and immigration restriction (the Espionage Act, the Sedition Act, the wartime suspension of German-American cultural institutions, and the 1921 Emergency Quota Act that preceded the 1924 National Origins Act) accelerated by the war.

The Progressive movement, in short, did not end with a specific event but dispersed across the late 1910s and early 1920s into substantially different political channels. The social-justice strand would re-emerge in the New Deal of the 1930s. The efficiency strand would persist throughout the twentieth century in administrative-state institutions. The moral strand would dominate the 1920s through Prohibition, immigration restriction, and the cultural conflicts the next several chapters take up.

## What the Progressive movement decided about race

A specific aspect of the Progressive movement is worth treating directly because it has been systematically minimized in many standard accounts: the movement's substantial accommodation of, and sometimes active complicity in, the post-Reconstruction Southern racial system.

The Progressive coalition was substantially a coalition of white reformers in the Northern cities, the Midwest, and parts of the West. It did not, with rare exceptions, include the political mobilization of Southern Black voters (who had been disfranchised across the 1880s and 1890s through state-level constitutional revisions, poll taxes, literacy tests, grandfather clauses, and white-primary systems). The Progressive movement's reform agenda did not, in most cases, address the Southern racial system. Progressive presidents (Roosevelt and Wilson particularly) made substantial accommodations with Southern Democratic political power on racial questions in exchange for Southern Democratic support on other Progressive priorities.

The institutional consequences are visible in specific policy outcomes:

- **Lynching legislation.** Federal anti-lynching legislation was repeatedly proposed across the Progressive era — the Dyer Anti-Lynching Bill passed the House in 1922 — and was repeatedly blocked in the Senate, primarily by Southern Democratic filibuster. The Senate would not pass an anti-lynching bill until 2018. The Progressive coalition that achieved the Eighteenth and Nineteenth Amendments did not produce comparable federal action against the lynching campaign that, between 1882 and 1968, killed approximately 4,743 documented victims (the actual number is substantially higher; the Equal Justice Initiative's documentation continues to expand the figure).
- **Federal segregation.** The Wilson administration's re-segregation of federal departments, as noted, reversed Reconstruction-era achievements. Black federal employees who had served alongside white colleagues in mixed offices were segregated into separate workspaces, separate restrooms, separate dining facilities, or in some cases fired outright.
- **Immigration restriction.** The Progressive movement's commitment to immigration restriction culminated in the National Origins Act of 1924, which set immigration quotas explicitly designed to favor Northern and Western European immigration and substantially restrict Southern and Eastern European immigration. The Act was advocated by some of the most prominent Progressive intellectuals (the economist John R. Commons; the sociologist Edward A. Ross; the eugenicists Madison Grant and Harry Laughlin). The intellectual framework was substantially eugenicist — the idea that some racial-ethnic groups were biologically superior and that immigration policy should be used to maintain the racial composition of the existing American population. The 1924 Act would shape American immigration policy for forty years until the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 substantially revised it.
- **Disfranchisement.** The Progressive movement's commitment to "direct democracy" — direct primaries, initiative-and-referendum, direct election of senators — did not, with limited exceptions, extend to challenging the disfranchisement of Black voters in the South. Most Progressive intellectuals who addressed the disfranchisement question framed it as a "Southern problem" outside the federal Progressive agenda.

The Black Progressive intellectual and organizational response to this is part of the chapter's story. W.E.B. Du Bois's *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903) had articulated a Black political-cultural agenda explicitly opposed to the Booker T. Washington accommodationist position; the Niagara Movement that Du Bois organized in 1905, and the NAACP that followed in 1909, were institutional responses to the failure of the broader Progressive coalition to address Black political-civil concerns. Ida B. Wells's anti-lynching work, Mary Church Terrell's club-women organizing, the National Negro Business League — these were substantial Black political institutions that operated alongside but not within the broader Progressive coalition.

The next chapter takes up the foreign-policy dimension of the Progressive era — the American empire that emerged from the Spanish-American War of 1898 and the global commitments the United States took on across the early twentieth century.
