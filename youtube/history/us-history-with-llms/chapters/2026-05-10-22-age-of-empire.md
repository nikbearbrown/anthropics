# Chapter 22 — Age of Empire: American Foreign Policy, 1890–1914

*The war that gets remembered and the war that gets forgotten.*

In February 1899, U.S. Army forces under General Elwell Otis fought a battle with Filipino soldiers under General Antonio Luna at the outskirts of Manila. The battle, which began on the night of February 4 when American soldiers fired on Filipino positions, marked the start of an armed conflict that the United States government would call the *Philippine Insurrection* and that subsequent historians have called the *Philippine-American War*. The war ran from 1899 through 1902 in its conventional phase and continued in low-intensity counterinsurgency operations through 1913. By the end of the conventional phase in 1902, approximately 4,200 American soldiers had been killed (more American military deaths than the Spanish-American War of 1898 had produced) and approximately 20,000 Filipino combatants had been killed. Estimates of Filipino civilian deaths from the war and its related famines and epidemics range from about 200,000 to over 600,000.

The Philippine-American War is, by American military deaths, larger than the Spanish-American War that nominally preceded it. By Filipino deaths, it is larger than almost any conflict the United States has been involved in. By duration, it lasted longer than the Spanish-American War, longer than U.S. participation in World War I, longer than U.S. participation in World War II. It produced atrocities — water torture (a precursor to what would later be called *waterboarding*) was a documented American interrogation method during the war; civilian massacres in Samar in 1901–02 were officially investigated by Congress in 1902; the U.S. concentration of Filipino civilians into camps to deny support to the insurgency was extensively documented. The Senate hearings on the war's conduct in 1902 — chaired by Senator Henry Cabot Lodge — produced testimony that would, in a different political context, have produced major political consequences.

The war is not in most American survey textbooks at the same scale as wars of comparable size and duration. It is one of the most reliably elided major American military conflicts. The standard popular narrative of American foreign policy from 1898 to 1914 treats the Spanish-American War (April–August 1898) as the major event, the Philippine-American War as either a footnote or absent altogether, and the subsequent period (Roosevelt's foreign policy, Taft's "dollar diplomacy") as a sequence of diplomatic episodes whose military violence is rarely foregrounded.

This chapter is about the construction of an American overseas empire across the quarter-century between the Frederick Jackson Turner thesis of 1893 and the outbreak of the First World War in 1914. The construction was deliberate, named at the time by its theorists (Turner, Mahan), debated in American political-cultural life as it was happening, and produced specific military operations and territorial acquisitions that subsequent American foreign-policy doctrine would build on. The puzzle of this chapter is why the period's American empire has been more substantially elided in subsequent popular memory than the comparable empires of the period's other major powers, and what the elision conceals.

## The intellectual case for empire

Two American intellectuals provided much of the explicit theoretical case for the imperial turn of the 1890s.

**Frederick Jackson Turner** was a thirty-two-year-old historian at the University of Wisconsin when he delivered his paper "The Significance of the Frontier in American History" at the meeting of the American Historical Association in Chicago on July 12, 1893. The paper argued that the existence of an open frontier had been the defining feature of American history, shaping American democratic institutions, character, and political culture. It also argued that the 1890 U.S. Census had shown the frontier "closed" — no more contiguous unsettled land available for further settlement — and that the closing was the central fact of contemporary American history. The thesis raised, by implication, a question Turner did not directly answer: if the frontier had been the engine of American development, what would happen now that the frontier was closed?

The thesis was widely influential in late-1890s American political-cultural debate. It gave a historical frame for thinking about the country's transition from continental expansion to overseas expansion. The thesis has been substantially criticized by subsequent historians — most fundamentally for treating the "frontier" as if it were empty land rather than land occupied by Indigenous nations whose presence Turner's framing erased; also for over-generalizing from American history to specific developmental causes; also for romanticizing the demographic and political processes of westward expansion. But as an intellectual influence on the period's foreign-policy discussion, the thesis was substantial.

**Alfred Thayer Mahan** was a U.S. Navy captain (later admiral) who taught at the Naval War College. His book *The Influence of Sea Power upon History, 1660–1783* (1890) argued that great powers in modern history had achieved their power through naval supremacy and overseas commerce, and that the United States should follow the British example by building a substantial blue-water navy, acquiring overseas coaling stations and naval bases, and establishing a Caribbean canal that would allow rapid naval movement between the Atlantic and Pacific. Mahan's book was substantially more influential than its scholarly reputation suggests. It was read by Theodore Roosevelt (then a young politician), by Henry Cabot Lodge, by Kaiser Wilhelm II, by Japanese naval officers, by British First Sea Lords. It substantially shaped the naval-construction programs of the major powers in the 1890s and 1900s. The American naval expansion of the McKinley and Roosevelt administrations was substantially executed on Mahanian principles.

The intellectual case the Turner-Mahan synthesis produced was approximately this: the United States needed to find new fields for the demographic and economic expansion that the closed frontier could no longer accommodate; the obvious fields were across the oceans, beginning with the Caribbean and the Pacific; and the United States should build the naval and commercial capacity to operate in those fields. The political coalition that endorsed this case — the imperialists of the 1890s — included Theodore Roosevelt, Henry Cabot Lodge, Albert Beveridge, William McKinley (eventually), much of the Republican Party leadership, and important Democratic figures. The opposing coalition — the anti-imperialists — included Mark Twain, William Jennings Bryan, Andrew Carnegie (despite his industrial-commercial interests; Carnegie actively funded the anti-imperialist movement), Jane Addams, and a substantial fraction of the Progressive social-justice coalition. The anti-imperialist case was that overseas empire contradicted American republican principles, that the Constitution provided no clear authority for indefinite colonial administration of non-citizen populations, that the empire's costs would substantially outweigh its benefits, and that the racial-cultural projects of empire (the "civilizing missions" the imperialist rhetoric invoked) were morally and politically corrosive.

The imperial coalition won the political debate of the late 1890s. The anti-imperialists' political institutions — the American Anti-Imperialist League, founded 1898 — operated for several decades but never achieved the political-electoral leverage that would have reversed the imperial commitments.

## The Spanish-American War

The Spanish-American War of April–August 1898 was the political-military event that consolidated the imperial turn. It is worth tracing because the war's specific dynamics are often elided in standard accounts that treat it as a single coherent event.

The proximate cause was the Cuban war of independence against Spanish colonial rule. Cuba had been in armed revolt against Spain on and off since the 1860s; the most recent phase had begun in 1895 under José Martí. The Spanish counterinsurgency under General Valeriano Weyler had concentrated Cuban civilians into camps to deny support to the rebels — a tactic that produced widespread civilian mortality and that American newspapers covered with substantial sensationalism. The "yellow journalism" of William Randolph Hearst's *New York Journal* and Joseph Pulitzer's *New York World* substantially shaped American public opinion in favor of intervention.

The U.S.S. *Maine*, an American battleship sent to Havana harbor in early 1898 to protect American interests during the Cuban conflict, exploded and sank on the night of February 15, 1898, killing 266 American sailors. The cause of the explosion was contested at the time and remains contested in some historical accounts. A 1976 U.S. Navy investigation concluded that the explosion was most likely caused by an internal coal-bunker fire that ignited the ship's magazine; a 1898 U.S. Navy board of inquiry had concluded it was an external mine. The 1898 conclusion was the politically operative one. "Remember the Maine!" became a rallying cry; McKinley requested and received a congressional declaration of war against Spain on April 25, 1898.

The military operations were brief. Commodore George Dewey's Asiatic Squadron destroyed the Spanish Pacific fleet at the Battle of Manila Bay on May 1, 1898. American forces under General William Shafter landed in Cuba in June; the major land engagement at San Juan Hill on July 1, 1898 included a charge by the First U.S. Volunteer Cavalry (the "Rough Riders") under Theodore Roosevelt, an action that Roosevelt's subsequent political career would substantially trade on. Spanish naval forces in the Caribbean were destroyed at the Battle of Santiago de Cuba on July 3. Spanish forces in Cuba surrendered on July 17. The war's military phase had lasted ten weeks.

The Treaty of Paris of December 1898 ended the war and transferred Spanish colonial possessions to the United States: Cuba (which the United States agreed to allow to become nominally independent, subject to the Platt Amendment of 1901 that gave the United States substantial control over Cuban foreign policy and the right to military intervention), Puerto Rico (which became an unincorporated U.S. territory and remains one today), Guam (similarly), and the Philippines (which the United States purchased from Spain for $20 million as part of the treaty settlement). The Hawaiian Islands had been annexed by the United States in July 1898 during the war, in a separate but related action.

The Spanish-American War's outcome made the United States, almost overnight, a substantial colonial power. The territorial acquisitions added approximately ten million people to U.S. administrative control across regions stretching from the Caribbean to the western Pacific. The constitutional and political questions the acquisitions raised — what was the constitutional status of inhabitants of "unincorporated territories"? what were their citizenship rights? what political institutions would govern them? — would be answered by the Supreme Court in the so-called *Insular Cases* of 1901, which held that the Constitution did not apply fully to inhabitants of the new territories. The *Insular Cases* established a constitutional framework in which the federal government could administer colonial possessions on substantially different legal terms than it administered the states. The framework remains in force; Puerto Rico, for example, continues to be governed under doctrines derived from the *Insular Cases*.

## The Philippine-American War

The Philippine-American War began three days before the U.S. Senate ratified the Treaty of Paris. American troops were occupying Manila; Filipino independence forces under Emilio Aguinaldo — who had declared Philippine independence in June 1898, before the Spanish surrender — had been fighting Spanish colonial forces for the previous year and had expected the Americans to recognize Philippine independence after the Spanish surrender. They did not. President McKinley's decision to annex the Philippines rather than recognize the independence Aguinaldo had declared produced the war.

The conventional phase ran from February 1899 through July 1902. The American military commitment grew from about 11,000 troops in early 1899 to a peak of approximately 70,000 in 1900–01. The U.S. Army adopted counterinsurgency tactics that included the concentration of Filipino civilians into camps (mirroring the Spanish tactic that the U.S. had condemned in Cuba), the destruction of villages suspected of supporting the insurgency, and the use of water torture (then called the "water cure") in interrogations. The Philippine-American War's brutality was extensively documented at the time. Mark Twain wrote one of his most pointed political essays — "To the Person Sitting in Darkness" (1901) — denouncing the war. The American Anti-Imperialist League collected and published witness accounts of atrocities.

The conventional phase ended with President Roosevelt's declaration of victory on July 4, 1902. The war did not end. The Philippine Constabulary, an American-officered Filipino paramilitary force, continued counterinsurgency operations against Filipino independence forces — most consequentially against the Moro (Muslim) populations of the southern Philippines — through 1913. The Moro Rebellion of 1899–1913 killed thousands; the Bud Dajo massacre of March 1906, in which American forces killed approximately 600 to 1,000 Moro men, women, and children at a volcanic crater on Jolo island, was one of the worst civilian-death incidents in American military history.

The total death toll of the Philippine-American War is contested in part because of methodological disagreements about how to count war-related civilian mortality (from direct violence, famine, epidemic disease in the camps and devastated regions). The contemporary American government estimate was that approximately 250,000 Filipinos died. Subsequent historians have produced both higher estimates (the historian Glenn Anthony May's calculations have run toward 600,000) and lower estimates. The most-cited current range is approximately 200,000 to 600,000 Filipino dead — almost all of them civilians.

The Philippines remained an American colony until 1946, when Philippine independence was formally granted after the islands' liberation from Japanese occupation. The American colonial administration across the four decades 1902–1946 produced both substantial public-infrastructure development (schools, roads, public-health programs) and substantial economic-extractive operations (the Philippine sugar industry was substantially restructured to favor American interests). The relationship between the United States and the Philippines remains shaped by this colonial history — the American military bases that operated in the Philippines through the early 1990s; the continuing migration patterns; the political-cultural connections — in ways that the standard accounts of American foreign policy substantially elide.

## The Open Door, the canal, and dollar diplomacy

The other major foreign-policy initiatives of the period operated in the background of these wars and consolidated the structural framework of the new American empire.

**The Open Door Notes** were Secretary of State John Hay's 1899 and 1900 diplomatic communications regarding the European partition of China. Britain, France, Germany, Russia, and Japan had established spheres of influence in China across the 1890s, taking advantage of the weakness of the Qing dynasty after the Sino-Japanese War of 1894–95. The Open Door Notes proposed that the major powers commit to maintaining equal commercial access to China for all nations, rather than allowing the partition to harden into exclusive colonial trading systems. The Notes did not produce a formal agreement, but the framework — the principle of equal commercial access — became the rhetorical position the United States would advance in East Asian foreign policy for the following half-century. The Boxer Rebellion of 1900, an anti-foreign uprising in northern China that produced an international military intervention (including American troops) to relieve besieged foreign legations in Beijing, was a related crisis. The settlement that followed required China to pay substantial indemnities to the foreign powers; the American share, after some pressure from Chinese-American advocacy, was eventually returned in the form of educational scholarships that funded Chinese students at American universities.

**The Panama Canal** was the major infrastructure project of the period. The strategic and commercial case for a Central American canal had been developed by Mahan; Roosevelt made its construction a major presidential project. The original French canal-construction effort under Ferdinand de Lesseps had failed in the 1880s; the rights had been bought by an American consortium. Colombia, which then held sovereignty over the canal zone, refused to ratify the treaty that would have granted the United States the construction rights. Roosevelt's response was to support a Panamanian revolt against Colombia in November 1903; the new Panamanian government immediately granted the United States the construction rights on substantially better terms. The canal was built between 1904 and 1914 at extraordinary cost (about 27,500 workers died during the construction phase, primarily from disease) and was operated by the United States until 1999. The Roosevelt-supported Panamanian revolt is one of the cleanest examples in modern history of a major power detaching a province from a smaller state by sponsoring secession; the standard American-foreign-policy accounts of the canal's construction frequently elide the Colombian-detachment aspect.

**The Roosevelt Corollary** to the Monroe Doctrine, announced in December 1904, extended the doctrine in ways its original framers had not anticipated. The original Monroe Doctrine (1823) had stated that the United States would oppose European colonization in the Western Hemisphere; the Roosevelt Corollary stated that the United States would intervene in the internal affairs of Western Hemisphere states that the United States considered to be politically or economically unstable, in order to prevent European intervention. The Corollary produced a series of American military interventions in the Caribbean and Central America across the following three decades — in Cuba, in the Dominican Republic, in Haiti, in Nicaragua, in Honduras, in Panama itself. The interventions were typically justified on the Corollary's grounds (stabilizing finances, preventing political instability) and typically operated to favor American commercial interests in the affected countries.

**Dollar Diplomacy** was the term applied to the foreign-policy approach of the William Howard Taft administration (1909–13). The framework was to use American financial investment — loans, banking operations, investments in infrastructure — as the instrument of American foreign policy, with American military force as backup when financial-interest commitments needed protection. The approach was applied in China (American banking participation in international railroad-financing consortia) and in the Caribbean and Central America (where American banks substantially controlled the finances of multiple smaller states). The framework was less aggressive than Roosevelt's "Big Stick" but operated to similar ends: extending American economic and political control over smaller states through a combination of financial leverage and intermittent military intervention.

By 1914, when the First World War broke out in Europe, the United States had built an overseas empire that included formal colonies (the Philippines, Puerto Rico, Guam, parts of the Caribbean), protectorates (Cuba, Panama, several Central American states under intermittent American military occupation), commercial spheres of influence (in China, in much of Latin America), and a substantial blue-water navy capable of projecting force globally. The country was, by the standard measures of the period, a major colonial power comparable to the European empires that had been operating since the eighteenth century.

The next chapter takes up the First World War and what it did to this framework.
