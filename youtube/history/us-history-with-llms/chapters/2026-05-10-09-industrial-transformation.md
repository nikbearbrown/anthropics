# Chapter 9 — Industrial Transformation in the North, 1800–1850

*Two regions on the same continent producing different futures.*

In the autumn of 1822, a sixteen-year-old named Lucy Larcom arrived in Lowell, Massachusetts, to work in the textile mills. Her father had died; her widowed mother needed her wages. She joined what was, by that point, one of the most ambitious industrial-labor experiments in the world: the Lowell System, in which the textile mills of the Merrimack River valley were staffed primarily by young unmarried women from the New England countryside, housed in company boardinghouses, paid wages they could send home or save, and required to attend lectures, libraries, and churches that the company maintained for their moral improvement. The Lowell "mill girls," as they were called, worked twelve to fourteen hours a day in noisy, lint-clouded, increasingly dangerous factories. They also produced a literary magazine, the *Lowell Offering*, which published their poetry and essays. By 1840 the Lowell mills employed roughly 8,000 women.

The Lowell System was designed to do something specific. It was designed to industrialize textile production in the United States without producing the urban industrial proletariat that European industrialization had produced — without the slums of Manchester, the political radicalism of the Chartists, the visible misery that English factory workers had brought to the attention of European reformers. Francis Cabot Lowell and the Boston Associates who built Lowell were betting that a New England Protestant labor force, recruited briefly and rotated home, would generate cheap industrial textiles without generating a permanent class of resentful urban workers. For about twenty years the bet seemed to work. By the 1840s the conditions deteriorated, the wages fell, the working hours stretched, and the New England daughters were replaced — first by Irish immigrants, then by a more diverse immigrant labor force, and finally by the standard industrial proletariat the Lowell System had been designed to prevent.

This chapter is about the economic transformation that produced Lowell, Lowell's eventual conventional industrialization, and the wider remaking of the Northern American economy between roughly 1800 and 1850. The transformation produced what the historian Charles Sellers called the Market Revolution — the integration of the previously local and household-based American economy into a single national market through transportation infrastructure (canals, then railroads), financial integration (banks, paper currency, commercial credit), and the spread of wage labor.

The puzzle of this chapter is what the Market Revolution looked like from inside, what it did to the people it transformed, and how its development in the North alongside the simultaneous expansion of the slave plantation economy in the South set the conditions for the sectional conflict the next several chapters take up.

## What changed about how work was done

In 1800, most American economic production happened in households and small workshops. Farms produced their own food and most of their own clothing. Artisans — shoemakers, blacksmiths, weavers, coopers, printers — worked alongside one or two apprentices in small shops attached to their houses. Goods that were not produced locally were either imported (luxury goods from Britain, sugar from the Caribbean) or moved short distances along rivers and rough roads. Most Americans lived more than a day's travel from any city of consequence; most Americans bought, sold, and consumed almost entirely within a network of local relationships.

By 1850, this had substantially changed. The change happened in several distinct but overlapping waves.

**The putting-out system, c. 1800–1820.** Merchants in coastal cities began purchasing raw materials, distributing them to rural farming families who would perform specific finishing tasks (spinning yarn, weaving cloth, stitching shoe uppers) on their own time at home, and then collecting and selling the finished goods. The putting-out system kept production in households but moved control of production to the merchant class. Farm wives and daughters who had previously spun yarn for household use now spun for sale; the wages produced cash income that bought goods the family had previously made themselves. The system was a transitional form. It introduced wage labor without yet centralizing production in factories.

**The factory system, c. 1815–1840.** Beginning with Samuel Slater's small textile mill in Pawtucket, Rhode Island (built in 1790, after Slater had memorized the design of a British power loom and emigrated to the United States carrying it in his head), and accelerating with the larger integrated mills at Waltham (1814) and Lowell (1823), American textile production moved into water-powered factories. The factory system centralized production, mechanized previously hand-performed tasks, and replaced the artisan-with-apprentice model with the supervisor-with-wage-workers model. The first American industries to factorize were textiles and shoes; iron and machinery followed in the 1830s and 1840s. By 1850 the Northern states had become a manufacturing economy with a substantial urban workforce, while the Southern states remained primarily agricultural.

**Deskilling and division of labor.** What the factory system did to work was not just to centralize it physically but to break it down analytically. Previously, a shoemaker had performed all the steps of shoe production from leather cutting through final stitching. In a factory, those steps were divided among different workers, each performing one specialized operation. The division reduced the skill required for any single position; the work became repetitive and replaceable. Wages fell as more workers could perform the simplified tasks. The artisan tradition — apprentice, journeyman, master — that had organized craft labor in the colonial and early national periods was substantially destroyed by the factory system. The new industrial workers were employed at wages set by labor-market competition rather than by craft custom, and their dependence on continuous employment for survival was a new economic reality.

The transformation produced political and social responses. Working-class urban men formed the first American labor unions in the 1820s and 1830s — the Working Men's parties of Philadelphia and New York, the General Trades Union of New York, the National Trades Union. These organizations agitated for shorter working days, free public education, the abolition of imprisonment for debt, and political representation for working-class interests. Most of these organizations were destroyed by the Panic of 1837, which produced sustained unemployment and weakened bargaining position; the American labor movement would have to rebuild from scratch in the 1840s and 1850s.

## The infrastructure that made the market national

The transformation of work in the factories depended on a parallel transformation of transportation that allowed manufactured goods to move cheaply over long distances and raw materials and food to move into the manufacturing cities.

**The canal era, c. 1817–1840.** The Erie Canal, opened in 1825 after eight years of construction, connected the Hudson River at Albany to Lake Erie at Buffalo. The canal allowed goods to move between New York City and the Great Lakes region — and from there, via the Mississippi-Ohio river system, to most of the American interior — at roughly one-tenth the cost of overland transport. The Erie Canal made New York City the dominant Atlantic port of the new United States; it allowed wheat from the Ohio Valley to feed the East Coast and the manufactured goods of the East Coast to supply the West. The Erie Canal's success produced a national canal boom: by 1840 the United States had over 3,300 miles of canal.

**The railroad era, c. 1830–.** The first commercial railroad in the United States, the Baltimore & Ohio, began operations in 1830. By 1840 the United States had about 3,000 miles of track, by 1850 about 9,000, by 1860 about 30,000. Railroads outperformed canals in speed (steam locomotives were faster than canal boats), in seasonality (canals froze in winter; rails did not), and in topography (canals required relatively flat terrain; rails could climb hills). By the 1850s railroads were the central transportation technology of American industrial development, and a substantial fraction of American capital was invested in railroad construction.

The infrastructure was substantially federally and state subsidized. The federal government provided land grants to railroad companies — eventually amounting to about 130 million acres, an area larger than France. State governments financed canal construction directly or through state-backed bonds. The myth of American industrial development as a purely private-enterprise achievement is mostly that — a myth. The infrastructure was built with massive public subsidies, on land the federal government had acquired through treaties with (and wars against) Indigenous nations, and the political economy of the development involved continuous federal and state intervention in markets in ways that the official ideology of laissez-faire did not always acknowledge.

## The Panic of 1819 and the political economy that followed

The first major economic depression in American history began with the Panic of 1819. It was caused by a combination of factors that would recur throughout the nineteenth century: overextension of credit during a postwar economic boom, speculation in western lands, the contraction of credit by the Second Bank of the United States after a period of excessive expansion, and the collapse of European demand for American agricultural exports as European agriculture recovered from the Napoleonic Wars. Land prices in the western states collapsed. Farmers who had bought land on credit during the boom could not pay their mortgages. Banks failed. The economic distress was particularly severe in the West, where the boom had been most aggressive.

The Panic of 1819 had political consequences that would shape the next several decades. Andrew Jackson's anti-Bank campaign of the 1830s drew its emotional energy from the resentment that western farmers and small-business owners had developed toward the Second Bank during and after the Panic. The doctrine of *hard money* — that the only legitimate currency was specie (gold and silver), and that bank paper currency was a fraud — became a substantial political position, often called Jacksonian, that opposed the entire financial-system-building project that Hamilton had begun and that the Second Bank embodied.

The political economy of the antebellum North developed in the shadow of the Panic of 1819 and the subsequent panic of 1837 (an even worse one, triggered substantially by Jackson's destruction of the Second Bank — see Chapter 10). The development of American banking, currency, and credit took place in a political environment in which financial institutions were systematically opposed by populist political movements that suspected them of being instruments of elite domination. The pattern would recur. American politics, on questions of banking and finance, has been organized around this populist-versus-elitist axis from the 1830s through the present.

## Social classes consolidate

By 1850, the Northern states had developed visible class divisions that had not existed in eighteenth-century America. The historian E. P. Thompson's framework for the English working class — that class is something people *make* through shared experience and political mobilization, not something they passively inherit — applies usefully to the American case as well.

At the top, an industrial-commercial elite consolidated in the major Northeastern cities. The Boston Associates who had built Lowell; the Philadelphia merchants who funded canal construction; the New York merchant-banking families (the Astors, the Browns, the early Morgans) — these were families whose wealth had begun in colonial-era Atlantic trade and had pivoted into industrial-era manufacturing and finance. They built large urban houses, supported elite cultural institutions (the Boston Athenaeum, the New York Society Library), and intermarried within their own class. By 1850 the top 1 percent of households in major Northern cities owned 40 to 50 percent of the wealth — substantial wealth concentration by modern measures, comparable to the concentration in the early twenty-first century.

A middle class of small-business owners, professionals, and clerical workers expanded across the antebellum period. This was a new social formation. The previous social structures had been largely binary: landowners and tenants; masters and servants; gentry and commoners. The expanding middle class of antebellum America — shopkeepers, lawyers, doctors, ministers, teachers, accountants, government clerks — was a third position that did not fit easily into older social categories. Middle-class households were defined increasingly by what the historian Stuart Blumin called *bourgeois domesticity*: a household pattern in which men worked outside the home for wages or fees, women managed the household as full-time domestic labor (a new gender division), children attended school rather than working in family enterprises, and the household consumed an expanding array of mass-produced goods.

The growing working class — the urban wage laborers, the factory operatives, the dockworkers, the day laborers — lived under conditions that varied widely but that in the worst cases (the Five Points district of New York City, the slums that grew up around industrial Boston and Philadelphia) approached the conditions Charles Dickens was describing in contemporary London. Working-class wages were precarious. Working-class housing was crowded and frequently unsanitary. Working-class political representation was minimal (most states still required property qualifications for voting in 1820, though most had eliminated these by 1840). Working-class life expectancy in the urban industrial slums was substantially lower than in rural America.

A free Black community existed in most Northern cities by 1850. New York City, Philadelphia, Boston, and the smaller Northern industrial cities all had communities of free African Americans — descendants of those manumitted under the gradual emancipation laws of the post-Revolutionary period, plus migrants from the slave states, plus escaped or formally manumitted slaves. The free Black communities developed their own institutions: African Methodist Episcopal and African Methodist Episcopal Zion churches; mutual-aid societies; abolitionist newspapers (most prominently Frederick Douglass's *North Star*, founded in 1847); the National Negro Conventions that began meeting in 1830. Free Black Northerners faced legal restrictions that varied by state — disenfranchisement was common; segregated schools were universal; legal protections were minimal. The Northern free Black community was a major political force in the antebellum abolitionist movement and would be central to the political dynamics of the Civil War decade.

A growing immigrant working class — Irish from the 1840s onward, Germans through the 1830s and 1840s, smaller numbers of British, Scandinavian, and other European immigrants — transformed the urban demographics of the Northern cities. The Irish Famine of 1845–52 produced approximately one million emigrants to the United States; the Germans were displaced by the failed revolutions of 1848 and by ongoing agricultural displacement. By 1860 the Irish-born population of the United States was about 1.6 million, the German-born about 1.3 million. These were the first major non-Anglo Protestant migrations to the country, and they produced both the labor force for industrial expansion and the political conflicts (anti-Catholic nativism, the Know-Nothing party of the 1850s) that would shape antebellum politics.

## What the North and South were becoming

The economic divergence between the Northern and Southern states across the first half of the nineteenth century was not gradual or partial. It was substantial and accelerating. By 1860 the two regions had developed into economies of fundamentally different structure: the North an industrial-commercial economy with diversified manufacturing, extensive railroad networks, urban concentrations, and substantial European immigration; the South an agricultural-export economy specializing in cotton, organized around plantation slavery, with limited industrial development and minimal immigration.

The divergence was not because the South was failing to develop. The Southern plantation economy of 1860 was, by some measures, the most economically successful agricultural-export economy in the world. Cotton was the largest single American export by value through most of the antebellum period (it averaged about half of all American export earnings from 1815 through 1860). The wealthiest individuals in the United States in 1860 were Southern planters, not Northern industrialists. The South was developing rapidly. It was developing toward a different economic structure than the North.

The two regions were also economically interconnected. Southern cotton supplied Northern textile mills and the British textile industry that depended on American cotton. Northern factories produced the manufactured goods (textiles, tools, household goods) that Southern plantations and Southern families purchased. Northern banks held the credit that financed the Southern cotton trade. The two regional economies were specialized rather than independent. They needed each other.

But the political-cultural divergence the economies produced was profound. The North was developing a wage-labor economy, an urban-immigrant demographic, a public-education infrastructure, a manufacturing base, and an emerging Republican electoral coalition. The South was developing a slave-plantation economy, a rural demography, very limited public education, an agricultural base, and a Democratic electoral coalition committed to defending and expanding slavery. The two political-economic formations would prove unable to share federal political institutions indefinitely. The crisis that produced the Civil War was substantially the crisis of two divergent economic systems competing for control of federal policy on the territories, on tariffs, on internal improvements, on slavery's extension — all of the issues the antebellum decades fought over.

This is the longer-term context for the political conflicts the next several chapters will trace. Chapter 10 takes up the political restructuring of the 1820s and 1830s — the Jacksonian transformation, the Bank War, the rise of mass democratic politics, and the simultaneous violent expansion of slavery and Indian removal. Chapter 11 takes up westward expansion and the territorial-extension question that would, by the 1850s, make the slavery question impossible to compromise about further.
