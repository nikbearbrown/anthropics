## A Note about AI

US history is a discipline where the events are largely fixed and the interpretations are largely contested. The model is fluent in both and casual about the distinction. The note marks where the model is reliable and where it is structurally not.

The model has read enormous quantities of US history — textbook accounts, primary sources, scholarly monographs, and contemporary commentary. It will produce competent paragraphs on any major event, period, figure, or movement in American history. The factual fluency is useful and largely reliable for major facts (dates, names, established sequences). The interpretive fluency is where the problems begin, because US history is a field where competing interpretations of the same events are politically charged, and the model has read the competition without resolving it.

Where the model genuinely helps: producing the basic narrative of an event or period (when something happened, who was involved, what the immediate consequences were), introducing major scholarly interpretations of contested topics (the causes of the Civil War, the nature of Reconstruction, the meaning of the Cold War, the Civil Rights Movement's relationship to earlier and later eras), walking through the major primary sources of a period, and surveying the historiographical evolution of how the discipline has read a particular question over time.

Where the model does damage: characterizing contested events in language that subtly takes a side, treating still-contested interpretations as settled, and producing confident accounts of recent history where the historical literature has not yet stabilized. The model has read commentary across the political spectrum and reproduces aggregated views that may carry the inflection of whichever sources dominated its training. Its prose on the New Deal, the Cold War, the Vietnam War, the Reagan era, the post-9/11 era, and recent presidencies is the kind of writing a student should read assuming it has perspective even when it is presented as neutral.

A specific failure mode worth naming: the model is particularly fluent in the *consensus narrative* of US history — the version that appears in standardized textbooks — and casual about the revisionist or recently revised interpretations that are now standard in scholarly practice. A student writing a paper based on the model's framing may produce work that reads as decades behind the field.

The rule that covers all three: established events from the model; interpretations from named historians whose work you can date and place in scholarly conversation; primary sources from archives, document collections, and recognized digital editions. The model is a useful introduction. It is not a citation, and treating it as authority on a contested interpretation is the kind of error scholarly work is structured to catch.
