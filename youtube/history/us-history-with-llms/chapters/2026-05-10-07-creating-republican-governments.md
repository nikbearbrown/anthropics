# Chapter 7 — Creating Republican Governments, 1776–1790

*A constitution written around the institution its drafters could not undo.*

In Massachusetts in the summer of 1786, a former Continental Army captain named Daniel Shays — a farmer from Pelham, Massachusetts, who had fought at Lexington, Bunker Hill, Saratoga, and Stony Point — found himself unable to pay his taxes. He was not unusual. The Massachusetts state legislature, controlled by eastern commercial interests, had raised property taxes sharply after the war to pay down state debt. The taxes had to be paid in hard currency, which western farmers did not have. Foreclosures on western farms ran into the hundreds in 1786. When the courts came to seize the farms, Shays and several thousand western Massachusetts farmers — most of them Revolutionary War veterans — closed the courts down by force, then marched in January 1787 on the federal arsenal at Springfield. A state militia force under General Benjamin Lincoln, funded by Boston merchants in the absence of any federal capacity to respond, broke up the rebellion in February 1787.

George Washington, watching from Mount Vernon, read about Shays' Rebellion and concluded that the Articles of Confederation — the constitutional framework under which the new United States had operated since 1781 — were inadequate. He wrote to James Madison in November 1786: "Influence is no government." Three months later, the Confederation Congress authorized a convention in Philadelphia "for the sole and express purpose of revising the Articles of Confederation." The convention met from May 25 to September 17, 1787. It did not revise the Articles. It replaced them.

The document the Philadelphia Convention produced is the Constitution of the United States. It is the oldest written constitution still in force in any nation in the world. It is also, in its original 1787 text, a document that includes slavery in three specific clauses (the Three-Fifths Compromise, the Slave Trade Clause, the Fugitive Slave Clause) without using the word *slavery* once. The Constitution is, simultaneously, the founding document of American constitutional government and a sustained accommodation to the institution its drafters could not bring themselves to dissolve.

The puzzle of this chapter is what the Constitution did, what it postponed, and what it built into American politics that would not be settled until 1865 — and in important ways not until the present. The drafters thought they were ending a constitutional crisis. They were also writing the next century's constitutional crisis into the text.

## What the Articles failed at

The Articles of Confederation were drafted by the Second Continental Congress in 1776–77, approved by Congress in November 1777, and finally ratified by all thirteen states in March 1781. They created a "perpetual Union" with a single-house Congress in which each state had one vote, regardless of population. The federal government under the Articles had no power to tax (it could only request voluntary contributions from the states), no power to regulate interstate commerce, no executive branch, no federal judiciary, and no authority to enforce its decisions on the states. Amendments to the Articles required unanimous consent of all thirteen states. Any major change required Rhode Island and Delaware and Georgia and the ten other states to all agree.

This framework had been designed deliberately. The colonists had just fought a war against what they understood as an over-centralized imperial state with the power to tax them without their consent; they were not going to replace that state with a similarly strong central authority. The Articles' weak federal government was a feature, not a bug.

The features became bugs as the 1780s wore on. The federal government could not pay the war debt because it could not tax; the states paid (or did not pay) according to their own political dynamics, with Massachusetts taxing aggressively (which produced Shays' Rebellion) and Rhode Island defaulting altogether. The federal government could not regulate trade with foreign nations because each state set its own tariffs. The federal government could not respond to British or Spanish encroachments on the western frontier because it had no army to send. By 1786 the new nation had become difficult to govern and increasingly difficult to defend.

The Annapolis Convention of September 1786 — called by Virginia to address interstate commerce disputes — drew delegates from only five states. Alexander Hamilton, representing New York, used the Annapolis meeting to call for a larger convention in Philadelphia the following spring with a broader mandate. Shays' Rebellion, breaking out in the months between the Annapolis Convention and the planned Philadelphia meeting, gave the larger gathering the urgency that the September meeting had lacked.

## What happened in Philadelphia

Fifty-five delegates from twelve states attended the Constitutional Convention in Philadelphia from May 25 to September 17, 1787. (Rhode Island did not send delegates; New Hampshire's arrived in July.) The Convention operated under a strict rule of secrecy. The proceedings were not recorded for public release; the most extensive surviving record is James Madison's personal notes, which were not published until after his death in 1840. The Convention's debates would not become part of the public record for fifty years after the fact.

The Convention produced its document through a series of compromises among delegations whose interests diverged on several axes. Three of those axes mattered most.

**The large-state / small-state question.** The Virginia Plan, drafted by James Madison and presented by Edmund Randolph on May 29, proposed a strong national government with a bicameral legislature in which both houses would be apportioned by state population. The plan favored the large states (Virginia, Pennsylvania, Massachusetts, New York), which would have dominated both houses. Smaller states (Delaware, New Jersey, Connecticut) objected. The New Jersey Plan, proposed by William Paterson on June 15, proposed a unicameral legislature with one vote per state — essentially a strengthened version of the Articles. The Connecticut Compromise (also called the Great Compromise), proposed by Roger Sherman of Connecticut and adopted on July 16, produced the structure that survives: a House of Representatives apportioned by population (favoring large states) and a Senate with two senators per state regardless of size (favoring small states), with revenue bills required to originate in the House.

**The slave-state / free-state question.** The southern states (Virginia, the Carolinas, Georgia, with Maryland and Delaware as border cases) wanted enslaved people counted in determining state population for representation in the House. The northern states (Massachusetts, Pennsylvania, the rest) objected that the southerners were trying to count people they refused to recognize as people. The result was the Three-Fifths Compromise (Article I, Section 2): "Representatives and direct Taxes shall be apportioned among the several States... according to their respective Numbers, which shall be determined by adding to the whole Number of free Persons... three fifths of all other Persons." *Other Persons* meant enslaved people; the word *slavery* did not appear. The mathematical effect was substantial. In 1790, the first U.S. census counted 3.9 million people in the new nation, of whom approximately 700,000 were enslaved. Counting enslaved people at three-fifths gave the slave states approximately 18 percent more House seats and electoral votes than they would have had if enslaved people had not been counted at all. The Three-Fifths Compromise gave the slave states disproportionate political power for the next seventy years.

**The slave-trade question.** Several northern delegates wanted to give the federal government power to prohibit the importation of enslaved Africans. Georgia and South Carolina threatened to leave the Convention if the federal government were given that power immediately. The compromise (Article I, Section 9): "The Migration or Importation of such Persons as any of the States now existing shall think proper to admit, shall not be prohibited by the Congress prior to the Year one thousand eight hundred and eight." The Constitution forbade the federal government from regulating the slave trade for twenty years. The trade was, in fact, abolished by federal law effective January 1, 1808 — the earliest date the Constitution allowed.

**The fugitive-slave question.** Article IV, Section 2 required that "No Person held to Service or Labour in one State, under the Laws thereof, escaping into another, shall, in Consequence of any Law or Regulation therein, be discharged from such Service or Labour, but shall be delivered up on Claim of the Party to whom such Service or Labour may be due." Again, *slavery* unnamed. The clause required free states to return enslaved people who escaped from slave states. It made the federal compact a slave-catching compact. The Fugitive Slave Act of 1793, and the much harsher Fugitive Slave Act of 1850, were both passed under this clause's authority.

The three slavery-related clauses, taken together, did several things. They committed the new federal government to the protection of slavery where it existed. They gave the slave states political weight in federal politics proportional to (three-fifths of) their enslaved population, a population whose lack of political agency was the entire point. They postponed (but did not abolish) federal action on the slave trade. And they treated enslaved people as a national problem to be managed politically, not as a problem with a moral or constitutional answer the convention was prepared to give.

This is the structural feature of the Constitution that the historian Paul Finkelman has called "a covenant with death" — quoting William Lloyd Garrison's antebellum description. It is also the feature that makes the Constitution, in its original form, fundamentally different from the document its modern admirers sometimes describe. The Constitution was a compromise. The compromise was, in substantial part, with slavery.

## Federalist and Anti-Federalist

The Constitution, having been signed by thirty-nine of the delegates on September 17, 1787, then went to the states for ratification. Ratification required nine of the thirteen states. The ratification debates of 1787–88 are some of the richest political-theoretical literature in American history. The Federalists, supporting ratification, were led by James Madison, Alexander Hamilton, and John Jay, whose essays in support of the Constitution were published in New York newspapers in 1787–88 and collected as *The Federalist Papers* (sometimes simply *The Federalist*). The Anti-Federalists, opposing ratification or demanding amendments, included Patrick Henry of Virginia, George Mason of Virginia, Elbridge Gerry of Massachusetts, and many others whose essays and speeches have been collected under names like "Brutus" and "Federal Farmer."

The Anti-Federalists made arguments that, in hindsight, look more prescient than they are usually credited with being. They argued that the proposed federal government was too distant from ordinary citizens to be responsive to them — that the House of Representatives, with one member per 30,000 people, could not adequately represent local interests. They argued that the absence of a Bill of Rights left individual liberties unprotected from federal overreach. They argued that the federal taxing power, the federal judiciary, and the necessary-and-proper clause would expand over time to allow the federal government to do almost anything. They argued that a republic so geographically large could not maintain the civic virtue that republican government required.

The Federalist response was, in *Federalist 10* (Madison) and elsewhere, that a large republic was actually *safer* than a small one because the multiplicity of interests would prevent any single faction from achieving permanent dominance. Hamilton in *Federalist 78* defended the federal judiciary as the "least dangerous branch" because it had no power to enforce its decisions. Madison in *Federalist 51* argued that the structural separation of powers among three branches and the federal-state division would protect liberty by making any concentration of power difficult.

The Constitution was ratified, but only after the Federalists agreed to add a Bill of Rights through the amendment process the document itself provided. The first ten amendments, drafted by James Madison and ratified December 15, 1791, became part of the Constitution within four years of its original ratification. They responded to the Anti-Federalists' criticism by enumerating specific limits on federal power — protection of free speech, free assembly, free exercise of religion, the right to bear arms, freedom from unreasonable searches, due process and equal protection in criminal proceedings, the reservation to the states or the people of powers not delegated to the federal government.

The Bill of Rights, importantly, originally applied only to the federal government. The Supreme Court would not begin applying its protections to the states until the early twentieth century, through the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause — a development that, as Chapter 16 discusses, was partly a response to the *Slaughter-House* decision's gutting of the Privileges or Immunities Clause. The Bill of Rights as you encounter it in twenty-first-century American law is the product of more than a century of judicial development beyond what the 1791 amendments said on their face.

## The republic the founders thought they had built

The political theory the framers were operating within is worth naming, because it differs in important ways from how most modern Americans think about democracy.

The framers were *republicans*, not democrats. The distinction mattered to them. A republic, in eighteenth-century usage, was a state governed through elected representatives chosen by a property-holding citizenry that constituted a small fraction of the total population. A democracy, in the same usage, was a state governed by direct vote of the whole population — a system the framers associated with the chaotic Athenian democracy of the fifth century BCE, which they read as a cautionary tale about the dangers of unconstrained majority rule. James Madison, in *Federalist 10*, drew the contrast explicitly: "A pure democracy... can admit of no cure for the mischiefs of faction... A republic, by which I mean a government in which the scheme of representation takes place, opens a different prospect, and promises the cure for which we are seeking."

This conception had practical implications for who got to vote and who got to govern. The state constitutions of the 1770s and 1780s — and the federal Constitution that followed — restricted political participation through property qualifications, gender exclusions, racial exclusions, age requirements, residency requirements, and (in most states) religious tests for office-holding. The Federalist defense of the new framework treated these restrictions as features. The wisdom of the propertied and the educated would, in the framers' view, filter out the passions of the propertyless and the uneducated. The Senate, indirectly elected by state legislatures rather than directly by voters, was designed to be insulated from popular pressure. The Electoral College was designed to prevent the direct popular election of the president. The federal judiciary was designed to be permanently insulated from the electorate by lifetime appointments.

The decades after 1790 would see most of these structures liberalized — gradually expanded suffrage, direct election of senators (Seventeenth Amendment, 1913), and so on — but the original constitutional framework was designed by people who explicitly did not believe in majority rule as a governing principle. The transformation of the United States into something a modern reader would call a democracy was the work of generations, none of it inevitable, much of it contested.

## What the Revolution did not change

The Revolution and the Constitutional settlement of 1787–91 transformed American political institutions. They did much less to transform American social structures. Women remained legally under the doctrine of *coverture* — married women had no independent legal or economic existence; their husbands controlled their property, their wages, and their persons. Abigail Adams's famous March 1776 letter to her husband John ("Remember the Ladies... Do not put such unlimited power into the hands of the Husbands") received the reply she has been remembered for: John Adams laughed it off. Coverture would persist as the legal status of married women in most states for another century.

Free Black Americans saw very partial expansions of their political status. Some northern states allowed property-holding free Black men to vote; New Jersey's 1776 constitution allowed property-holding free women to vote until 1807. Most states excluded free Black men from voting and many excluded them from other civic activities. Enslaved Black Americans remained, in the slave states, in the legal status that had been built in the seventeenth century. The Revolution had not been a slave rebellion, and the Revolutionary settlement did not end slavery.

Indigenous nations lost almost everything the Revolution decided. The Treaty of Paris transferred sovereignty over the territory east of the Mississippi from Britain to the new United States — sovereignty over land that was, on the ground, occupied and politically controlled by Indigenous nations whose strategic interests the British had partially protected and the new American government did not. The treaty cycles, removals, and wars that followed were the working out of this transfer over the next century.

The Revolution and the Constitution made the United States into a politically independent republic. They did not make it a society of equals. The work of expanding the Revolution's claims to all of its people — work that began with the Declaration of Independence's "all men are created equal" — was not the founders' work to do. It was the work of the people whose lives the Constitution did not initially serve.
