# Chapter 14 — Troubled Times: The Tumultuous 1850s

*A decade in which each settlement made the next crisis worse.*

On the morning of May 22, 1856, Congressman Preston Brooks of South Carolina walked onto the floor of the United States Senate, found Massachusetts Senator Charles Sumner seated at his desk, and beat Sumner unconscious with a metal-tipped walking cane. The attack went on for about a minute. Brooks struck Sumner more than thirty times, breaking the cane on his head. When other senators attempted to intervene, a South Carolina congressman named Laurence Keitt held them off at gunpoint. Sumner, unable to free his legs from his desk (which was bolted to the floor), tried to escape under the desk; the violence of his struggle eventually wrenched the desk free of its bolts. He collapsed bleeding in the aisle.

Brooks was responding to a speech Sumner had given two days earlier, "The Crime Against Kansas," in which Sumner had condemned the Kansas-Nebraska Act and personally attacked Senator Andrew Butler of South Carolina (Brooks's cousin) in graphic language. Brooks framed his attack as a matter of personal and Southern honor. In the South, Brooks was celebrated. Citizens sent him replacement canes — many of them inscribed with phrases like "Hit Him Again." A House vote to expel him fell well short of the required two-thirds. Brooks resigned voluntarily, returned to South Carolina, and was reelected by his district in a special election. Sumner's injuries kept him out of the Senate for three years. His empty chair, draped in mourning cloth, became a Northern political symbol.

The caning of Sumner is one of those events whose specifics tell you what the 1850s had become. The previous generation of American senators had argued bitterly across sectional lines without resort to physical violence on the Senate floor. The institutional norms of the federal Senate had held through the most contentious fights of the 1830s and 1840s. In May 1856, those norms broke. A sitting senator was beaten nearly to death by a sitting congressman over a political speech. The political class on both sides treated the event as a confirmation of what they already suspected about the other side. Northern Republicans concluded that Southern political culture was incompatible with parliamentary democracy. Southern Democrats concluded that Northern abolitionist rhetoric had become an insult to Southern honor that could not be answered short of violence.

This chapter is about the decade in which the institutional capacity to resolve the slavery question through federal political institutions collapsed. The 1850s opened with the Compromise of 1850 — the last great sectional settlement the federal political institutions would produce. It closed with the 1860 election and the secession that began in December of that year. Each major event of the decade — the Compromise of 1850, the Fugitive Slave Act, the Kansas-Nebraska Act, the *Dred Scott* decision, the Lincoln-Douglas debates, John Brown's raid on Harpers Ferry, the 1860 election — narrowed the political space for compromise. By 1860 the political space had effectively closed.

The puzzle of this chapter is how a constitutional system that had been managing sectional conflict for seventy years lost the capacity to do so over the course of a decade. The compressed answer is that the slavery question was no longer compressible. Every attempt to settle it by political compromise raised new questions that the compromise itself could not address; every new question produced political mobilization that made the next compromise harder to construct.

## What the Compromise of 1850 traded

The Compromise of 1850 was a package of five separate pieces of legislation passed across the summer of 1850, after months of debate that occupied both chambers of Congress. The package addressed the territorial questions raised by the Mexican Cession. Senator Henry Clay drafted the original framework; after Clay's package failed to pass as a single bill, Senator Stephen Douglas of Illinois maneuvered the five components through separately by assembling different majorities for each. The five components:

1. California was admitted as a free state. This unbalanced the Senate by giving the free states a permanent one-state advantage.
2. The Mexican Cession territories of New Mexico and Utah were organized as territories without specifying their slave-state or free-state future; the question was deferred to *popular sovereignty* — the principle that the territory's settlers would decide for themselves at the moment of statehood.
3. The Texas–New Mexico boundary dispute was settled in Texas's favor in exchange for federal assumption of Texas's pre-statehood debt of $10 million.
4. The slave trade — though not slavery itself — was abolished in the District of Columbia.
5. A new Fugitive Slave Act required federal officials in free states to assist in the capture and return of enslaved people who had escaped from slave states, with substantial penalties for officials who failed to enforce the law and for civilians who interfered with enforcement.

The package was the last sectional compromise the federal political institutions produced. Each component traded something the other section wanted; the package together was acceptable to most of Congress because each side could point to specific provisions it had won. The price of the package, however, was paid in the years that followed.

The new Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 was the component that did the most political damage. The earlier Fugitive Slave Act of 1793 had been federal law but had been largely unenforced in the Northern states, which had passed personal-liberty laws restricting state officials' cooperation with slave-catchers and had often allowed local juries to free recaptured slaves on procedural grounds. The 1850 Act was much harsher. It required federal commissioners to issue certificates of return on summary process, denied alleged fugitives the right to testify on their own behalf, denied them the right to a jury trial, paid the commissioners more if they returned the alleged fugitive than if they freed him, and required federal marshals and ordinary citizens to assist in capture under penalty of fine.

The Act produced sustained Northern resistance. Federal courts in Boston, Syracuse, and other Northern cities heard fugitive-slave cases that became major political events. The Anthony Burns case in Boston in 1854 — in which federal authorities returned an escaped enslaved man named Anthony Burns to Virginia after a trial that occupied federal troops and most of Boston's police force — produced sustained Northern outrage and provoked the formation of vigilance committees in many Northern cities. Harriet Beecher Stowe's *Uncle Tom's Cabin*, published in 1852, drew much of its political energy from the Fugitive Slave Act; the novel sold 300,000 copies in its first year in the United States and became one of the most influential political-cultural artifacts of the antebellum period. The Act, designed to reassure Southern slaveholders that the federal government would protect their property rights, instead radicalized Northern opinion against slavery to an extent that no purely abolitionist organizing had accomplished. Within five years, Northern political organization around the slavery question had reorganized itself substantially around opposition to the Act and its implications.

## The Kansas-Nebraska Act and what it broke

The Kansas-Nebraska Act of May 1854 was the legislative move that broke the political-institutional capacity to manage the slavery question. The Act was sponsored by Stephen Douglas of Illinois, the same senator who had brokered the Compromise of 1850. Douglas wanted to organize the territory west of Iowa and Missouri so that a transcontinental railroad — the central infrastructure project of the 1850s — could be built across it. The territory in question lay above the latitude 36°30', which under the Missouri Compromise of 1820 had been the boundary above which slavery was prohibited. Douglas needed Southern Democratic support to pass the bill. The Southern Democrats demanded, as the price of their support, that the Missouri Compromise's slavery prohibition above 36°30' be repealed.

Douglas agreed. The Kansas-Nebraska Act repealed the Missouri Compromise's slavery-prohibition provision and applied popular sovereignty to the new territories — the principle that the settlers of each territory would decide for themselves whether slavery would be allowed at the moment of statehood. The Act passed both houses with Southern Democratic and some Northern Democratic support. President Franklin Pierce signed it in May 1854.

The Act's political effects were immediate and substantial. The Missouri Compromise had been the basic political framework for slavery's geographic extension for thirty-four years. Its repeal was, for Northern political opinion, a Southern aggressive move that broke an existing settlement. Northerners who had been Whigs or Democrats began leaving their parties. New political coalitions formed at the state level. By the fall of 1854, a new political party — the Republican Party — had organized itself in Michigan, Wisconsin, and several other Midwestern states; the party's central commitment was the prevention of slavery's extension into the territories. By 1856 the Republican Party was running a presidential candidate (John C. Frémont, who lost to Democrat James Buchanan); by 1858 it was the dominant political party in most of the Northern states; by 1860 it was running Abraham Lincoln, who won the presidency.

The Kansas territory itself became the site of armed civil war between proslavery and antislavery settlers. The contested elections of 1855 — in which thousands of Missouri "Border Ruffians" crossed into Kansas to vote illegally for proslavery candidates — produced a proslavery territorial legislature that the federal government recognized as legitimate. Antislavery settlers, organized by the New England Emigrant Aid Company and similar Northern groups, established their own competing territorial government at Topeka. Armed conflict between the two sides killed somewhere between 50 and 200 people across 1855–58 — the violence the contemporary press called "Bleeding Kansas." John Brown's first major act of antislavery violence — the Pottawatomie massacre of May 1856, in which Brown and his sons killed five proslavery settlers — happened in this context. The Kansas-Nebraska Act had been intended to settle the territorial question through popular sovereignty. It instead produced organized political violence that the federal government could not contain.

## *Dred Scott v. Sandford* and what the Court held

The Supreme Court's decision in *Dred Scott v. Sandford*, handed down on March 6, 1857 — two days after James Buchanan's inauguration — was an attempt to resolve the slavery-extension question through judicial fiat. Chief Justice Roger Taney's majority opinion held three things, each of which exceeded what the Court strictly needed to decide.

The first holding: Dred Scott himself was not free. Scott had been enslaved in Missouri; his enslaver had taken him to Illinois (a free state) and to the Wisconsin Territory (where slavery was prohibited under the Missouri Compromise); Scott had then been returned to Missouri and had sued for his freedom on the grounds that his residence in free territory had emancipated him. The Court held that the law of Missouri (the state of his current residence) governed his status, and that under Missouri law he remained enslaved. This holding alone would have decided the immediate case. The Court went substantially further.

The second holding: African Americans, whether free or enslaved, could not be citizens of the United States and therefore could not sue in federal court. Taney's opinion held that the framers of the Constitution had considered African-descended people to be "beings of an inferior order, and altogether unfit to associate with the white race, either in social or political relations; and so far inferior, that they had no rights which the white man was bound to respect." The framing was Taney's; it was widely understood at the time as a deliberate provocation. The holding meant that approximately 500,000 free African Americans in the United States — including, for example, Frederick Douglass — were not citizens of the country in which they lived.

The third holding: the Missouri Compromise of 1820 was unconstitutional. Congress, the Court held, did not have the constitutional authority to prohibit slavery in federal territories. The Fifth Amendment's protection of property rights extended to slaveholders' rights to bring their enslaved property into any federal territory. By implication, all federal restrictions on slavery's expansion — including the popular-sovereignty framework that Stephen Douglas had been advancing — were constitutionally questionable.

The third holding was particularly consequential. Taney was attempting to write a Southern political position into the Constitution as judicial doctrine. The opinion held that slavery had a constitutional right to expand into all federal territories regardless of the wishes of the settlers, the wishes of Congress, or the wishes of free-state political majorities. The opinion would have, if accepted, made slavery a permanent national institution rather than a regional one. The political coalition organizing under the Republican Party banner rejected the *Dred Scott* holding flatly. Abraham Lincoln, in his political speeches of 1858–60, treated the decision as an illegitimate exercise of judicial power that a Republican administration would seek to reverse — through new constitutional amendments, through reshaping the Court, through legislative action.

The *Dred Scott* decision is one of the most consequentially-wrong rulings in American Supreme Court history. The decision narrowed the political space for compromise. It also gave Northern political opposition a constitutional grievance that united Whigs, Democrats, and free-soilers across the partisan spectrum. By 1860, the Republican Party's position — that slavery should not be extended into the territories — was unconstitutional under *Dred Scott*. The implication was clear: the constitutional framework now required either Southern political acquiescence to a Northern political coalition that rejected *Dred Scott*, or Southern political resistance to that coalition through extra-constitutional means. The framework no longer accommodated the political differences.

## Brown, Lincoln, and the 1860 collapse

The final two years of the decade ran through three events that, taken together, made political compromise impossible.

**John Brown's raid on Harpers Ferry, October 16–18, 1859.** Brown, the antislavery militant who had already participated in the Kansas violence, led a force of twenty-one men (including five Black men, three of whom were free) into the federal armory at Harpers Ferry, Virginia. Brown intended to seize the armory's weapons, distribute them to enslaved people in the surrounding region, and initiate a general slave uprising. The plan failed. Local militia trapped Brown's force in the armory's engine house; federal troops under Colonel Robert E. Lee (a U.S. Army officer at the time) stormed the building on October 18, killing several of Brown's men and capturing Brown himself. Brown was tried for treason in Virginia state court, convicted in early November, and hanged on December 2, 1859.

Brown's raid had several political consequences. In the North, the raid produced a strange double reaction: most political leaders condemned the violence, but Brown's hanging produced an outpouring of public mourning, sermons, and political demonstrations that turned Brown into a martyr figure across substantial parts of Northern political culture. Henry David Thoreau called him "a transcendent moral hero." In the South, the raid produced something close to panic. Southern political leaders read the raid as evidence that the antislavery North was organizing armed resistance to slavery; they read the Northern public's response to Brown's hanging as evidence that the broader Northern political coalition supported armed antislavery action. Both readings were exaggerated, but both became politically operative. After Harpers Ferry, Southern political leaders began organizing seriously for the possibility of secession in the event of a Republican presidential victory.

**The 1860 presidential election.** The Democratic Party's convention in Charleston, South Carolina, in April 1860 split on the question of whether the party platform should commit to a federal slave code for the territories (the Southern Democratic position) or stand on Stephen Douglas's popular-sovereignty framework (the Northern Democratic position). The convention adjourned without nominating a candidate. The two factions held separate conventions later in the year and nominated separate candidates: Stephen Douglas for the Northern Democrats, John C. Breckinridge for the Southern Democrats. A fourth candidate, John Bell of Tennessee, ran on the Constitutional Union Party ticket, attempting to revive a cross-sectional party of moderate slaveholders and former Whigs. The Republicans nominated Abraham Lincoln.

The four-candidate election produced a result that, in any other format, would have been an electoral landslide. Lincoln received 39.8 percent of the popular vote — substantially less than a majority — but won 180 electoral votes (well over the 152 required) by sweeping the Northern states. He won no Southern state. In ten Southern states he received zero popular votes, because no Republican electors had been placed on the ballot. The election demonstrated, for the first time, that a sectional Northern political coalition could win the presidency without any Southern support at all. The 1860 election broke the cross-sectional partisan framework that had been the basis of American party politics since the 1830s.

**Secession.** South Carolina passed an ordinance of secession on December 20, 1860, six weeks after Lincoln's election. By February 1, 1861, six other Deep South states had followed: Mississippi, Florida, Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, and Texas. Delegates from the seven seceding states met in Montgomery, Alabama, in February 1861 and organized the Confederate States of America, electing Jefferson Davis of Mississippi as provisional president. Four more states (Virginia, Arkansas, Tennessee, North Carolina) would secede after the firing on Fort Sumter in April 1861. The political crisis that had been intensifying for a decade had now produced a political-military separation that the constitutional framework had not anticipated and could not, by its terms, resolve.
