# Chapter 29 — Contesting Futures: America in the 1960s

*The decade in which the federal government did the most expansive things it has ever done, on top of fighting a war that destroyed the political coalition that produced them.*

On July 2, 1964, in the East Room of the White House, President Lyndon Johnson signed the Civil Rights Act. He used approximately seventy-five ceremonial pens to make the signature, distributing them as souvenirs to civil-rights leaders, members of Congress, and federal officials who had been involved in the Act's passage. One pen went to Reverend Martin Luther King Jr., who had been at the Birmingham campaign sixteen months earlier and the March on Washington eleven months earlier. One went to Senator Hubert Humphrey, who had managed the Act on the Senate floor through a 75-day filibuster — the longest filibuster in Senate history — that Senator Richard Russell of Georgia had organized against the legislation. One went to Roy Wilkins of the NAACP. The Act prohibited racial discrimination in public accommodations, in employment, and in federally-funded programs. It substantially ended the legal infrastructure of Jim Crow segregation that had operated across the Southern states for the previous seven decades.

The Voting Rights Act, signed by Johnson on August 6, 1965, completed the dismantling of the formal legal infrastructure of Southern racial disfranchisement. The Act prohibited literacy tests, established federal supervision of voter registration in covered jurisdictions (substantially the former Confederate states), and produced an immediate substantial increase in Black voter registration across the South. Black voter registration in Mississippi rose from approximately 7 percent of eligible Black adults in 1965 to approximately 60 percent in 1968; comparable increases happened across the affected states. The political-electoral consequences of the Voting Rights Act would substantially shape American politics for the next half-century — including the Republican Party's "Southern Strategy" of the 1968 Nixon campaign and after, which substantially built Republican electoral majorities in the former Confederate states that had been Democratic for the previous century.

This chapter is about the 1960s — the decade in which the federal government did the most expansive social-policy work of any peacetime period in American history, alongside fighting the Vietnam War that substantially destroyed the political coalition that had produced the social-policy work. The puzzle of this chapter is what the 1960s actually were: the Great Society's substantive achievements (Medicare and Medicaid, the Civil Rights and Voting Rights Acts, the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965, the Higher Education Act, the federal Housing and Urban Development department, the National Endowment for the Arts and the Humanities, dozens of other major federal programs), the Vietnam War (which killed approximately 58,000 American military personnel and over 1 million Vietnamese, while substantially destroying American political consensus), the cultural-political upheavals (the civil-rights movement's transformation across 1965–68, the anti-war movement, the New Left, the women's-liberation movement, the gay-rights movement, the Black Power movement, the Chicano movement, the American Indian movement), and the political collapse at the decade's end (the 1968 election that produced Nixon's narrow victory; the political-cultural conflicts that would shape the next twenty years).

## The Great Society and the Civil Rights revolution

Lyndon Johnson became president on November 22, 1963, after the assassination of John F. Kennedy in Dallas. Johnson — the most experienced legislative tactician to occupy the White House in the twentieth century, having served as Senate Majority Leader before becoming Kennedy's vice president — substantially used Kennedy's death and his own electoral mandate from the 1964 landslide (Johnson won 61.1 percent of the popular vote against Republican Barry Goldwater, the most lopsided popular-vote margin since 1820) to push through Congress the most ambitious peacetime legislative program in American history.

The major legislation included:

**The Civil Rights Act of 1964** as described above.

**The Voting Rights Act of 1965** as described above.

**Medicare and Medicaid** (Social Security Amendments of 1965, July 30, 1965). Medicare provided federal health insurance for Americans 65 and older; Medicaid provided federal-state matching funds for health insurance for low-income Americans. The two programs together substantially extended American health-insurance coverage to populations that had been substantially excluded from private health-insurance markets. The programs continue operating; by 2025 they covered approximately 165 million Americans combined.

**The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965** (Hart-Celler Act, October 3, 1965) substantially revised the National Origins Act of 1924 that had structured American immigration for forty years. The new framework substantially abolished the racial-national quotas of the 1924 Act and replaced them with preference categories based on family reunification and skills. The Act substantially restructured American immigration patterns across the following half-century: post-1965 immigration substantially shifted from European to Asian, Latin American, African, and Middle Eastern origin populations. By 2020 approximately 14 percent of the U.S. population was foreign-born, the highest proportion since the 1910 census.

**The Higher Education Act of 1965** (November 8, 1965) established the federal financial-aid framework for higher education that has substantially operated since — Pell Grants, federal student loans, federal work-study, and substantial federal support for historically Black colleges and universities.

**The Department of Housing and Urban Development** (1965), the Department of Transportation (1966), the Public Broadcasting Act (creating the Corporation for Public Broadcasting, 1967), the National Endowment for the Arts and the National Endowment for the Humanities (1965), the federal Food Stamp Act (1964), the Economic Opportunity Act of 1964 (creating Head Start, VISTA, Job Corps, and other antipoverty programs), the Clean Air Act of 1963 and 1965, the Water Quality Act of 1965 — the federal-government activity across 1964–66 was substantially the largest peacetime legislative wave in American history.

The Great Society's substantive achievements were durable. The Civil Rights and Voting Rights Acts substantially ended the formal legal infrastructure of Southern segregation and disfranchisement. Medicare and Medicaid substantially extended health insurance to elderly and low-income Americans. The Immigration Act substantially restructured the country's demographic future. Most of the Great Society programs continue operating to the present and have been substantially extended by subsequent legislation.

The political coalition that passed the Great Society legislation was the New Deal Democratic coalition operating at the substantial peak of its electoral and legislative power. The 1964 election had produced a Democratic supermajority in both houses of Congress (68–32 in the Senate; 295–140 in the House) that was substantially larger than any subsequent congressional majority. The political conditions for the legislation's passage would not survive the decade.

## The Vietnam War

The Vietnam War substantially destroyed the political coalition that had produced the Great Society. The war ran for the United States from 1965 (when American combat forces began substantial operations) to 1973 (when American forces withdrew under the Paris Peace Accords). The American military commitment peaked at approximately 543,000 personnel in 1969. American military deaths were approximately 58,000; American wounded were approximately 304,000. Vietnamese deaths — combatants and civilians, North and South Vietnamese, on all sides — are estimated at between 1 and 3 million; the lower estimate is the conventional figure, but the upper estimates have substantial support in the historiographic literature.

**The path to American involvement.** The Vietnam War's origins ran back to the French colonial period and the Vietnamese independence movement that had been fighting French colonial control since the 1930s. The Geneva Accords of 1954, which had ended the French Indochina War, had divided Vietnam temporarily at the 17th parallel pending elections that were never held. American support for the South Vietnamese government, beginning under Eisenhower and substantially expanded under Kennedy, was framed as part of the broader containment policy. The Gulf of Tonkin Resolution of August 1964, passed by Congress in response to a contested incident involving American and North Vietnamese naval forces, gave Johnson substantial authority to commit American forces to combat. The decision to commit substantial American ground forces was made by the Johnson administration in spring–summer 1965; American combat operations expanded rapidly across 1965–68.

**The Tet Offensive.** The most consequential single event of the war was the Tet Offensive of January–February 1968. The North Vietnamese and Viet Cong forces launched coordinated attacks across South Vietnam, including in Saigon (where the American embassy compound was briefly penetrated) and in dozens of other major cities. The offensive was a tactical defeat for the North Vietnamese — American and South Vietnamese forces repelled the attacks and inflicted heavy casualties on the attackers — but a strategic victory in political-cultural terms. American media coverage of the Tet Offensive substantially destroyed American public confidence in the military leadership's claims that the war was being won. Walter Cronkite's CBS Evening News special of February 27, 1968, in which Cronkite (then the most-respected American television journalist) concluded that the war was unwinnable and that "we are mired in stalemate," was substantially the political-cultural turning point of American public opinion on the war.

**The political collapse.** President Johnson announced on March 31, 1968, that he would not seek reelection. The Democratic primary that followed was the most chaotic in modern American history: Senator Eugene McCarthy ran an anti-war campaign that nearly defeated Johnson in the New Hampshire primary; Senator Robert Kennedy entered the race after Johnson's withdrawal and was assassinated in Los Angeles on June 5, 1968 (after winning the California primary); Vice President Hubert Humphrey won the nomination at the Democratic National Convention in Chicago in August 1968 against the substantial political-electoral preference of the anti-war Democratic voters. The Chicago convention produced major political-cultural violence: Chicago police attacking anti-war demonstrators in the streets outside the convention hall while delegates inside the hall confronted Mayor Richard J. Daley's machine over the convention's procedures.

**The 1968 election** was won by Republican Richard Nixon, who ran a campaign that combined "law and order" appeals to white voters concerned about urban riots and anti-war protests with a "Southern strategy" that substantially shifted white Southern voters from Democratic to Republican allegiance. Nixon won 43.4 percent of the popular vote against Humphrey's 42.7 percent and George Wallace's third-party 13.5 percent (Wallace, the segregationist former Alabama governor, ran on the American Independent Party). The electoral pattern that emerged in 1968 — Republican strength in the South and the Plains, Democratic strength in the Northeast and the urban West Coast — would substantially structure American politics for the following half-century.

## The transformations the 1960s produced

The 1960s produced multiple cultural-political transformations that would shape American life across subsequent decades.

**The Civil Rights movement's transformation.** The early 1960s phase of the movement — substantially nonviolent direct action under the leadership of the SCLC, SNCC, CORE, and the NAACP — substantially achieved its legislative goals with the Civil Rights and Voting Rights Acts of 1964 and 1965. The movement's late-1960s phase shifted toward Black Power (Stokely Carmichael's articulation in summer 1966; the Black Panther Party's founding in Oakland in 1966; the Black Power conferences and organizational expansions across 1966–70). The transformation reflected the substantial limits of the earlier legislative achievements — the persistent economic inequality, the substantial Northern urban segregation that the Civil Rights Act had not addressed, the continued police violence against Black communities. Martin Luther King's assassination on April 4, 1968, in Memphis produced a final substantial transformation: the urban riots that followed his assassination (in 110 American cities; producing 39 deaths and substantial property destruction) substantially ended the political consensus around nonviolent direct action.

**The women's liberation movement.** The 1960s produced the second major American women's-rights movement, building on the postwar accumulation of women's higher education and labor-force participation. The President's Commission on the Status of Women (1961–63), the publication of Betty Friedan's *The Feminine Mystique* (1963), the inclusion of "sex" in the Civil Rights Act of 1964 (added on the House floor by a Southern Democratic congressman who hoped to defeat the bill — the inclusion remained when the bill passed), the founding of the National Organization for Women (NOW) in 1966, the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission's enforcement of sex-discrimination provisions, and the substantial radical-feminist organizing of the late 1960s (the Redstockings, the New York Radical Women, the Boston Combahee River Collective in 1974) substantially transformed American gender politics. The Equal Rights Amendment, which Congress passed in 1972 and sent to the states for ratification, failed to achieve the required state ratifications by the 1982 deadline.

**The gay-rights movement** emerged in its modern form with the Stonewall Riots of late June 1969, in which patrons of the Stonewall Inn in Greenwich Village resisted a police raid in ways that substantially institutionalized the resistance into a sustained political movement. The Gay Liberation Front (founded 1969), the Gay Activists Alliance (founded 1970), and the subsequent organizational infrastructure of gay-rights organizing substantially built the movement that would produce the legal-political changes of the following half-century.

**The Native American movement.** The American Indian Movement (founded 1968), the 1969–71 occupation of Alcatraz, the 1973 Wounded Knee occupation, the broader Red Power movement, and the federal Indian Self-Determination Act of 1975 substantially transformed federal Indigenous policy and Indigenous political organizing.

**The Chicano movement.** Cesar Chavez's United Farm Workers (founded 1962), the California grape boycott (1965–70), the Chicano civil-rights organizing across the Southwest, the founding of MEChA (1969) and the broader Chicano student and political organizing substantially built the Mexican-American political movement.

**The counterculture.** The cultural-political phenomena that emerged across the late 1960s — the hippie movement, the music and arts of the period (the San Francisco psychedelic scene; Woodstock in August 1969 with about 400,000 attendees; the substantial cultural production of the period), the broader skepticism of established institutions, the experimentation with sexuality and drugs and alternative lifestyles — substantially shaped subsequent American cultural development in ways that would continue across the following decades.

The cultural-political transformations of the 1960s were substantial and durable. The next chapter takes up the political backlash that the transformations produced.
