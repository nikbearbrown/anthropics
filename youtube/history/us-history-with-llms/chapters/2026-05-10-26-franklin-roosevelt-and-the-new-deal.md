# Chapter 26 — Franklin Roosevelt and the New Deal, 1932–1941

*A social safety net built around the institution its drafters could not undo.*

The Social Security Act, signed by President Franklin Roosevelt on August 14, 1935, was the most consequential piece of social legislation in American history. The Act created old-age insurance (the program that Americans still call "Social Security"), unemployment insurance, and federal grants to states for aid to dependent children, the blind, and the aged poor. It established the institutional framework of the American welfare state that would shape federal social policy for the next ninety years. By 2025 the Social Security old-age program would distribute approximately $1.5 trillion in annual benefits to about 75 million Americans.

The Act, as passed, excluded two categories of workers from old-age insurance coverage: agricultural workers and domestic workers. Together these two categories included approximately 65 percent of the Black labor force in 1935. Approximately 27 percent of the white labor force was also excluded, but the racial concentration of the exclusion was substantial: Black workers were excluded at roughly twice the rate of white workers.

The exclusions were not accidental. The historian Robert Lieberman's *Shifting the Color Line* (1998), the political-economic work of Ira Katznelson (*When Affirmative Action Was White*, 2005), and the recent quantitative scholarship of Larry DeWitt have substantially documented how the Social Security Act's exclusions were structured. The legislative debate on the Social Security Act in 1934–35 was conducted under the political constraint that Southern Democratic senators controlled, through their committee chairmanships and through the filibuster, the political fate of any federal legislation that affected the Southern racial-labor system. The Southern Democrats insisted on excluding agricultural and domestic workers — the two principal occupational categories of the Southern Black labor force — as the price of their support for the Act. The Roosevelt administration accepted the exclusions because it judged, probably correctly, that the alternative was no Social Security Act at all.

The pattern repeated across most major New Deal programs. The National Labor Relations Act of 1935 (the Wagner Act, which established federal protection for labor unionization) excluded agricultural and domestic workers. The Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 (which established minimum wage and maximum hour standards) initially excluded agricultural and domestic workers. The Federal Housing Administration's mortgage-insurance program, established under the National Housing Act of 1934, implemented racial-residential restrictions ("redlining" maps that designated predominantly Black neighborhoods as high-risk and excluded them from FHA mortgage insurance) that substantially structured American residential segregation patterns for the following half-century. The Tennessee Valley Authority operated under Jim Crow employment practices in the Southern states where its operations were concentrated. The Civilian Conservation Corps operated under segregated camps and substantially smaller Black enrollment per capita than white enrollment.

This is the New Deal as it actually operated. The chapter that follows describes the substantial transformation of American federal-government function that the New Deal accomplished, and the substantial racial exclusions that the transformation contained.

## The First Hundred Days

Roosevelt was inaugurated on March 4, 1933, three days after his fifty-first birthday and at the depths of the banking crisis described in the previous chapter. His inaugural address — "the only thing we have to fear is fear itself" — established the rhetorical framework of his administration. The substantive program would take shape over the following several months.

Roosevelt called Congress into special session and, across approximately one hundred days from March 9 through June 16, 1933, signed fifteen major pieces of legislation that substantially restructured American federal-government function. The First Hundred Days produced legislative output at a scale that no subsequent American administration would match.

**The Emergency Banking Act** (March 9, 1933) established federal authority to reopen banks under federal supervision and was the immediate response to the banking crisis. Roosevelt's March 12 fireside chat — the first of approximately thirty radio addresses he would give across his presidency — explained the Act in language ordinary depositors could understand. When the banks reopened on March 13, deposits substantially exceeded withdrawals. The banking crisis was resolved in roughly a week.

**The Glass-Steagall Banking Act** (June 16, 1933, the famous Glass-Steagall) separated commercial banking from investment banking, established the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC), and substantially restructured the American banking system. The FDIC, in particular, eliminated the bank-run dynamic that had destroyed the 1929–33 banking system. The separation of commercial from investment banking would remain in force until the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act of 1999 partially repealed it; the FDIC has operated continuously since 1934 and is one of the most institutionally successful federal creations of the twentieth century.

**The Securities Act** (May 27, 1933) and the **Securities Exchange Act** (June 6, 1934) established federal regulation of securities markets and created the Securities and Exchange Commission. The SEC's first chairman was Joseph P. Kennedy (the father of John F. Kennedy and a major Wall Street figure of the 1920s; Roosevelt reportedly chose Kennedy on the theory that "it takes a thief to catch a thief"). The SEC operates continuously to the present.

**The Agricultural Adjustment Act** (May 12, 1933) attempted to raise agricultural prices by paying farmers to reduce production. The Act paid Southern landowners to take cotton acreage out of production, and the landowners frequently used the payments to displace Black sharecroppers from the land rather than to share the federal payments with them. The Supreme Court struck down the AAA in *United States v. Butler* (1936); a revised Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938 reinstated the basic framework on a different constitutional basis.

**The National Industrial Recovery Act** (June 16, 1933) attempted to stabilize industrial prices and labor conditions through industry codes administered by a National Recovery Administration (NRA). The NRA was the most ambitious peacetime federal economic-planning institution in American history. The Supreme Court struck it down unanimously in *Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States* (1935).

**The Tennessee Valley Authority** (May 18, 1933) was a federal corporation created to develop the Tennessee River basin through dam construction, electric power generation, agricultural improvement, and economic development. The TVA was substantially the largest federal economic-development project ever undertaken in peacetime; it transformed the regional economy of the Tennessee River basin across the following half-century. The TVA continues operating as a federal corporation today.

**The Civilian Conservation Corps** (March 31, 1933) enrolled approximately 3 million young men across its nine years of operation (1933–42) in environmental-conservation work — reforestation, soil-erosion control, fire prevention, infrastructure construction in national parks and forests. The CCC was one of the most institutionally successful New Deal programs; its environmental legacy remains visible in the American national-park and national-forest systems.

**The Federal Emergency Relief Administration** (May 12, 1933) provided federal grants to states for emergency relief. The Harvard economist Harry Hopkins ran FERA across 1933–35 and would subsequently run the Works Progress Administration. FERA was eventually superseded by the larger and more administratively ambitious WPA in 1935.

These (and several other major Acts of the period) collectively transformed the federal government's role in the American economy. By the end of June 1933, the federal government was insuring bank deposits, regulating securities markets, paying agricultural producers, supervising industrial production codes, employing hundreds of thousands of workers directly, providing emergency relief through state grants, building dams and power infrastructure, and providing federal credit to multiple sectors. The federal government's substantive function in 1933 was substantially different from what it had been a year earlier.

## The Second New Deal and the political coalition

The 1934 midterm elections substantially expanded Democratic majorities in both houses of Congress — an unusual pattern for an incumbent administration's first midterm. The Roosevelt coalition was assembling. Across 1935 the administration pushed the second major wave of legislation that historians call the *Second New Deal*.

**The Social Security Act** (August 14, 1935) established the federal old-age insurance and unemployment-insurance programs described above.

**The National Labor Relations Act** (Wagner Act, July 5, 1935) established federal protection for labor-union organization, prohibited specific employer "unfair labor practices," and created the National Labor Relations Board to administer the framework. The Act produced one of the substantial industrial-organizing waves of American labor history; the Congress of Industrial Organizations (CIO), founded in 1935 and led by John L. Lewis of the United Mine Workers, organized industrial unions in steel, automobiles, rubber, electrical manufacturing, and other major industries across the late 1930s. The CIO's organizing was supported by the Wagner Act's federal framework and produced approximately 9 million unionized industrial workers by 1941.

**The Works Progress Administration** (April 8, 1935) was the largest federal employment program in American history. The WPA employed approximately 8.5 million Americans across its eight years of operation (1935–43) on public-works projects — road construction, building construction, bridge construction, park construction, federal art and theater projects (the WPA's Federal Writers' Project, Federal Theater Project, and Federal Art Project supported approximately 40,000 cultural workers and produced substantial cultural output, including the WPA-state guidebook series that remains a substantial American historical resource). The WPA's annual employment peak of approximately 3.4 million in 1938 represented about 7 percent of the entire American labor force.

**The Banking Act** (August 23, 1935) substantially restructured the Federal Reserve System, centralizing authority in the Federal Reserve Board in Washington (which had previously shared authority with the regional Federal Reserve Banks). The Banking Act of 1935 is the institutional framework of the modern Federal Reserve.

**The Revenue Act of 1935** (the "Wealth Tax Act") substantially increased federal income tax rates on high incomes and corporate income. The top marginal income-tax rate, which had been 25 percent in 1932, rose to 75 percent by 1936 under the new tax structure. The federal income tax of the New Deal period was substantially more redistributive than any subsequent American tax system.

The political coalition that the New Deal assembled — the "New Deal coalition" — became the dominant American electoral coalition for the following three and a half decades. The coalition included the Solid South (Southern white voters who had been Democratic since Reconstruction); urban Catholic and Jewish ethnic communities; organized labor (substantially expanded by the Wagner Act and the CIO); Northern Black voters (who began shifting to the Democratic Party in 1934–36, in one of the most significant electoral realignments of the twentieth century; the realignment would not be complete until the 1960s, when the southern white component of the coalition began moving to the Republican Party in response to civil-rights legislation); and substantial fractions of Northern white middle-class voters. The 1936 election, in which Roosevelt won 60.8 percent of the popular vote and 523 to 8 electoral votes against Republican Alf Landon, was the New Deal coalition's electoral demonstration.

## The Supreme Court and the court-packing crisis

The Supreme Court of 1933–37 was substantially opposed to the New Deal's constitutional framework. The Court's "Four Horsemen" — Justices James Clark McReynolds, George Sutherland, Willis Van Devanter, and Pierce Butler — were committed to a substantive-due-process doctrine that protected property rights and contractual freedom against federal economic regulation. The Four Horsemen, together with one additional swing justice, struck down major New Deal legislation across 1935–36: the NRA in *Schechter Poultry*; the AAA in *Butler*; the Bituminous Coal Conservation Act in *Carter v. Carter Coal Company* (1936); the New York minimum-wage law in *Morehead v. New York ex rel. Tipaldo* (1936).

Roosevelt's response, after his 1936 reelection, was the so-called "court-packing plan" of February 1937 — a proposed federal statute that would have allowed the president to appoint an additional Supreme Court justice for each sitting justice over 70 years old, up to a maximum total of 15 justices. The plan was constitutionally permissible (the Constitution does not specify the number of Supreme Court justices; the number had been changed by Congress multiple times in the nineteenth century) but politically disastrous. The plan generated substantial opposition from across the political spectrum, including from Democratic senators who would otherwise have been Roosevelt allies. The plan failed in the Senate Judiciary Committee in July 1937.

The court-packing plan failed legislatively but succeeded substantively. Across 1937 the Supreme Court substantially reversed its prior anti-New Deal positions. *West Coast Hotel Co. v. Parrish* (March 1937) upheld a state minimum-wage law, reversing *Morehead*. *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel* (April 1937) upheld the Wagner Act. *Helvering v. Davis* and *Steward Machine Co. v. Davis* (May 1937) upheld the Social Security Act. The shift — sometimes called "the switch in time that saved nine" — was a reversal of constitutional doctrine that substantially settled the constitutional questions that had been blocking the New Deal. The Court's composition changed across 1937–41 through deaths and retirements, and Roosevelt eventually appointed eight justices — a Court substantially supportive of the New Deal constitutional framework.

The substantive constitutional doctrine that emerged from 1937 — the federal government's authority under the commerce clause to regulate economic activity at a scale that the pre-1937 doctrine had not allowed — has been the dominant constitutional framework for the eight decades since. The court-packing crisis was, in retrospect, the political-constitutional turning point that secured the New Deal's substantive achievements.

## What the New Deal did not do

The New Deal's racial exclusions described above were not the only substantial gaps in the New Deal's achievement.

**Anti-lynching legislation.** Federal anti-lynching legislation — which the NAACP and other civil-rights organizations had been pushing for since the early 1920s — was proposed repeatedly across the New Deal years and failed repeatedly in the Senate, primarily through Southern Democratic filibuster. Roosevelt declined to push for the legislation despite his political alliance with the NAACP and the civil-rights organizations. Eleanor Roosevelt was substantially more publicly aligned with the anti-lynching movement than her husband; her resignation from the Daughters of the American Revolution in 1939 (after the DAR refused to allow the Black contralto Marian Anderson to perform at Constitution Hall, leading to the famous concert at the Lincoln Memorial) was one of the more public political-civil-rights actions of the period. The federal government's failure to address the post-Reconstruction Southern racial system across the New Deal years was a substantial failure of the broader political coalition.

**The persistence of Southern segregation.** The New Deal accepted, as the price of Southern Democratic support, the continued operation of the Southern Jim Crow system. New Deal programs operated in the Southern states on substantially segregated terms. The federal-government transformation that the New Deal accomplished did not extend to challenging the legal-segregation framework of the South. The Civil Rights movements of the 1950s and 1960s would do that work, building on the political-institutional infrastructure the New Deal had constructed.

**Universal health insurance.** Roosevelt initially considered including health insurance in the Social Security Act of 1935 and decided to drop it from the proposed legislation when his advisors warned that opposition from the American Medical Association would jeopardize the entire Act. The federal-government provision of health insurance would not be substantially attempted until Medicare and Medicaid in 1965; universal health insurance has remained politically unachieved.

**Substantive economic recovery.** The New Deal substantially reduced American unemployment from its 1933 peak of approximately 25 percent — but only to approximately 14 percent by 1937 and back up to approximately 19 percent in the 1937–38 recession that followed Roosevelt's attempt to reduce federal deficit spending. American unemployment did not return to pre-Depression levels until the wartime mobilization of 1941–42. The historiographic debate on what produced the eventual full recovery is contested — Keynesian economists generally attribute it to wartime deficit spending; monetarist economists attribute it to the gradual restoration of normal monetary conditions after the gold-standard abandonment; structural economists attribute it to the long-term industrial reorganization the period produced. The New Deal substantially mitigated the Depression's worst conditions but did not, by itself, produce full recovery.

## What the New Deal did

The substantive achievements of the New Deal were durable and structural.

The federal government became, across the 1930s, a substantively activist economic and social institution that the pre-1933 federal government had not been. The institutional infrastructure built across the New Deal — the SEC, the FDIC, the Social Security Administration, the Federal Housing Administration, the National Labor Relations Board, the Tennessee Valley Authority, the Federal Communications Commission (1934), the Civil Aeronautics Authority (1938), the Federal Crop Insurance Corporation (1938), and dozens of other agencies and programs — would remain operating institutions for the rest of the twentieth century and into the twenty-first.

The substantive constitutional framework — the federal government's authority under the commerce clause and the taxing-and-spending power to regulate economic activity and to provide social-welfare programs — would shape American federal-government function for the following eight decades. The Civil Rights Act of 1964, the Medicare and Medicaid Acts of 1965, the Clean Air Act of 1970, the Affordable Care Act of 2010, and most major federal legislation of the post-1937 period have operated under the constitutional framework the New Deal established.

The political coalition the New Deal assembled would dominate American electoral politics for the following three and a half decades. The New Deal Democratic coalition produced the substantial majorities that passed the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965; its electoral demographic was substantially the same as the demographic that elected Lyndon Johnson in 1964 and that produced the Great Society legislation Chapter 29 will discuss.

The cultural transformation the New Deal accomplished was substantial. The federal government became, in popular consciousness, an institution that ordinary Americans could expect to provide specific economic and social services — Social Security checks, unemployment compensation, federal mortgage insurance, federal infrastructure construction. The political-cultural relationship between the federal government and the American citizenry changed substantively across the 1930s; the relationship would not return to the pre-1933 pattern.
