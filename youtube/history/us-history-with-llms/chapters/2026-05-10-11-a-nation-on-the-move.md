# Chapter 11 — A Nation on the Move: Westward Expansion, 1800–1860

*A phrase invented to justify a single annexation, which became an ideology.*

In the July–August 1845 issue of the *United States Magazine and Democratic Review*, a Democratic Party journalist named John L. O'Sullivan published an unsigned editorial arguing for the annexation of Texas. The editorial defended annexation against critics who saw it as a war provocation against Mexico and an expansion of slavery. O'Sullivan was thirty-one years old, a New York editor who had founded the *Democratic Review* eight years earlier. The phrase he used in the editorial — buried in a long argument, not signaled as important by the author — read like this: "the fulfillment of our manifest destiny to overspread the continent allotted by Providence for the free development of our yearly multiplying millions."

The phrase did not catch fire immediately. O'Sullivan used it again in December 1845, in a different essay arguing for U.S. claims to the Oregon Country against British counterclaims; that essay was reprinted widely. Within a year *Manifest Destiny* had become the standard phrase in American political discourse for the proposition that the United States was destined by Providence to control the entire North American continent. Within five years it was the central rhetorical justification for the Mexican-American War (1846–48), the annexation of Texas (December 1845), the negotiated acquisition of the Oregon Country up to the 49th parallel (1846), and the seizure of more than half of Mexico's national territory (1848). Within fifteen years it had become so closely identified with the political-cultural project of American expansion that subsequent historians used it as the standard label for that project.

The phrase was an invention. It had a coinage date, a named author, and a specific political occasion. O'Sullivan was not articulating an existing American consensus; he was building one. The American conviction that the United States was destined to control the continent was something the political-cultural work of the 1840s and 1850s constructed, not something that pre-existed it. The phrase became an ideology because it served the political interests of the people deploying it.

This chapter is about that construction and what it accomplished. The Mexican-American War, by area the second-largest territorial acquisition in American history (after the Louisiana Purchase) and by far the largest acquired by direct military force, doubled the territory of the United States. The acquisition reignited the slavery question that the Missouri Compromise had attempted to settle in 1820. The political crisis the acquisition produced — the question of whether slavery would be allowed in the new territories — produced the breakdown of the second American party system over the 1850s and, by 1860, the breakdown of the federal union itself.

The puzzle of this chapter is how a journalistic phrase produced an empire, and how the empire produced a constitutional crisis.

## What "Manifest Destiny" hid

The phrase compressed three claims that, separated, are worth examining.

The first claim was theological. *Providence* — the Christian God's purposeful direction of historical events — had allotted the North American continent to the United States. The argument was a version of the older Puritan claim that the American settlement was divinely sanctioned. By the 1840s the religious framing had become casual rather than literal: it was a rhetorical move more than a working theology. But it did political work. The theological framing made expansion non-negotiable. It made resistance to expansion a refusal of divine purpose. The framing immunized the expansionist project against the kinds of moral and political objections that might otherwise have constrained it.

The second claim was demographic. The "yearly multiplying millions" — the rapidly growing white American population — *needed* the western land for settlement. The argument was that American population pressure made expansion an economic necessity. The argument had some empirical basis: the U.S. white population had grown from about 5 million in 1800 to about 17 million in 1840 to about 27 million in 1860, a growth rate that did create real demand for new agricultural land. But the demographic claim ignored that the territory in question was not empty. The American interior west of the Mississippi was occupied by Indigenous nations whose populations had survived the post-contact demographic catastrophe and whose territorial sovereignty was, in some cases, treaty-recognized. The northern Mexican territories (California, New Mexico, Arizona, Texas) were occupied by Mexican citizens whose property rights and political existence the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo would eventually nominally protect. "Multiplying millions" needed land. The land was, in the framing, *available* — meaning available to be seized.

The third claim, less explicit but doing the most political work, was racial. The "free development" of the multiplying millions referred specifically to the *white* population, and the phrase carried the assumption that white Americans were specifically suited — by temperament, by political institutions, by racial character — to govern the continent. The argument that emerged in the polemical literature of the 1840s and 1850s was that the Indigenous nations were destined to disappear (as they were doing under removal and disease pressure), that the Mexican population of the conquered territories was racially inferior and would be assimilated or excluded, and that the institution of slavery — for those expansionists who supported it — would be either expanded into the new territories or be checked by the simultaneous expansion of free-soil settlement. The phrase *Manifest Destiny* gave a religious-sounding cover to a racial-political program.

The three claims taken together explain how Manifest Destiny worked rhetorically. It connected economic interest (the demand for new agricultural land), political identity (white Anglo-American superiority), and religious legitimation (Providence) into a single phrase that could be deployed against any specific objection to specific expansion. The phrase compressed an argument that, fully articulated, would have been politically harder to make. The compression was the point.

## The Mexican-American War

The Mexican-American War of 1846–48 was a war of choice. The Polk administration manufactured the conditions for war in order to acquire territory that it could not have acquired peacefully. The historical record on this is unusually clear, because the principal actors left an extensive documentary trail and the political opposition at the time — including Whig politicians like Henry Clay and Abraham Lincoln — said so explicitly.

The proximate occasion was a disputed border. After Texas declared independence from Mexico in 1836 and was admitted to the United States in 1845, the two governments disagreed about where Texas ended. Mexico claimed the Nueces River as the boundary, which had been the historical border of the Spanish and Mexican province. The United States claimed the Rio Grande, about 150 miles south. The territory between the two rivers was sparsely populated and not strategically significant, but the principle of the dispute mattered. President James K. Polk, elected in 1844 on a platform of "reannexation of Texas" and "reoccupation of Oregon" (both phrasings implied that the territories were rightfully American and were merely being recovered), wanted the Rio Grande boundary. He also wanted California and New Mexico, which were Mexican provinces with substantial Mexican populations and small American settler presences.

In January 1846 Polk ordered General Zachary Taylor to march south to the Rio Grande and occupy the disputed territory. Taylor's force reached the Rio Grande in March. Mexican forces engaged American patrols on April 25, killing eleven American soldiers in a skirmish across what Mexico considered to be its own territory. Polk requested a declaration of war from Congress on May 11, citing the Mexican attack as evidence that "American blood has been spilled on American soil." Congress declared war on May 13 by overwhelming margins in both chambers.

Whig politicians who opposed the war made the constitutional argument that Polk had provoked the war by deliberately ordering American troops into disputed territory. Abraham Lincoln, then a freshman Whig congressman from Illinois, introduced what came to be called the "Spot Resolutions" in December 1847, demanding that the president identify the specific spot on which American blood had been shed and prove that the spot was on American soil rather than Mexican soil. Polk did not respond directly; the resolutions died in committee. Lincoln's anti-war position was politically costly in Illinois, and he chose not to seek reelection. The Whig opposition to the war was articulated and persistent, but it could not stop the war once it had been declared.

The war itself was militarily one-sided. The American forces under Winfield Scott captured Mexico City in September 1847 after a series of campaigns through northern Mexico and the central plateau. Mexico had no realistic capacity to defeat the American military. The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, signed February 2, 1848, ended the war and ceded to the United States the territory that became California, Nevada, Utah, most of Arizona and New Mexico, and parts of Colorado, Wyoming, Kansas, and Oklahoma — approximately 525,000 square miles, or about 55 percent of Mexico's national territory before the war. The United States paid Mexico $15 million in compensation, plus the assumption of $3.25 million in American citizens' claims against Mexico. The price was nominal relative to the territorial value. The Gadsden Purchase of 1853 acquired an additional 30,000 square miles in southern Arizona and New Mexico for $10 million.

The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo also nominally protected the rights of the approximately 80,000 Mexican citizens living in the ceded territory. Article VIII gave them the option of remaining in the territory or returning to Mexico; those who remained were to be granted U.S. citizenship "at the proper time." Article IX guaranteed their property rights, religious rights, and (in a phrase that would be litigated for decades) "all the rights of citizens of the United States."

The implementation of these protections was inconsistent in some cases and a fraud in others. Mexican-Americans in California faced systematic land dispossession through fraudulent land-title litigation, racial violence, and discriminatory state laws (the California Land Act of 1851 required Mexican landowners to prove their titles to a federal commission, a process so expensive and time-consuming that many lost their land regardless of the merits). Mexican-Americans in New Mexico had a longer political life — New Mexico would not be admitted as a state until 1912, partly because of its Hispanic majority — but faced parallel pressures. The promises of the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo were betrayed in practice across most of the conquered territories.

## What the war produced politically

The territorial acquisition reignited the slavery question. The Missouri Compromise of 1820 had established a policy for the existing Louisiana Purchase territory: slavery would be allowed south of the latitude 36°30' and prohibited north of it (with Missouri itself admitted as a slave state above the line as part of the compromise). The compromise had held for twenty-five years. The acquisition of the Mexican territory in 1848 produced new land that the 1820 compromise did not address. The question of whether the new territory would be open to slavery was the central political issue of the late 1840s and the entire 1850s.

The Wilmot Proviso was the first major moment in this fight. In August 1846, three months after the war had begun, Pennsylvania Democratic congressman David Wilmot introduced an amendment to a war-appropriations bill stipulating that slavery would be prohibited in any territory acquired from Mexico. The Wilmot Proviso passed the House of Representatives — and the pattern of the vote was extraordinarily revealing. The House voted along sectional lines rather than party lines: Northern Whigs and Northern Democrats together against Southern Whigs and Southern Democrats together. This was the first major federal vote on slavery that broke the second party system's traditional cross-sectional discipline. The Proviso failed in the Senate, where Southern overrepresentation (two senators per state regardless of population, with the same number of slave states as free states until 1850) gave the South a working veto.

The Wilmot Proviso was reintroduced repeatedly over the next several years and never passed in both houses. But the pattern it revealed — that the slavery-expansion question would split parties along sectional lines, that Northern voters would not consent indefinitely to Southern control of federal slavery policy — was the political pattern of the 1850s. The Whig Party would dissolve under this pressure during the 1850s. The Democratic Party would split during the 1860 presidential election. The Republican Party — organized in 1854 specifically around the platform of preventing slavery's extension into the territories — would emerge as the political vehicle for Northern free-soil sentiment.

Chapter 14 takes up the specific crises of the 1850s. What this chapter establishes is the political structure those crises operated in. The Mexican-American War produced a vast new territory whose status was disputed; the dispute had to be settled politically; the political institutions were not capable of settling it through compromise indefinitely.

## The forty-niners and what California became

The discovery of gold at Sutter's Mill in California's Sierra Nevada foothills in January 1848 — about nine days before the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo was signed — added a complication to the political crisis the Mexican Cession had created. News of the discovery reached the eastern United States in late 1848; the resulting gold rush brought approximately 80,000 people to California in 1849 and ultimately about 300,000 across the rush years. The Forty-Niners came from across the United States, from Mexico, from Chile, from China (substantial Chinese migration to California began in 1849–50), from Australia, and from Europe. They were predominantly young, male, and willing to take extreme risks for the chance at sudden wealth.

The population of California exploded. The pre-gold-rush Californio Mexican-American population of perhaps 14,000 was overwhelmed by hundreds of thousands of new arrivals. California applied for admission to the Union as a free state in 1850, less than two years after its acquisition from Mexico. This created a political problem: California had been entirely south of the Missouri Compromise line, where slavery had been permitted. Admitting California as a free state would break the parity of free and slave states in the Senate (15 each at that point) that had been carefully maintained.

The Compromise of 1850 — five separate pieces of legislation passed across the summer of 1850 — settled the immediate California question by admitting it as a free state, organized the rest of the Mexican Cession as two territories (New Mexico and Utah) with the slavery question deferred to popular sovereignty within each territory, abolished the slave trade (but not slavery itself) in the District of Columbia, settled a Texas boundary dispute, and — most consequentially — passed a new Fugitive Slave Act that required federal officials in free states to assist in the capture and return of escaped enslaved people. The 1850 Compromise was the last major sectional compromise the federal political institutions would produce. It satisfied almost no one and resolved almost nothing. The political crisis it deferred would explode again in 1854 with the Kansas-Nebraska Act.

The California Gold Rush also accelerated the racial-ethnic restructuring of the conquered territories. Chinese migrants to California faced violent discrimination from the earliest months of the rush; the California Foreign Miners' Tax of 1850 (and its 1852 successor) specifically targeted Chinese miners with prohibitive license fees. Indigenous people in California — the Yokuts, Pomo, Maidu, and others — faced something approaching deliberate extermination. The California Indigenous population, estimated at perhaps 150,000 in 1845, fell to about 30,000 by 1860. Some of this was disease; much of it was direct violence, including state-funded militia operations explicitly aimed at "extermination" (the language is from contemporary California state documents). California governor Peter Burnett, in his 1851 address to the state legislature, predicted that "a war of extermination will continue to be waged between the races until the Indian race becomes extinct." The state legislature appropriated funds for militia operations that pursued this policy. The historian Benjamin Madley, in *An American Genocide* (2016), has documented this period in California history under the legal framework of genocide; the evidence is substantial enough that the state of California formally apologized for the period in 2019.

The territorial acquisitions of the 1840s, in other words, produced not just a political crisis over slavery but a series of regional catastrophes for the populations conquered and displaced. The Manifest Destiny rhetoric obscured both. The constitutional crisis over slavery's extension would dominate national politics for the next decade. The regional catastrophes faded from national attention almost as soon as they had been accomplished.
