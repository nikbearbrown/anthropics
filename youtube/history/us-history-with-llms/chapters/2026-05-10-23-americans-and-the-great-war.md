# Chapter 23 — Americans and the Great War, 1914–1919

*A war the United States entered late and a peace it could not deliver.*

In June 1918, the U.S. Supreme Court heard oral arguments in *Schenck v. United States*, a case involving a Philadelphia socialist named Charles Schenck who had been convicted under the federal Espionage Act of 1917 for distributing leaflets that opposed the wartime draft. Schenck's leaflets had compared conscription to involuntary servitude, cited the Thirteenth Amendment, and urged readers to "assert your rights" against the draft. He was not accused of producing draft evasion or sabotage; he was accused of advocacy against the war. The Espionage Act, passed two months after the U.S. declaration of war, criminalized speech that "shall willfully cause or attempt to cause insubordination, disloyalty, mutiny, or refusal of duty, in the military or naval forces of the United States." Schenck had been sentenced to six months in prison.

Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes wrote the unanimous Court opinion upholding the conviction. The most-quoted phrase of Holmes's opinion — "the most stringent protection of free speech would not protect a man in falsely shouting fire in a theatre and causing a panic" — articulated the doctrine the case is remembered for: that the First Amendment did not protect speech that presented a *clear and present danger* of producing substantive evils Congress had a right to prevent. The doctrine governed federal free-speech law for the next half-century, until the *Brandenburg v. Ohio* (1969) decision substantially narrowed it.

Holmes himself would, six months after *Schenck*, write a dissent in *Abrams v. United States* (a parallel Espionage Act case involving Russian-Jewish immigrants who had distributed anti-war leaflets) in which he reversed himself on the practical operation of the clear-and-present-danger test. The 1919 Holmes dissent — with its image of the "free trade in ideas" as the appropriate constitutional framework for free speech — became one of the founding texts of twentieth-century American First Amendment doctrine. The shift between Holmes's June 1918 *Schenck* opinion and his November 1919 *Abrams* dissent reflects the rapid political-cultural change of the wartime and immediate postwar period.

This chapter is about that year and a half. The United States entered the First World War in April 1917 — almost three years after the war began in August 1914 — and was an active belligerent for about nineteen months until the armistice on November 11, 1918. The American military commitment was substantial (about 4 million men mobilized; about 117,000 American military deaths, of whom roughly half died from disease, primarily the 1918 influenza pandemic). The American military contribution was decisive in the late stages of the war (fresh American forces tipped the balance at the Second Battle of the Marne in summer 1918 and in the subsequent Hundred Days Offensive). The political-cultural consequences at home were substantially larger than the brief duration of American belligerence might suggest.

The puzzle of this chapter is what the war did to the United States in the brief period of active engagement and the longer period of post-war restructuring. The war produced both the largest civil-liberties contraction in American history to that point (the Espionage Act, the Sedition Act of 1918, the Palmer Raids of 1919–20, the systematic suppression of dissent across multiple categories) and substantial political-economic developments (the Great Migration's acceleration, the women's-suffrage movement's success, the rise of organized labor's wartime gains and post-war defeats). The war also produced a foreign-policy commitment that the country immediately repudiated: Wilson's vision of a League of Nations and a transformed international order was the centerpiece of his post-war political project, and the Senate rejected it.

## The path to American belligerence

The United States entered the war late and reluctantly. Wilson had been reelected in November 1916 on the campaign slogan "He Kept Us Out of War." The decision for American belligerence in spring 1917 was the product of specific events.

**Submarine warfare.** Germany's primary strategic problem in the war was the British naval blockade, which substantially restricted German imports of food, raw materials, and manufactured goods. Germany's strategic response was unrestricted submarine warfare against Allied (and neutral) shipping. The May 1915 sinking of the *Lusitania* (a British passenger liner traveling from New York to Liverpool, sunk off the Irish coast with 1,198 deaths including 128 Americans) produced substantial American protests. The August 1915 sinking of the *Arabic* and the March 1916 sinking of the *Sussex* produced subsequent diplomatic crises. Germany temporarily restricted its submarine warfare after the May 1916 *Sussex* Pledge. In January 1917, the German government decided to resume unrestricted submarine warfare, calculating that the disruption of American supplies to the Allies would force a German victory before American intervention could matter.

**The Zimmermann Telegram.** In January 1917, the German foreign minister Arthur Zimmermann sent a coded telegram to the German ambassador in Mexico City proposing that, if the United States entered the war, Mexico should join Germany as an ally; Germany would in turn support Mexican reconquest of Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona. The telegram was intercepted and decoded by British naval intelligence and passed to the Wilson administration in February 1917. The State Department released it to the press in late February. The publication produced substantial American public outrage and substantially eroded American resistance to entering the war.

**The Russian Revolution.** The February Revolution of 1917 (March in the Gregorian calendar) deposed the Russian Tsar. The new Russian Provisional Government allowed the Wilson administration to frame American entry into the war as a war for democracy against the autocracies (German, Austro-Hungarian, Ottoman, and previously Russian — the Russian removal from the autocratic side made the framing rhetorically cleaner). Wilson's April 2, 1917, war message to Congress framed American entry as the project of making "the world safe for democracy." Congress declared war on April 6, 1917 — 82–6 in the Senate, 373–50 in the House.

The combination of unrestricted submarine warfare, the Zimmermann telegram, and the Russian removal from the autocratic side made the political conditions for war substantially different in spring 1917 from what they had been a year earlier. Wilson's decision to seek war was not predetermined by these events but was made in their immediate context.

## What the home front became

The wartime political-cultural environment in the United States was substantially more restrictive of dissent than the war's actual military requirements would have suggested. The federal government's wartime mobilization included a sustained effort to suppress political opposition, criminalize anti-war speech, and enforce conformity in ways that went well beyond the legitimate operational needs of the war effort.

**The Espionage Act of June 1917** criminalized a wide range of speech and activity related to the war: interference with military recruitment, advocacy of refusal of military duty, "false reports" intended to interfere with military operations. The Act was applied broadly. Approximately 2,000 people were prosecuted under the Act during the war, of whom approximately 1,000 were convicted. The Socialist Party leader Eugene V. Debs, who had run for president four times by 1917, was prosecuted under the Act for a June 1918 anti-war speech in Canton, Ohio; he was sentenced to ten years in prison. The IWW (Industrial Workers of the World, the radical labor union) was substantially destroyed by federal prosecutions of its leaders under the Act. Most of the convictions involved speech, not espionage in any conventional sense.

**The Sedition Act of May 1918** extended the Espionage Act to criminalize "disloyal, profane, scurrilous, or abusive language" about the U.S. government, the Constitution, the flag, the military uniform, or the war effort. The Sedition Act was even broader than its 1798 predecessor (the Alien and Sedition Acts of the Adams administration) and was the most restrictive federal speech legislation in American history. It was repealed in 1920 but had produced thousands of additional prosecutions during its two years of operation.

**The Committee on Public Information** (the Creel Committee, run by the progressive journalist George Creel) was the federal propaganda organization that operated across the war. The CPI produced an enormous quantity of pro-war propaganda — speeches, pamphlets, films, posters, newspaper articles — and coordinated with private organizations to distribute it. The Committee's "Four-Minute Men" — about 75,000 volunteer speakers who gave short pro-war speeches at movie theaters, churches, schools, and civic events — was one of the largest American propaganda operations of the twentieth century. The Committee's framing of dissent as disloyalty contributed substantially to the wartime political-cultural environment in which Espionage and Sedition Act prosecutions could be politically defended.

**The American Protective League** was a quasi-official private organization that operated under loose Justice Department supervision to investigate suspected disloyalty in American communities. The APL had approximately 250,000 members at peak operation. It conducted illegal searches, opened private mail, conducted dragnets ("slacker raids") that swept up suspected draft evaders by the thousands, and substantially harassed German-American, Irish-American, and Socialist communities. The APL was dissolved after the war but its operations during the war substantially expanded the surveillance state of the period.

**The Sedition campaign against German-Americans** was particularly extensive. German-language newspapers were suppressed. German-language instruction in schools was banned in many states (the Supreme Court would strike down some of these laws in *Meyer v. Nebraska* in 1923). German cultural institutions — choral societies, Turner gymnastic clubs, the German theater — were substantially destroyed. German-American businesses were boycotted; some were attacked by mobs. The cumulative effect was the substantial destruction of German-American cultural distinctiveness, which had been a major feature of American urban and Midwestern life since the 1840s. After 1920, German-Americans were substantially assimilated into "white American" identity in ways that the antebellum and immediate post-Civil-War period would not have anticipated.

The wartime civil-liberties record is sometimes treated in subsequent accounts as a brief excess that was corrected after the war ended. The framing is partly true: the Sedition Act was repealed in 1920, the Palmer Raids ended in 1920 after the Justice Department's leadership lost public credibility, the immediate postwar reaction against wartime restrictions produced some legal-doctrinal correction (Holmes's *Abrams* dissent foreshadowed the eventual liberal interpretations of the First Amendment that would emerge in the 1960s). The framing is also partly wrong: the institutional infrastructure built during the war — the FBI's expanded political-surveillance capacities, the federal-state cooperation in suppressing dissent, the precedents established for restricting speech in national-security crises — would be available for use in subsequent moments (the Second Red Scare of 1947–57, the COINTELPRO operations of 1956–71). The wartime restrictions were not a brief excess but a substantial institutional precedent.

## The Great Migration and Black soldiers

The wartime mobilization accelerated developments that would become central to American political-cultural life for the rest of the twentieth century.

**The Great Migration** of African Americans from the rural South to the urban North, which had begun in the post-Civil-War decades, accelerated dramatically during the war. Northern industrial labor demand (created by the war's interruption of European immigration plus the mobilization of white American workers into military service) drew Black workers north in numbers the previous period had not produced. Approximately 500,000 Black Americans migrated north between 1916 and 1919; another 1.1 million migrated in the 1920s. The major destinations — Chicago, New York, Detroit, Pittsburgh, Philadelphia, Cleveland — would be transformed demographically and politically by the migration. Detroit's Black population grew from about 6,000 in 1910 to about 41,000 in 1920 to about 120,000 in 1930. Chicago's Black population grew from about 44,000 in 1910 to about 109,000 in 1920 to about 234,000 in 1930. The political institutions that would shape twentieth-century urban America — the Black-led labor organizations, the Northern Black political coalitions, the institutions of the Harlem Renaissance, the Black newspapers that became major national publications (the *Chicago Defender*, the *Pittsburgh Courier*, the *Baltimore Afro-American*) — were substantially built around the migration's demographic transformation.

**African-American soldiers** served in the U.S. military during the war in segregated units. About 380,000 Black Americans served, of whom about 200,000 served overseas. The most-distinguished Black combat unit was the 369th Infantry Regiment ("the Harlem Hellfighters"), which was attached to the French Fourth Army (the U.S. Army did not permit Black combat troops to fight alongside white American units) and which spent more time in combat than any other American unit. The 369th was awarded the French Croix de Guerre as a unit. The treatment of Black American soldiers by the American military command was substantially worse than their treatment by the French — a contrast that radicalized many Black veterans, including W.E.B. Du Bois, who had urged Black Americans to support the war on the grounds that wartime service would secure post-war civil-rights advances. (Du Bois publicly regretted this position after the war.)

**The Red Summer of 1919** was the postwar racial-political crisis that demonstrated the gap between the war's democratic rhetoric and the post-war American racial system. Between April and October 1919, approximately 25 major racial violence incidents — anti-Black riots, lynchings, attacks on Black communities — happened in American cities. The Chicago race riot of July–August 1919, triggered by the killing of a Black teenager who had swum across the unofficial racial line at a South Side beach, lasted seven days and killed 38 people (23 Black, 15 white) with hundreds injured and approximately 1,000 buildings destroyed. The Elaine, Arkansas massacre of October 1919, in which white mobs (with federal troop participation) killed somewhere between 100 and 800 Black sharecroppers who had been organizing for better economic terms, was the deadliest single incident of the Red Summer. The Tulsa Race Massacre of 1921, while technically after the Red Summer, was part of the same pattern.

The Red Summer's specific dynamic was that returning Black soldiers, having served in the war on the democratic-rhetoric framing Wilson had advanced, returned to an American racial system that had not changed (and in some respects had worsened during the war). The political and emotional gap produced substantial Black political organizing (the NAACP's anti-lynching campaign accelerated through the period; the Universal Negro Improvement Association under Marcus Garvey emerged as a major Black political organization in the immediate postwar period) and substantial white reaction (the second Klan, which had been founded in 1915 in Georgia, expanded enormously in the immediate postwar period, peaking at perhaps 4 to 5 million members in the mid-1920s). The racial-political conflict that would shape the rest of the twentieth century was substantially structured by the immediate postwar period.

## The 1918 influenza pandemic

The First World War was overlapped, in its closing months and immediate aftermath, by what was — until the COVID-19 pandemic of 2020 — the most consequential pandemic in modern history. The 1918 influenza ("Spanish flu," a label that misleadingly suggested Spanish origin; the virus's actual origin is contested but probably American or French) killed approximately 50 million people globally and approximately 675,000 Americans across roughly fifteen months. The American death toll was approximately six times the country's military death toll in the war itself.

The pandemic's worst phase in the United States ran from September 1918 through January 1919. The disease was unusual among influenza pandemics in that it killed young adults disproportionately — possibly because of an immune-system overreaction in healthy adults, possibly because older adults had partial immunity from prior exposure to a related virus. The U.S. Army was substantially affected; about half of American military deaths in the war were from disease, primarily the influenza pandemic, rather than from combat.

The American government's response to the pandemic was inconsistent. Some cities (St. Louis, San Francisco, Seattle) implemented closures, mask mandates, and other public-health measures that produced substantially better outcomes than cities that did not. Philadelphia, which held a Liberty Loan parade with 200,000 attendees on September 28, 1918, despite known influenza spread, suffered one of the highest urban death rates of the pandemic. The federal government's wartime priorities — maintaining troop deployment, maintaining war-production output, avoiding panics that would interfere with the war effort — substantially constrained the public-health response. The historical-memory question of why the 1918 pandemic was substantially elided from American public memory across the twentieth century is a related puzzle.

## The Versailles Settlement and the Senate

Wilson arrived in Paris in December 1918 to participate in the post-war international conference at Versailles. He brought with him the *Fourteen Points*, his January 1918 speech outlining his vision for the post-war settlement: open diplomacy, freedom of the seas, free trade, disarmament, adjustment of colonial claims, self-determination for the nationalities of Eastern Europe, and a League of Nations as a permanent international organization that would prevent future wars through collective security.

The Treaty of Versailles, signed in June 1919, incorporated some of the Fourteen Points (the League of Nations centrally; some adjustment of colonial claims) but substantially abandoned others. France and Britain, having borne most of the war's costs, were primarily concerned with imposing terms on Germany that would prevent future German aggression and that would compensate them for war damages. The Treaty's terms on Germany included substantial territorial losses, severe restrictions on the German military, and reparations of $33 billion (in 1921 dollars; equivalent to perhaps $500 billion today) that the German economy could not realistically pay. The "war guilt clause" (Article 231) required Germany to accept moral responsibility for the war. The terms produced the political-economic crisis in Germany across the 1920s that would contribute substantially to the rise of National Socialism.

Wilson's principal accomplishment at Versailles was the inclusion of the Covenant of the League of Nations as Part I of the Treaty. The League — modeled on Wilson's vision of a collective-security organization that would resolve international disputes through arbitration and mutual defense — was the centerpiece of his post-war program. Wilson believed the League would correct, in subsequent operation, the deficiencies of the Versailles settlement.

The Senate refused to ratify the Treaty. Senator Henry Cabot Lodge, the Republican leader of the Foreign Relations Committee, organized opposition based partly on substantive objections to the League's collective-security provisions (which, opponents argued, would commit the United States to participate in future European wars without congressional consent) and partly on Wilson's refusal to accept reservations to the Treaty that would have qualified American obligations. The Treaty failed in the Senate in November 1919 and again in March 1920. The United States never joined the League of Nations.

Wilson had suffered a major stroke in October 1919 while on a national speaking tour advocating for the Treaty. The stroke left him substantially incapacitated for the remainder of his term; his second wife Edith Galt Wilson is widely believed to have managed much of the presidency's business in the period between October 1919 and Wilson's leaving office in March 1921. The post-war political settlement Wilson had envisioned was thus negotiated by an incapacitated president and rejected by a Senate operating substantially independently of the executive.

The decision not to join the League of Nations had major consequences for the interwar period. The League operated for two decades but was substantially weakened by American absence; its inability to constrain Italian, Japanese, and German aggression in the 1930s contributed to the failure of the international system that produced the Second World War. The American return to "isolation" — a term Wilson supporters used to describe the post-war retreat from international engagement, though American commercial and financial involvement abroad continued and expanded across the 1920s — would be substantially reversed only by the Second World War.
