# Chapter 18 — Industrialization and the Rise of Big Business, 1870–1900

*The invention of the corporation, the trust, and the modern strike.*

In November 1873, Andrew Carnegie was traveling on a train in Pennsylvania when he saw a Bessemer converter operating at a steel mill. Carnegie was thirty-eight years old, a Scottish immigrant who had risen from telegraph messenger to senior official of the Pennsylvania Railroad, and a successful investor in bridge-building and iron production. The Bessemer process — invented in England in 1856 by Henry Bessemer — blew air through molten pig iron to remove impurities, converting iron into steel in a fraction of the time and at a fraction of the cost of older methods. Carnegie watched the converter, did some calculations, and decided that the future of American industry was steel made by the Bessemer process at a scale no one had yet attempted.

The Edgar Thomson Steel Works that Carnegie opened in Braddock, Pennsylvania, in 1875 was the first integrated Bessemer plant of its scale in the United States. Over the next twenty-five years, Carnegie expanded by vertical integration — buying the coal mines that supplied the coke, the iron mines that supplied the ore, the lake-shipping fleet that moved ore from the Mesabi Range to the Lake Erie ports, the rail lines that connected the mines to the mills, and the coke ovens that converted coal to coke. By 1900, Carnegie Steel was the largest steel producer in the world. Carnegie sold the company to J. Pierpont Morgan in 1901 for $480 million — equivalent to perhaps $15 billion in modern terms — making Carnegie one of the richest individuals in human history at that point. Morgan combined Carnegie's company with several others to form U.S. Steel, the first billion-dollar corporation in world history.

Carnegie's career is one of the more documented arcs of the period this chapter covers. It illustrates several patterns: the technical innovation that transformed entire industries, the organizational innovation (vertical integration) that allowed individual firms to dominate those industries, the financial innovation (the trust, the holding company, the corporate consolidation) that allowed control to be exercised across multiple firms, and the political-economic transformation that made the United States, by 1900, the world's largest industrial economy.

The puzzle of this chapter is what late-nineteenth-century industrialization actually was — as a technical revolution, as an organizational transformation, and as a political-economic restructuring of American society. The "Gilded Age" framing that Mark Twain coined as social satire in his 1873 novel of that name (co-authored with Charles Dudley Warner) became the conventional historical label for the period. The label captures something true about the era's wealth-and-poverty contrasts. It does not, by itself, describe the structural changes the period produced.

## What got invented

The technical innovations of the 1860s through the 1890s transformed American material life in ways that compare to the transformations of any other thirty-year period in human history.

The **Bessemer steel process** (commercialized in the United States in the 1860s and 1870s) reduced the cost of structural steel by roughly 80 percent. Steel became affordable for rail construction, building construction, machinery, ship-building, and ultimately the structural skeletons of skyscrapers. The first steel-frame skyscraper, the Home Insurance Building in Chicago, was completed in 1885; within fifteen years, steel-frame construction had become standard in urban building.

The **electric power industry** emerged in the 1880s. Thomas Edison's incandescent light bulb (1879) and Edison's Pearl Street generating station in New York (1882, the first commercial electric power plant) made electric lighting available in urban areas. The competition between direct current (Edison) and alternating current (Nikola Tesla and George Westinghouse) was decided in the 1890s in favor of AC, primarily because AC could be transmitted over long distances without prohibitive line losses. By 1900, electric streetlights had replaced gas lamps in most major American cities, and electric streetcars had replaced horse-drawn omnibuses on urban transit lines.

The **telephone** was patented by Alexander Graham Bell in 1876 (in one of the more contested patent disputes in U.S. history; Bell's patent was challenged by Elisha Gray and others, and the legal validation of Bell's priority required extensive litigation across several decades). The Bell Telephone Company, founded in 1877, became American Telephone and Telegraph (AT&T) in 1885; by 1900 the company had built telephone networks across most of the United States.

The **typewriter** (the Sholes and Glidden model, marketed by Remington from 1874) transformed office work. The standard QWERTY keyboard arrangement, designed to slow down typists so the mechanical typebars wouldn't jam, has persisted unchanged for 150 years. Office labor — clerks, stenographers, accountants — became one of the fastest-growing employment categories of the late nineteenth century, and the typewriter substantially feminized clerical labor (which had been male before the typewriter's spread).

The **chemical industry** — sulfuric acid production, fertilizers, dyes, eventually pharmaceuticals — grew rapidly across the late nineteenth century. Industrial chemistry was the foundation for the twentieth-century pharmaceutical and synthetic-materials industries.

The **railroad network**, which had been about 35,000 miles at the end of the Civil War, grew to over 200,000 miles by 1900. Air brakes (invented by George Westinghouse in 1869), automatic couplers, standard gauges, and the standardization of time zones (the railroads created the four U.S. time zones in 1883; Congress would not ratify them until 1918) transformed the rail system from a collection of regional networks into a national system. Railroad freight rates declined by approximately 60 percent between 1865 and 1900, making long-distance shipping of bulk commodities economical and enabling the further industrial integration the period produced.

The **petroleum industry** emerged from a niche regional product (lamp oil, lubricants) to a major industrial sector. John D. Rockefeller's Standard Oil Company, organized in Ohio in 1870, used a combination of horizontal integration (buying or destroying competing refineries) and vertical integration (controlling crude oil, refining, and distribution) to dominate the industry. By the 1880s, Standard Oil controlled approximately 90 percent of American oil refining. The internal-combustion engine that would transform petroleum's importance was still in its experimental phase (Karl Benz built the first practical automobile in Germany in 1885; Henry Ford's first Quadricycle was built in 1896), but the petroleum infrastructure that would fuel the twentieth-century automobile age was being built in the late nineteenth century around lamp-oil and lubricant markets.

The cumulative effect of these innovations was an enormous expansion of American material production. American real Gross Domestic Product grew at approximately 4 percent per year across the late nineteenth century — one of the most sustained growth rates in modern economic history. By 1900 the United States had the largest manufacturing economy in the world, surpassing Britain. American per-capita income was higher than any other country's at the start of the twentieth century. The country had transformed from an agricultural-export economy in 1865 to an industrial-manufacturing economy in 1900.

## What got organized

The organizational innovations of the period are at least as important as the technical ones, and they were substantially American innovations.

The **modern corporation** as a legal-organizational form was substantially developed in the late nineteenth-century United States. Earlier American businesses had been organized as partnerships, sole proprietorships, or limited-purpose corporations chartered for specific projects (most early American corporations were turnpike or canal companies). The late-nineteenth-century corporation — a permanent legal entity, limited-liability for shareholders, governed by a board of directors elected by shareholders, capable of issuing publicly-traded stock — became the standard organizational form for American business by 1900. The legal infrastructure for this was substantially built in New Jersey, which in 1888–89 passed corporate laws that allowed corporations chartered in New Jersey to operate in any state. New Jersey became the corporate-headquarters state of choice for late-nineteenth-century industrial corporations; Delaware would inherit this role in the early twentieth century after revising its own corporate code to be more business-friendly than New Jersey's.

The **trust** was an organizational form that Standard Oil pioneered in 1882. A trust was a legal arrangement in which the shareholders of multiple corporations transferred their stock to a board of trustees, who then operated the multiple corporations as a single integrated business. The Standard Oil Trust integrated dozens of nominally separate corporations into a single operating entity. The trust form allowed industrial consolidation that more limited corporate forms had not permitted. By the 1890s, trusts had been organized in sugar (the Sugar Trust), tobacco (the American Tobacco Company), whiskey (the Distillers' Securities Corporation), and several other industries. The Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890 was passed in response to the trust form, but its enforcement was minimal in the 1890s and did not produce significant anti-monopoly results until the early twentieth century.

The **holding company** replaced the trust as the dominant consolidation form after court decisions in the 1890s constrained the trust form. A holding company is a corporation that owns the stock of other corporations, exercising control through stock ownership rather than through the trustee-arrangement of the older trust form. The legal innovations of New Jersey's 1888–89 corporate law made the holding-company form practical. U.S. Steel (formed in 1901 by combining Carnegie Steel with several other major steel producers) was a holding-company consolidation, as were most major early-twentieth-century industrial combinations.

The **modern managerial corporation** — with separate departments for finance, marketing, production, and human resources, organized hierarchically with clear chains of command — was substantially developed in late-nineteenth-century American business. The historian Alfred Chandler's *The Visible Hand* (1977) traced this organizational innovation across the period. The American railroads were the first large enterprises that required sophisticated managerial coordination; the steel, oil, and electric-power industries adopted railroad management techniques. By 1900 the structure of a large American industrial corporation looked recognizably like a twentieth-century corporation.

The combined effect of these organizational innovations was the concentration of American industrial production into a small number of very large firms in most major industries. Standard Oil dominated petroleum. Carnegie (then U.S. Steel) dominated steel. American Tobacco dominated tobacco. The Sugar Trust dominated sugar refining. The railroad consolidations of the 1890s reduced the number of independent railroad systems substantially. By 1900, a small number of corporations controlled large fractions of American industrial output — the "Gilded Age" wealth concentration that the period is remembered for was partly the product of this organizational concentration.

## What got resisted

The industrial labor force that the new corporations employed faced working conditions that produced sustained labor conflict across the late nineteenth century.

**Working conditions** in industrial workplaces were extreme by modern measures. Steel mills operated continuously, with twelve-hour shifts in temperatures that frequently exceeded 130°F near the furnaces. Coal miners worked in unventilated shafts where black-lung disease and roof collapses produced high mortality. Garment workers in New York and other major cities worked in tenement sweatshops with minimal lighting, ventilation, or safety equipment. Children worked in textile mills, coal mines, and glass factories from age eight or nine. American industrial fatality rates around 1900 were the highest in the industrial world — approximately 35,000 workers killed each year, primarily in railroad operations, coal mining, and construction. Working hours averaged about 60 per week in manufacturing; substantially longer in some industries.

**Wages** were low and fell during economic depressions. The Panic of 1873 produced a depression that lasted into the late 1870s; the Panic of 1893 produced an even worse depression that lasted until about 1897. During the depression years, industrial unemployment ran as high as 18 percent, and workers who kept their jobs frequently saw wage cuts of 20 to 40 percent.

**The strike** as an organized labor tactic emerged in its modern form during this period. The Great Railroad Strike of 1877 — triggered by wage cuts on the Baltimore & Ohio Railroad and rapidly spreading to other railroad systems — was the first major national labor conflict in American history. Federal troops were called out in multiple cities to suppress the strikes; about 100 strikers and bystanders were killed. The Haymarket Affair in Chicago in May 1886 — a labor rally for the eight-hour workday at which a bomb was thrown at police, killing eight officers — produced a wave of anti-labor repression and the execution of four anarchists who were convicted on minimal evidence. The Homestead Strike of 1892, at Carnegie's Homestead steel works near Pittsburgh, ran for five months and involved a battle between strikers and 300 Pinkerton private detectives in which seven strikers and three Pinkertons were killed. The Pullman Strike of 1894, a sympathy strike across the railroad industry triggered by wage cuts at the Pullman Palace Car Company, was suppressed by federal troops under presidential order — over the objection of Illinois governor John Peter Altgeld, who argued that the federal intervention was unconstitutional. The Pullman Strike's leader, Eugene V. Debs, was imprisoned for six months and emerged from prison a socialist; he would later run for president as the Socialist Party candidate five times between 1900 and 1920.

**Labor unions** emerged but had limited success. The Knights of Labor (founded 1869, peaked at about 700,000 members in the mid-1880s, declined rapidly after Haymarket) attempted to organize all workers — skilled and unskilled, male and female, Black and white — into a single national labor organization. The Knights' broad-based organizing was undermined by internal conflicts, the loss of public support after Haymarket, and employer counter-organizing. The American Federation of Labor, founded in 1886 under Samuel Gompers, took a more restrictive approach: it organized craft workers (skilled tradesmen) into trade-specific unions and avoided the political-reform agenda of the Knights. The AFL was more durable but excluded most industrial workers (it generally did not organize unskilled factory labor) and most Black and women workers. The American Railway Union, founded by Eugene V. Debs in 1893, attempted a more inclusive industrial-unionism approach; its destruction in the Pullman Strike effectively ended industrial unionism in the United States until the rise of the Congress of Industrial Organizations (CIO) in the 1930s.

The labor history of the period produced two durable patterns. American organized labor remained substantially weaker than its European counterparts, with lower union-membership rates and less political influence. American labor conflict was substantially more violent than European labor conflict — the historian Philip Foner has documented that the United States had the highest level of labor-related deaths and injuries of any industrialized country across the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Both patterns were the result of specific political-legal choices: American courts in the late nineteenth century routinely issued injunctions against strikes (the Sherman Antitrust Act was used more effectively against labor unions than against industrial trusts in its first two decades), American police and federal troops were routinely deployed against strikers, and the American political system did not produce a major labor-aligned political party as the European systems did.

## What got bought

The flip side of the industrial transformation was the emergence of a modern American consumer culture.

Department stores transformed urban retail across the period. Macy's in New York (founded 1858), Wanamaker's in Philadelphia (1876), Marshall Field's in Chicago (1881) — these were enormous urban retail spaces with multiple floors, hundreds of employees, fixed prices, and an enormous range of merchandise. The department store's experience was new: customers could browse without intent to buy, could touch merchandise before purchasing, could return goods if dissatisfied. Department stores feminized urban retail — the workforce was substantially female (especially saleswomen on the lower-paid retail floors) and the customer base was substantially female-targeted.

Mail-order catalogs reached rural America. Montgomery Ward, founded in 1872, served rural customers with merchandise that could be ordered from a catalog and shipped by rail. Sears, Roebuck and Company, founded in 1886, became the dominant mail-order retailer; the Sears Catalog ("the consumer's bible") reached approximately 7 million households annually by 1908. Mail-order retail broke the local monopolies of rural general stores and gave rural consumers access to manufactured goods at substantially lower prices.

Mass-circulation magazines and the related advertising industry expanded substantially. *Ladies' Home Journal*, founded 1883, was the first American magazine to reach a million subscribers (in 1903). *The Saturday Evening Post* (revived by Cyrus Curtis in 1897) became a major mass-market periodical in the early twentieth century. The advertising industry — Procter and Gamble (founded 1837 but pioneered branded-product advertising in the late nineteenth century), the National Biscuit Company (which launched Uneeda biscuits in 1898 with the first major national advertising campaign for a packaged-food product) — transformed how American consumers encountered manufactured goods.

The cumulative effect was the emergence of a mass consumer market that would, across the early twentieth century, become the central engine of American economic growth. The transformation from a society in which most goods were produced by households or small workshops to a society in which most goods were mass-produced and mass-marketed by national corporations was substantially complete by 1900.
