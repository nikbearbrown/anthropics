# Chapter 17 — Go West, Young Man: Westward Expansion, 1840–1900

*The federal subsidy that produced the West and the violence it required.*

On December 29, 1890, a detachment of the U.S. Army Seventh Cavalry, the same regiment that had been destroyed at the Little Bighorn fourteen years earlier, surrounded a Lakota encampment at Wounded Knee Creek on the Pine Ridge Reservation in South Dakota. The Lakota — roughly 350 people, including about 230 women and children — were under the command of Spotted Elk (often called Big Foot), a chief who had brought his band to seek shelter at Pine Ridge after the Ghost Dance religious movement had alarmed the Army. The cavalry's orders were to disarm the encampment.

What happened next is contested in some specifics and clear in outline. The cavalry deployed four Hotchkiss artillery pieces on the surrounding ridges. As soldiers searched Lakota men for weapons, a shot was fired — by whom is disputed. The cavalry opened fire on the encampment with the Hotchkiss guns and with rifle volleys. Lakota men who had weapons returned fire briefly; most were unarmed. When the firing stopped, between 250 and 300 Lakota were dead, including about 200 women and children. About 25 soldiers were dead, many of them killed by their own cavalry's crossfire. The Army awarded approximately 20 Medals of Honor to soldiers for actions at Wounded Knee. The dead Lakota were buried in a mass grave; some of their bodies were stripped of clothing as souvenirs.

The Wounded Knee Massacre is conventionally treated as the closing event of the Indian Wars on the Great Plains. The Frederick Jackson Turner thesis — Turner's 1893 paper *The Significance of the Frontier in American History*, delivered at a meeting of the American Historical Association in Chicago — declared that the 1890 U.S. Census had shown the frontier "closed," with no more contiguous unsettled land available, and that the closing was the central fact of American history to date. The frontier-closing framing treats Wounded Knee as a tragic epilogue to a heroic westward expansion. The framing this chapter offers is different.

What happened in the American West between roughly 1840 and 1900 was the federally subsidized destruction of Indigenous political existence across a continent. The destruction was carried out through a combination of mechanisms: military force (the Army fought about 1,500 distinct engagements with Indigenous nations between 1860 and 1890), treaty cycles followed by treaty violations, deliberate destruction of the bison herds that supported Plains Indigenous economies, the Dawes Act of 1887 that systematically dismantled tribal landholding through forced allotment, and the assimilationist boarding-school system that removed children from their families to break cultural transmission. The end of this process was the Indigenous demographic and political nadir of the 1890s — the American Indigenous population in the contiguous United States had fallen to perhaps 250,000 by 1890, the lowest figure in the historical record.

The puzzle of this chapter is what the post-Civil-War westward expansion actually was. The popular framing centers homesteaders, pioneers, and the rugged individualism of "the frontier." The historical reality involves a federal-government project of land redistribution, infrastructure subsidy, and Indigenous removal at a scale that the popular framing systematically obscures.

## What the federal government built

The American West that emerged in the second half of the nineteenth century was substantially a federal-government construction. The mythology of the self-reliant pioneer obscures how much the post-Civil-War Western settlement depended on federal action.

The **Homestead Act** of May 1862 — passed by the wartime Congress with the Southern Democratic bloc absent through secession — offered 160-acre plots of federal land to any adult citizen (or any intending citizen) who would settle on the land, improve it, and live on it for five years. Filing fees were nominal. The Act eventually distributed approximately 270 million acres — about 10 percent of the total land area of the United States — to approximately 1.6 million homesteaders. Many homestead claims failed (the 160-acre plot was too small for sustainable agriculture in the arid West; many claims were abandoned within a few years), but the cumulative effect was the transfer of an enormous quantity of federal land to private ownership at no charge.

The **Pacific Railway Acts** of 1862, 1864, and 1866 authorized federal land grants and federal bond subsidies for the construction of transcontinental railroads. The Union Pacific (building west from Omaha) and the Central Pacific (building east from Sacramento) joined at Promontory Summit, Utah, on May 10, 1869, completing the first transcontinental rail line. The federal land grants to the railroads eventually totaled approximately 130 million acres — more land than the entire state of California. The railroads sold this land to settlers, used it as collateral for additional financing, and made enormous fortunes from the appreciation in value. Subsequent transcontinental routes (the Northern Pacific to Tacoma, completed 1883; the Santa Fe to Los Angeles, completed 1885; the Great Northern to Seattle, completed 1893) received similar federal subsidies. The transcontinental railroad system was substantially the largest federal infrastructure investment of the nineteenth century, and it was structurally a public-private partnership in which the federal government provided the land and most of the financing and private corporations took the profits.

The **Morrill Land-Grant Colleges Act** of 1862 provided federal land grants to the states for the establishment of public universities specializing in agriculture, mechanical arts, and military training. The Morrill Act eventually established or supported about 70 land-grant universities — including most of the major state university systems west of the Mississippi (the University of California, the University of Wisconsin, Iowa State, Texas A&M, Oregon State, and many others). These institutions transformed Western higher education and agricultural research over the following decades.

The **Newlands Reclamation Act** of 1902 — extending into the next chapter's period — established federal funding for irrigation projects in the arid West. The reclamation system that emerged across the twentieth century, including dam construction, irrigation canals, and federal water distribution, made large-scale agriculture possible in California's Central Valley, the Snake River basin, the Colorado River system, and much of the rest of the arid West.

The Western settlement that the popular narrative attributes to individual pioneer initiative depended on this federal infrastructure. The homesteaders rode on federally-subsidized railroads, settled on federally-distributed land, sent their children to federally-supported state universities, irrigated their crops with federally-funded water projects. The federal government was, for most of the post-Civil-War West, the central economic actor. The "rugged individualism" framing of Western settlement is, to a substantial degree, ideology projected backward onto a federal-government project.

## What the federal government destroyed

The other side of the federal Western project was the systematic destruction of Indigenous political existence across the continent.

**The Indian Wars.** The U.S. Army fought somewhere between 1,000 and 2,000 distinct engagements with Indigenous nations between 1865 and 1890, depending on what counts as an engagement. The Plains wars against the Lakota, Cheyenne, Arapaho, Comanche, and Kiowa lasted roughly from 1862 (the Dakota War in Minnesota) through 1890 (Wounded Knee). The Apache wars in the Southwest lasted from the 1860s through Geronimo's surrender in 1886. The Nez Perce war of 1877 (Chief Joseph's attempted flight to Canada) lasted three months and produced one of the most-quoted speeches in nineteenth-century American history ("I will fight no more forever"). The Modoc War of 1872–73 in northern California killed about 200 people; the Red River War of 1874–75 destroyed the southern Plains buffalo economy and forced the Comanche and Kiowa onto reservations.

The Army's tactical methods varied. Some engagements were conventional battles between Army units and Indigenous warriors. Many were attacks on encampments — the November 1864 Sand Creek Massacre in Colorado, in which Colorado militia under John Chivington attacked a Cheyenne and Arapaho village that had been promised federal protection, killing about 230 people of whom two-thirds were women and children, was perhaps the most notorious. The Washita River attack of November 1868 (George Armstrong Custer's predawn assault on a Cheyenne camp under Black Kettle, the same chief who had survived Sand Creek) followed similar tactics. The Marias Massacre of January 1870 (Army cavalry attacked a Blackfeet camp suffering from a smallpox outbreak, killing about 200) was a Montana version. The pattern was sufficiently common that the historian Ned Blackhawk and others have argued that it constitutes a systematic Army doctrine of attacking encampments rather than warriors.

**The destruction of the bison.** The North American bison population had been perhaps 30 million at the start of the nineteenth century. By 1890 it had been reduced to fewer than 1,000. The destruction was rapid (most of it happened in the 1870s and 1880s), commercial (professional buffalo hunters supplied bison hides for industrial belting, an important industrial commodity), and at least partly deliberate. General Philip Sheridan, commanding the Army in the West, told the Texas legislature in 1875 that buffalo hunters had "done more in the last two years, and will do more in the next year, to settle the vexed Indian question, than the entire regular army has done in the last thirty years." Sheridan recommended that "for the sake of lasting peace, let them kill, skin, and sell until the buffaloes are exterminated. Then your prairies can be covered with speckled cattle." The Army's tacit and sometimes explicit encouragement of the bison destruction was a deliberate part of the strategy to end Plains Indigenous resistance, because the bison was the central economic resource of Plains Indigenous nations.

**The Dawes Act.** The General Allotment Act of February 1887, sponsored by Senator Henry Dawes of Massachusetts, was the legislative architecture of the post-1880 federal Indigenous policy. The Act dismantled communally-held tribal land into individual allotments — 160 acres per head of household, 80 acres per single adult, 40 acres per child. The "surplus" tribal land remaining after allotment was opened to non-Indigenous settlement. Allotments were held in federal trust for 25 years, during which they could not be sold; after the trust period, the allottee received fee simple ownership and (under the 1906 Burke Act, accelerating this) U.S. citizenship.

The Dawes Act's purpose was assimilationist. Its supporters argued that converting tribal members into individual freehold farmers would dissolve tribal political structures and integrate Indigenous people into white American society. The actual operation of the Act was a massive transfer of land away from Indigenous ownership. Total tribal landholding in the United States fell from approximately 138 million acres in 1887 to approximately 48 million acres by 1934, when the Indian Reorganization Act formally ended the allotment policy. The two-thirds of tribal land lost during the allotment period was redistributed to non-Indigenous settlers, sold to non-Indigenous buyers after the trust period expired, or seized for non-payment of state property taxes. The Dawes Act was, in retrospect, one of the largest single land-redistribution programs in American history — a transfer of approximately 90 million acres from Indigenous to non-Indigenous ownership.

**The boarding-school system.** The federal Indian Schools — beginning with the Carlisle Indian Industrial School in Pennsylvania, founded in 1879 by Richard Henry Pratt — removed Indigenous children from their families and tribes to boarding schools where their hair was cut, their traditional clothing replaced with uniforms, their Indigenous languages punished, and their cultural practices forbidden. The schools' explicit philosophy, in Pratt's formulation, was "kill the Indian, save the man" — the cultural destruction of the Indigenous identity in the next generation through educational assimilation. The federal boarding-school system eventually enrolled approximately 100,000 Indigenous children across about 350 schools at peak operation. The mortality at some schools was substantial (the Carlisle cemetery contains the graves of more than 180 children who died at the school); the cultural damage was severe; the effects on subsequent generations of Indigenous communities are still being measured. The federal government formally apologized for the boarding-school system in 2021–22 under Interior Secretary Deb Haaland, who commissioned an investigation of the federal Indian Schools' history.

The combination of military force, bison destruction, land allotment, and boarding-school assimilation amounted to a coordinated federal program of Indigenous political and cultural destruction across roughly four decades. The Indigenous nadir of the 1890s was the result of the program working as designed.

## Other populations the West reshaped

The post-Civil-War Western settlement also reshaped two other populations whose stories deserve naming.

**Chinese immigrants.** Chinese migration to the United States began with the California Gold Rush of 1849; substantial numbers came across the 1850s through the 1870s, working in mining, railroad construction (the Central Pacific's labor force was 80–90 percent Chinese), and various service industries. By 1880 there were approximately 105,000 Chinese in the United States, almost all of them on the West Coast.

Chinese immigrants faced what may have been the most severe racial hostility directed at any immigrant group in American history. Local and state governments passed discriminatory laws — California's Foreign Miners' Tax of 1850 and 1852, the San Francisco municipal ordinances of the 1860s and 1870s, the California state constitution of 1879 (which forbade municipalities and corporations from employing Chinese workers). Racist mob violence killed Chinese residents in incidents at Rock Springs, Wyoming (1885; about 30 killed), Hells Canyon, Oregon (1887; about 34 killed), and dozens of smaller events. The federal **Chinese Exclusion Act** of May 1882 — the first major federal immigration restriction in American history — prohibited the immigration of Chinese laborers for ten years; it was renewed in 1892, made permanent in 1902, and not repealed until 1943 (when the wartime alliance with China made the Exclusion Act politically untenable). The Chinese Exclusion Act was the first time the federal government had explicitly used racial categories to restrict immigration. The framework it established would shape American immigration law for decades, eventually contributing to the National Origins Act of 1924 that more broadly restricted immigration along racial lines.

**Hispanic citizens.** The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo of 1848 had nominally protected the property and citizenship rights of the approximately 80,000 Mexican residents of the ceded territories. Implementation of these protections was inconsistent and often fraudulent. In California, the Land Act of 1851 required Mexican landowners to prove their titles before a federal commission; the process was expensive, lengthy, and structured in ways that favored Anglo-American claimants. Many Mexican Californians lost their land through court rulings, through the cost of litigation, or through forced sale to pay legal expenses. The historian Tomás Almaguer's *Racial Fault Lines* (1994) documents the systematic dispossession of Mexican Californians across the second half of the nineteenth century.

In New Mexico, which had a larger and more politically organized Mexican-American population, the dispossession was slower and less complete. New Mexico would not be admitted as a state until 1912, partly because of its Hispanic majority — the federal political system found it politically uncomfortable to admit a state whose voting electorate would be majority Hispanic. The Mexican-American population of the Southwest was, by 1900, a politically marginalized minority on land their families had occupied for generations.

The Western settlement of the 1840–1900 period was a project of population replacement. It did not, in most places, eliminate the populations it displaced — but it dispossessed them of their land, their political institutions, and (in important cases) their cultural transmission. The American West that emerged in the twentieth century was a different place from the West of 1840 in fundamental ways. Most of the difference was the result of federal policy implemented over the intervening sixty years.
