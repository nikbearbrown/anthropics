# Chapter 13 — Antebellum Idealism and Reform Impulses, 1820–1860

*Two movements that emerged from the same religious revival and shaped each other.*

In July 1848, about three hundred people gathered at the Wesleyan Methodist Chapel in Seneca Falls, New York, for a convention that the organizers had advertised in the *Seneca County Courier* the week before. The convention had been organized over tea at the home of Jane Hunt, a Quaker, ten days earlier; the conveners were Lucretia Mott, Mary Ann McClintock, Martha Coffin Wright, Jane Hunt herself, and Elizabeth Cady Stanton. The five women were all veterans of the abolitionist movement; the convention they organized has become, in subsequent historical memory, the founding event of American organized feminism.

The Declaration of Sentiments that Elizabeth Cady Stanton drafted for the convention deliberately echoed the Declaration of Independence. "When in the course of human events," it began, "it becomes necessary for one portion of the family of man to assume among the people of the earth a position different from that which they have hitherto occupied... a decent respect to the opinions of mankind requires that they should declare the causes that impel them to such a course." The substitution Stanton made was the central rhetorical move of the document. Where the 1776 Declaration had listed the grievances of the American colonies against the British crown, the 1848 Declaration listed the grievances of women against the laws and customs that constrained them. "He has never permitted her to exercise her inalienable right to the elective franchise," it began. "He has compelled her to submit to laws, in the formation of which she had no voice... He has made her, if married, in the eye of the law, civilly dead..."

The Declaration of Sentiments was adopted by the Seneca Falls convention. The convention also passed eleven resolutions on specific reform measures. Ten passed unanimously; one — the resolution calling for women's suffrage — passed by a narrow margin after extensive debate. The Seneca Falls convention is remembered for many things, but the central historical fact is this: it was organized by women who had been working in the abolitionist movement for at least a decade, who had been excluded from the leadership of that movement on grounds of their sex, and who had decided to convene a separate political movement to address the constraints on women's political and personal lives.

This chapter is about the antebellum reform movements that produced Seneca Falls and the broader political-cultural transformation it represented. The reforms were various: abolitionism, women's rights, temperance, public-school expansion, prison reform, mental-health reform, dietary reform, and a constellation of utopian-community experiments that attempted to remake American social organization at the small-community scale. Many of them shared an institutional and intellectual origin in the Second Great Awakening — the Protestant revival movement that swept the American Northeast from the 1820s through the 1840s. All of them ran into the structural fact that the institutions they sought to reform did not want to be reformed.

The puzzle of this chapter is how a religious revival produced a political opposition movement, and what the relationships between the various reforms reveal about the structure of antebellum American politics.

## The Second Great Awakening as engine

The Second Great Awakening was a sustained Protestant religious revival that ran from roughly 1800 through the 1840s. It began in the rural revival meetings of the Kentucky-Tennessee frontier — the Cane Ridge revival of 1801, where perhaps 20,000 people attended a week-long camp meeting and witnessed conversion experiences that the participants described as bodily, immediate, and overwhelming — and spread across the Northeast and Midwest through the 1820s and 1830s. The Awakening's central theological move was the rejection of the older Calvinist doctrine of predestination, under which the saved had been chosen by God before birth and the unsaved were beyond salvation. The Awakening's preachers — most prominently Charles Grandison Finney in the upstate New York region called the "Burned-Over District" for the intensity of revivalism there — taught that salvation was available to anyone who chose it, and that the choice was an act of will the individual sinner could make.

The theological shift had political consequences. If salvation was available through individual choice, and if Christian commitment required not just personal piety but active engagement with the world's sin, then social reform became part of religious practice. The "perfectionist" theology of the Awakening — the doctrine that human beings could, through grace and effort, achieve substantial moral perfection — produced a generation of evangelical Protestants who understood political reform as religious work. The major American reform movements of the 1830s and 1840s drew their organizational base, their volunteer labor, their moral language, and much of their leadership from the evangelical churches the Awakening had energized.

The geographic concentration was striking. The Burned-Over District of upstate New York — the region between the Adirondacks and Lake Erie that had been intensively revivalist for decades — produced a disproportionate share of antebellum reform leadership: the Mormons (Joseph Smith founded the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints in Palmyra, New York in 1830), the Spiritualists (the Fox sisters of Hydesville claimed to communicate with spirits in 1848), the Oneida Community (John Humphrey Noyes's experimental commune founded in 1848 in the Burned-Over District itself), and a large fraction of the antebellum abolitionist and women's-rights leadership. The geographic pattern is partly explained by the Erie Canal — the 1825 canal brought economic transformation, immigration, and cultural exchange to the region in ways that destabilized older communities and produced the conditions for religious and social experimentation. The Second Great Awakening was both a cause and a symptom of the region's transformation.

The reform movements that grew out of the Awakening shared several features. They were organized through evangelical Protestant networks — churches, missionary societies, denominational publications, revival camp meetings. They were predominantly Northern, with much weaker presence in the slave-state South (the Southern wing of evangelical Protestantism remained largely uninvolved in reform organizing through the antebellum decades). They drew disproportionately on female volunteer labor — women constituted a majority of the membership of most major reform organizations even when the leadership was male. They produced an enormous quantity of printed material — pamphlets, books, newspapers, periodicals — that circulated through the same evangelical networks. And they reinforced each other: the volunteers who organized temperance societies often also organized abolitionist societies; the women who became prominent in women's-rights organizing were typically veterans of abolitionist work first.

## Abolitionism and what it had to fight

The abolitionist movement that emerged in the 1830s was a transformation of an older anti-slavery tradition. The earlier tradition — represented by the American Colonization Society, founded in 1816 — had treated slavery as a problem to be managed through the gradual removal of free Black people from the United States to colonies in Africa. The Colonization Society included substantial Southern slaveholder support (Thomas Jefferson, James Madison, and Henry Clay all endorsed colonization); its program assumed that the United States could not become a multiracial society of equals and that the removal of free Black people was a humane solution to the racial problem. The Society's main practical achievement was the founding of Liberia on the West African coast in 1822, which received approximately 13,000 emigrants from the United States across the antebellum decades.

The immediate-abolitionist movement that emerged in the early 1830s rejected colonization explicitly. William Lloyd Garrison's *The Liberator*, first published in Boston on January 1, 1831, made the immediate-abolitionist position uncompromising. Garrison demanded the immediate emancipation of all enslaved people, without compensation to slaveholders and without removal to Africa. In his first issue he wrote: "On this subject, I do not wish to think, or speak, or write, with moderation... I am in earnest — I will not equivocate — I will not excuse — I will not retreat a single inch — AND I WILL BE HEARD." Garrison and the American Anti-Slavery Society he co-founded in 1833 mounted a sustained moral and political campaign against slavery that, by the 1850s, had become one of the central political forces in Northern public life.

The abolitionist movement's central tactic was *moral suasion* — the attempt to convince slaveholders (and the broader American public) that slavery was a moral wrong that they should abandon by their own choice. The tactic operated through pamphlets, newspapers, public lectures, and petition campaigns directed at Congress. The American Anti-Slavery Society's 1837–38 petition campaign collected over 400,000 petitions with over 2 million signatures (many people signed multiple petitions) demanding congressional action against slavery; the campaign was sustained by an organizing network that operated across the Northern states.

The abolitionist movement was not popular in the antebellum North in the 1830s. It was, in many places, actively detested. Northern mobs attacked abolitionist meetings and abolitionist printers throughout the decade. In 1835, a Boston mob led by "gentlemen of property and standing" dragged Garrison through the streets with a rope around his neck. In 1837, a mob in Alton, Illinois, murdered the abolitionist editor Elijah Lovejoy as he attempted to defend his printing press. The U.S. House of Representatives, in 1836, passed the so-called "gag rule" that tabled all abolitionist petitions without consideration; the gag rule remained in effect until 1844, when John Quincy Adams (a former president serving as a Massachusetts congressman in his post-presidential career) succeeded in repealing it.

The internal divisions in the abolitionist movement were substantial and shaped the women's-rights movement that emerged from it. Garrison's wing of the movement — radical, refusing to participate in the federal government on the grounds that the Constitution was a "covenant with death" because of its slavery clauses, supportive of women's full participation in abolitionist organizing — divided in 1840 from a more moderate wing led by Lewis Tappan that wanted to organize within the political-electoral system and that opposed women's leadership in the movement on conventional gender-role grounds. The split produced two organizations: the American Anti-Slavery Society (Garrisonian) and the American and Foreign Anti-Slavery Society (Tappan). The political-electoral wing of antislavery eventually produced the Liberty Party (1840), the Free Soil Party (1848), and ultimately the Republican Party (1854) that ran Lincoln in 1860.

The Black abolitionist movement, often treated as auxiliary in older accounts, was actually central to the antislavery organizing of the antebellum period. Frederick Douglass — born into slavery in Maryland around 1818, escaped to freedom in 1838, became the most influential Black orator and editor in nineteenth-century America — founded the *North Star* in 1847 and became, by the 1850s, the most widely known abolitionist in the country. Sojourner Truth, Harriet Tubman, Henry Highland Garnet, Martin Delany, William Wells Brown, and others built Black abolitionist organizations, lectured to white audiences, organized escape routes (the Underground Railroad), and provided the moral testimony that white abolitionist organizing depended on. The historian Manisha Sinha's *The Slave's Cause* (2016) recovers the centrality of Black political organizing to the abolitionist movement; earlier accounts had often treated the movement as primarily a white moral-reform project. The Black abolitionists were not auxiliaries. They were the movement's center of gravity on most of the questions that mattered most.

## How abolitionism produced organized feminism

The 1840 split in the abolitionist movement was triggered partly by the question of women's leadership. Lucretia Mott, a Pennsylvania Quaker and one of the founders of the Philadelphia Female Anti-Slavery Society, had been chosen as a delegate to the World Anti-Slavery Convention in London in June 1840. When she arrived, she and the other female delegates were refused seats on the convention floor on grounds of their sex; they were seated in a curtained gallery and not allowed to speak. Elizabeth Cady Stanton, also in London on her honeymoon (her husband Henry Stanton was an American abolitionist delegate), met Mott in the gallery. The two women spent the convention talking. They agreed that the political constraints on women's public action required a separate organized movement. Eight years later they organized the Seneca Falls convention.

The 1848 convention's Declaration of Sentiments listed grievances that drew on the legal-political reality of antebellum women's status. Married women in most American states were under the common-law doctrine of *coverture*, under which a married woman had no independent legal existence — she could not own property in her own name, could not sign contracts, could not retain her own wages (her husband was legally entitled to her earnings), could not initiate legal action, and could not divorce except under extremely restricted conditions. The legal status of unmarried adult women was somewhat better — they could own property and sign contracts — but they faced exclusion from most professions (medicine and law admitted essentially no women in the 1840s; the first American woman doctor, Elizabeth Blackwell, graduated from medical school in 1849), exclusion from most universities (Oberlin had begun admitting women in 1837 but most institutions remained closed), and exclusion from voting in every state. Married women's property acts had begun to pass in some states in the 1840s — Mississippi in 1839, New York in 1848 — but the underlying doctrine of coverture remained largely intact until late in the nineteenth century.

The Seneca Falls convention's eleventh resolution — the suffrage resolution — was the only resolution that produced sustained internal debate. The fight was over whether to ask for the vote at all. Some delegates (Lucretia Mott included) believed that asking for the vote was politically premature and would discredit the broader movement. Stanton insisted on including the suffrage demand. Frederick Douglass, who attended the convention, supported the suffrage demand and helped persuade the delegates to adopt it. The resolution passed by a narrow margin.

The women's-rights movement that grew out of Seneca Falls developed in parallel with the abolitionist movement through the 1850s. Many of the same activists (Susan B. Anthony, Elizabeth Cady Stanton, Lucy Stone, Sojourner Truth, Frances Ellen Watkins Harper) participated in both. The two movements were entangled organizationally and ideologically; the rhetoric of abolitionism (slavery as the negation of natural rights) supplied the women's-rights movement with much of its argumentative structure (legal subjection of married women as the negation of natural rights). The split between the two movements that would come later — the 1869 split over whether to support the Fifteenth Amendment, which extended voting rights to Black men but not to women — was a product of choices made in the immediate post-Civil-War period, not of the antebellum organizing pattern.

## The other reforms

The antebellum reform impulse produced movements beyond abolitionism and women's rights that are worth at least mentioning.

The **temperance movement** was, by membership and political reach, the largest antebellum reform movement. The American Temperance Society, founded in 1826, had perhaps a million and a half members by 1836. The movement began as moderation advocacy and shifted to total abstinence in the 1830s; the term "teetotal" (probably from "T-total," meaning total abstinence as marked on temperance pledges) entered American English in the 1830s. Temperance produced its first political success with Maine's prohibition law in 1851; a dozen states followed in the early 1850s. The temperance movement's connection to women's organizing was substantial — drunkenness was understood as a domestic problem that women bore disproportionately, and many of the women who became prominent in women's-rights organizing began their political work in temperance organizations.

The **public-school movement**, led by Horace Mann in Massachusetts from 1837, transformed American education across the 1840s and 1850s. Mann's model of state-funded, tax-supported, professionally-taught, secular (or at least non-denominational) common schools spread across the Northern states in the antebellum decades. By 1860 most Northern states had publicly-funded common school systems; the Southern states largely did not, and the absence of public education in the slave-state South was one of the structural differences between the regions that the Civil War would partly resolve.

The **utopian-community movement** produced perhaps a hundred experimental communities across the 1820s through the 1850s — Brook Farm (Massachusetts, 1841–47, Transcendentalist), the Oneida Community (New York, 1848–81, religious-perfectionist), New Harmony (Indiana, 1825–27, secular-socialist under Robert Owen), the Shaker communities (multiple sites, religious-celibate, founded by Ann Lee in the late eighteenth century and lasting into the twentieth), and others. Most were small (a few dozen to a few hundred members) and short-lived. Some — the Oneida Community in particular — survived for decades and developed sophisticated systems of communal property, group marriage, and shared child-rearing. The utopian movement was a sustained American experiment in social-organizational alternatives. Most of its specific experiments failed; the broader cultural footprint shaped twentieth-century American communitarianism and progressive movements in significant ways.

The reform impulse of the antebellum decades was, in retrospect, the most ambitious sustained attempt at social transformation that any American period before the Progressive Era would produce. Most of its specific projects were incomplete or unsuccessful in their own terms. The political coalition the movements collectively built — the Northern reformist political constituency that would, by 1860, elect Abraham Lincoln on the Republican Party ticket — was the political force that produced the Civil War and the constitutional transformations of Reconstruction. The reformers did not personally accomplish the political ends they had imagined for themselves; they built the coalition that did.
