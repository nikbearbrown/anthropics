# Chapter 25 — Brother, Can You Spare a Dime? The Great Depression, 1929–1932

*The crash that was not the depression, and the depression that was not the crash.*

In summer 1932, an army of about 17,000 unemployed World War I veterans — accompanied by approximately 26,000 family members and supporters — marched on Washington to demand early payment of the "bonus" certificates Congress had promised them in 1924 (a delayed wartime service compensation that, by the original 1924 statute, was not to be paid until 1945). The Bonus Army, also called the Bonus Expeditionary Force, set up encampments at Anacostia Flats and at various locations near the Capitol and the White House. They petitioned Congress for early payment of the bonus, which would have provided economic relief at a moment when most of them had been unemployed for months or years.

The bonus bill failed in the Senate on June 17, 1932. About half of the Bonus Army veterans returned home over the following weeks; the remainder, with their families, stayed in the encampments. President Hoover ordered the U.S. Army to clear the camps. On July 28, 1932, U.S. Army cavalry and infantry under General Douglas MacArthur — with junior officers including Dwight Eisenhower and George Patton — drove the veterans out of their downtown encampments and pursued them across the Anacostia River to their main camp at Anacostia Flats. MacArthur, exceeding his orders from Hoover, attacked the Anacostia camp with cavalry, infantry, and tanks, dispersing the encampment and burning it. Several veterans were killed (the exact number is contested; the official figures showed two veterans dead, but contemporary accounts suggested more); over 1,000 were injured; about 135 were arrested.

The Bonus Army episode was, in retrospect, the political-cultural endpoint of the Hoover presidency. The image of the U.S. Army attacking unemployed veterans on the Mall — broadcast in newsreels and photographs across the country — substantially destroyed what remained of Hoover's political position. Hoover lost the 1932 election to Franklin D. Roosevelt in a 472–59 electoral-vote landslide; the Democratic Party also took control of both houses of Congress. The political coalition that would build the New Deal across the following four years had been substantially assembled by the time of the November 1932 election.

This chapter is about the three and a half years between the October 1929 stock-market crash and the March 1933 inauguration of Roosevelt. The puzzle of this chapter is what the Great Depression was — as an economic event, as a political-cultural crisis, as the precipitating condition for the substantial federal-government transformation the next chapter will take up. The popular framing that treats the stock-market crash as the cause of the Depression is wrong on the economics. The crash was a symptom of underlying structural problems, and the Depression itself was produced by the structural problems compounded by specific policy choices over the following several years.

## What the Depression actually was

The Great Depression was not the stock-market crash. The crash was the trigger; the depression that followed was a much longer, broader, and more structurally severe phenomenon.

The American real Gross National Product fell by approximately 30 percent between 1929 and 1933. American unemployment rose from approximately 3 percent in 1929 to approximately 25 percent at the depression's nadir in 1933 — a figure that in itself substantially understates the labor-market collapse because the official unemployment measure did not capture the underemployment, the part-time work, the wage cuts, the elderly and women workers pushed out of the labor force, the agricultural workers whose product prices had collapsed. Approximately 9,000 American banks failed between 1929 and 1933, taking depositor funds with them in the absence of federal deposit insurance (which the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, established under the Banking Act of 1933, would substantially change). Approximately one third of American mortgages were in default by 1933. The American banking system, the American industrial system, the American agricultural system, and the American consumer-credit system were all simultaneously in crisis by 1932–33.

The structural causes of the Depression are contested in their precise weighting but reasonably well-understood in their broad outline. The historian Peter Temin's *Did Monetary Forces Cause the Great Depression?* (1976) and the subsequent macroeconomic-history literature have identified several mechanisms:

**Monetary contraction.** The Federal Reserve, which had been established by the Federal Reserve Act of 1913, responded to the post-1929 banking crisis by contracting the money supply rather than expanding it. The orthodox monetary policy of the period — that central banks should respond to financial crises by tightening credit to defend the gold standard — produced a deflationary spiral in which falling prices, falling wages, falling output, and falling employment compounded each other. The historian Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz's *A Monetary History of the United States* (1963) argued that the Federal Reserve's monetary contraction was the principal cause of the Depression's severity; the Friedman-Schwartz framing has been substantially influential in subsequent monetary-economic scholarship.

**The gold standard.** The international gold standard, which had been disrupted during WWI and partially reconstructed in the 1920s, was a major transmission mechanism for the Depression across national economies. Countries that left the gold standard early in the depression (Britain in 1931, Japan in 1931–32) recovered substantially faster than countries that maintained gold parity (the United States until 1933, France until 1936). The historian Barry Eichengreen's *Golden Fetters* (1992) has documented this pattern. The Roosevelt administration's early abandonment of the gold standard (the Emergency Banking Act of March 1933; the gold-clause repudiation of June 1933) was one of the substantial policy choices that distinguished the New Deal from Hoover's earlier responses.

**Underlying weaknesses.** The 1920s prosperity had concealed several substantial economic weaknesses: the agricultural depression that had been running since 1921; the weakness of major industrial sectors (coal, textiles, shipbuilding) that had peaked during WWI; the substantial inequality of income distribution (the top 1 percent of households held approximately 30 percent of total American wealth in 1929) that limited consumer demand; the international financial structure that depended on continuous American capital export to maintain European demand for American goods. The Depression's structural causes ran substantially beyond the stock-market crash that triggered the immediate financial crisis.

**Tariff policy.** The Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act of June 1930 raised American tariff rates to historically high levels. The Act was substantially counterproductive in the depression conditions — it produced retaliation from major trading partners that reduced American exports, contributing to the global trade collapse of 1930–34. Approximately 1,000 American economists signed a petition against Smoot-Hawley in 1930; Hoover signed it anyway under political pressure from the Republican congressional coalition.

The combination of monetary contraction, gold-standard maintenance, underlying structural weakness, and counterproductive tariff policy produced a depression substantially worse than would have been required by the immediate financial crisis. The Depression's severity was a function of policy choices as well as structural conditions.

## What Hoover did and did not do

The standard popular framing treats Hoover as having done nothing to address the Depression — the "rugged individualism" framing in which Hoover's commitment to private-sector and local-government solutions left the federal government unable to respond. The framing is partly true and partly wrong, and getting the partly-wrong dimension right matters for understanding what the New Deal substantively built on.

Hoover did substantial things in response to the Depression. The Reconstruction Finance Corporation, established in January 1932, was a federal lending institution that provided emergency credit to banks, insurance companies, railroads, and (eventually) state and local governments. The RFC operated through the 1930s and 1940s and was, in important ways, the institutional model that subsequent federal credit programs followed. The Glass-Steagall Act of February 1932 (a different, earlier Glass-Steagall Act than the 1933 banking-separation Act that is now better known) allowed the Federal Reserve to use government securities as collateral, expanding the Fed's ability to lend during banking crises. The Federal Home Loan Bank Act of July 1932 established federal support for mortgage credit. The Emergency Relief and Construction Act of 1932 authorized $2 billion in federal lending to states for relief and public-works projects.

What Hoover did not do, in retrospect, was as important. He refused to support direct federal relief payments to unemployed Americans, on the ground that direct federal aid would create dependency and would substitute federal authority for state and local responsibility. He refused to support substantial federal public-works employment programs. He maintained the gold standard until forced off it by the post-1933 banking crisis. He maintained the orthodox monetary policy that the Federal Reserve had been pursuing. He signed Smoot-Hawley despite the substantial economic and political costs.

The Hoover administration's actions and inactions were not, in retrospect, the products of indifference. Hoover was substantially more activist than his nineteenth-century Republican predecessors had been; the Reconstruction Finance Corporation and the other 1932 federal-credit programs were substantial federal interventions by the standards of the time. What constrained Hoover was a substantive political-philosophical commitment to limited federal direct intervention combined with the orthodox economic understanding of the period. The combination produced a federal response that, by the standards of what was structurally required, was substantially inadequate; but the response was not nothing, and it produced institutional infrastructure that the New Deal would substantially extend rather than substantially abandon.

The political-cultural memory has substantially flattened this distinction. "Hoover did nothing" is the standard popular framing; "Hoover did substantially the wrong things and did not do enough of what was needed" is closer to the historical record.

## What the Depression was like

The economic statistics described above translated into specific human conditions that the standard accounts can substantially obscure.

**Unemployment.** With approximately 25 percent of the labor force unemployed at the Depression's nadir, an additional substantial fraction working only part-time or at sharply reduced wages, the practical situation of American working-class families was severe. The most vulnerable populations — single women, Black workers, immigrant workers — faced unemployment rates substantially higher than the average. Black unemployment in major Northern cities ran as high as 50 percent in 1932–33. The federal Bureau of Labor Statistics did not publish unemployment figures by race in this period; the figures available come from later reconstruction by economic historians.

**Homelessness.** The Depression produced what was, by all available measures, the largest single experience of American homelessness across the twentieth century. *Hoovervilles* — the encampments of homeless Americans that emerged in and around major cities — housed somewhere between hundreds of thousands and several million Americans across 1930–33. The Hoovervilles ranged from substantial settlements (the Hooverville on the Seattle waterfront housed about 1,200 people for several years; the Riverside Park Hooverville in New York City housed several hundred) to small encampments on vacant lots. They were demolished by municipal authorities at various points across the early 1930s.

**Hunger.** Federal nutrition surveys conducted later in the 1930s found that substantial fractions of American children had experienced sustained malnutrition during the early Depression years. School milk programs (where they existed; most states had no public school feeding programs in 1932) were substantially overwhelmed. Private charities — the Red Cross, religious organizations, neighborhood mutual-aid groups — provided substantial emergency food aid but at scales that were inadequate to the demand. Bread lines and soup kitchens became standard features of urban life across the period.

**The Dust Bowl.** A drought that began in the early 1930s on the southern Great Plains compounded the agricultural depression. The drought, combined with several decades of intensive plowing that had destroyed the native grassland's root systems, produced massive dust storms — the "black blizzards" — that destroyed crops, buried farms in dust, and produced respiratory diseases in the affected regions. The geographic core was the panhandle regions of Oklahoma, Texas, Colorado, Kansas, and New Mexico; the affected region extended substantially beyond that. Approximately 2.5 million people migrated out of the Plains states across the 1930s; about 250,000 of them moved to California, producing the "Okie" migration that John Steinbeck's *The Grapes of Wrath* (1939) documented. The Dust Bowl was both an environmental and an economic disaster; its specific timing within the Depression compounded the suffering across the agricultural Plains.

**Children's labor.** The economic pressure produced a substantial expansion of child labor — particularly in agricultural work, in domestic service, in marginal industrial operations. The federal Children's Bureau's investigations of the period documented children as young as eight working in cotton fields, in vegetable canneries, in shoeshine work in cities. The Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 — which would substantially restrict child labor — was a partial response to the patterns documented during the Depression.

**Race.** The Depression's impact on Black Americans was, in most regions, substantially worse than its impact on white Americans. The agricultural collapse displaced sharecropping families across the South; the urban industrial collapse produced higher unemployment in Black communities than in white communities; the legal-discriminatory environment of the South made federal relief programs (where they existed) substantially less available to Black recipients than to white recipients. The Black political-cultural mobilization that would shape twentieth-century civil-rights organizing — the Nation of Islam (founded 1930), the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters (founded 1925, but the major organizing happened in the 1930s), the Communist Party USA's substantial Black membership in the 1930s — emerged in the conditions the Depression produced.

## The 1932 election

The November 1932 election was the most consequential American election since 1860. Franklin D. Roosevelt — the Democratic governor of New York, a distant cousin of Theodore Roosevelt, who had been the 1920 vice-presidential candidate and had recovered from a 1921 polio attack — defeated Hoover by 472 to 59 electoral votes and by about 17 percentage points in the popular vote. The Democratic Party also took control of both houses of Congress with substantial majorities (60–35 in the Senate; 313–117 in the House). The political conditions for the major federal restructuring that the New Deal would attempt were assembled by the election.

Roosevelt's campaign had been substantively cautious. His specific policy commitments were limited; his rhetorical commitment — "a new deal for the American people," in his July 1932 nomination acceptance speech — was substantially open in its actual policy content. The phrase "New Deal" had not yet been associated with a specific legislative program; it became the program's name in subsequent months as the program was developed. The substantive coalition that would build the New Deal across 1933–35 was substantially assembled after the November 1932 election rather than during the campaign.

The four-month interregnum between the November 1932 election and the March 1933 inauguration was one of the worst political-economic periods in American history. The 1933 banking crisis — beginning with the failure of major Detroit banks in February 1933 and spreading rapidly across the country — produced the most severe banking-system collapse of the Depression. By Roosevelt's March 4, 1933, inauguration, banking systems in 38 of 48 states had been closed by emergency state proclamation. The Hoover administration was unable to coordinate a federal response because the incoming Roosevelt administration's policy positions had not been articulated and Hoover's authority was substantially gone. The Twentieth Amendment to the Constitution, ratified in January 1933, moved subsequent inauguration dates from March 4 to January 20 — substantially because of the 1932–33 transition's demonstration that a four-month interregnum was incompatible with the modern political-economic system.

The next chapter takes up what Roosevelt's administration did after March 4, 1933.
