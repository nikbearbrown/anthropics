# Chapter 20 — Politics in the Gilded Age, 1870–1900

*Three decades of evenly-matched parties fighting over money.*

On July 9, 1896, a thirty-six-year-old former congressman named William Jennings Bryan rose at the Democratic National Convention in Chicago to give a speech on the question of bimetallism. Bryan was speaking against the incumbent Cleveland administration's commitment to the gold standard and in favor of the "free silver" position — that the federal government should freely coin silver at a fixed ratio (16 ounces of silver to 1 ounce of gold) and use that monetary expansion to reverse the deflationary policies that had been crushing American farmers across the previous two decades. He spoke for about thirty-five minutes without notes. The peroration is one of the most-quoted passages in nineteenth-century American oratory:

"We have petitioned, and our petitions have been scorned. We have entreated, and our entreaties have been disregarded. We have begged, and they have mocked when our calamity came. We beg no longer; we entreat no more; we petition no more. We defy them... If they dare to come out in the open field and defend the gold standard as a good thing, we will fight them to the uttermost. Having behind us the producing masses of this nation and the world, supported by the commercial interests, the laboring interests and the toilers everywhere, we will answer their demand for a gold standard by saying to them: You shall not press down upon the brow of labor this crown of thorns, you shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold."

The "Cross of Gold" speech won Bryan the Democratic nomination for president. He was nominated again by the Populist Party and the Silver Republican Party — the only time in American history that a single candidate received three major-party nominations. The 1896 election that followed was the most consequential American election since 1860 and the most consequential before 1932. Bryan campaigned by traveling some 18,000 miles by train and giving about 600 speeches across the Midwest, the South, and parts of the Northeast. The Republican candidate William McKinley conducted the original "front-porch campaign" from his home in Canton, Ohio, while Republican manager Mark Hanna organized what was, by far, the largest political fundraising and campaign operation in American history to that point — an estimated $3.5 million budget against Bryan's roughly $600,000. McKinley won 51 percent of the popular vote to Bryan's 47 percent. The Republican electoral coalition that emerged — Northeastern industrial workers, Midwestern industrial cities, the corporate and financial classes — would dominate American politics for the next thirty-six years until the New Deal realignment.

This chapter is about the politics that produced 1896 and what the election decided. The "Gilded Age" framing of the period (Mark Twain's 1873 phrase, as discussed in Chapter 18) captures the period's wealth-and-poverty contrasts. It does not, on its own, describe the political conflicts that organized the era's electoral politics. Those conflicts were substantially over money — specifically, over whether the federal government would maintain a deflationary gold-standard monetary policy that benefited creditors (banks, bondholders, the industrial and financial elite) or would shift to an inflationary silver-or-greenback policy that would benefit debtors (farmers, small-business owners, anyone who owed money in dollars).

The puzzle of this chapter is what the Gilded Age political conflict was actually about and why it produced such evenly-matched partisan competition for about thirty years.

## The party system the period operated in

The post-Civil War electoral system in the United States was characterized by extraordinarily close national elections, intense partisan loyalty along regional lines, and a federal government with limited substantive policy capacity.

The presidential elections from 1876 through 1892 produced popular-vote margins that were almost always within 3 percentage points. Two of these elections (1876 and 1888) were won by candidates who lost the popular vote but won the Electoral College — the Hayes-Tilden election (Chapter 16) and the Harrison-Cleveland election of 1888. Three of the seven elections in this period (1876, 1880, 1888) were decided by electoral margins of fewer than 50,000 votes; Harrison won 1888 by about 100,000 votes in the popular tally despite Cleveland winning the popular vote. The pattern was unprecedented in American politics; the elections of the antebellum period and of the early twentieth century have been substantially less close. The 1876–1892 pattern reflects what political scientists call the *Third Party System* — the partisan alignment that emerged from the Civil War and that persisted, with extraordinary stability, for a quarter-century after the war.

The two parties of the period — the Republicans and the Democrats — had distinct geographic bases. The Republican Party was the party of the North: New England, the upper Midwest, the urban-industrial Northeast, the Western states. Republican support was strongest among Protestants, former Union soldiers (the Grand Army of the Republic, the major Civil War veterans' organization, was a politically powerful force in Republican politics through the 1890s), commercial-industrial classes, and African Americans (where they could vote — the disfranchisement of Southern Black voters across the 1880s and 1890s removed a substantial Republican constituency in the South). The Democratic Party was the party of the South: the entire former Confederacy, plus the southern Midwest, plus the northeastern urban Catholic immigrant working class. Democratic support was strongest among white Southerners (the disfranchisement of Southern Black voters made Democratic dominance in the South essentially unchallenged across most of the post-Reconstruction period), among Catholics of all national-origin groups, and among the Northern industrial working class (in alliance with the political machines that organized those communities).

The parties did not differ substantially on most policy questions of the period. Both supported relatively high tariffs (Republican tariffs were generally higher; Democratic tariffs were generally lower; both parties agreed that some tariffs were necessary). Both supported the gold standard during most of the period (Republican support was firmer; the Cleveland Democratic administration committed to gold against substantial intra-party Democratic opposition). Both opposed substantial federal interference with the operations of large corporations. Both opposed federal involvement in the post-Reconstruction Southern racial system. Both ran patronage operations that distributed federal jobs to party loyalists.

The relative substantive similarity of the parties' policy positions, combined with the closeness of their electoral competition, produced a politics organized around different questions than the modern American observer might expect. The presidential elections of the 1870s and 1880s turned on candidate personalities (was Garfield well-qualified? was Cleveland morally suitable?), on regional appeals (the "bloody shirt" Republican rhetoric reminding Northern voters of the Civil War), on patronage controversies (the Pendleton Act of 1883, which established federal civil service for some positions; the Mugwump revolt against the Republican Party in 1884), and on relatively limited policy questions about tariff rates and currency.

The structural reasons for the period's political stalemate are worth naming. The federal government was small (federal employees in 1880 numbered about 130,000; the entire federal budget was about $400 million); state and local governments did most of the substantive governing that touched ordinary Americans' lives. The most consequential political conflicts of the period (the labor conflicts of the 1870s and 1880s; the farmer organizing of the 1880s and 1890s; the Populist movement of the 1890s; the racial-political crisis of the post-Reconstruction South) happened largely outside the formal federal political system, in state legislatures, in courts, in extra-political institutions. Federal electoral politics, during most of the period, was an evenly-balanced standoff between two parties whose substantive policy differences were modest enough that other political dynamics could not break through.

## What "money" meant politically

The central substantive political question of the period was monetary policy. The question had been latent in American politics since the Civil War, when the federal government had issued *greenbacks* — non-gold-backed paper currency — to finance the war. The greenbacks had inflated the money supply substantially during the war; the post-war question was what to do with them.

The conservative Republican position, adopted by the Grant administration in 1869 and consolidated under subsequent Republican administrations, was to gradually contract the money supply by redeeming greenbacks for gold. The Resumption Act of 1875 set January 1, 1879 as the date when greenbacks would be redeemed for gold at face value, completing the return to a pure gold standard.

The monetary contraction this produced was deflationary in the strict economic sense. Prices in 1890 were about 30 percent lower than they had been in 1865. For creditors — banks, bondholders, anyone who had lent money in pre-1879 dollars and was being repaid in post-1879 gold-backed dollars — this was an enormous wealth transfer in their favor. For debtors — farmers who had borrowed money to buy land and equipment, small businesses with bank loans, anyone who owed money in dollars — the same monetary contraction was a slow disaster. A farmer who had borrowed $1,000 in 1870 (when wheat sold for about $1.30 a bushel) was repaying that loan in 1890 at a wheat price of perhaps $0.70 a bushel — meaning the loan now required almost twice as many bushels of wheat to repay as had been originally borrowed against. The political economy of the deflation was that creditors got richer and debtors got poorer through a mechanism that did not feel like anyone's specific decision.

The political response to the deflation came in three waves.

**The greenback movement** of the 1870s argued for keeping greenbacks in circulation as inflationary money. The Greenback Party ran presidential candidates in 1876, 1880, and 1884; its best showing was 1878, when it elected fifteen congressmen. The greenback position lost its political viability after the 1879 resumption of specie payments worked as designed.

**The free-silver movement** of the 1880s and 1890s argued that the federal government should freely coin silver alongside gold, at a fixed silver-to-gold ratio (the historical ratio of 16 to 1 became the slogan). Free silver would have inflated the money supply by adding silver dollars to the existing gold-based currency. The silver position had a populist political base in the silver-mining states of the West (Nevada, Colorado, Montana, Idaho) and in the agricultural South and Midwest, where farmers had been the principal victims of the deflation. The silver position was supported by the Sherman Silver Purchase Act of 1890 (which required the federal government to purchase substantial quantities of silver each month) but reversed by the Cleveland administration's repeal of the Sherman Act in 1893.

**The Populist movement** of the 1890s emerged from the farmer organizing that the Grange (the Patrons of Husbandry, founded 1867) and the Farmers' Alliances (the Northern and Southern Alliances, founded in the 1870s and 1880s) had built across the previous twenty years. The Populist Party — founded at conventions in Cincinnati in 1891 and St. Louis and Omaha in 1892 — ran James B. Weaver for president in 1892 on a platform that included free silver, federal regulation of railroads, a graduated income tax, direct popular election of senators, the secret ballot, and a federal sub-treasury system that would provide low-interest loans to farmers against stored crops. Weaver received about 8.5 percent of the popular vote and 22 electoral votes — the best Third Party performance in American politics between the 1850s and the early 1900s. The Populist movement was, at its peak, the most serious challenge to the two-party system in nineteenth-century American politics.

The 1893 depression that followed Cleveland's election produced widespread economic distress — unemployment running as high as 18 percent, dozens of major railroad bankruptcies, hundreds of bank failures. The economic conditions accelerated the Populist movement; by 1894 the Populists were a serious threat to the Democratic Party's electoral position in much of the South and Midwest.

## The 1896 election and what it decided

The Democratic Party convention of July 1896 broke from Cleveland — the sitting Democratic president — on the silver question. Bryan's "Cross of Gold" speech won him the nomination on the strength of the silver position; the Populist Party followed by also nominating Bryan (rather than running an independent Populist candidate as in 1892) in an attempt to coordinate the silver-and-reform coalition.

The 1896 election was structured as a referendum on the silver-versus-gold question. Bryan campaigned across most of the country, primarily in the South, Midwest, and West, on the silver platform. McKinley's campaign — managed by Mark Hanna, the Cleveland industrialist and political operative who became one of the most influential American political figures of the late nineteenth century — focused on what Hanna called *sound money* (the gold standard) and economic stability. The Republican campaign budget of about $3.5 million was about six times Bryan's. Most of the Republican fundraising came from banks, industrial corporations, and individual financial figures who saw the silver question as an existential threat to the financial system.

McKinley won with 51.0 percent of the popular vote to Bryan's 46.7 percent. The geographic pattern was substantial. McKinley swept the Northeast, the upper Midwest, the Pacific Coast, and the urban-industrial parts of the country. Bryan won the entire South, the agricultural Midwest, and most of the silver-mining West. The election produced what political scientists call the *Fourth Party System* — a partisan alignment dominated by Republicans nationally, with Democrats reduced to a regional Southern base. The Republican electoral coalition of 1896 would win every presidential election except 1912 (when the Republican vote split between Theodore Roosevelt's Progressive Party and William Howard Taft's Republican Party, allowing Woodrow Wilson to win) and 1916 (when Wilson narrowly won reelection on a peace platform) until 1932.

The 1896 election also decided the silver-versus-gold question. The Gold Standard Act of 1900, passed by the McKinley administration after the election, formally placed the United States on a pure gold standard. The free-silver movement, which had been the central political question of the previous twenty years, was essentially extinct as a political force by 1900. The Populist Party, which had folded its 1896 campaign into the Bryan-Democratic candidacy, was destroyed as an independent political force by the same defeat. The reform agenda the Populists had developed across the 1880s and 1890s — federal regulation of railroads, a federal income tax, direct election of senators, the secret ballot — would mostly be enacted across the following two decades by Progressive-era reformers, but the Populist movement that had developed and advocated for these reforms had been absorbed into the Democratic Party and was no longer a distinct political force.

The Gilded Age, in short, ended in 1896. The political-economic stalemate that had produced thirty years of close elections and evenly-balanced parties was replaced by a new partisan alignment in which the Republican Party dominated national politics, the gold standard was secured, the corporate-industrial economic system was politically protected against substantial reform, and the Southern Democratic Party was reduced to a regional bloc that would not threaten Republican national dominance until economic conditions changed in the 1930s.
