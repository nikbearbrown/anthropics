# Chapter 31 — From Cold War to Culture Wars, 1980–2000

*The end of one international order and the assembly of the domestic political conflicts that followed.*

In November 1989, the East German government — facing a sustained wave of citizen demonstrations and the collapse of the surrounding Warsaw Pact governments — opened the border crossings to West Berlin. The Berlin Wall, which had divided the city since 1961 and which had been the most-visible symbol of the Cold War for three decades, was substantially dismantled by celebrating crowds across the following weeks. The German reunification that followed in October 1990, the collapse of the Soviet-allied governments across Eastern Europe (Poland, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, Romania, Bulgaria, East Germany) across 1989–90, and the dissolution of the Soviet Union itself on December 25, 1991, substantially ended the Cold War that had organized international politics since 1945.

The Cold War's end produced a substantial restructuring of the international system that the United States had built and led across the previous half-century. The transformation was substantially unanticipated by the institutional infrastructure that had been organized around the bipolar Cold War framework. The military-industrial complex, the intelligence agencies, the diplomatic-foreign-policy framework, the academic disciplines of international relations — all of these had been substantially organized around the Soviet-American confrontation that suddenly ended. The post-1991 transition to what President George H.W. Bush had called the "new world order" was substantially improvised across the 1990s.

This chapter is about the two decades between Reagan's 1980 inauguration and the 2000 election. The puzzle of this chapter is what these decades substantively produced — both internationally (the end of the Cold War, the substantial transformation of the global economic system through what came to be called globalization) and domestically (the consolidation of the Republican Party's conservative coalition, the partial Democratic counter-mobilization under Clinton, the substantial cultural-political conflicts that would shape the early twenty-first century).

## The Reagan administration

Ronald Reagan's two-term administration (1981–89) substantially restructured American domestic policy in ways that the subsequent decades have substantially extended rather than substantially abandoned.

**Reagan's economic policy.** The framework — called *supply-side economics* by its proponents and *Reaganomics* in popular usage — substantially combined large federal tax cuts (the Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 reduced the top marginal income-tax rate from 70 percent to 50 percent; the Tax Reform Act of 1986 further reduced it to 28 percent), substantial increases in defense spending, substantial cuts to non-defense discretionary spending, and substantial deregulation of industries that had been federally regulated since the New Deal period.

The supply-side claim — that the tax cuts would produce sufficient additional economic growth to substantially offset the reduced revenue — has been substantially contested in subsequent economic-historical analysis. The empirical record shows that federal revenues fell substantially as a share of GDP across 1981–84 and recovered partially after the 1982 and 1984 tax-increase legislation that partially offset the 1981 cuts. The federal deficit grew substantially across the Reagan years — the public debt rose from approximately 31 percent of GDP in 1981 to approximately 51 percent of GDP in 1989. The supply-side hypothesis, in its strong form (that tax cuts pay for themselves), has been substantially rejected by the mainstream economic-historical analysis.

**The Reagan recession and recovery.** The Reagan administration inherited the inflationary cycle that the Volcker Federal Reserve had been combating since 1979. The Fed's substantial tightening of monetary policy across 1981–82 produced the substantial recession of 1981–82 (American unemployment peaked at approximately 10.8 percent in November 1982, the highest figure since the Great Depression). The subsequent recovery across 1983–89 — what the Reagan campaign of 1984 called "Morning in America" — was substantial and produced approximately 16 million new American jobs across the decade. The macroeconomic interpretation of the 1983–89 recovery is contested: supply-side proponents have attributed it to the tax cuts; monetarists have attributed it to the substantial post-1982 monetary easing; Keynesians have attributed it substantially to the substantial federal deficit spending that the tax cuts and defense increases produced. The recovery was real; its causation is debated.

**Deregulation.** The Reagan administration substantially extended the deregulation framework that the Carter administration had begun. Major industries were substantially deregulated across 1980–86: airlines, trucking, railroads, telecommunications (the breakup of AT&T in 1984 was the most-publicized regulatory action), banking (the substantial weakening of the Glass-Steagall framework began in the mid-1980s and accelerated through the 1990s), and energy. The substantial deregulation produced substantial efficiency gains in some sectors and substantial market-failure problems in others. The savings and loan crisis of 1986–95 — in which approximately 1,000 savings and loan institutions failed, requiring approximately $124 billion in federal bailouts — was substantially a consequence of the deregulation framework combined with substantial regulatory-enforcement failures.

**The conservative movement.** The Reagan administration substantially institutionalized the political coalition that had been assembling since the Goldwater campaign of 1964. The coalition included economic conservatives (the supply-side framework; the deregulation agenda); social conservatives (the substantial mobilization of evangelical Christians under leaders like Jerry Falwell — whose Moral Majority was founded in 1979 — and Pat Robertson; the political opposition to abortion rights following *Roe v. Wade* in 1973); foreign-policy conservatives (the substantial military buildup; the anti-communist framework); and substantial fractions of the white working class (who had been Democratic during the New Deal period but who shifted to Republican allegiance across the 1968–80 partisan realignment). The Reagan coalition would substantially dominate American politics from 1980 through the 1990s and would shape Republican politics through the present.

**The Iran-Contra affair.** The Reagan administration's most substantial political-criminal scandal involved the sale of weapons to Iran (in violation of the administration's stated policy of not negotiating with terrorists) in order to fund the Contra insurgency against the Nicaraguan Sandinista government (in violation of the Boland Amendment that Congress had passed in 1982 to restrict American military aid to the Contras). The scandal was substantially exposed across 1986–87; the resulting Tower Commission report (1987) and Independent Counsel investigation (Lawrence Walsh, 1986–93) produced indictments and convictions of multiple senior administration officials, though most convictions were either overturned on appeal or pardoned by President George H.W. Bush in 1992. Reagan's substantive involvement in the operation has been contested; his administration's framework that allowed it has been substantially documented.

## The end of the Cold War

The Cold War's end was, for the Reagan administration, substantially unanticipated and substantially providential. The substantial military buildup of the early 1980s and the rhetorical confrontation with the Soviet Union ("evil empire" was Reagan's March 1983 phrase) were substantially predicated on a Cold War framework that would continue for decades. The substantial change came from internal developments within the Soviet Union under Mikhail Gorbachev, who became General Secretary of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union in March 1985.

**Gorbachev's reforms.** *Glasnost* (openness) and *perestroika* (restructuring) — the two named reform programs of the Gorbachev administration — were substantial attempts to address the structural problems of the Soviet economic and political system. The reforms substantially failed in their stated objective (reforming the Soviet system to be sustainable) but substantially succeeded in producing the conditions for the system's collapse. The October 1986 Reykjavik summit between Reagan and Gorbachev, the December 1987 Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty, the substantial Soviet military withdrawal from Afghanistan across 1988–89, and the substantial relaxation of Soviet political control over the Warsaw Pact countries across 1988–89 — all of these reflected Gorbachev's substantial commitment to a substantially restructured relationship between the Soviet Union and the West.

**1989.** The Eastern European revolutions of 1989 — Poland's Solidarity-led elections in June; Hungary's opening of its border with Austria in May; East Germany's substantial protests across summer and the opening of the Berlin Wall in November; Czechoslovakia's Velvet Revolution in November; Romania's violent overthrow of the Ceausescu regime in December — substantially ended the Soviet political-military bloc system. The Soviet Union itself dissolved across 1991: the August 1991 attempted coup by Soviet hard-liners produced the political ascendancy of Russian president Boris Yeltsin; the December 25, 1991 dissolution of the Soviet Union produced the fifteen independent successor states.

**American post-Cold-War foreign policy.** The Bush administration that succeeded Reagan in January 1989 substantially managed the end of the Cold War. The Persian Gulf War of August 1990–February 1991 — in which the United States led an international coalition that expelled Iraqi forces from Kuwait after the Iraqi invasion of August 1990 — was substantially the first major American military operation of the post-Cold-War period. The relatively decisive military outcome and the relatively limited American casualties (148 Americans killed in combat) substantially restored American political-cultural willingness to commit military force after the Vietnam syndrome.

The post-Cold-War international system that emerged across the 1990s was substantially American-dominant in ways that no previous global power had been since perhaps the British in the mid-nineteenth century. The American military, the American economy, the American cultural production, the American-led international institutions (the IMF, the World Bank, the WTO that succeeded the GATT in 1995, NATO with substantial Eastern European expansion across the late 1990s) all operated without substantial competitive rival across the 1990s. The "unipolar moment" framing that Charles Krauthammer articulated in 1990 substantially described the international structure that operated for approximately a decade.

## The Clinton administration

Bill Clinton's two-term administration (1993–2001) operated within the substantial conservative framework that the Reagan years had assembled. Clinton's "Third Way" framework — substantially associated with the Democratic Leadership Council's efforts to reposition the Democratic Party toward the political center — accepted substantial elements of the conservative framework (welfare reform, fiscal discipline, free-trade agreements) while attempting to maintain Democratic positions on civil rights, environmental protection, and substantial federal social spending.

**Welfare reform.** The Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of August 1996 substantially restructured the American welfare system. The Act replaced the Aid to Families with Dependent Children program (which had operated since 1935 and which had been the principal federal cash-assistance program for low-income families) with the Temporary Assistance for Needy Families block grant. TANF substantially imposed work requirements, time limits (typically five years lifetime maximum), and substantial state discretion in program design. The Act substantially reduced the federal welfare rolls (from approximately 12.6 million recipients in 1996 to approximately 4 million by 2007) but the substantive effects on poverty have been contested. The Act was a substantial Democratic concession to the Reagan-era framework on the welfare question.

**The 1994 election and the Republican Revolution.** The midterm election of 1994 produced substantial Republican gains: Republicans took control of the House of Representatives for the first time since 1955 (a gain of 54 seats) and the Senate. Newt Gingrich, the Republican Speaker from January 1995, organized the House Republican majority around the "Contract with America" — a substantial conservative legislative agenda. The Gingrich-Clinton confrontation across 1995–98 produced substantial budget conflicts (including two government shutdowns in late 1995 and early 1996), substantial substantive policy outcomes (the welfare reform; the Balanced Budget Act of 1997), and the Clinton impeachment of 1998–99.

**The Clinton impeachment.** The Independent Counsel investigation under Kenneth Starr, which had been investigating various aspects of Clinton-era political and financial activity since 1994, substantially focused after 1997 on Clinton's relationship with White House intern Monica Lewinsky and the substantive question of whether Clinton had lied under oath about the relationship in a deposition in the Paula Jones sexual-harassment civil case. The House impeached Clinton in December 1998 on charges of perjury and obstruction of justice. The Senate trial in January–February 1999 produced acquittal on both charges (the perjury charge was rejected 45–55; the obstruction charge was rejected 50–50; conviction would have required 67 votes). Clinton served out the remainder of his second term. The impeachment was the substantial political-cultural confrontation of the late Clinton years.

**The 1990s economic boom.** The American economy across the second half of the 1990s produced sustained growth, low inflation, low unemployment (American unemployment fell to approximately 4 percent by 2000, the lowest level since the late 1960s), and substantial federal-budget surpluses (the federal budget produced surpluses across 1998–2001, the only sustained surpluses since the 1960s). The boom was substantially driven by the technology sector — the substantial commercial development of the Internet across 1993–2000 produced what came to be called the "dot-com boom" — and by the substantial international-trade expansion under the GATT/WTO framework. The boom would substantially end with the dot-com crash of 2000–02 and the subsequent recession.

**Globalization.** The 1990s produced the substantial international-economic-integration framework that came to be called *globalization*. The North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA, signed by Bush in 1992 and ratified by Clinton in 1993, taking effect in 1994), the substantial expansion of the GATT into the World Trade Organization in 1995, the substantial integration of China into the international trading system (China's WTO accession was negotiated across the late 1990s and completed in 2001), the substantial financial-market integration across the 1990s, and the substantial relocation of manufacturing production from the United States and Western Europe to East Asia, Mexico, and other lower-cost locations substantially restructured the global economic system. The substantive consequences for American workers were uneven: the consumer benefits (lower prices for manufactured goods, expanded retail options) were widely shared; the substantial deindustrialization of the American Midwest produced substantial regional economic dislocation that would shape American politics across the following two decades.

## The culture wars

The 1980s and 1990s produced what came to be called *the culture wars* — the substantial political-cultural conflicts over questions of religion, sexuality, race, education, and national identity that substantially divided the American political coalitions.

**The Religious Right.** The substantial mobilization of evangelical Christians as a political force across the 1970s and 1980s produced one of the substantial new components of the post-1968 Republican coalition. The Moral Majority (founded 1979, dissolved 1989), the Christian Coalition (founded 1989), and the substantial network of evangelical media (Christian broadcasting, Christian publishing, Christian higher education) substantially shaped Republican politics across the period. The substantive policy positions — opposition to abortion rights, opposition to LGBT rights, support for school prayer and against secular education, support for traditional family structures — substantially defined the Religious Right's political agenda.

**The AIDS epidemic.** The Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome epidemic, first identified by the U.S. Centers for Disease Control in June 1981, substantially affected the gay community across the 1980s and 1990s. The Reagan administration's substantial inaction on the epidemic across 1981–87 — partly attributed to the political-cultural pattern of the period and partly to substantive scientific uncertainty about the disease in its early years — produced substantial AIDS-related deaths (approximately 250,000 Americans died from AIDS-related causes between 1981 and 1995). The activist response — ACT UP (founded 1987), the broader AIDS-activist organizing of the late 1980s and 1990s — substantially shaped both the public-health response and the broader gay-rights movement.

**The abortion conflict.** The post-*Roe v. Wade* (1973) political conflict over abortion rights substantially intensified across the 1980s and 1990s. The Supreme Court's *Planned Parenthood v. Casey* (1992) decision substantially preserved the *Roe* framework while allowing substantial state-level abortion restrictions. The political-cultural conflict — including substantial violence against abortion providers across the period (eleven abortion providers were killed by anti-abortion violence between 1993 and 2009; the Eric Rudolph bombing campaign of 1996–98 included an Olympic Park bombing) — substantially structured the partisan-political alignment.

**The LGBT-rights movement.** The substantial expansion of the gay-rights movement across the 1980s and 1990s — driven substantially by the AIDS crisis but extending beyond it — produced substantial changes in American social-cultural attitudes and in legal-political frameworks. The "don't ask, don't tell" framework for military service that Clinton negotiated in 1993 was a substantial Democratic compromise with the conservative coalition; the Defense of Marriage Act of 1996, which Clinton signed, defined marriage at the federal level as between one man and one woman. Both of these would be substantially reversed in the subsequent decades.

**Multiculturalism and the academy.** The substantial cultural-political conflicts within American higher education across the 1980s and 1990s — over canon formation in literature departments, over the substantial expansion of ethnic studies and women's studies programs, over the substantive content of historical and cultural-studies scholarship — substantially shaped American intellectual life across the period. The "political correctness" debates of the early 1990s, the Lynne Cheney-led conflicts over national history standards in the mid-1990s, the substantial development of conservative think tanks and conservative media institutions across the period (Fox News was founded in 1996; talk radio expanded substantially across the 1990s) substantially restructured American intellectual-political institutions.
