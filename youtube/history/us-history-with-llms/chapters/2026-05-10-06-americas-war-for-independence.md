# Chapter 6 — America's War for Independence, 1775–1783

*Not a war of colonists against Britain. A civil war among British subjects.*

On November 7, 1775, John Murray, Earl of Dunmore and the royal governor of Virginia, issued a proclamation from on board the British warship *William* in Norfolk harbor. He had lost control of the Virginia colony to the Patriot militias the previous summer, had retreated to British ships in the Chesapeake, and was now offering a deal. "I do hereby further declare all indented Servants, Negroes, or others (appertaining to Rebels) free, that are able and willing to bear Arms, they joining His Majesty's Troops, as soon as may be, for the more speedily reducing this Colony to a proper Sense of their Duty, to His Majesty's Crown and Dignity."

The proclamation offered freedom to enslaved Africans who would leave their American masters and join the British army to fight against the colonial rebellion. It was, as a piece of British military strategy, a calculated improvisation. It was also the first formal offer of emancipation in North America. Approximately 800 enslaved people made it to Dunmore's lines within months; over the course of the war, by the most careful current estimates, between 80,000 and 100,000 enslaved African Americans escaped to British lines or otherwise used the war's disruption to gain their freedom. Some served in the British forces in named regiments — Dunmore's "Ethiopian Regiment," the Black Loyalist companies in the southern campaigns of 1779–81. Most spent the war in support roles or in the Loyalist refugee communities that gathered around British military positions.

The Dunmore Proclamation is one of the documents that breaks the standard narrative of the Revolution. The story most American readers learn in childhood is a war for liberty fought by colonial Patriots against an imperial British government. The war was that. It was also a domestic civil war in which Loyalists and Patriots fought each other, in which enslaved Africans pursued their own freedom by allying with whichever side promised it (most often the British, since most American Patriot leaders refused on principle to arm enslaved people), and in which Indigenous nations chose sides for their own strategic reasons (most often the British, since the British government had been the institutional protector of the 1763 Proclamation Line against colonial westward expansion).

A more complete picture of the war is one in which there were four major populations doing politics, often at cross purposes. White American Patriots, perhaps a third of the colonial white population. White American Loyalists, perhaps another fifth to a third of the white population. Enslaved Africans, who composed about a fifth of the total colonial population and whose political choices were constrained by their legal status but who acted with as much agency as circumstances allowed. And Indigenous nations — the Iroquois Confederacy, the Cherokee, the Creek, the Shawnee, the Lenape, and others — who fought their own war alongside the European-descended one.

The puzzle of this chapter is what the war was, when it is told without flattening these four populations into a Patriot-versus-British binary. The military narrative is approximately what the standard accounts say: Lexington and Concord in April 1775, the Declaration of Independence in July 1776, the Continental Army's near-collapse and revival under Washington's leadership, the French alliance after Saratoga, the British southern strategy, Yorktown in October 1781, the Treaty of Paris in 1783. The social and political history underneath that narrative is messier and harder to compress.

## The shape of the war

The military conflict ran for eight and a half years from the first shots at Lexington (April 19, 1775) to the British evacuation of New York City (November 25, 1783). It had three roughly defined phases, each with its own theater and its own British strategic objective.

**The northern phase, 1775–1778.** The early war was fought primarily in New England, New York, and the middle colonies. The British evacuated Boston in March 1776 after Henry Knox's artillery, hauled overland from Fort Ticonderoga, made the British position untenable. They returned in force at New York in summer 1776 with about 32,000 troops under General William Howe — the largest single overseas military expedition Britain had ever launched. Howe routed Washington's Continental Army on Long Island in August, took New York City, and held it for the rest of the war. Washington retreated through New Jersey, surprised the British garrison at Trenton on the night of December 25–26, 1776, and won a second small victory at Princeton on January 3, 1777. These victories were not strategically decisive, but they kept the Continental Army in the field and the Patriot cause alive at a moment when both could have collapsed.

The decisive moment of the northern phase was Saratoga in autumn 1777. British General John Burgoyne marched south from Quebec with about 7,800 troops, intending to link up with Howe's forces at Albany and cut New England off from the rest of the colonies. Howe instead moved on Philadelphia, leaving Burgoyne without support. American forces under Horatio Gates surrounded Burgoyne's army near Saratoga, New York, and on October 17, 1777, the British surrendered 5,800 troops. The American victory at Saratoga persuaded France, which had been covertly supporting the American cause since 1776, to commit to an open military alliance. The Treaty of Alliance, signed February 6, 1778, brought France into the war as an American ally. Spain followed in 1779; the Dutch Republic in 1780. The American War of Independence became a global war for the British Empire.

**The southern phase, 1778–1781.** With the war stalemated in the north and France in the conflict, the British adopted a new strategy: take the war south, where the Loyalist population was reportedly larger and where enslaved Africans might be mobilized against their Patriot owners. The southern strategy began with the capture of Savannah, Georgia, in December 1778 and the siege and capture of Charleston, South Carolina, in May 1780 — the largest American military disaster of the war, with about 5,500 Continental troops surrendered. The British then attempted to subdue the Carolina interior under General Cornwallis.

The southern campaign produced some of the bloodiest civil-war fighting of the war. Carolina backcountry militias, some Patriot and some Loyalist, raided each other's communities throughout 1780 and 1781 in a pattern that resembled the partisan wars of nineteenth-century Spain or twentieth-century Greece more than it resembled the formal European-style battles of the northern phase. American General Nathanael Greene, given command of the southern Continental forces in December 1780, conducted a campaign of strategic retreat and selective engagement that wore down Cornwallis's army without ever winning a decisive open battle. By the late summer of 1781, Cornwallis had pulled his army back to Yorktown on the Chesapeake Bay, where he expected resupply by sea.

**The endgame, autumn 1781.** A French fleet under Admiral de Grasse, sailing up from the Caribbean, blocked the entrance to the Chesapeake on September 5, 1781. Washington's army from New York and the French expeditionary force under Rochambeau marched south, joined by Lafayette's Continental troops already in Virginia. The combined Franco-American force besieged Yorktown beginning September 28. On October 19, 1781, Cornwallis surrendered approximately 8,000 troops to the combined American and French armies.

Yorktown did not formally end the war. British forces still occupied New York, Charleston, and Savannah; the British government did not immediately abandon the conflict. But Yorktown ended the British political will to continue. Lord North's government fell in March 1782; the new ministry opened peace negotiations. The Treaty of Paris, signed September 3, 1783, recognized American independence, set the new nation's western boundary at the Mississippi River (subject to ongoing disputes with Spain), and committed the British to evacuate their remaining American positions. The British evacuated New York on November 25, 1783, taking with them the Loyalist refugees and Black Loyalists who had gathered there.

## The four wars at once

The military narrative above is a story about armies. The four overlapping civil wars beneath it are worth distinguishing.

**Patriots versus Loyalists.** Estimates of Loyalist proportion vary widely; the consensus range is that 15 to 20 percent of the white colonial population was actively Loyalist, another 40 to 45 percent actively Patriot, and the remaining 35 to 45 percent attempting to remain neutral. (The much-cited "one-third Patriot, one-third Loyalist, one-third neutral" formulation is approximate but probably overstates the Loyalist share.) The active Loyalists were not a single demographic: they included royal officials, Anglican clergy, merchants with British business connections, recent Scots-Irish immigrants who distrusted the Patriot leadership, frontier farmers who feared Patriot land speculators, Hudson Valley tenant farmers angry at their Patriot landlords, and several other distinct groups. The Revolution's most violent fighting in the south was Patriot-versus-Loyalist militia combat, not Continental-versus-British regular combat. After the war, approximately 60,000 to 80,000 Loyalists left the United States — a substantial share of them to Canada (transforming that province's demographics from predominantly French to predominantly English-speaking), others to England, the Caribbean, or Africa. The Loyalist diaspora is one of the largest political emigrations in early modern history.

**Black Americans' war.** Enslaved Africans pursued freedom through whichever side offered it. Dunmore's Proclamation (November 1775) and the later Philipsburg Proclamation (June 1779, issued by British Commander-in-Chief Sir Henry Clinton in New York) offered freedom to enslaved people who escaped to British lines. The Continental Congress refused, on the urging of Southern delegates, to make a parallel offer. Some northern states — Rhode Island, Massachusetts, Connecticut — eventually allowed enslaved people to enlist in Continental units in exchange for freedom; the First Rhode Island Regiment was substantially Black. Estimates of the number of enslaved people who reached British lines or who otherwise achieved freedom during the war run from a conservative 20,000 to over 100,000; the most careful recent estimate (by historians Cassandra Pybus, Sylvia Frey, and others) is approximately 80,000 to 100,000. Of these, perhaps 3,000 to 4,000 were evacuated with the British in 1782–83 to Nova Scotia, where many lived in segregated communities and several hundred subsequently migrated to Sierra Leone in 1792 to help found the colony that became Freetown. The Black Loyalists of Sierra Leone are the lineal descendants of one of the more remarkable freedom movements of the eighteenth century, and one of the least taught in U.S. survey courses.

**Indigenous nations' wars.** The Indigenous nations east of the Mississippi were political actors with their own strategic interests in the conflict between the British and the American colonies. The Iroquois Confederacy — the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca, and Tuscarora — split in 1777, with most nations supporting the British and the Oneida and Tuscarora supporting the Americans. The civil war within the Confederacy was settled by force; American General John Sullivan's 1779 campaign destroyed forty Iroquois villages and the food supplies that supported them, in a deliberate scorched-earth operation that reduced the Iroquois population by famine and dislocation in the winter of 1779–80. Southern nations made similar calculations: the Cherokee under Dragging Canoe fought against the Americans through the war's southern phase. The Creek attempted to remain neutral but were drawn into the fighting by the partisan civil war in the Carolinas. The Lenape, Shawnee, and other Ohio Valley nations fought a separate war against American frontier settlements that continued past the 1783 Treaty of Paris and would not be settled until the 1795 Treaty of Greenville.

For Indigenous nations the Revolution was a strategic catastrophe. The British government had been the institutional defender of the 1763 Proclamation Line; American independence removed that defender. The Treaty of Paris, signed without Indigenous participation, treated the territory between the Appalachians and the Mississippi as belonging to the new United States — territory that was, in fact, occupied and politically controlled by Indigenous nations. The pattern that followed — American expansion, treaty cycles, displacement — was substantially set by the war's outcome.

**Women's war.** Women's political and material work, as in the prewar protests, was essential to the Patriot cause. They organized fundraising drives (the Ladies' Association of Philadelphia raised $7,500 in gold for the army in 1780, equivalent to perhaps $250,000 today). They maintained farms and businesses while men were at the front. They served as nurses, cooks, and laundresses in army camps (the so-called "camp followers," a derisive term that obscures the essential support functions they performed). Some — Deborah Sampson is the most documented example — disguised themselves and fought as soldiers. The political and social claims women made on the new republic at the war's end — articulated in writings by Abigail Adams, Mercy Otis Warren, and others — anticipated the formal women's rights movements that would emerge in the nineteenth century.

## What independence meant, and what it postponed

The American victory in the Revolutionary War produced a politically independent nation. It did not, in 1783, produce most of the things that the Declaration of Independence had asserted as principles. The Declaration's language — that "all men are created equal" with "unalienable Rights" to "Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness" — was treated as aspiration, not as immediate policy. Two extensions of that principle were contested almost from the moment the war ended.

The first was the question of slavery. Northern states began passing gradual emancipation laws in the 1780s — Pennsylvania in 1780, Massachusetts effectively through court decisions in 1781–83, Connecticut and Rhode Island in 1784, New York in 1799, New Jersey in 1804. Most of these laws emancipated only the children of enslaved women, born after a specific date, when they reached adulthood; the existing enslaved adult population in those states remained enslaved until death. The southern states made no comparable moves. The Constitutional Convention of 1787 would write the institution of slavery into the federal compact in ways the next chapter takes up.

The second was the question of who was a citizen of the new republic. The Declaration's "all men" was, in 1783, a phrase whose extension was contested. Did it include Black men, free or enslaved? Women? Indigenous people? Property-less white men? The answer the new state constitutions of the 1770s and 1780s gave varied by state and by question. Most states restricted suffrage to property-holding men, with substantial variation in what counted as sufficient property. Some — New Jersey's 1776 constitution being the most prominent — initially allowed women and free Black men to vote if they met property qualifications; New Jersey rescinded these rights in 1807. The republic that the war produced was, in 1783, narrower than the principles it had been founded on.

These questions — what slavery would mean in the new nation, who would be a citizen, what kind of federal structure would govern thirteen states with sharply divergent economies — were the political work of the decade after the war. They are the work of Chapter 7.
