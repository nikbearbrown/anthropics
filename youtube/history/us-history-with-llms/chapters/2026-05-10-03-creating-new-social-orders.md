# Chapter 3 — Creating New Social Orders: Colonial Societies, 1500–1700

*How race became a legal category.*

In September 1676, an army of about a thousand men under the command of Nathaniel Bacon burned the colonial capital of Virginia to the ground. The army was a coalition. It included indentured English servants, recently freed servants who had failed to find land, frontier farmers angry about the colonial government's tax policies, and a contingent of Africans — some enslaved, some indentured, some free. They had been fighting a campaign against Native Americans on the Virginia frontier that the colonial governor, William Berkeley, had refused to authorize. When Berkeley moved against Bacon, Bacon marched on Jamestown, burned the colonial buildings, and took control of much of the colony for several months. Bacon died of dysentery in October. The rebellion collapsed without him. Berkeley returned, hanged twenty-three of the rebel leaders, and was recalled to England in disgrace, where he died within a year.

The historian Edmund Morgan, in *American Slavery, American Freedom* (1975), argued that Bacon's Rebellion is the most important event in seventeenth-century North America for what came after it — not because the rebellion itself accomplished anything lasting, but because of what it taught the Virginia planter elite. The rebellion's terrifying feature, from the planters' perspective, was that it had been multiracial. Poor English servants and enslaved or unfree Africans had armed themselves together against the colonial government. If that coalition could form once, it could form again. The planter class's response, over the next thirty years, was to engineer a legal and economic distinction between English-descended workers (who were given new rights and a path to freedom) and African-descended workers (whose status was hardened into permanent, hereditary, racial slavery). Race, as a legal category in American law, was substantially built in the half-century after 1676.

The puzzle of this chapter is how that engineering happened. We are going to walk through the specific statutes in specific years, because the legal codification of race-based slavery in the English colonies is one of those moments where the abstract argument — *race is a social construct* — can be made concretely. There are dates. There are statute numbers. There are named legislatures voting on specific texts. The construction is visible in the public record.

Before that, though, we have to deal with the framing problem. The English colonies that became the United States were not the only colonial society in the Americas, and the institutions that took shape in Virginia and Massachusetts did not develop in isolation. They developed alongside — and in competition with — Spanish, French, Dutch, and a half-dozen other colonial systems, each of which produced its own answers to the same labor-and-status problems. Some of those answers were different in instructive ways.

## Four colonial systems, not one

The Americas in 1650 hosted at least four distinguishable European colonial systems, each with its own internal logic. Treating them as variations on a single theme — "European colonization" — flattens out the most interesting differences. The differences explain a lot.

**The Spanish system** was the oldest and the largest. By 1650, Spanish colonial governments administered an empire that stretched from northern New Mexico to southern Chile, with major urban centers at Mexico City, Lima, Bogotá, Quito, and Buenos Aires. The Spanish empire was administered through a centralized bureaucracy reporting through colonial *viceroys* to the Council of the Indies in Madrid. The economic engine was silver mining — primarily at Potosí (in present-day Bolivia) and Zacatecas (in Mexico) — extracted with forced indigenous labor through the *mita* (a corvée system inherited from the Inca) and the *repartimiento* (a draft labor system that replaced the *encomienda* after the 1542 New Laws). The Spanish empire also produced a complex racial classification system, the *casta* system, that named legal categories for the children of mixed unions: *mestizo* (Spanish and Indigenous), *mulato* (Spanish and African), *zambo* (Indigenous and African), and dozens of finer-grained terms. The *casta* system was a hierarchy — Spanish-descended persons were at the top, Indigenous and African-descended persons at the bottom — but it was a continuous gradient with intermediate legal statuses, not a binary. Spanish colonial society was also Catholic, which meant the church played central roles in education, charity, marriage, and the legal regulation of intimate life.

**The Portuguese system** in Brazil was structurally similar to the Spanish system but specialized differently. The Brazilian colony was organized around sugar plantations on the northeastern coast, worked from the late sixteenth century onward primarily with enslaved Africans. The Portuguese had access to the West African slave-trade ports they had built in the fifteenth century, and by 1650 Brazil was importing more enslaved Africans than any other colony in the Atlantic world. Brazil received approximately 5 million Africans across the full span of the slave trade, more than ten times what the British North American colonies received. Brazilian colonial society also produced a racial classification system with multiple intermediate categories, similar to the Spanish *casta*.

**The French system** in North America was, by 1650, modest. New France — centered on the St. Lawrence River valley, with major settlements at Quebec (founded 1608) and Montreal (founded 1642) — had a settler population of perhaps three thousand in 1650. The economic engine was the fur trade, which depended on partnership with Indigenous peoples (primarily the Huron until their dispersal in the 1640s, then the Algonquin and others) rather than displacement of them. French *coureurs de bois* traveled into the interior to trade with Indigenous nations and frequently married Indigenous women, producing the *Métis* communities that became significant political actors in the Great Lakes region and the prairies. New France was Catholic and patriarchal, but it was demographically too small to displace Indigenous populations the way the English colonies eventually did. The Mississippi River valley territory France would later claim was, throughout the seventeenth century, primarily Indigenous land that French traders and missionaries passed through on terms set by the people who lived there.

**The Dutch system** in New Netherland (centered on New Amsterdam, the future New York City) was the smallest of the major systems. Founded in 1624 as a commercial venture of the Dutch West India Company, it functioned primarily as a trading post for furs from the Hudson River valley and as a transhipment point in the Dutch Caribbean trade. The Dutch West India Company brought enslaved Africans to New Amsterdam from 1626 onward — the labor for the wall that gave Wall Street its name was performed by enslaved Africans owned by the company. New Netherland was the most ethnically diverse of the seventeenth-century North American colonies. By the 1650s an observer reported eighteen languages spoken on Manhattan streets, and the colony admitted Sephardic Jews from Brazil in 1654, after the Portuguese reconquered Dutch Brazil. The colony's population in 1664, when it was seized by the English and renamed New York, was about 9,000 — a fraction of New England's already.

**The English colonies** in 1650 consisted of two distinct subsystems with different economies and different social structures. The Chesapeake colonies — Virginia (founded 1607) and Maryland (founded 1634) — were tobacco-export economies, settled primarily by single young men coming as indentured servants on labor contracts that bound them for four to seven years in exchange for transatlantic passage. The Chesapeake colonies were demographically male-dominated, had high mortality rates, and depended for their labor force on a continuous inflow of indentured servants from England. The New England colonies — Plymouth (founded 1620), Massachusetts Bay (1630), Connecticut (1636), Rhode Island (1636), and New Haven (1638) — were settled primarily by family units, often migrating together as religious communities. Their economies were subsistence farming, fishing, lumber, and shipping. They had lower mortality, more balanced sex ratios, and reproduced demographically through natural increase rather than through immigration after the first major migration of the 1630s. By 1650 the New England colonies had a combined population of about 25,000.

The relevant point for what follows is that the Chesapeake colonies in 1650 — Virginia in particular — were the place where the labor question would force a decision. By 1670 the supply of English indentured servants was tightening: wages were rising in England, opportunities for the English poor were better at home than they had been mid-century, and fewer were willing to take Chesapeake contracts. The planters who had been depending on indentured labor needed an alternative. The alternative they chose was enslaved Africans, in numbers that grew sharply after 1680. The question of how that labor force would be legally classified was the question Bacon's Rebellion forced.

## The mechanism: how race became law

In 1619 a Dutch ship sold "twenty and odd" Africans to colonists at Jamestown — the first documented arrival of Africans in what became British North America. The legal status of those people is contested in the surviving records. Some appear to have been treated as indentured servants on the English model — bound for a term of years and then freed. Others were treated as enslaved for life. Early Virginia did not have a coherent legal category for "enslaved person" that was distinct from the existing categories of indentured servant, apprentice, and household dependent. The legal infrastructure that produced American chattel slavery had to be built. It was built in dated steps. The steps are visible in the Virginia statutes.

**1640: *Punch v. Virginia.*** A Virginia court considered the case of three indentured servants — two white men (a Scotsman and a Dutchman) and one Black man (John Punch) — who had run away from their master and been recaptured. The court sentenced all three to additional time on their indentures. The two white men received an additional four years. John Punch was sentenced to serve his master "for the time of his natural life." This is the earliest documented case in the Virginia colonial record where a court explicitly distinguished penalty on the basis of race. The case did not establish a general rule. It established a precedent.

**1662: *Partus sequitur ventrem.*** The Virginia House of Burgesses passed a statute declaring that "children gotten by any Englishman upon a negro woman shall be bond or free according to the condition of the mother." In a single sentence the Virginia legislature reversed the common-law principle that a child's legal status followed the father (*partus sequitur patrem*) and replaced it with its opposite (*partus sequitur ventrem* — "offspring follows the womb"). The change was specifically reasoned. Children fathered by English colonists on enslaved African women would be enslaved at birth. The change had two consequences. First, it made the institution self-reproducing — the children of enslaved women became part of the enslaved labor force without any need for additional importation. Second, it removed any deterrent against sexual exploitation of enslaved women by colonial men; pregnancies that resulted increased the slaveholder's property rather than diminishing it. *Partus sequitur ventrem* was a single statute that changed the entire economic structure of American slavery.

**1667: *Conversion does not equal manumission.*** The Virginia legislature passed a statute declaring that baptism into Christianity did not change an enslaved person's legal status. The statute existed because some enslaved Africans had used conversion to Christianity as grounds for petitioning for their freedom, and some courts had accepted the argument that one Christian could not enslave another. The 1667 statute closed that loophole. From this point forward, conversion in Virginia was legally severed from emancipation.

**1670: *Free Black persons cannot purchase white servants.*** A statute restricted property in human beings along racial lines. Earlier in the seventeenth century, some free Black people in Virginia (men who had been indentured servants, completed their terms, and become small landowners) had themselves owned indentured English servants. The 1670 statute prohibited this. Property in servants was now racially restricted.

**1691: *Banishment for interracial marriage.*** A statute decreed that "whatsoever English or other white man or woman being free shall intermarry with a negro, mulatto, or Indian man or woman bond or free shall within three months after such marriage be banished and removed from this dominion forever." This statute is the earliest American antimiscegenation law. Such statutes would proliferate across the colonies and the states over the next two and a half centuries; the last of them was struck down by the Supreme Court in *Loving v. Virginia* (1967), 276 years after the Virginia legislature first wrote the prohibition.

**1705: *Slave Code consolidation.*** Virginia revised and consolidated its slavery laws into a comprehensive Slave Code. The 1705 code defined enslaved persons as "real estate" — that is, real property, the same legal category as land and buildings — for purposes of inheritance and transfer. It also formalized the distinction between "white" servants (whose terms were limited and whose persons remained legally distinct from livestock) and "Negro" or "mulatto" slaves (who were legally property, hereditarily, for life).

These six statutes — spread over sixty-five years — built the legal architecture of racial chattel slavery in the English colonies. Other colonies followed Virginia's lead with their own statutes through the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries. By 1720, every English colony in North America had legal codes that recognized hereditary, racial, chattel slavery as a coherent institutional category distinct from indentured servitude. The institution was not inherited from English common law (which had not recognized chattel slavery in this form); it was not borrowed from West African practices (which did not include racial heritability); it was built, in dated steps, by colonial legislatures responding to the labor and political problems they faced.

The point this mechanism makes is one this book will return to repeatedly. American chattel slavery was not "always there." It was constructed, in specific places, through specific legal decisions, over a specific period of time. The construction is visible in the documentary record. It is the result of human beings making decisions, not of timeless inevitable processes. The same set of choices could have been made differently. They were not.

## The patterns the seventeenth century settled

By the end of the seventeenth century, the structure of the British North American colonies had largely consolidated around three regional patterns that would persist for the next century and a half.

In the **Chesapeake** — Virginia, Maryland, eventually North Carolina — tobacco was the cash crop, large landholdings were the social structure, and enslaved African labor was the workforce after about 1680. The Chesapeake had a small enslaved population (perhaps 15,000 by 1700, growing rapidly) and a larger free white population, structured into a stratified society with a planter elite at the top, yeoman farmers in the middle, and indentured or formerly indentured laborers near the bottom. The Anglican Church was the established religion. Politics was dominated by the planters who controlled the colonial assemblies.

In **New England** — Massachusetts, Connecticut, Rhode Island, New Hampshire — the pattern was different. Congregational church communities organized into towns formed the basic social and political unit. Land was held in smaller parcels by family farmers. The economy was diversified: subsistence farming, fishing, shipping, lumber, small-scale manufacturing. Enslaved Africans were present (the New England colonies were never slavery-free) but in much smaller proportions than in the Chesapeake — perhaps 2 to 3 percent of the population by 1700, versus 15 to 20 percent in the Chesapeake. New England's wealth from the Atlantic trade was substantial; Boston merchants made fortunes in the slave trade itself, even though their home communities used relatively little enslaved labor.

In the **Middle colonies** — New York (the renamed New Netherland after 1664), New Jersey, Pennsylvania (founded 1681), Delaware — a hybrid pattern emerged. Religious diversity was greater than in either of the other regions. Pennsylvania, founded by William Penn as a Quaker refuge, became the most ethnically and religiously diverse of the seventeenth-century colonies; by 1700 Philadelphia was a port city with German, Welsh, Scotch-Irish, Dutch, and English communities, along with smaller groups of Jews and freethinkers. The Middle colonies' economy combined commercial agriculture (wheat especially) with port-city trade. Enslaved Africans were present in numbers between the Chesapeake's and New England's — perhaps 8 to 12 percent of the population by 1700.

The regional pattern was not destiny — colonies changed across the next century and a half — but it set the conditions under which the eighteenth-century British Empire developed in North America. The Chesapeake's plantation economy depended on enslaved African labor and political power proportional to landholding. New England's commercial-yeoman economy produced a different political culture: town meetings, congregational governance, smaller-scale wealth. The Middle colonies sat between. When the political conflicts of the eighteenth century produced the American Revolution and then the Constitution, the divergent interests of these regions would shape every major compromise the new country made.
