# Chapter 28 — Post-War Prosperity and Cold War Fears, 1945–1960

*A boom built on federal subsidies that excluded some Americans by design.*

In June 1944, six months before the German surrender and fourteen months before the Japanese, President Roosevelt signed the Servicemen's Readjustment Act — the *GI Bill*. The Act provided returning veterans with educational benefits (tuition, fees, books, and a living stipend at any college or university that would admit them), unemployment compensation for up to fifty-two weeks while looking for civilian work, and federally-guaranteed mortgages at favorable rates for home purchase. The educational benefit alone would, across the following decade, fund the college education of approximately 7.8 million veterans — about half of the men who had served in the war. The mortgage benefit would fund approximately 2.4 million home purchases by 1955. The GI Bill was, by most measures, one of the most successful pieces of federal social legislation in American history. The federal expenditure on the educational benefit alone produced returns to the U.S. Treasury (through subsequent income-tax revenues from veterans whose lifetime earnings were substantially increased by their education) that exceeded the program's costs by a factor of seven, by the most careful subsequent calculations.

The GI Bill, like the Social Security Act of 1935, was administered in ways that substantially excluded Black veterans from its benefits. The educational benefit was technically race-neutral; in practice, Southern universities (where most Black veterans lived) excluded Black applicants, and Northern universities admitted Black veterans in numbers substantially below the proportions of Black veterans in the eligible population. The mortgage benefit was technically race-neutral; in practice, the Federal Housing Administration's underwriting standards excluded predominantly-Black neighborhoods from mortgage insurance (the redlining maps that had emerged in the 1930s continued operating across the post-war period), and white-flight suburban developments that absorbed most post-war federal housing subsidies were structured by racially-restrictive covenants that excluded Black buyers. The historian Ira Katznelson's *When Affirmative Action Was White* (2005) documents the systematic ways the GI Bill's benefits flowed disproportionately to white veterans and substantially built the post-war white-middle-class prosperity while excluding Black veterans from similar benefits. The post-war wealth gap between white and Black Americans — which by 2025 has been measured at approximately 6 to 1 (white household median net worth approximately $185,000; Black household median net worth approximately $33,000) — was substantially produced by the racially-disparate operation of federal post-war programs across 1944–60.

This chapter is about the post-war American boom and the institutions — federal, military, cultural — that produced and structured it. The puzzle of the chapter is how the United States simultaneously achieved unprecedented mass prosperity and built systematic exclusion into the prosperity's institutional infrastructure. The two are not separable: the post-war boom for white middle-class Americans was substantially constructed by federal programs that, in their operational specifics, were not available on equal terms to Black, Mexican-American, or Indigenous Americans.

## The shape of post-war prosperity

The American economic position in the immediate post-war years was substantially better than in any other major industrial economy. The American physical infrastructure had not been damaged by the war; the European and Japanese physical infrastructures had been substantially destroyed. The American economy emerged from the war producing approximately 50 percent of world manufacturing output. American real GDP per capita grew by approximately 60 percent between 1945 and 1960. American household incomes substantially exceeded the household incomes of any other industrialized country. The American working class entered, for the first time in American history, a sustained period of mass middle-class consumption.

The institutional infrastructure of the boom is worth naming because the post-war prosperity is sometimes treated in popular accounts as a natural outcome of American economic capacity rather than as the product of specific federal programs.

**The GI Bill** as described above.

**Federal housing finance.** The Federal Housing Administration (created 1934) and the Veterans Administration (which administered the GI Bill mortgage benefits) substantially structured American housing finance across the post-war years. The FHA's standardized mortgage products (the 30-year fixed-rate mortgage that has become the standard American home loan) were federal innovations; private mortgage lending before the FHA had typically required 50-percent down payments and 5-to-10-year terms. The federal mortgage insurance allowed lenders to offer 20-percent down payments and 30-year terms, which substantially expanded home ownership. By 1960, approximately 62 percent of American households were home owners, up from approximately 44 percent in 1940.

**Federal highway construction.** The Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956, signed by President Eisenhower, authorized federal construction of the Interstate Highway System — approximately 41,000 miles of limited-access highways across the United States, at federal cost of approximately $114 billion (about $1.2 trillion in modern terms). The Interstate Highway System was substantially the largest federal infrastructure project in American history. Its construction substantially produced the post-war suburban-development pattern (suburbs accessible by highway to central-city employment) and substantially restructured American freight transportation (truck-based long-distance freight largely displaced railroad freight across the post-war decades).

**The defense industrial base.** Federal defense spending across the Cold War years (approximately 6 to 10 percent of GDP across most of the period) substantially funded American industrial development. The aerospace industry, the computer industry, the nuclear industry, the chemicals industry, and substantial parts of the electronics industry developed across the 1950s and 1960s with major federal defense contracts. The "military-industrial complex" that President Eisenhower warned about in his January 1961 farewell address was substantially the product of post-war federal economic policy.

**Federal research funding.** The National Science Foundation (established 1950), the National Institutes of Health (substantially expanded across the post-war years), and the federal defense and energy departments substantially funded American scientific and technological research. The post-war American scientific dominance — which produced major innovations in biology, physics, chemistry, and engineering across the post-war decades — was substantially built with federal research funding.

The federal-government role in the post-war prosperity was substantial and is sometimes substantially elided in popular accounts that emphasize the private-sector character of American economic growth. The post-war boom was, in important ways, a federal-government project.

## The Cold War

The post-war international system that the United States built was substantially organized around the political-military confrontation with the Soviet Union that emerged across 1945–48.

**The origins of the Cold War.** The Soviet-American alliance of 1941–45 had been a tactical wartime cooperation rather than a substantive political-ideological alignment. The post-war political-economic visions of the two governments were substantially incompatible: the United States wanted an open international economic system structured by the Bretton Woods institutions; the Soviet Union wanted security through the political control of Eastern Europe and through state-managed economic integration of the Soviet-bloc countries. The political-military conflicts of 1945–48 — over the political composition of post-war Eastern European governments, over the partition of Germany, over the status of Iran, Turkey, and Greece — substantially produced the political framework of the Cold War.

The historiographic debate on the Cold War's origins is contested. The traditional American framing (the Cold War was caused by Soviet aggressive expansionism, with American policy substantially reactive) was substantially challenged by the revisionist historiography of the 1960s and 1970s (William Appleman Williams, Walter LaFeber, Gabriel Kolko, others) which argued that American policy was substantially driven by economic-expansionist interests and that the Cold War was produced by mutual rather than unilateral aggressive policy. The post-revisionist synthesis (John Lewis Gaddis, others) argues that the Cold War was substantially produced by misperception, security-dilemma dynamics, and the substantive incompatibility of the two political-economic systems. The historiography continues to be active; the post-1991 opening of Soviet archives has produced substantial new documentation on the Soviet side of the conflict.

**The Truman Doctrine and containment.** The March 1947 announcement by President Truman that the United States would support "free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or by outside pressures" — articulated in the specific context of the Greek civil war and Turkish-Soviet tensions, but applied more broadly across the following decades — became the framework of American foreign policy across the Cold War. The doctrine of *containment*, articulated in George Kennan's "Long Telegram" of February 1946 and subsequently elaborated in his "Mr. X" article in *Foreign Affairs* in July 1947, became the conceptual framework. The implementation included the Marshall Plan (1948–52) for European economic reconstruction; the North Atlantic Treaty (1949) for collective security; the substantial expansion of American military commitments globally.

**The Korean War.** The first major hot war of the Cold War period ran from June 1950 to July 1953. North Korean forces, with substantial Soviet and Chinese support, invaded South Korea in June 1950. American forces, operating under United Nations authorization (which the Soviet Union allowed to pass by boycotting the Security Council in protest of the seat being held by the Republic of China rather than the People's Republic), substantially repelled the invasion. The Chinese intervention in October 1950 substantially extended the war. The war ended with an armistice in July 1953 that substantially restored the pre-war boundary. American military deaths were approximately 36,000; Korean military and civilian deaths were approximately 3 million on the two sides combined. The war substantially institutionalized American Cold War military commitments — peacetime military spending approximately tripled across 1950–53 and would remain at substantially higher levels for the rest of the Cold War.

**McCarthyism and the Second Red Scare.** The domestic political-cultural environment of the early Cold War produced the most substantial American civil-liberties contraction since the First World War. The House Un-American Activities Committee (HUAC, established 1938 but particularly active across 1947–55), the Senate Internal Security Subcommittee, and Senator Joseph McCarthy's subcommittee operations across 1950–54 substantially investigated alleged Communist influence in the federal government, in Hollywood, in universities, in labor unions, and in cultural institutions. The political-cultural effect was substantial: a generation of left-leaning Americans was substantially purged from public life; American cultural production was substantially constrained by political-loyalty requirements; the federal-employee loyalty programs administered by the Truman and Eisenhower administrations across 1947–60 substantially restricted employment for thousands of people. McCarthy's specific political position collapsed across 1954 (the Army-McCarthy hearings of April–June 1954 produced the public exposure of his demagogic methods; he was censured by the Senate in December 1954 and died of alcoholism-related illness in 1957); the broader political-cultural framework substantially persisted into the 1960s.

The Cold War would continue to shape American foreign and domestic policy through 1991. The post-war American boom and the Cold War were the parallel transformations that substantially constructed the second half of the twentieth-century American experience.

## The early Civil Rights movement

The post-war years also produced the early phase of what would become the major Civil Rights movement of the 1950s and 1960s.

**Truman's civil-rights initiatives.** President Truman's administration produced several substantial civil-rights actions despite the constraints of the Southern-Democratic political coalition. The President's Committee on Civil Rights (established 1946) produced the *To Secure These Rights* report of 1947, which recommended substantial federal civil-rights legislation. Truman's Executive Order 9981 (July 1948) desegregated the U.S. armed forces — a federal action that did not require congressional support and that substantially produced the integration of the military across 1948–54. The Democratic Party's 1948 platform included substantial civil-rights provisions, producing the Dixiecrat walkout from the convention and the third-party presidential candidacy of South Carolina governor Strom Thurmond, who won 39 electoral votes in the 1948 election on a segregationist platform.

**The NAACP legal-strategy campaign.** The NAACP's Legal Defense Fund (LDF), organized as a separate institution from the NAACP in 1940 and led across the 1940s and 1950s by Thurgood Marshall, conducted a sustained legal campaign against the legal infrastructure of segregation. The strategy involved a series of test cases against segregated graduate and professional education (*Murray v. Pearson*, 1936, on the University of Maryland law school; *Missouri ex rel. Gaines v. Canada*, 1938, on the University of Missouri law school; *Sweatt v. Painter*, 1950, on the University of Texas law school; *McLaurin v. Oklahoma State Regents*, 1950, on the University of Oklahoma graduate school), culminating in *Brown v. Board of Education* (1954), which substantially overturned *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896) and the "separate but equal" doctrine that had governed federal segregation jurisprudence for fifty-eight years.

**The implementation of *Brown*.** The 1954 *Brown* decision held that segregated public schools were unconstitutional under the Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause. The 1955 follow-up decision (*Brown II*) instructed lower federal courts to oversee desegregation "with all deliberate speed" — a phrase that allowed substantial Southern resistance across the following decade. The Southern Manifesto of March 1956, signed by 19 senators and 82 House members from the former Confederate states, declared *Brown* to be an unconstitutional usurpation of state authority and called for systematic resistance. The Little Rock crisis of September 1957, in which Arkansas Governor Orval Faubus used the state National Guard to prevent nine Black students from entering Little Rock Central High School and President Eisenhower responded by federalizing the Guard and sending the 101st Airborne Division to enforce the desegregation order, was the most-publicized confrontation of the early *Brown* implementation period.

**The Montgomery bus boycott.** Rosa Parks's December 1955 arrest for refusing to give up her bus seat to a white passenger in Montgomery, Alabama, produced the bus boycott that ran from December 1955 to December 1956 and that substantially launched the public phase of the Civil Rights movement. The Montgomery Improvement Association, organized to coordinate the boycott, was the institution through which Martin Luther King Jr. — then a twenty-six-year-old Baptist minister in his first major pastorate — emerged as the public leader of the movement. The Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC), founded in 1957, institutionalized the boycott's organizational model on a regional basis. The Civil Rights movement of the late 1950s through the mid-1960s would substantially shape the next chapter's politics.

The early Civil Rights movement of the post-war years built on the institutional infrastructure that Black Americans had constructed across the previous half-century — the NAACP, the Black churches, the Black colleges and universities, the Black newspapers and magazines, the Black labor organizations (A. Philip Randolph's Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters had been central to the 1941 March on Washington Movement that produced the wartime FEPC). The movement's substantial achievements would not come until the 1960s. The institutional and political foundation was substantially built across the 1940s and 1950s.
