# Chapter 30 — Political Storms at Home and Abroad, 1968–1980

*A decade in which the political coalitions of the previous generation came apart.*

On the night of June 17, 1972, five men were arrested inside the Democratic National Committee headquarters at the Watergate office complex in Washington, D.C. They had been installing surveillance equipment and photographing documents. The arrest was, at the time, a minor news story. The investigation that followed — conducted across more than two years by the FBI, by the *Washington Post* reporters Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein, by the federal grand jury under U.S. District Court Judge John Sirica, and ultimately by the Senate Watergate Committee under Senator Sam Ervin and the House Judiciary Committee under Representative Peter Rodino — substantially destroyed the Nixon presidency. The investigation traced the burglary to the Committee to Re-Elect the President (CRP), to senior White House staff, and ultimately to President Nixon himself. The disclosure of the White House taping system in July 1973 — by White House staffer Alexander Butterfield's testimony to the Senate Watergate Committee — and the subsequent legal battle over the tapes (resolved by the unanimous Supreme Court decision in *United States v. Nixon*, July 24, 1974, requiring Nixon to release the tapes) produced the evidence that ended the presidency. Nixon resigned on August 9, 1974 — the only American president to resign — rather than face certain impeachment by the House and almost-certain conviction by the Senate.

Watergate is, in its specifics, one of the cleaner stories in twentieth-century American politics. The political-cultural significance was substantially larger than the specifics. The Nixon administration's specific crimes — burglary, obstruction of justice, illegal campaign-finance practices, abuse of federal investigative agencies for political purposes — were elements of a broader pattern of executive-branch behavior that the post-Watergate reform legislation attempted to constrain (the War Powers Resolution of 1973, the Budget and Impoundment Control Act of 1974, the Ethics in Government Act of 1978, the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act of 1978, the Freedom of Information Act amendments of 1974, the Federal Election Campaign Act amendments of 1974). The substantive political-cultural effect of Watergate was a substantial decline in American public confidence in federal institutions that has not recovered. Gallup polling showed approximately 75 percent of Americans expressed substantial trust in the federal government in 1964; the figure fell to approximately 33 percent by 1976 and has fluctuated in the 20–40 percent range since.

This chapter is about the decade between the 1968 election and the 1980 election — the period in which the political coalitions of the previous half-century came apart and the political conditions for the subsequent conservative-era politics were assembled. The puzzle of this chapter is what produced the partisan realignment that 1968 began and that 1980 substantially consolidated.

## The Nixon administration

Nixon's domestic policy was substantially less conservative than his rhetorical framing might suggest. The Environmental Protection Agency (created by executive order in 1970), the Clean Air Act of 1970, the Clean Water Act of 1972, the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (1970), the Endangered Species Act of 1973, the Comprehensive Employment and Training Act (CETA, 1973), and the expansion of Social Security benefits through automatic cost-of-living adjustments (1972) were substantial domestic policy achievements that operated within the New Deal-Great Society federal-government framework rather than against it. The Nixon administration also proposed (though did not enact) a Family Assistance Plan that would have substantially expanded federal direct cash payments to low-income families on terms that were, in some respects, more generous than the existing welfare framework.

The Nixon administration's substantial political innovations were in foreign policy and in the politics of race and culture. The opening to China — Henry Kissinger's secret July 1971 visit to Beijing, followed by Nixon's February 1972 trip — substantially restructured the international system by formalizing American relations with the People's Republic of China after 22 years of non-recognition. The détente with the Soviet Union — the Strategic Arms Limitation Treaty (SALT I) of 1972, the Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty of the same year — substantially modified the Cold War framework. The administration's withdrawal from the Vietnam War, completed in March 1973 with the final American combat troops leaving under the Paris Peace Accords, was the political endpoint of the American military commitment.

**The Southern Strategy** was the political framework by which the Nixon administration substantially shifted the Republican Party's electoral base. The strategy — articulated by Nixon's political advisors Kevin Phillips (in *The Emerging Republican Majority*, 1969) and others — recognized that the Civil Rights and Voting Rights Acts had substantially destroyed the Democratic Party's century-long hold on white Southern voters. The Republican Party could substantially absorb these voters by adopting positions that signaled cultural-political sympathy without (in the explicit formulation) being overtly racist. The framework operated through coded appeals to "law and order," "states' rights," "neighborhood schools" (opposition to school busing for desegregation), "welfare reform" (criticism of programs that were popularly associated with Black recipients), and similar themes. The strategy was substantively successful: white Southern voters substantially shifted from Democratic to Republican allegiance across 1968–80, and the former Confederate states became substantially Republican in presidential elections after 1968 and substantially Republican at all electoral levels by the 1990s and 2000s.

The Southern Strategy's substantive content was systematically race-based, though the rhetorical packaging substantially obscured this. Lee Atwater, the Republican political operative who managed the 1988 George H.W. Bush campaign, gave a famous (and originally anonymous) 1981 interview in which he explicitly described the strategy: "You start out in 1954 by saying, 'nigger, nigger, nigger.' By 1968 you can't say 'nigger' — that hurts you. Backfires. So you say stuff like forced busing, states' rights and all that stuff. You're getting so abstract now you're talking about cutting taxes, and all these things you're talking about are totally economic things and a by-product of them is, blacks get hurt worse than whites... 'We want to cut this' is much more abstract than even the busing thing, and a hell of a lot more abstract than 'nigger, nigger.'" The Atwater quote is one of the clearest contemporary articulations of the Southern Strategy's mechanism.

## Watergate and its aftermath

The Watergate scandal substantially destroyed the Nixon presidency and substantially shaped American political culture for the following half-century. The specific facts of the scandal have been substantially documented; the broader political-cultural effects are worth naming.

**The institutional reforms.** The post-Watergate Congress passed substantial legislation aimed at constraining the executive branch's capacity for abuse:

- The War Powers Resolution (November 1973, passed over Nixon's veto) required presidential consultation with Congress before committing American military forces to hostilities and required congressional authorization for continued operations beyond 60 days. The Resolution has been substantially contested in practice — most subsequent presidents have asserted that it is unconstitutional — but it has shaped the political-legal framework of post-1973 American military operations.

- The Budget and Impoundment Control Act of 1974 substantially restructured the congressional budget process and constrained presidential authority to impound (decline to spend) appropriated funds.

- The Freedom of Information Act amendments of 1974 substantially expanded public access to federal-government records.

- The Federal Election Campaign Act amendments of 1974 established public financing for presidential elections, limited individual campaign contributions, and created the Federal Election Commission.

- The Ethics in Government Act of 1978 created the Office of Government Ethics and required substantial financial disclosure from federal officials.

- The Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act of 1978 established the FISA Court and substantial procedural framework for federal foreign-intelligence surveillance.

- The Inspector General Act of 1978 created independent inspectors general in federal agencies.

The post-Watergate institutional reforms substantially constrained the executive branch's capacity for the kinds of abuse that had produced Watergate. The reforms have been partially eroded across subsequent decades — the post-9/11 expansions of executive authority substantially restored some of the constraints — but the institutional framework established in the 1970s has shaped American political-legal practice across the following half-century.

**The political-cultural effects.** Watergate substantially destroyed American public confidence in federal institutions in ways that the subsequent decades have not corrected. The press's substantial role in the Watergate investigation — particularly the *Washington Post*'s coverage — substantially elevated investigative journalism as an American political institution; the journalistic generation that came of age during Watergate substantially shaped American political journalism for the following half-century. The political-cultural framework of "scandal investigation" — in which alleged executive-branch wrongdoing produced congressional investigation, special-prosecutor appointments, and substantial media coverage — became substantially institutionalized after Watergate; subsequent scandals (Iran-Contra in 1986–87, the Clinton impeachment of 1998, the Trump impeachments of 2019 and 2021) substantially operated within the framework Watergate established.

## The end of the Vietnam War

The Vietnam War ended for the United States with the Paris Peace Accords of January 1973 and the final withdrawal of American combat forces in March 1973. The North Vietnamese substantially violated the Accords across 1973–75 and the Saigon government collapsed in April 1975 with the North Vietnamese capture of Saigon (the iconic image of the war's end was the April 29–30 evacuation of Americans and South Vietnamese collaborators by helicopter from the U.S. Embassy roof). The war ended with substantial American failure in its stated political objective (preventing the unification of Vietnam under North Vietnamese-communist control) but with the American military commitment ended.

The war's substantive consequences for American political culture were durable. The "Vietnam syndrome" — the American political-cultural reluctance to commit American military forces to major foreign operations without substantial public support — substantially shaped American foreign-policy options across the late 1970s and 1980s. The Reagan administration's substantial efforts to overcome the Vietnam syndrome (the Grenada invasion of 1983, the Lebanon intervention of 1982–83, the substantial military buildup of the 1980s) produced partial recovery of American willingness to project military force; the post-Vietnam political-cultural framework would not be substantially overcome until the Persian Gulf War of 1991.

The war's substantive consequences for Vietnam and Southeast Asia were substantial. The unified communist government's policies across 1975–86 produced substantial economic dislocation, political repression of South Vietnamese former officials and military personnel (substantial "reeducation camp" internment), and the major refugee outflow ("boat people") of the late 1970s. The Khmer Rouge's takeover of Cambodia in April 1975 produced the Cambodian genocide of 1975–79 (approximately 1.5 to 2 million Cambodian deaths under Pol Pot's regime, primarily from execution, forced labor, and starvation). The broader Southeast Asian political-economic system was substantially destabilized for at least a decade.

## The economic-political crisis of the 1970s

The 1970s produced the most substantial American economic-political crisis since the 1930s.

**The energy crises.** The October 1973 OPEC oil embargo, imposed in response to American military support for Israel during the Yom Kippur War, substantially restricted American oil imports and produced an immediate fourfold increase in oil prices. The 1979 oil crisis, triggered by the Iranian Revolution, produced a second substantial price increase. The two crises substantially transformed the American economy: industries dependent on cheap petroleum (the American automobile industry centrally) lost competitive position to foreign competitors with more fuel-efficient products; energy-intensive industrial production substantially relocated; American household budgets were substantially reorganized around higher energy costs.

**Stagflation.** The combination of substantial inflation (American consumer-price-index inflation peaked at approximately 13.5 percent in 1980) and substantial unemployment (which rose from approximately 4 percent in the early 1970s to approximately 9 percent in 1975) was a structural combination that the dominant Keynesian economic framework had not predicted as possible. The framework had assumed that inflation and unemployment would be substantially inversely related — the Phillips Curve relationship. The 1970s' simultaneous high inflation and high unemployment substantially destabilized the Keynesian framework. The Federal Reserve's response, under Chairman Paul Volcker (appointed by Carter in 1979), was substantially tight monetary policy that drove interest rates to record levels (the prime rate reached 21.5 percent in December 1980) and produced the substantial recession of 1981–82, which substantially ended the inflationary cycle but at the cost of substantial unemployment.

**Deindustrialization.** The 1970s produced the substantial decline of American heavy manufacturing — steel, automobiles, machine tools, textiles, shipbuilding — that had been the foundation of the post-war American economic position. Foreign competition (particularly from Japan, which had substantially rebuilt its industrial economy across the post-war decades), domestic productivity stagnation, energy-cost pressures, and the substantial offshoring of production to lower-cost foreign locations produced the substantial decline of the American industrial Midwest that subsequent decades would substantially intensify. The "Rust Belt" — the deindustrializing industrial Midwest, from Buffalo through Pittsburgh through Cleveland through Detroit through Gary — became a political-cultural category in the 1970s and would substantially shape subsequent American political geography.

## The Carter administration

Jimmy Carter, the former governor of Georgia and a peanut farmer, won the 1976 presidential election as a substantially anti-establishment Democratic candidate in the substantial post-Watergate political environment. His administration (1977–81) was, in retrospect, a transitional period between the New Deal-Great Society Democratic political framework and the Reagan-era conservative framework.

Carter's substantive accomplishments included the Camp David Accords of September 1978 (mediating the Egyptian-Israeli peace agreement that produced the Egypt-Israel Peace Treaty of March 1979 — the first peace agreement between Israel and an Arab state), the Panama Canal Treaties of 1977 (which transferred sovereignty over the Canal Zone to Panama, taking effect in 1999), substantial energy legislation (the Department of Energy, created 1977; substantial federal investment in renewable energy and energy efficiency), and substantial deregulation (airline deregulation in 1978; trucking and railroad deregulation in 1980; the substantial deregulation framework that would continue through the Reagan years was substantially begun under Carter).

The Carter administration's political-electoral collapse was produced by multiple factors. The Iran hostage crisis — the Iranian Revolutionary Guard's November 1979 seizure of the American embassy in Tehran and the holding of 52 American diplomats hostage for 444 days — substantially destroyed Carter's foreign-policy credibility. The failed April 1980 military rescue attempt (Operation Eagle Claw, which produced no rescues and the deaths of eight American servicemen) compounded the political damage. The economic conditions — peak inflation, peak interest rates, the recession that was beginning — substantially destroyed Carter's economic-policy credibility.

The 1980 election was won by Republican Ronald Reagan with 50.7 percent of the popular vote against Carter's 41.0 percent and independent John Anderson's 6.6 percent. The Reagan landslide produced 489 electoral votes against Carter's 49. The Republican Party also took control of the Senate (53–46) for the first time since 1955. The political-electoral conditions for the next chapter's politics had been substantially assembled.
