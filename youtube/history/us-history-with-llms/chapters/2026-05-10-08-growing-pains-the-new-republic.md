# Chapter 8 — Growing Pains: The New Republic, 1790–1820

*A constitutional system designed against parties, immediately producing them.*

In the autumn of 1790, Alexander Hamilton — thirty-five years old, Treasury Secretary in the new Washington administration, born out of wedlock on the Caribbean island of Nevis — submitted to Congress a report titled *On the Public Credit*. The report ran to fifty-one pages and proposed what was, in 1790, the most ambitious financial program any republican government had ever attempted. The federal government would assume the war debts of the thirteen states. It would pay off the combined federal-and-state debt of approximately $54 million at face value, in new federal bonds. It would establish a national bank to manage federal finances and to issue a stable currency. It would fund the new debt through tariffs on imports and excise taxes on domestic production — including a tax on whiskey, the principal commodity export of the western Pennsylvania frontier.

The report touched off the first sustained political conflict of the new republic. Thomas Jefferson, in his capacity as Secretary of State, opposed nearly every element. So did James Madison, the floor leader of the new House of Representatives and the principal drafter of the Constitution Hamilton was attempting to operate. Madison's objection was that the assumption of state debts at face value would reward speculators — many of them New York and Massachusetts financiers — who had bought up depreciated state bonds for pennies on the dollar from the original veterans and farmers who had been paid in them. Jefferson's objection was broader: Hamilton's program would build a strong centralizing federal government tied to the commercial-merchant class of the Northeast, of a kind that the Anti-Federalists had warned against during ratification.

The fight was settled by what historians call the Compromise of 1790 (or, more vividly, the Dinner Table Bargain). Jefferson invited Hamilton and Madison to dinner at his home in New York. Hamilton agreed to support locating the new federal capital on the Potomac River, in the slaveholding South, between Virginia and Maryland. Madison agreed to allow the Assumption Bill to pass without further opposition. Both sides got what they most wanted. The capital moved south. The federal government took on the state debts.

This story is worth telling not because it determined the financial architecture of the new republic — Hamilton's program substantially survived even after Jeffersonians took political power — but because it shows the structure of what was about to happen. The Constitution had been drafted in 1787 by people who believed in republican government and disdained political parties; party formation, in the framers' theory, was the symptom of factional corruption that the Constitution had been designed to prevent. By 1796 the country had two organized political parties, and by 1800 the parties were so polarized that the peaceful transition from one to the other — the first such transition in modern republican history — was treated by both sides as either a deliverance or a disaster.

The puzzle of this chapter is how the new republic moved from the framers' anti-party theory to the early party system within a single decade, and what that movement reveals about how the constitutional design actually operated.

## What Hamilton built, and what the opposition was for

Hamilton's financial program had four major components. They are worth distinguishing because each produced its own political opposition.

**The assumption of state debts.** The federal government took on approximately $25 million of state debt, in addition to the existing $40 million of federal debt. The assumption advantaged states with large unpaid debts (Massachusetts, South Carolina) over states that had already paid most of theirs (Virginia, Maryland), which is part of why the program had to be paired with the capital-relocation deal.

**The funding of the debt at face value.** Holders of federal and state debt instruments would receive new federal bonds at full face value, regardless of what the holders had paid for the instruments. This advantaged the financiers who had bought up depreciated paper from the original recipients. Madison proposed a compromise in which original holders would receive part of the face value, but his proposal failed. Hamilton's plan passed substantially intact.

**The Bank of the United States.** Chartered by Congress in February 1791, the Bank was a quasi-private institution with private investors (Hamilton among them) and federal-government participation. It served as the federal government's fiscal agent, issued notes that functioned as paper currency, and provided commercial credit. Jefferson and Madison argued that the Constitution gave Congress no enumerated power to charter a bank; Hamilton argued that the necessary-and-proper clause (Article I, Section 8) implicitly authorized any institution useful to executing Congress's enumerated powers. The constitutional argument was the first major dispute over the doctrine of *implied powers* — a doctrine that would expand the federal government's scope for the next two centuries. Washington sided with Hamilton, and the Bank was chartered for twenty years. (It was not renewed in 1811, restored in 1816 as the Second Bank, and would be destroyed by Andrew Jackson in the Bank War of the 1830s — see Chapter 10.)

**The whiskey tax.** Federal excise taxes were levied on a range of goods, but the tax on distilled spirits hit the western Pennsylvania frontier particularly hard. Western farmers, who could not transport bulk grain to coastal markets economically, distilled their grain into whiskey for sale and barter; whiskey was the local currency of the frontier economy. The whiskey tax — assessed at the still rather than at the point of sale, payable in federal currency that frontier farmers had little of — produced the Whiskey Rebellion of 1791–94, a sustained resistance movement that the Washington administration eventually suppressed by leading a federalized state militia of about 13,000 men into western Pennsylvania in October 1794. The federal force found no rebellion to suppress; the rebels dispersed before its arrival. But the demonstration that the federal government would enforce its tax laws with military force, in person, against citizens of a state, was itself a major precedent. The Whiskey Rebellion's resolution showed that the new Constitution's promise of federal enforcement was real.

The opposition that crystallized against Hamilton's program had several distinguishable strands. Jefferson and Madison opposed it on constitutional grounds (implied powers were dangerous), on economic grounds (it favored commercial interests over agrarian ones), and on political-philosophical grounds (it created concentrated financial power that would corrupt the republic). Western farmers opposed it on practical grounds (it taxed them and helped financiers they did not know). Southern planters opposed it on sectional grounds (it benefited the Northeast more than the South). By the mid-1790s these strands had organized themselves into the *Democratic-Republican Party* (sometimes called the *Republican Party*, distinct from the modern Republican Party founded in 1854), with Jefferson and Madison as its leading figures. Hamilton's supporters — increasingly the merchants, financiers, and commercial-interest groups of the Northeast — organized as the *Federalist Party*. By 1796, the United States had a two-party system that the framers had explicitly designed against.

## Why the parties became what they did

The framers had assumed that representatives elected on the basis of district-level local interests would deliberate in Congress and arrive at policies serving the common good. Party organization — disciplined groups voting together according to platform positions — would, in this view, subvert the deliberative process by substituting partisan loyalty for individual judgment. The framers were wrong about how their system would operate. The institutions they had designed produced political parties almost immediately. The mechanisms by which this happened are worth examining.

First, the Electoral College gave a powerful incentive to coordinate. Picking a president required organizing electors across multiple states; coordinating that organizing required something that functioned like a party, even if it was not called one. By the 1796 election, the Federalists were running a slate of candidates (John Adams for president, Thomas Pinckney for vice president) and the Democratic-Republicans another (Jefferson for president, Aaron Burr for vice president). The original Constitution did not distinguish presidential from vice-presidential ballots — the candidate with the most electoral votes became president, the second-most became vice president. This produced the awkward result in 1796 of Adams winning the presidency with Jefferson, his political rival, as vice president. In 1800, Jefferson and Burr tied in electoral votes (each was supposed to be voted for separately by the Democratic-Republican electors, but the coordination failed), and the election went to the House of Representatives for resolution. The Twelfth Amendment, ratified in 1804, separated the presidential and vice-presidential ballots. The amendment was a structural acknowledgment that parties had become the operating units of American electoral politics; the Constitution was being amended to accommodate the party system the framers had hoped to prevent.

Second, the legislative process required coordination. Members of Congress voting on Hamilton's financial program needed to know how other members were going to vote; they organized themselves into caucuses to coordinate. The caucuses became the institutional core of the early parties. By 1800 the congressional caucuses were nominating presidential candidates — a function that party national conventions would take over in the 1830s.

Third, foreign-policy questions polarized the political class along axes that pre-existed and intensified the domestic divisions. The French Revolution (1789–) produced enthusiasm among Democratic-Republicans and horror among Federalists; the British war against revolutionary France (from 1793) divided American sympathies. The Jay Treaty of 1794 with Britain, which Hamilton's faction supported and Jefferson's faction opposed, became a major partisan flashpoint. The XYZ Affair of 1797–98 — in which French diplomats demanded bribes to receive an American diplomatic mission — turned American sentiment against France for a period; the resulting Quasi-War with France (1798–1800) and the Alien and Sedition Acts of 1798 (which criminalized criticism of the federal government) marked the height of Federalist political dominance and the moment when the partisan-press conflict was most intense.

The Alien and Sedition Acts deserve a closer look because they raised, for the first time, the question of how far a Federalist government could go in suppressing partisan opposition. The Sedition Act criminalized the publication of "false, scandalous, and malicious writing" against the federal government, Congress, or the president — but conveniently exempted the vice president, who happened to be Jefferson. Federalist editors were unaffected; Democratic-Republican editors were prosecuted. The acts were not used at a particularly large scale (about twenty-five arrests, ten convictions), but they made the partisan question a constitutional one. Jefferson and Madison responded with the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions (1798–99), which argued that states could "nullify" federal laws that exceeded constitutional authority — a doctrine that anticipated the South Carolina nullification crisis of 1832 (Chapter 10) and the Confederate secession of 1860–61 (Chapter 15). The constitutional question of whether states could resist federal laws was raised in 1798, was unresolved in the 1790s, and would not be settled until the Civil War decided it by force of arms.

## The election that did not destroy the republic

The presidential election of 1800 was contested between the incumbent Federalist John Adams and the Democratic-Republican challenger Thomas Jefferson, with substantial intra-party complications. The campaign was vicious by the standards of the era — partisan newspapers on both sides accused the other side's candidate of treason, atheism, depravity, and worse. Jefferson and his running mate Aaron Burr tied in electoral votes (73 each); under the original Constitution this threw the election to the lame-duck Federalist House of Representatives. The House, voting by state, deadlocked through thirty-five ballots before Alexander Hamilton — who despised Jefferson but considered Burr unprincipled even by his low standards — helped tip several Federalist votes. Jefferson became president on the thirty-sixth ballot.

The election is remembered as the first peaceful transfer of power between rival political parties in modern republican history. It was. It was also, importantly, the first occasion when a sitting Federalist government left office under genuine constitutional pressure rather than because the framework prescribed. Adams could have refused to accept the result. Some Federalist hardliners proposed extra-constitutional resolutions. The president did not pursue them. The transfer happened.

Jefferson called his inauguration the *Revolution of 1800* — the moment when the country chose the Democratic-Republican vision of the republic (decentralized, agrarian, suspicious of commercial interests) over the Federalist vision (centralized, commercial, deferential to elite expertise). The transfer was, in important ways, a permanent shift. The Federalist Party would never again win a presidential election. By 1820 the Federalist Party had dissolved as a national force, and the brief *Era of Good Feelings* — actually the era of one-party Democratic-Republican dominance — would last until new partisan divisions emerged in the 1820s. But the constitutional framework had survived its first electoral stress test. It would face many more, with varying outcomes, over the next two centuries.

## The Louisiana Purchase and Jefferson's constitutional improvisation

In 1803, Napoleon Bonaparte — facing imminent war with Britain and needing money — offered to sell to the United States the entire Louisiana Territory, an area of approximately 828,000 square miles that doubled the size of the new republic. The price was $15 million, equivalent to roughly $400 million in modern terms — extraordinarily cheap by any reasonable estimate of the land's value.

The Louisiana Purchase was attractive to almost everyone. The acquisition gave the United States control of the entire Mississippi River system, the Port of New Orleans (the strategic key to Mississippi River commerce), and an enormous expanse of land for future settlement. It eliminated France as a North American imperial competitor. It was being offered at a price the federal government could afford to pay.

It was also, as Jefferson explicitly acknowledged in private correspondence, unconstitutional. The Constitution gave the federal government no enumerated power to purchase territory from foreign nations. Jefferson — who had been the principal opponent of the doctrine of implied powers when Hamilton had used it to justify the Bank — had two options. He could insist on a constitutional amendment authorizing the purchase, which would take time he did not have (Napoleon might withdraw the offer). Or he could accept Hamilton's expansive reading of federal power and proceed without an amendment.

Jefferson chose the second option. He proceeded with the purchase, sent the treaty to the Senate, and saw it ratified in October 1803. The constitutional principle he had argued against in the 1790s — that the federal government could exercise powers not explicitly enumerated in the Constitution, when implied by other powers it did possess — had now been used by Jefferson himself, on a scale that dwarfed anything Hamilton had attempted.

The Louisiana Purchase is one of the cleanest demonstrations in early American history that the constitutional theory of strict construction is difficult to maintain when actual government has to operate. The Jeffersonian political coalition's commitment to limited federal government had real content in many areas; on questions where the practical interests of the United States (or, on related matters, the interests of slavery — the Louisiana Purchase brought vast new territory into the federal compact, almost all of which was open to slavery under the Constitution as it then stood) intersected with constitutional principle, the principle bent. The pattern would repeat through American political history. Strict-construction ideology has been, with rare exceptions, an instrument used by political coalitions out of power against the coalitions in power; coalitions in power tend to find that the federal government's powers are broader than they had claimed before they held the executive.

## The War of 1812 and the war that decided nothing

The War of 1812 ran from June 1812 to February 1815. It was caused by a cluster of unresolved issues from the Napoleonic Wars: British impressment of American sailors (the Royal Navy forced American sailors into British naval service when ships were boarded on the high seas, a practice the British justified as the seizure of British deserters), British support for Indigenous resistance to American westward expansion in the Ohio and Mississippi valleys, and American resentment at British and French restrictions on American neutral shipping during the European war.

The war was, by most military measures, a stalemate or a defeat for the United States. The American invasion of Canada in 1812 failed at every theater. The British occupied and burned Washington, D.C. in August 1814 — including the White House and the Capitol — in response to an earlier American burning of York (modern Toronto). The American treasury was nearly bankrupt by 1814. The New England states were so opposed to the war that the Federalist Hartford Convention of December 1814–January 1815 considered (and ultimately rejected) secession.

Then two things happened in quick succession. American and British negotiators in Ghent, Belgium, signed a peace treaty on December 24, 1814, restoring the prewar status quo. The treaty addressed none of the war's stated causes — impressment, Indigenous policy, neutral rights — because the underlying problems (the Napoleonic Wars) had ended with Napoleon's first abdication in April 1814, making the issues moot. The war had become unnecessary before it was officially ended.

And then, on January 8, 1815, fifteen days after the treaty was signed but before news of it had reached the Gulf Coast, Andrew Jackson's American force at the Battle of New Orleans inflicted the lopsided defeat on a British army described in Chapter 00. The battle, fought after the war had been agreed to end, transformed the political memory of the war. The actual record of inconclusive campaigns and burned capitals was overlaid in public consciousness by the spectacular American victory at New Orleans. Andrew Jackson's political career was made. The Federalist Party, which had opposed the war and whose Hartford Convention had been ridiculed after the New Orleans victory, was destroyed as a national political force.

The War of 1812 is one of those events whose historical significance is mostly downstream of its political memory. The military results were inconclusive. The diplomatic results restored the prewar status quo. The economic results were severe disruption followed by recovery. But the political results — Jackson's rise, the destruction of the Federalist Party, the consolidation of national identity around a particular reading of the war's outcomes — were durable. The country that emerged from 1815 was politically reorganized in ways that the Treaty of Ghent did not capture.
