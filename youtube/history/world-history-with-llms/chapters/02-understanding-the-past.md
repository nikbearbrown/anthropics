*The Method, Not the Memorization.*

- Understanding the Past: How Historians Actually Work
- The Four Questions That Make a Source Speak

## Chapter overview

By the end of this chapter you will know what historians actually do. You will know the difference between a primary source and a secondary one and why the distinction matters. You will know the four questions — author, audience, intent, context — that turn an old document or object into useful evidence. You will know the three levels of causation and how to rank them. You will know the major schools of historical interpretation, from Carlyle's "great man" to Berger and Luckmann's social construction of reality, and you will know what each one lets you see that the others hide. You will know what historical empathy is and why it is harder than it sounds. By the end, the rest of this textbook stops being a list of names and dates and starts being a workshop in human inquiry.

---

## Learning objectives

1. **(Remember)** Distinguish primary from secondary sources, citing examples of each from at least three different time periods.
2. **(Understand)** Explain why the four interrogation questions — author, audience, intent, context — are the discipline that turns a source into evidence rather than a fact.
3. **(Apply)** Given a historical document or object, perform the four-question analysis and produce a defensible interpretation.
4. **(Analyze)** Given an event with disputed causes, rank the causes as primary, secondary, and tertiary, and defend the ranking.
5. **(Evaluate)** Recognize the major schools of historical thought — great man, progressive, social, intellectual, Marxist, gender, postcolonial — and identify which lens illuminates a given historical question best.

## Opening case — one building, many histories

In May 1453, after fifty-three days of siege, an Ottoman sultan named Mehmed II rode into the city of Constantinople. The defending Byzantine emperor had just been killed in the breach. Mehmed was twenty-one years old. His soldiers were already looting. According to contemporary accounts, when the new sultan reached the great church at the city's heart — the Hagia Sophia, "Holy Wisdom" in Greek — he stopped and recognized its beauty. He did not destroy it. He converted it to a mosque, ordered minarets added at its four corners, and let Arabic calligraphy join the Greek iconography on its walls.

The building had been built by the Byzantine emperor Justinian I more than nine centuries earlier, in the 530s CE. Its dome was the engineering wonder of the late antique world. For nearly a thousand years it had been the largest cathedral in Christendom. After 1453 it was the most prominent mosque in the Ottoman Empire. In the twentieth century it became a museum. In 2020 it became a mosque again.

If you stand inside the Hagia Sophia today, you can read four civilizations off the walls. Sixth-century Greek mosaics show Christ Pantocrator and the Virgin Mary. Fifteenth-century Arabic medallions name the first four caliphs. Twentieth-century plaster, applied during the museum era, covered some of the older work; some of the plaster has since been removed. The building is a single object that contains its own history. It is a primary source for the reign of Justinian, the Ottoman conquest of 1453, the early-twentieth-century secularization of Turkey under Atatürk, and the religious-political turn under Erdoğan. None of those readings is wrong. None of them is sufficient. To get any of them, you have to know what to ask.

This chapter is about what to ask.

History is not — most of all — the memorization of dates. It is a discipline. It is a set of methods for taking evidence (a building, a letter, a coin, a song, a court record, a satellite image) and producing an interpretation that other careful readers can check. The interpretation is not the same as the evidence. The interpretation is a claim about what the evidence supports and what it does not. The discipline is what makes the difference between a claim worth defending and a guess worth ignoring.

The OpenStax authors of this textbook open with the question *what is history?* and offer three candidate answers. Maya Angelou said history is a way to meet the pain of the past and overcome it. Winston Churchill said it is a chronicle by the victors, an interpretation by those who write it. The textbook adds a third: history is a path to knowing why we are the way we are. All three are partly true. What unites them is that none is automatic. Each requires the historian to do something — to choose what to study, to interpret what is found, to argue for one reading against another.

The Hagia Sophia stands as a working example for this whole chapter. We will return to it.

## Theoretical grounding — Herodotus, Sima Qian, and the long discipline

History as a deliberate, written practice has two ancient parents. In the Greek world, Herodotus, writing in the fifth century BCE, produced the first work the West calls *history* — an account of the Greco-Persian wars that combined eyewitness testimony, second-hand reports, and the author's own travels. He is called the father of history in the West. In China, Sima Qian, born in the second century BCE, produced *Records of the Grand Historian*, a sweeping narrative of the Han dynasty and its predecessors that established the model for two thousand years of Chinese historical writing. He is called the father of history in China.

The two traditions did not know about each other. They invented many of the same disciplines independently. Both insisted on naming sources. Both distinguished what the author saw from what the author was told. Both grappled with the problem that participants in great events tend to remember themselves favorably. Both wrote at length about leaders whose biographies were already legendary, and both occasionally wrote about ordinary people whose names they bothered to record because the record otherwise would have been blank.

The Western tradition continued through Polybius (200s BCE), Tacitus (first century CE), Bede (the eighth century CE), and the medieval chroniclers, into Renaissance scholarship and the eventual professionalization of history as an academic discipline in nineteenth-century Germany. The Chinese tradition continued through the dynastic histories — each new dynasty wrote the history of its predecessor — and produced an unbroken record of more than two millennia. The Middle East had its own deep historiographical tradition, including Ibn Khaldun, the fourteenth-century North African scholar whose *Muqaddimah* anticipated several modern social-science methods. India's textual traditions are equally old.

Other regions have less complete written records. Africa's history is complicated by climate (which destroys documents and artifacts), by colonial powers that sometimes destroyed or ignored what they found, and by the dominance until recently of European scholars who wrote with European biases. Latin America's pre-conquest record was deliberately destroyed in many cases — Spanish conquerors burned codices on the grounds that they spoke of religions the conquerors meant to replace. The historical record everywhere is a partial record. The historian's job includes naming what the record cannot show.

The methods the rest of this chapter teaches are neither European nor recent. They are the slow accumulation of two and a half thousand years of practice across the writing cultures of the world. They are the discipline that turns evidence into knowledge.

## Concept 1 — Primary and secondary sources

A **primary source** is evidence that comes directly from the time period under study. A **secondary source** is evidence that comes from after the fact — usually a scholar's interpretation of primary sources.

The distinction is not pedantic. It is the load-bearing distinction in historical method.

Primary sources include government documents, diaries, letters, photographs, paintings drawn from life, court records, menus, songs, sermons, news reports written when the news was news, weapons, tools, buildings, mosaics, hieroglyphs, oral traditions recorded by ethnographers, archaeological remains, and — increasingly — emails, tweets, GPS traces, and source code. They are the closest the historian can get to the actual moment. They are not always reliable. A diary may lie. A letter may be a deliberate deception. A photograph may be staged. A statue may flatter the king who paid for it. But they are evidence written or made within the period the historian is trying to understand.

Secondary sources include biographies, scholarly monographs, museum-curator blog posts, encyclopedias, and textbooks (this one, for instance). They are interpretations. They draw on primary sources, but they do so through the questions and assumptions of a later era. A twentieth-century biography of an Egyptian pharaoh is a secondary source. So is a 1960s map of World War II battle sites. So is a Wikipedia article. Secondary sources are useful — often essential — for context and orientation, especially when the primary sources are in a language the reader does not know or in an archive they cannot reach. But the historian's claim of expertise rests on the primary sources, not on the secondaries.

The reason the distinction matters is the same reason a court trial is held quickly after the alleged events. Memory degrades. Interpretation accumulates. With every year between the event and the account, more layers of explanation, justification, and selection sit between the reader and what actually happened. A primary source has the fewest layers. A secondary source has more.

There is a further hierarchy *within* primary sources, and the textbook is honest about it. Written sources are generally more interpretable than non-written ones. Imagine, the textbook offers, that a future historian inherited only a single magazine cover from our era — a pop star in performance dress. Without context, the picture supports many readings. With even a one-line caption ("Pop star rising to the top of the charts"), the picture's meaning sharpens dramatically. Words constrain interpretation. Objects without words are evocative but ambiguous. This is one reason history as an academic discipline is conventionally said to begin with the invention of writing — not because preliterate societies have no history, but because their history is harder to read with confidence.

That said, the privilege of writing has costs. Most pre-modern writing was done by elites. Most surviving written sources speak about elites. To recover the lives of the poor, the enslaved, the colonized, women, children, and minority communities, historians have to use sources beyond the obvious. Court records are unusually valuable for this — they record the words of accused criminals and witnesses regardless of social class. Parish registers, tax rolls, ship manifests, and prison inventories all preserve names and circumstances that no biographer would have written down. Twentieth-century social historians built whole careers reading these "ordinary" records against the grain. The chapter on hidden voices, later in this chapter, returns to this.

## Concept 2 — The four interrogation questions

Every primary source — written, visual, material — is interrogated with four questions. The historian asks them every time. They are the discipline.

**Author.** Who made this source? What was their position in society? What was their relationship to the events described? What did they have to gain or lose from one telling rather than another? Was the author an eyewitness, a participant, a hearer of rumors, or a much-later compiler? Are there reasons to suspect the author is unreliable? Are there reasons to suspect the author is *more* reliable than usual?

**Audience.** For whom was the source made? A diary written for the writer alone is interpreted differently than a letter written to a king. A sermon delivered at a public funeral is interpreted differently than a sermon written for a clerical audience. A government report intended for internal circulation says different things than a government report intended for publication. Sometimes the audience is the historian's first clue to the source's meaning.

**Intent.** Why was the source made? To record? To persuade? To deceive? To celebrate? To accuse? To survive? Most sources have more than one intent, and the intents may pull against each other. A medieval chronicle might intend to glorify the king and also to warn the king's heir against repeating his mistakes — both at once. The historian's job is not to pick one intent and ignore the others. It is to name them.

**Context.** What was happening when the source was made? Was the writer in war or peace? In prosperity or famine? Under intimidation or freely? Was the source produced during a religious revival, a political crisis, an economic boom? Context is the door from the source to the world. A speech is a different thing in 1936 than in 1945. A treaty is a different thing on the morning of its signing than thirty years later.

The four questions are mechanical. They are also, in practice, a set of attitudes. A historian who asks them habitually starts to read every text — a politician's tweet, a corporate report, a viral video — through the same lens. The discipline transfers. This is one of the practical reasons the textbook insists that studying history develops critical thinking, analysis, and media literacy. The four questions are the engine.

A worked example will make the questions concrete.

## Concept 3 — Two accounts of one event

In 1519, the Spanish conquistador Hernán Cortés wrote a letter to Charles V, the king of Spain. Cortés had landed in what is now Mexico the year before and was preparing to march inland toward the Aztec capital, Tenochtitlán. The letter is one of a series Cortés wrote, collectively known as the *Cartas de Relación*. In it, he reports that the Indigenous peoples he had encountered were, in his telling, eager to become subjects of Spain. He describes a province of fifty thousand warriors and fifty towns and fortresses already in peaceful allegiance to Charles V. He describes Indigenous people complaining about the Aztec ruler Moctezuma, who, in Cortés's telling, was a tyrant who took their sons to be sacrificed to idols. The letter is fluent, confident, and politically careful. Cortés wanted Charles V's continued backing.

A few years later, in the same city of Tenochtitlán, an Aztec eyewitness account began to be recorded. The account was preserved by a sixteenth-century Franciscan friar, Bernardino de Sahagún, who learned Nahuatl and worked with Indigenous informants. It was later compiled and translated by the twentieth-century Mexican scholar Miguel León-Portilla in his book *The Broken Spears*. The account describes an event known as the Massacre in the Great Temple — an attack by Cortés's lieutenant, Pedro de Alvarado, on a religious festival at which Aztec nobles and warriors were celebrating, unarmed, in their ceremonial dress. According to the Aztec account, Alvarado's men attacked the musicians first, killed the singers, killed the spectators, and continued for three hours. The Aztec king Moctezuma, then under house arrest, protested: *Our lords, that is enough! What are you doing? These people are not carrying shields or macanas. Our lords, they are completely unarmed!*

What is the historian to do with two accounts that disagree this completely?

The four questions discipline the answer.

**Author.** Cortés is a participant. He has direct access to events, but he also has a personal interest in their telling. The Aztec account comes from informants who were present at or immediately after the events. They are also participants, and they also have an interest — to record what their own community had suffered. Both authorships bring power and bias.

**Audience.** Cortés's letter is addressed to Charles V, his patron and judge. Cortés needs Charles V to keep funding the expedition and to recognize Cortés's authority on the ground. Every paragraph of the letter is calibrated to those needs. The Aztec account is a different artifact: it was preserved by Sahagún for missionary purposes and by León-Portilla for scholarly ones, but the immediate audience of the original account was the community itself, recording what the community had lost.

**Intent.** Cortés's intent is partly factual and partly persuasive. He needs Charles V to believe that the conquest is righteous, that the Indigenous people consented, and that the violence is justified. The Aztec account's intent is to preserve memory of what was done. The two intents do not produce the same selection of events.

**Context.** Cortés is writing in 1519 from a foreign field with shaky communication lines, in a political situation where he has technically exceeded his authority by going inland at all. The Aztec account is recorded in the aftermath of conquest, by a culture whose written records are being burned and whose religion is being suppressed. The contexts shape what each can say.

Once the four questions are asked, the historian stops asking *which account is true* and starts asking *what each account contributes to the truth.* Cortés's letter is excellent evidence for what Cortés wanted Charles V to believe. It is poor evidence for the actual sentiments of Indigenous people, because it serves Cortés's interests too directly to be trusted there. The Aztec account is excellent evidence for what the conquest looked like from the receiving end. It does not give us Cortés's tactical reasoning. Together, the two accounts constrain the possible narratives. Combined with archaeological evidence, with later Indigenous chronicles like the Florentine Codex, with Spanish administrative records, with epidemic-disease data, the modern historian can reconstruct a fuller picture than either account alone supports.

This is what historical method looks like when it works. Not the choice of one source over another. The combination of many sources, each interrogated, each understood for what it can and cannot say.

### Rhetoric — Roosevelt's Day of Infamy

A second worked example, briefer, on the role of language. On December 8, 1941, U.S. President Franklin D. Roosevelt addressed a joint session of Congress to ask for a declaration of war against Japan. The previous morning, Japanese forces had attacked the U.S. naval base at Pearl Harbor in Hawaii. Roosevelt's speech opened: *Yesterday, December 7th, 1941 — a date which will live in infamy — the United States of America was suddenly and deliberately attacked by naval and air forces of the Empire of Japan.*

Read those words slowly. Note the rhetorical work being done. *Suddenly and deliberately* — the two adverbs do different jobs. *Suddenly* establishes that the United States was caught off guard, blameless. *Deliberately* establishes that the attack was planned, treacherous, calculated. Note the phrase *Empire of Japan* — not *Japan*, but *Empire*, with its connotations of expansionist menace. Note the cadence later in the speech: *Last night Japanese forces attacked Hong Kong. Last night Japanese forces attacked Guam. Last night Japanese forces attacked the Philippine Islands. Last night the Japanese attacked Wake Island. And this morning the Japanese attacked Midway Island.* The repetition compresses time and geography, making the offensive feel inescapable. The closing — *we will gain the inevitable triumph — so help us God* — places the United States in moral and divine alignment.

Linguists call the deliberate use of words to achieve a desired effect **rhetoric**. Every primary source has rhetoric, even sources that look transparent. The historian's job is to read past the rhetoric to the act being performed. Roosevelt's speech is excellent evidence for the political work the U.S. government wanted to do on December 8. It is partial evidence for the actual decision-making in Tokyo or for the experience of soldiers and civilians in Hawaii. The four questions, again, do the work.

## Concept 4 — Causation: why an event happened

Once the historian has assembled and interpreted sources, the next question is *why*. Why did this event happen? Why did this person make this choice? Why this empire and not another?

History uses a hierarchy of causes. A textbook diagram of historical causation looks like a pyramid.

**Primary cause.** The most immediate trigger. The spark. The thing that, had it not happened, the event would not have happened in the way and at the time it did.

**Secondary cause.** One step removed. The conditions that made the spark possible. Without these conditions, the spark would have fizzled.

**Tertiary cause.** The deep background. The long-running structures, ideologies, demographics, economic patterns that set the stage on which the actors performed. These are the slowest-moving forces in history. They explain why a society was the kind of society in which the secondary and primary causes could combine.

A worked example, from the textbook itself. *Why did the United States enter World War II in 1941?*

**Primary cause.** Japan's attack on Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941. Without that attack, on that morning, in that form, the United States would not have declared war on Japan on December 8, 1941. The attack is the spark.

**Secondary causes.** Long-standing tensions between the United States and Japan over influence in the Pacific. President Roosevelt's pre-war policy of supporting Britain against Nazi Germany — the Lend-Lease Act, the destroyers-for-bases deal — which had effectively committed the United States to the Allied cause without formal entry. American economic sanctions on Japan, including an oil embargo, that had narrowed Japan's strategic options. These conditions made the December 7 attack possible and meaningful in a way it would not have been in, say, 1935.

**Tertiary causes.** The structural shift in global power following World War I, in which the United States had emerged as the world's largest economy. The collapse of the Versailles system through the 1930s. The rise of fascist and militarist regimes in Germany, Italy, and Japan. The Great Depression and its political consequences. These large-scale forces are what made the 1930s the kind of decade in which a Pearl Harbor attack was thinkable in the first place.

A historian who names only the primary cause is doing journalism. A historian who names only the tertiary causes is doing sociology. A good historical account does all three.

The pyramid is a tool, not a law. Historians disagree about how to rank causes. The disagreements are productive. A historian who emphasizes Mehmed II's personal ambition as the primary cause of the 1453 siege of Constantinople is making a "great man" argument. A historian who emphasizes the Ottoman Empire's structural need for territorial expansion is making a different argument. Both can be defensible. Neither is automatically right. The discipline lies in producing the strongest version of your ranking and being willing to defend it against the strongest version of someone else's.

A second worked example. *Why did Mehmed II besiege Constantinople in 1453?*

**Primary cause.** Mehmed's personal need to prove himself after a difficult succession. He had been treated badly by his father, Murad II. He took the throne at twenty-one. Conquering the city that had defied the Ottomans repeatedly for a century would establish his authority beyond question. Without his personal drive and his willingness to gamble, the siege of 1453 may not have happened in 1453.

**Secondary cause.** The geographic and economic value of Constantinople. The city sat at the crossroads of Europe and Asia, controlled access between the Black Sea and the Mediterranean, and was the surviving piece of the Roman Empire. It had been a long-standing target for Ottoman policy.

**Tertiary cause.** The Ottoman Empire's structural reliance on continuous expansion. Conquest brought tribute, slaves, soldiers, and prestige. An empire built on expansion needs to keep expanding or its political system begins to fray.

Different historians weight these causes differently. The textbook is honest that the ranking is contested. A historian who privileges great-man explanations weights Mehmed first. A historian who privileges economic structure weights the third cause first. Each ranking implies a different theory of how history works.

## Concept 5 — The schools of historical interpretation

No historian writes from no perspective. Every historian writes from one. Naming the perspective — the *school* — is part of the discipline. The major schools are the lenses through which historians have seen the past. Each one reveals what the others miss.

**Great man theory.** The oldest. Associated with the nineteenth-century Scottish historian Thomas Carlyle, whose 1840 lecture series declared: *Universal History, the history of what man has accomplished in this world, is at bottom the History of the Great Men who have worked here.* Carlyle's argument was that the actions of exceptional leaders — kings, generals, prophets, poets — drive history. Everyone else is, in his telling, the canvas on which great men paint. The great-man view dominated Western historiography for centuries. It is not extinct. It survives in popular biographies, in much of journalism, and in the way most public history is taught. Its strength is that great men *do* matter — Napoleon's choices reshaped Europe; Genghis Khan's reshaped Eurasia. Its weakness is that it cannot explain why the great man is great in this place at this time, or why the same kind of figure produced different effects in different societies.

**Tolstoy's response.** Carlyle's near-contemporary, the Russian novelist Leo Tolstoy, was unconvinced. In *War and Peace*, published in 1869, Tolstoy argued: *we need only penetrate to the essence of any historic event — which lies in the activity of the general mass of men who take part in it — to be convinced that the will of the historic hero does not control the actions of the mass but is itself continually controlled.* Tolstoy compared great-man theory to pre-Copernican astronomy: the kings and emperors are like the planets, but they only seem to be the cause of the motion. The actual cause is the laws governing the masses. Tolstoy's argument anticipated much of what was later called social history.

**Progressive history.** A nineteenth-century European school. It viewed history as a directional movement toward a specific end — usually toward democracy, republican government, and Western-style modernization. Its assumption was that societies were getting better over time and that the European trajectory was the model. Progressive history was a confident school. It explained much. It also flattered the powers writing it. After World War I, when Europe had spent four years killing each other on an industrial scale, the confidence began to look ridiculous, and progressive history lost its hold. A useful corrective comes from the Nigerian novelist Chinua Achebe, who, in an interview with *The Atlantic* about his novel *Things Fall Apart*, observed how a class of South Korean schoolgirls related to his book about British colonialism in Igbo society — because they had their own colonial history, with Japan as the colonizer. *People from different parts of the world can respond to the same story, if it says something to them about their own history and their own experience.* Progress, viewed from outside the West, did not look like a straight line. It looked like a wave that broke differently on every shore.

**Intellectual history.** Studies the ideas that drive people to choose what they choose. What did medieval Europeans believe about salvation? What did Han Chinese officials believe about the Mandate of Heaven? What did Enlightenment philosophers believe about the relationship between citizen and state? Intellectual history takes seriously the proposition that ideas shape action. Its risk is over-attributing change to a single influential text or thinker. Its strength is that it takes the conscious life of the past seriously rather than reducing it to material conditions.

**Social history.** The historiographical revolution of the 1960s. Influenced by the counterculture politics of the era, social historians turned their attention to the lives of people the great-man tradition had ignored — peasants, workers, women, slaves, children, the urban poor, religious minorities, indigenous communities. They used non-traditional sources: parish registers, court records, tax rolls, oral histories, archaeological remains, songs, recipes. The work was painstaking. It changed the discipline. A history of the French Revolution that includes only the National Assembly is now considered incomplete; the price of bread, the wages of seamstresses, the politics of the bread riot are all part of the story.

**Marxist history.** Argues that history is driven by class struggle — the conflict between those who own the means of production and those who do not. Marxism predates the social-history turn but informed it; many social historians worked in a Marxist or Marxist-adjacent framework. The strength of Marxist history is its insistence that economic structure shapes everything — that the medieval church, the absolutist state, and the modern corporation are all explicable in terms of who benefits from them. The weakness is that not every historical event is best explained by class. Religious wars, in particular, often resist the framework.

**Gender history.** Originally women's history; now broadened to consider gender as a category of historical analysis. The school argues that the social construction of masculinity and femininity is a force in history as real as economic class. It asks why certain spaces, professions, and choices were available to men and not to women, or to women and not to men, and what changed when those constraints shifted. Gender history overlaps with intellectual history when it examines the ideas that justified gendered exclusion, with social history when it studies the daily lives of women across class lines, and with political history when it studies suffrage movements, family law, and reproductive policy.

**Postcolonial history.** Studies the long aftermath of colonization. It asks why the categories established during colonial rule — racial categories, national borders, legal codes, educational systems — persist long after the colonizers leave. It asks what the colonized contributed to the metropole and what was extracted. It is an explicitly political school: it claims the past as a tool for understanding present injustice. Its strength is that it forces the historian to take seriously the perspective of the colonized, which the older sources often suppressed. Its risk is that it sometimes reads back present concerns onto past situations they did not exactly fit.

**Social construction of reality.** A meta-school. Drawn from the 1966 book *The Social Construction of Reality* by sociologists Peter Berger and Thomas Luckmann, which argued that what people take to be obvious — gender roles, class distinctions, the meaning of work, the boundaries of childhood — are not natural but constructed. Different societies in different periods construct different realities. The historian who reads the past through this lens asks what was *taken for granted* in a given era and how those taken-for-granted assumptions shaped behavior. This school does not replace the others; it adds a layer of self-awareness to all of them.

The point of naming the schools is not to choose one. It is to recognize that every historical account uses one or more, often without saying so. A reader who can identify the school is a more powerful reader than one who cannot. A historian who can switch schools — reading the same event through great-man, social, gender, and postcolonial lenses in succession — produces a richer interpretation than one who locks in early.

The process of revising older interpretations by adding new lenses is called **revisionism**. The word sometimes carries a pejorative ring in popular usage; in the discipline, it is just another word for *doing the work*. Each generation of historians revises the previous one, adding new sources, new perspectives, and new questions. The result is not chaos but accumulation. We know more about the Aztec conquest than we did in 1900. We know more about the lives of medieval European peasants than we did in 1950. We know more about the African past than we did in 1970. The picture sharpens with each revision.

## Extended worked example — reading the Hagia Sophia

We opened with the Hagia Sophia. We will close the chapter's main argument with it.

Imagine you are standing in the building today. You are practicing the discipline this chapter teaches. The four questions, the levels of causation, and the schools of interpretation give you a working method.

**The building as primary source.** The walls, mosaics, calligraphy, dome, minarets, plaster, marble revetments, and architectural geometry are all primary evidence. Every layer is from a specific moment. Every layer was placed by specific people for specific reasons. The job of reading the building is to ask, of every visible feature, when it was placed and why.

**The four questions, applied to the dome.** *Author:* the dome was designed by the Byzantine architects Anthemius of Tralles and Isidore of Miletus, working under the imperial command of Justinian I. *Audience:* the people of Constantinople, the Christian church, foreign visitors, and (always) God. *Intent:* to demonstrate imperial and religious power; to provide a space large enough for the most important Orthodox liturgies; to advance Byzantine architectural prestige beyond anything seen in the Mediterranean. *Context:* the 530s CE were a period of imperial confidence under Justinian, after the suppression of the Nika riots in 532, and the building replaced an earlier basilica that had been destroyed in those riots. The dome's geometry — a sphere on pendentives over a square base — was an engineering innovation that Justinian's regime broadcast across the empire.

**The four questions, applied to the minarets.** *Author:* the minarets were added in stages after 1453 under Mehmed II and his successors, using local Ottoman architectural conventions. *Audience:* the Muslim faithful summoned to prayer, the Ottoman administration, the city's mixed population, and foreign visitors. *Intent:* to convert the building's function from church to mosque, to reorient the building toward Mecca, and to mark Ottoman sovereignty in the visual language of the city's skyline. *Context:* the immediate aftermath of conquest, when the new regime needed to legitimate itself by claiming the city's most prominent religious building.

**Levels of causation, applied to the conversion.** *Primary cause:* Mehmed II's choice to convert the Hagia Sophia rather than destroy it. *Secondary cause:* the Ottoman tradition of repurposing sacred spaces — a tradition with deep roots in the Middle East, where buildings often passed from one religion to another rather than being torn down. *Tertiary cause:* the Ottoman Empire's broader policy of accommodating non-Muslim communities (the *millet* system) within an Islamic political framework, which made the conversion of one church to a mosque a workable compromise rather than a political crisis.

**Schools of interpretation.** A great-man historian foregrounds Mehmed's personal decision and aesthetic recognition. A social historian asks who used the building day-to-day in the years after 1453, what changed for the parish-level Christian community in the city, how the building functioned in the lives of ordinary Ottomans. A gender historian asks who was admitted to which spaces, how the building's gendered geography (separate prayer areas) worked in practice. A postcolonial historian — using a framework that often gets applied beyond strict colonial situations — asks how the conversion narrative was deployed in later European writing about "Eastern" civilizations, and how that narrative continues to shape modern reception of the building. None of these readings is complete on its own. All of them constrain the others.

The Hagia Sophia is a single object. It supports a thousand interpretations. The discipline is what keeps the interpretations honest.

## Chapter summary

You can now do five things you could not do before this chapter.

You can distinguish a primary source from a secondary one and explain why the difference matters for how confidently a claim can be made.

You can interrogate any source with the four questions — author, audience, intent, context — and produce a defensible reading of what the source can and cannot tell you.

You can identify the levels of causation behind a historical event, rank them, and defend your ranking against alternative rankings produced by historians using different frameworks.

You can recognize the major schools of historical thought — great man, progressive, intellectual, social, Marxist, gender, postcolonial — and use any of them as a lens to illuminate a particular question. You also know that no single lens is complete.

You can practice historical empathy — meeting the past on its own terms, without the imposition of present-day attitudes, while still being clear about what you find admirable and what you find harmful.

The thing to watch for, going forward, is the temptation to skip the discipline. Sources can look obvious. Causes can look simple. Interpretation can look automatic. None of those temptations is reliable. The discipline this chapter teaches is the slow part — the part that turns a confident reader into a careful one. Every chapter that follows assumes you are doing the slow part.

## Key terms

- **Chronological approach.** An organization of history that proceeds from earlier to later in time. The default for this textbook.
- **Global citizen.** A person who sees themselves as responsible to a world community rather than only a national one. The textbook frames the study of world history as preparation for global citizenship.
- **Great man theory.** The view that elite leaders — kings, generals, prophets — are the primary agents of historical change. Associated with Thomas Carlyle's 1840 lectures.
- **Historical empathy.** The discipline of meeting the past on its own terms, without imposing modern attitudes or judgments. Hard to practice; essential for honest interpretation.
- **Historiography.** The study of how historians have interpreted the past. Not the same as history itself; rather, the history of history-writing.
- **Iconography.** The use of images and symbols in art. Reading iconography is a key technique for interpreting non-textual primary sources.
- **Intellectual history.** The history of ideas and the philosophical frameworks that drive choices. Studies the conscious life of the past.
- **Postcolonial history.** A school that studies the long aftermath of colonization and treats the perspective of the colonized as historiographically essential.
- **Primary cause.** The most immediate, proximate cause of an event; the spark.
- **Primary source.** Evidence created during the period under study. The closest the historian can get to the actual moment.
- **Progressive history.** A nineteenth-century school that viewed history as a directional movement toward democracy and republican government. Discredited after World War I.
- **Revisionism.** The process of revising historical interpretations by adding new sources, perspectives, or questions. The default mode of the discipline.
- **Rhetoric.** The deliberate use of language to achieve a desired effect. Every primary source contains rhetoric; identifying it is part of source analysis.
- **Secondary cause.** The conditions that made the primary cause possible. One step removed from the immediate trigger.
- **Secondary source.** Evidence created after the period under study. Useful for context, less direct than primary evidence.
- **Social constructs.** Ideas — class, gender, race, professionalism, childhood — that are created and accepted by a society and influence behavior. Drawn from Berger and Luckmann's 1966 *Social Construction of Reality*.
- **Social history.** A school that studies all classes of people, not just elites. Revolutionized the discipline beginning in the 1960s.
- **Tertiary cause.** The deep, slow-moving structural conditions behind an event. The historical background.

### Warm-up

**Exercise 2.1** — *Source classification.* Classify each of the following as primary or secondary, and justify the classification: (a) a 1543 ledger from a Lyons silk merchant; (b) a 2018 documentary about the silk trade in early modern Europe; (c) a coin minted under the emperor Augustus; (d) a Wikipedia article about Augustus; (e) the Augustan-era *Res Gestae* (Augustus's own account of his accomplishments); (f) a 1980s academic article analyzing the *Res Gestae* as propaganda.

**Exercise 2.2** — *Four-question application.* Take Cortés's 1519 letter to Charles V (excerpts in this chapter). Apply each of the four questions in turn — author, audience, intent, context — and write one paragraph of analysis per question.

**Exercise 2.3** — *Causation pyramid.* Take a current event from this week's news. Identify the primary, secondary, and tertiary causes. Note where you are confident and where you are guessing.

### Application

**Exercise 2.4** — *Two accounts.* Find two accounts of a single historical event written from opposing perspectives — for example, accounts of the partition of India written by an Indian and a Pakistani historian, or accounts of a labor strike written by management and by union organizers. Apply the four questions to each. Identify what each account does well, what each account omits, and what a combined account would look like.

**Exercise 2.5** — *Object analysis.* Find a museum object online with a detailed catalog entry. Treat it as a primary source. Apply the four questions. Then check your interpretation against the museum's curatorial notes. Where did you get it right? Where did you miss?

**Exercise 2.6** — *Lens-switching.* Take a historical event you already know well. Sketch the same event through four different lenses: great man, social history, intellectual history, and postcolonial. Each sketch is one paragraph. Note which lens produces the most surprising reading.

### Synthesis

**Exercise 2.7** — *Hagia Sophia revisited.* Find a recent (post-2020) article about the Hagia Sophia's reconversion to a mosque. Apply the four questions to the article itself, treating it as a primary source for early-twenty-first-century discussions of religious-political symbolism. Identify the rhetorical choices the author makes and what political work they are doing.

**Exercise 2.8** — *Personal historiography.* Write a one-page autobiography. Identify which historical school it most resembles — great man (you as the agent of your own life), social history (you as shaped by class and structure), intellectual history (you as a chooser among ideas), or another. What do the alternative framings reveal about your own life that your default framing hides?

### Challenge

**Exercise 2.9** — *Hidden voices.* Pick a historical period for which the dominant sources speak mostly about elites. Identify three categories of people whose voices are likely missing. For each, propose one type of non-traditional source (court records, parish registers, oral tradition, archaeological remains, etc.) that could begin to recover those voices. What would you expect to find that the elite sources omit?

**Exercise 2.10** — *Defend a contested ranking.* The textbook offers the 1453 siege of Constantinople as an example of contested causal ranking. Build the strongest possible case for ranking the *tertiary* cause (the Ottoman Empire's structural reliance on continuous expansion) as the primary cause. Then build the strongest possible counter-argument. Decide which ranking you find more defensible and why. The exercise teaches you that historical interpretation is an argument, not a recital.

## Further reading

- **Bloch, Marc.** *The Historian's Craft.* 1949 (posthumous). The classic short statement of historical method by a French historian killed by the Gestapo. Begins with the question, *what is the use of history?* and answers it carefully across two hundred pages.
- **Carr, E. H.** *What Is History?* 1961. A series of lectures by a British historian arguing for the inseparability of historian and history. The most-assigned introduction to historiography in English.
- **Tuchman, Barbara W.** *Practicing History.* 1981. A working historian's essays on how she actually wrote her books. Concrete, opinionated, useful.
- **Trouillot, Michel-Rolph.** *Silencing the Past: Power and the Production of History.* 1995. A Haitian anthropologist's argument that historical silences are not accidents but products of power. Read alongside the Cortés/Aztec example in this chapter.
- **Spence, Jonathan.** *The Death of Woman Wang.* 1978. A short, masterful demonstration of social-history method on a single seventeenth-century Chinese county. Model for what the four questions can produce.
- **León-Portilla, Miguel.** *The Broken Spears: The Aztec Account of the Conquest of Mexico.* 1959 (English ed. 1962). The Indigenous-perspective companion to Cortés's letters. Read in dialogue.
- **Achebe, Chinua.** *Things Fall Apart.* 1958. Not a history book but a novel that has done as much as any historiographical text to reshape how late-colonial Africa is read.

```
Section 1 — The Commodity as Source.

Chapter 2 of the textbook taught the four interrogation questions
historians ask of any source: author, audience, intent, context. The
chapter also distinguished primary from secondary sources and named
schools of interpretation — great man, progressive, social,
intellectual, Marxist, gender, postcolonial.

Apply those tools to my commodity itself, treated as a *historical
object* I am about to follow across centuries. Write a ~600–1,000
word opening section that:

   am I actually tracing? Where does the definition get blurry
   (e.g., "salt" includes sea salt, rock salt, and brine evaporite;
   "silk" includes wild and cultivated forms; "paper" is not the

2. **State why this commodity is worth a biography.** What does

   or a person would not? Be specific. The Mark Kurlansky move is
   that *the commodity is the lens* — the commodity itself sees
   things that other lenses cannot.

3. **Apply the four interrogation questions to your commodity as
   evidence.** When you encounter [commodity] in a historical
   record — a tax document, a recipe, a tribute list, an

5. **State my stance going in.** Am I writing the biography of a

   mechanism)? The chapters ahead will test the stance.

Save the section to `commodity-biography.md` under the heading

secondary scholarship, and one image or artifact. Flag any
verification needs.
```
