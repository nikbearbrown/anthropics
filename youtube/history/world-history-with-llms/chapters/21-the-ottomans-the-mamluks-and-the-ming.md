# Chapter 21 — The Ottomans, the Mamluks, and the Ming

**Suggested titles**

- The Ottomans, the Mamluks, and the Ming
- 1453: How Three Empires Replaced Three Empires
- Slave-Soldiers, Cannon, and the Forbidden City: The Late-Medieval State Made Anew

**TL;DR.** Across the long century after the Black Death, three new state systems consolidated on the wreckage of older ones: the Ottoman Empire absorbed the Byzantine remnant in 1453, the Mamluk Sultanate ruled Egypt and Syria as a self-renewing slave-soldier oligarchy, and the Ming dynasty restored Confucian China after expelling the Mongols. Each was shaped by a common technology — gunpowder firearms — and a common institutional puzzle: how to recruit and control an elite military force without producing a hereditary aristocracy that could overthrow you. Their answers (Janissaries, mamluks, the Confucian examination civil service) defined how power worked in each empire and prefigured the early modern state.

---

## Overview

On the morning of May 29, 1453, the Theodosian Walls of Constantinople — six miles of triple fortification that had held against every army that had approached the city for almost a thousand years — finally broke. The breach came at the Saint Romanus Gate, between the second and third walls, where for fifty-seven days a Hungarian gunsmith named Urban had been firing twelve-hundred-pound granite balls at the masonry from a bombard so massive it required sixty oxen and four hundred men to move into firing position. The Ottoman army, perhaps eighty thousand strong, poured through the gap. The Byzantine emperor Constantine XI Palaeologus stripped off his imperial regalia, drew his sword, and died fighting in the breach, somewhere among the bodies that no one in subsequent centuries was ever able to identify with certainty.

The man who had ordered the bombardment was twenty-one years old. His name was Mehmed II. He would be remembered as Mehmed the Conqueror. By sunset he had walked into the Hagia Sophia — built by the emperor Justinian nine hundred years earlier, the largest cathedral in Christendom — and ordered it converted into a mosque. He had also forbidden his troops, who had spilled into the city for three days of looting under the customs of war, from killing or enslaving the Genoese and Venetian merchants who had stayed to defend the city alongside the Byzantines. He intended to rule Istanbul, not destroy it. The merchants were going to be useful.

This chapter is about that morning and what produced it. It is about three states — the Ottomans, the Mamluks, and the Ming — that consolidated themselves over the long century from 1350 to 1500 on the wreckage left by the Mongol collapse and the Black Death. It is about a common technology, gunpowder, that all three adopted and that none of them had invented. And it is about a common political problem — how to staff an elite army without raising a hereditary aristocracy that will overthrow you — that all three answered in different and ingenious ways. The Ottoman answer was the *devshirme* and the Janissaries; the Mamluk answer was the slave-soldier oligarchy itself; the Ming answer was a civil service of mandarins selected by examination, with the military pushed deliberately to the side.

A note on framing. The fall of Constantinople is the single most cited "end of the Middle Ages" date in Western historiography. It is also a slightly arbitrary date — the Mongol fragmentation, the Black Death, and the Ottoman expansion that produced 1453 had been underway for a century, and the Renaissance and gunpowder revolution that followed continued for two more. But 1453 is a useful anchor because it really did matter: the last institutional remnant of the Roman state, in continuous operation since Augustus, ended that morning at the Saint Romanus Gate. Whatever followed in Eurasia, the Roman frame in which medieval Mediterranean politics had been understood for a millennium was finished.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- Trace the rise of the Ottoman state from Osman's *beylik* in northwestern Anatolia to the empire of Mehmed II.
- Explain Timur's career and the Battle of Ankara, and account for why the Timurid empire did not consolidate as the Mongol or Ottoman states did.
- Describe the institution of slave-soldiery as it operated in the Mamluk Sultanate and the Ottoman *devshirme* — what problems each was designed to solve, and what costs each imposed.
- Account for the rise of the Ming dynasty, the Hongwu and Yongle reigns, and the long-term consequences of the Zheng He voyages and their abandonment.
- Connect the spread of gunpowder weaponry (handheld firearms, siege bombards, star forts) to the political consolidation of each of these three empires.
- Place the Italian Renaissance in the same frame, as an intellectual consequence of post-1453 Greek migration to Italy and Italian commercial enrichment from Ottoman trade.

## Opening case — The bombard at the Saint Romanus Gate

Urban the Hungarian had originally offered his services to the Byzantines. The emperor Constantine XI, with imperial finances in ruins, could not afford his asking price. Urban took his designs to Mehmed II, who could.

The bombard Urban built in early 1453 was probably the largest gun yet manufactured anywhere in the world. The barrel was bronze, twenty-seven feet long. The shot was a granite cannonball weighing twelve hundred pounds. Mehmed had the gun cast at Edirne, the Ottoman capital before Istanbul, and dragged the 150 miles to Constantinople by an enormous team of oxen, with three hundred bridge-builders going ahead to reinforce roads and bridges along the way.

The gun was not, strictly speaking, decisive on its own. It could fire perhaps seven shots a day. Each shot cracked a section of wall, but the Byzantines, working through the night, repaired the breach with timber, earth, and barrels of stones. The siege went on for fifty-seven days, with smaller bombards and conventional siege techniques (sapping, mining, naval blockade) doing most of the work. What the great gun did was set the limit. The Theodosian Walls — which had stood against Avars, Bulgars, Persians, Arabs, Russians, and Mongols across the previous eight hundred years — could be breached. They had now been breached. Once that fact became visible, the question of how long the city could hold became a question of supplies and morale.

The supplies ran out. The morale broke when, on the night of May 28, the Genoese commander Giovanni Giustiniani — who had been leading the defense at the inner wall — was wounded by a culverin shot and carried off to a ship in the harbor. By morning his men had abandoned their positions. The Ottomans poured through. Constantine XI rallied a small group at the gate and was killed; Mehmed II's troops, by lunchtime, had reached the Hagia Sophia.

Hold the moment. The same technology — gunpowder weapons — that had broken Constantinople was, that summer, available to every major Eurasian state. Mamluk armies had been using early cannon since Ain Jalut in 1260. The Ming had been forging cannon from bronze since the thirteenth century. Western European princes were buying bombards from Hungarian, Burgundian, and Italian foundries. What the Ottoman victory at Constantinople demonstrated was not new technology but new *organization*. A state that could finance, transport, and operate a siege train of this scale was a different kind of state from anything that had existed in the Mediterranean a century earlier. The Ottoman state had become that.

How? The chapter is about how.

## Concept 1 — The Ottoman ascent: from Osman to Mehmed

The Ottoman story begins on the northwestern fringe of Anatolia in the late thirteenth century, in a small *beylik* — a Turkish principality — led by a frontier warlord named Osman (d. circa 1324). Osman's *beylik* sat where the older Sultanate of Rum had collapsed under Mongol pressure after the Battle of Köse Dağ in 1243. The Anatolian political map after Köse Dağ was a patchwork of small Turkish *beyliks*, each fighting the others and each pressing the diminished Byzantine territories along the Aegean coast. Osman's *beylik* was one of perhaps fifteen.

What made it different was a combination of geography, ideology, and luck. Geography first: Osman's territory bordered Byzantine Bithynia, the rich agricultural plain just south of Constantinople. Conquest there was lucrative and gave the small Ottoman polity outsized wealth. Ideology second: Osman and his successors styled themselves *ghazis* — warriors of the faith on the frontier between Islam and Christendom — which gave them legitimacy with a steady supply of Turkic nomadic warriors who had been pressed west by the Mongol expansion. Luck third: the Byzantine state was in continuous dynastic crisis through the early fourteenth century, and the Ottomans were able to expand against an opponent that could not consistently defend itself.

Under Osman's son Orhan (r. 1324–1362), the Ottomans took Bursa in 1326 and made it their capital. They reached the Sea of Marmara. In 1354 — the same year an earthquake destroyed the walls of the Byzantine fortress at Gallipoli on the European side of the Dardanelles — Orhan's son Suleyman crossed and seized the ruined fortress before the Byzantines could rebuild it. The Ottomans were now in Europe. They would not leave. Within a generation, under Murad I (r. 1362–1389), they had taken Adrianople (Edirne), made it their European capital, and pushed deep into the Balkans. At Kosovo on June 28, 1389 — a date still observed as a Serbian national day — Murad's army defeated the Serbian prince Lazar in a battle so destructive that both rulers died on the field. (Serbian tradition holds that a Serbian knight named Miloš Obilić assassinated Murad in his tent during the battle; Ottoman sources say a wounded Serbian came forward to kiss the sultan's hand and stabbed him as he bent. The chronicles cannot be reconciled and both stories may contain elements of truth.)

The Ottoman trajectory from a fourteenth-century *beylik* to the empire of Mehmed II depended on two innovations.

First, the *timar* system. The Ottomans needed cavalry but did not have a settled aristocracy to recruit it from. They invented one. Conquered land was assigned in *timars* — non-hereditary fiefs — to cavalry officers (*timariots*) who were granted the right to collect specified taxes from a particular district in exchange for military service. The *timariot* lived off the *timar*, equipped himself for war out of its proceeds, and rode with the sultan when called. The land remained the sultan's; the *timar* could be revoked. On the *timariot*'s death the *timar* reverted to the sultan, who reassigned it. This system gave the Ottomans a large mobile cavalry without the cost of a standing salaried force, and without producing the hereditary noble class whose insubordination had wrecked many earlier Islamic dynasties. It also tied the *timariots*' interests to the productivity of their land — *timars* were not slaves' shares but tax farms, and a *timariot* whose peasants prospered prospered too.

Second, the *devshirme* and the Janissaries. We will return to this in detail in Concept 4. For now, the short version: from the late fourteenth century onward, Ottoman agents periodically conscripted Christian boys from Balkan villages — perhaps one in forty, every three to five years — converted them to Islam, gave them sustained military and administrative training, and assigned the most promising to the elite infantry corps known as the Janissaries (*yeni cheri*, "new soldiers") and to senior administrative posts. The Janissaries were the sultan's personal slaves, technically owned, completely dependent on him for their status, and had no kinship ties to the Anatolian Turkish nobility who might otherwise have constrained him.

The two innovations worked together. The *timar* system gave the Ottomans cavalry; the *devshirme* gave them infantry and bureaucrats; both were structured to prevent the formation of a hereditary class that could rival the dynasty. By the time of Mehmed II, the Ottoman state was institutionally distinctive in a way that almost no other large polity of its era was. It had a salaried standing infantry, a tax-farming cavalry, a meritocratic central bureaucracy whose senior members were technically slaves, and a sultan whose personal authority over all of these was unconstrained by any analog to the European nobility, the Byzantine senatorial class, or the Mamluk *ulama*.

What it did not yet have, in 1402, was Constantinople. And in that year the Ottomans nearly disappeared as a polity entirely.

## Concept 2 — Timur and the Battle of Ankara

Timur — Tamerlane, "Timur the Lame," from the limp he carried after a youthful injury that may have been a wound from sheep-rustling — was born in the 1330s in the Chagatai Khanate, in what is now Uzbekistan. He was a Turco-Mongol Muslim of the Barlas tribe, ethnically Turkic but culturally Mongol. He was not a Chinggisid — he could not legitimately claim the title of khan — and worked around the limitation by ruling through a puppet khan and by marrying a Chinggisid princess to claim the title "royal son-in-law" (*güregen*).

From 1370 until his death in 1405, Timur conquered most of the territory that Chinggis Khan had once held, with an army organized on the same decimal model and a strategic ruthlessness that was, if anything, more theatrical than his predecessor's. His characteristic punishment for cities that resisted was the *kelle minare*, the "tower of skulls" — minarets made of severed heads, sometimes thousands at a time. Isfahan in 1387 produced perhaps seventy thousand. Delhi in 1398 produced more. The destruction of Damascus in 1401 ended Mamluk control of Syria for a decade.

Unlike Chinggis, Timur made no serious effort at integrated administration outside his core territories in Persia and Transoxiana. His campaigns were essentially extractive: he took what he wanted from a region — gold, artisans, scholars — and sent it back to his capital at Samarkand. This is why Samarkand under Timur and his grandson Ulugh Beg (an astronomer who built one of the finest medieval observatories) became one of the great architectural and intellectual centers of the Islamic world, with the Bibi-Khanym Mosque and the Registan ensemble. It is also why Timur's empire fell apart very quickly after his death. He had built a tribute machine, not a state.

The collision with the Ottomans came in 1402. Bayezid I — Murad's son, called *Yıldırım*, "the Thunderbolt," for his battlefield speed — had been busy. He had crushed a crusading army of French, Burgundian, and Hungarian knights at the Battle of Nicopolis in 1396. He had besieged Constantinople. He had absorbed several smaller Anatolian *beyliks* that had been functioning as Mongol-era tributaries. The dispossessed *beys* fled to Timur and asked him to intervene.

Timur did. He took Sivas in 1400, defeating an Ottoman army and reportedly burying the captured garrison alive. Then, in July 1402, his army met Bayezid's at Ankara. The Ottoman army was perhaps the largest Bayezid had ever fielded, but it included contingents from the absorbed Anatolian *beyliks* — and at the decisive moment, those contingents defected to Timur, who they regarded as a liberator from Ottoman rule. Bayezid's Janissaries and his loyal Serbian cavalry under Stefan Lazarević fought to the end. Bayezid himself was captured. Timur kept him prisoner — by some accounts in a literal cage, though the cage detail may be a later European embellishment — for the eight months until Bayezid's death in March 1403.

The Ottoman state nearly ceased to exist. Bayezid's four surviving sons — Suleyman, Isa, Musa, and Mehmed — fought a civil war for the throne. Timur, having broken the Ottomans, declined to administer Anatolia. He left in early 1403, intending to invade Ming China. He died in February 1405 at Otrar, on the Syr Darya, before the campaign could begin.

The Ottoman recovery is the structural point. By 1413, Mehmed I — Bayezid's youngest surviving son — had defeated his brothers and reunited the empire. By 1422, his son Murad II was besieging Constantinople again. The Ottoman recovery was possible because the institutional infrastructure that Bayezid had built — the *timar* system, the Janissary corps, the central administration — had survived the dynastic war intact. The persons of the rulers had changed; the institutions had not. This is the test that distinguishes a durable state from a personal empire. Timur had built the latter and his collapsed. The Ottomans had built the former and theirs did not.

**The trade-off named.** Personal charisma can build extraordinary empires extraordinarily fast — Chinggis Khan, Timur, Napoleon — but they are catastrophically vulnerable to succession crises. Institutional empires grow more slowly but absorb shocks the personal empires cannot. The Ottoman state of the fifteenth century was institutional in the relevant sense; the Timurid state was not. This is a recurrent pattern in state-formation, and worth holding onto when you encounter it again later in this book — for instance, when comparing Napoleon's empire with the British system that defeated it.

## Concept 3 — The taking of Constantinople

By the time Mehmed II became sultan for the second time in 1451 — he had been briefly enthroned at twelve, then deposed, then re-enthroned at nineteen — Constantinople was a hollowed-out shell. Plague, sieges, Latin occupation in 1204, and the steady territorial erosion of the Byzantine Empire had reduced the city's population from perhaps half a million at its peak to around fifty thousand. The Byzantine territory outside the walls had shrunk to a few enclaves in southern Greece (the Despotate of the Morea) and along the Black Sea (the Empire of Trebizond). The emperor, Constantine XI, ruled a city whose walls were intact but whose hinterland was almost entirely Ottoman.

Mehmed prepared the siege carefully. He built Rumelihisarı, a fortress at the narrowest point of the Bosporus, in early 1452 to control the strait. He commissioned Urban's bombards. He built a fleet — the Ottomans had not had a serious navy before — capable of contesting the Genoese and Venetian galleys that were the city's principal lifeline. By April 1453 he had perhaps eighty thousand men around the walls. The Byzantines, including foreign defenders from Genoa, Venice, and the Papal States, numbered perhaps seven thousand fighting men.

The siege lasted from April 6 to May 29. The decisive moment on the Ottoman side was probably the night of April 22, when Mehmed had seventy-two of his ships transported overland on greased logs from the Bosporus into the Golden Horn (the city's inner harbor), bypassing the great chain that the Byzantines had stretched across the harbor mouth. The Byzantines now had to defend the harbor walls as well as the land walls. The defense was stretched too thin. On the evening of May 28, after a final Christian liturgy in the Hagia Sophia attended by Constantine XI and most of the city's clergy and laity, the troops returned to their posts. The next morning, Mehmed launched a general assault. By midday Constantinople had fallen.

Mehmed entered the city on horseback. He went directly to the Hagia Sophia. He prayed there. He ordered the structure preserved — converted to a mosque, with the addition of minarets and the whitewashing of some Christian mosaics, but otherwise intact. (The mosaics were not destroyed but covered over; they were rediscovered, intact, when the building was secularized as a museum in 1935.) Mehmed then walked through the abandoned imperial palace and is reported by the contemporary Greek historian George Sphrantzes to have quoted, in Persian, the medieval Persian poet Saadi:

> The spider weaves its curtain in the palace of the Caesars,
> The owl calls the watches in the towers of Afrasiab.

The sack continued for three days. Mehmed then halted the looting and began rebuilding. He repopulated the city by ordering the resettlement of Greek, Armenian, Jewish, and Turkish populations from across the empire. He confirmed the position of the Greek Orthodox Patriarch, Gennadius II Scholarius, as head of all Orthodox Christians within Ottoman territory — a status that gave the Patriarch more institutional authority over Orthodox Christianity than any single bishop had had under late Byzantine rule. He created the *millet* system — formal recognition of Christian, Jewish, and Armenian religious communities, each governed internally by its own leadership and its own law, paying a separate poll tax (*jizya*) to the Ottoman state in exchange. He invited Italian artists; Gentile Bellini, sent by the Republic of Venice, painted his portrait in 1480.

By the time of his death in 1481, Mehmed had also conquered the Despotate of the Morea (1460), Trebizond (1461), Bosnia (1463), Albania (1478), and the Black Sea ports including Caffa, the same city through which the Black Death had entered Europe a century earlier. The Ottoman Empire was now the dominant Mediterranean power east of Italy.

## Concept 4 — Slave-soldier systems: the Janissaries and the Mamluks

We need to slow down on a feature these states shared and that is unfamiliar from a modern perspective: institutional slavery as the *foundation* of elite military and administrative power.

The pattern starts in the ninth-century Abbasid caliphate. The early caliphs had relied on Arab tribal levies and on Persian client soldiers. In the ninth century, the caliph al-Mu'tasim (r. 833–842) began purchasing Turkic boys from the steppe north of the Oxus and training them as personal guards — the *ghulam* system. The political logic was clear: a Turkic slave-soldier had no kinship ties in the caliphate, no faction to mobilize, no land to defend. His loyalty went only to the man who had bought, freed, and trained him. The political risk of the previous Arab tribal armies — that they would reorganize into rival factions and overthrow the caliph — was substantially reduced.

The system, however, had a structural instability. The slave-soldiers, once trained, became politically powerful in their own right. They began choosing caliphs by themselves. By the tenth century, the Abbasid caliph was effectively a puppet of his Turkic guard. The system that had been designed to control elite military faction had produced a new elite military faction.

The Ayyubids, who took Egypt and Syria from the Fatimids in the late twelfth century, picked up the slave-soldier system on the same logic. Their *mamluks* — from Arabic *mamluk*, "owned" — were Kipchak Turks from the steppe north of the Black Sea, purchased as boys, raised in barracks, trained in archery and horsemanship, converted to Sunni Islam, and freed when their training was complete. By the mid-thirteenth century, the Ayyubid sultans of Egypt had so many *mamluks* and so few Ayyubid relatives that the *mamluks* themselves overthrew the dynasty. In 1250, after the death of Sultan as-Salih Ayyub during the Seventh Crusade, his *mamluks* murdered his successor and installed one of their own. The Mamluk Sultanate was born.

For the next 267 years, until the Ottoman conquest of 1517, Egypt and Syria were ruled by a self-perpetuating military oligarchy of slave-soldiers. The pattern was: a *mamluk* commander seized the sultanate, ruled for an average of seven years (often less), and was overthrown by another *mamluk* commander. Between 1250 and 1517, perhaps fifty sultans reigned. Most were murdered. Sons of sultans almost never inherited; the *mamluks* regarded biological inheritance as illegitimate, since a sultan's son had not undergone the slave training that was the mamluks' source of social authority.

The Mamluk system worked, in the sense that it ran a major Islamic state for two and a half centuries, defeated the Mongols at Ain Jalut, finished off the Crusader states (Tripoli 1289, Acre 1291), and presided over Cairo's golden age. It also imposed enormous costs. The succession instability meant constant urban violence. The mamluks rioted in Cairo in 1399, 1434, 1437, 1441, and almost every year of the late fifteenth century. Public order was a function of which faction was momentarily dominant. The mamluk economy was extractive: revenues from Syria and Egypt were converted into *iqta* land grants to active mamluks, who used them to support themselves and to purchase the next generation of mamluks. When the Black Death cut the Egyptian population by a third in 1347–1351, and again in subsequent waves, the mamluk fiscal base eroded badly. Cairo never fully recovered the pre-plague population.

The Ottomans drew on the same Islamic tradition but adapted it. Their solution was the *devshirme* — the "gathering" — a periodic levy of Christian boys from the conquered Balkans. The boys, ideally aged eight to ten, were taken from their villages by Ottoman agents, brought to Istanbul, taught Turkish, converted to Islam, and trained for between four and eight years. The most academically promising entered the Inner Service and trained for senior administrative posts including Grand Vizier. The most physically capable became Janissaries.

The Janissaries, by the late fifteenth century, were the most disciplined infantry force in Eurasia. They lived in barracks. They were paid salaries from the imperial treasury rather than from land grants. They were the first European force to adopt firearms in significant numbers. Their commander, the Aga of the Janissaries, was a senior court official. They were technically the personal slaves of the sultan; they were also, by the sixteenth century, his political masters. Like the Abbasid *ghulam* and the Ayyubid mamluks before them, the Janissary corps eventually became powerful enough to make and unmake sultans. The Janissary revolts of the seventeenth, eighteenth, and early nineteenth centuries (culminating in the violent abolition of the corps in 1826) are the recognizable pattern.

We have an unusual primary source on the *devshirme* experience. Konstantin Mihailović, a Serbian boy taken in the *devshirme* around 1455, became a Janissary, served in several Ottoman campaigns including the conquest of Bosnia, was captured by Hungarians in 1463, and eventually wrote a memoir in Polish (*Memoirs of a Janissary*, 1490s). He describes being separated from his brothers, attempting to flee with friends, being recaptured and beaten, and ultimately being trained at the Anatolian preserve. The tone of the memoir is unsentimental but not bitter; he treats his Janissary service as a fact about his life rather than as an injustice he is asking the reader to lament. This is, perhaps, what we should expect from a man whose adult life and identity had been substantially formed by the institution. The view from inside the slave-soldier system was different from the view from outside.

**The trade-off named.** A slave-soldier system solves one problem — controlling elite military faction — by producing another problem — the slaves themselves become a new elite military faction. The instability is structural. What different states did with the instability varied. The Ottomans buffered it by combining the *devshirme* with the *timar* cavalry and by maintaining strong dynastic continuity. The Mamluks did not buffer it at all; they let the instability run as a feature, and accepted constant succession violence as the price. The Abbasids, who pioneered the system, eventually lost their state to it. None of these arrangements is intuitive from a modern liberal perspective, where slavery and elite power are categorically opposed. The fifteenth-century Mediterranean shows that this opposition is historically contingent.

## Mid-chapter checkpoint

You have, so far, three things. First, an Ottoman state that rose from a small *beylik* in 1300, nearly disappeared after Ankara in 1402, and emerged by 1453 as the institutional successor to both Byzantium and the Mongol khanates. Second, a Mamluk state that ran Egypt and Syria as a self-renewing slave-soldier oligarchy for 267 years, defended Sunni Islam from the Mongols, and absorbed the post-Baghdad Abbasid caliph as a religious figurehead. Third, a structural feature shared between them — institutional slavery as the basis of elite military and administrative power — whose logic and trade-offs we have now made explicit.

Hold those. We turn now to the eastern end of the chapter: Ming China.

## Concept 5 — The Ming and the Confucian restoration

When Zhu Yuanzhang took Beijing in 1368 and proclaimed himself Hongwu Emperor of the Ming dynasty, he was answering a different problem than the Ottomans or Mamluks. The Ottomans were assembling a state from a frontier *beylik*. The Mamluks were perpetuating one through a slave-soldier oligarchy. The Ming were *restoring* one — putting Han Chinese rule back in place after a century of foreign Mongol Yuan rule.

Hongwu's biography is the rebuke to anyone who thinks Chinese imperial dynasties were always founded by aristocrats. He was born in 1328 to a tenant farming family in Anhui. Both parents and most of his siblings died in 1344, almost certainly of the plague that the Yuan chronicles record in northern China that year. He became a Buddhist novice at the Huangjue Temple. The temple ran out of food in the famine; he begged in the countryside for three years. He joined the Red Turbans — a millenarian Buddhist-led peasant rebellion explicitly invoking the Maitreya Buddha — at age twenty-four. By thirty-six, he was the strongest of the Red Turban warlords. By forty, he had taken Beijing and proclaimed the Ming.

His subsequent reign was shaped by his origins. He distrusted aristocrats (he had several thousand of them, including former allies, executed). He distrusted bureaucrats (he abolished the office of Chief Minister in 1380 after the Hu Weiyong purge in which roughly thirty thousand officials and their families were killed for alleged conspiracy). He distrusted the rich (he confiscated estates and forced thousands of wealthy southern families to relocate to the north). He distrusted foreign influence (he banned almost all maritime trade and dismantled the Yuan-era international commercial networks centered on Quanzhou and other ports).

What he did trust — having been a peasant — was the agricultural smallholder. The early Ming reorganized China's land system to favor small family farms over large estates, restored the granary system, and rebuilt the Grand Canal. The early Ming also restored the Confucian examination civil service that the Yuan had let lapse, with the difference that the Ming examinations focused tightly on the Four Books and Five Classics in the Zhu Xi Neo-Confucian interpretation. The model student was a scholar-official whose authority came from passing examinations, not from inherited wealth or military command.

The model official, in turn, was firmly subordinate to the emperor in a way that earlier dynasties had not enforced. Hongwu's abolition of the Chief Minister concentrated decision-making in the emperor's person. He worked, by his own count, on hundreds of memorials a day. The administrative load was unsustainable, and his successors quickly began to delegate to a Grand Secretariat — a kind of working cabinet that served at the emperor's pleasure but could not formally challenge him. The Ming bureaucracy that emerged from this was simultaneously enormously powerful (running a country of perhaps 100 million people across enormous distances) and structurally weak (unable to act without the emperor's approval, paralyzed when emperors became disengaged).

Hongwu's son and ultimate successor — after a brief reign by Hongwu's grandson, the Jianwen Emperor, whom he overthrew in 1402 — was Zhu Di, the Yongle Emperor (r. 1402–1424). Yongle is the figure most non-Chinese readers associate with the early Ming. He moved the capital from Nanjing to Beijing in 1421. He commissioned the *Yongle Encyclopedia* (*Yongle Dadian*), at the time the largest reference work ever compiled — over twenty-two thousand chapters in eleven thousand volumes. He extended the Great Wall in its current Ming form. He attempted, unsuccessfully, to conquer Vietnam (1406–1427). And he sent out the seven naval expeditions of Zheng He.

## Concept 6 — Zheng He, the Forbidden City, and the abandoned ocean

Zheng He's voyages are one of the great might-have-beens of world history. Between 1405 and 1433, Zheng — a Muslim eunuch from Yunnan, captured as a boy after the Ming conquest of his region and raised in the imperial palace as one of Yongle's most trusted servants — commanded seven expeditions across the South China Sea, the Indian Ocean, and the Arabian Sea. The fleets carried perhaps 27,000 men and as many as 250 ships. The largest treasure ships, by the most credible reconstructions, were over 400 feet long — perhaps four times the length of the carracks Christopher Columbus would sail to the Americas eighty years later.

Zheng's voyages reached Java, Sumatra, Ceylon, the Indian coast at Calicut and Cochin, the Persian Gulf at Hormuz, the Arabian Peninsula, and the Swahili coast as far south as Malindi. The expeditions were not military conquests; they were tribute missions, bringing the smaller polities of the Indian Ocean rim into formal tributary relationships with the Ming court. They returned with envoys, exotic animals (including, famously, a giraffe presented to Yongle in 1414 as a *qilin*, a mythical beast), and trade goods. Several local rulers who resisted (a Sumatran prince, the king of Ceylon) were defeated and brought to Beijing; most cooperated and accepted nominal tributary status.

What is striking is not that the voyages happened but that they stopped. After Yongle's death in 1424, his successors — particularly the Hongxi (r. 1424–1425) and Xuande emperors (r. 1425–1435) — found the expeditions enormously expensive and of limited strategic return. The court was already turning inward. The Mongol threat on the northern border was reasserting itself (we will get to Tumu in a moment). The southern expansion into Vietnam had failed. The Confucian scholar-officials, ascendant in the Yongle period, had lost ground to court eunuchs (including Zheng) under his successors and were keen to reverse the process. The seventh and final voyage in 1431–1433, after Yongle's death, was a coda. After that, the Ming court banned ocean-going Chinese ships from leaving port (the *haijin* policy), closed the Quanzhou customs office, and, as later sources tell us, eventually destroyed the records and ship-construction expertise of the Treasure Fleet.

By 1500, China had effectively withdrawn from the Indian Ocean. The Indian Ocean trading system continued — the Swahili and Gujarati and Malabar ports remained active, and Arab and Indian vessels continued to dominate the routes — but the largest navy in the world had ceased to exist as a maritime force. When Vasco da Gama rounded the Cape of Good Hope and reached Calicut in 1498, the Indian Ocean he entered was a Muslim and Hindu commercial system that had recently lost its largest player.

The Ming inland project, however, continued. The Forbidden City was completed in 1420. It was a walled imperial complex of nearly a thousand buildings on 178 acres, organized along a strict north-south axis and according to Confucian principles of hierarchy: the Hall of Supreme Harmony for state functions, the Palace of Heavenly Purity for the emperor's residence, the Palace of Earthly Tranquility for the empress, and a constellation of subsidiary palaces for the imperial concubines and household. The complex was simultaneously an administrative center, a residence, and a model of cosmological order. Its gates were arranged so that the central axis was reserved exclusively for the emperor, who alone could enter through the central gateway except in precisely defined ritual circumstances (the empress on her wedding day; men who had passed the highest examinations).

The Forbidden City makes a useful pairing with Topkapi Palace, which Mehmed II began constructing in Istanbul in 1459. Both complexes were built on the same logic: the imperial center as a hierarchical, cosmologically charged enclosure, with concentric zones of access, a private inner palace for the ruler and household, and elaborate ceremonial restrictions on movement. Both would be the centers of their respective empires for the next four centuries. The architectural language is different — Confucian symmetry in Beijing, Islamic and Byzantine pavilion structures in Istanbul — but the political idea is closely parallel.

The Ming also faced one major military catastrophe in this period that is worth narrating. In 1449, the Zhengtong Emperor (also known as Yingzong), then twenty-one, was talked into personally leading an expedition against the Oirat Mongols under their khan Esen Tayisi. The campaign was a disaster. Logistics broke down. The army of perhaps 500,000 became disorganized in the steppe. At Tumu Fortress, the Oirats annihilated the Chinese force and captured the emperor himself — the only Chinese emperor in the dynasty's nearly three-century run to be taken prisoner by a foreign enemy. The Ming court, refusing to ransom him, demoted him to "retired emperor" and put his half-brother on the throne as the Jingtai Emperor. Yingzong was eventually released by the Oirats — Esen could not extract a ransom and could not use his prisoner as leverage — and lived under house arrest in the Forbidden City for seven years before staging a coup against his brother and reclaiming the throne in 1457. The episode is sometimes called the Tumu Crisis. It revealed to everyone, including the Mongols, that the Ming military was not invulnerable, and contributed to the strategic decision in subsequent decades to invest in the Great Wall as a defensive infrastructure rather than to project force north of it.

**The trade-off named.** The Ming under Yongle made a strategic choice that historians have argued about ever since: to abandon ocean projection and concentrate on continental defense. The choice was not stupid in its context — the Mongol threat was real, the Vietnamese campaign had failed, the fiscal strain of the Treasure Fleets was substantial. But it had a long-tail consequence. By withdrawing from the Indian Ocean exactly as European maritime powers were arriving, the Ming left the field. The early modern globalization of the next four centuries would be conducted by ships from Lisbon, Amsterdam, and London, not from Nanjing or Quanzhou. The choice was reversible at the time and was occasionally debated within the Ming court. It was not, in fact, reversed.

## Concept 7 — The gunpowder revolution and the end of the cavalry empire

Across all three of these states — Ottoman, Mamluk, Ming — and across the Western European societies they were trading and fighting with, the technology that organized the late fifteenth century was gunpowder.

The technology was Chinese. The earliest recorded battlefield use was in 919 CE; bamboo "fire lances" (handheld flame projectors) appear in twelfth-century Chinese sources; bronze cannon are documented by the thirteenth century. The Mongol expansion carried the technology westward; Arab and Persian alchemical texts of the thirteenth century describe gunpowder formulas; by the early fourteenth century, primitive cannon were appearing in European battles (Crécy 1346 is the conventional first European use, though the cannon there were not decisive).

What changed in the fifteenth century was scale and accuracy. Bombards like Urban's grew large enough to break medieval walls. Smaller cannon became portable enough for field use. Handheld firearms — the arquebus, then the matchlock musket — became viable, although still cumbersome, slow to load, and inaccurate. The transformation happened across Eurasia roughly simultaneously, with regional variations.

The political consequences are the spine of the chapter's last argument.

First, gunpowder ended the era of the impregnable city. From 1453 forward, walls could be broken. Cities became dependent on field armies and on new forms of fortification (the *trace italienne*, the angled-bastion star fort, developed in late-fifteenth-century Italy and exported across Europe over the next century). The economics of city defense flipped: where medieval walls had been a one-time capital investment that protected the city for centuries, gunpowder fortifications required continuous massive investment. Only states with strong central tax bases could afford them. The fragmented feudal Europe of small lords and modest castles could not. The Ottoman, Mamluk, and Ming states could.

Second, gunpowder ended the era of the cavalry-aristocrat. The mounted warrior had been the backbone of European, Persian, Indian, and steppe armies for two thousand years. Knight, *furusiyya* horseman, *kshatriya*, mamluk cavalry, Mongol horse-archer — the mounted aristocrat or near-aristocrat, expensive to equip and trained from childhood, dominated battlefields. Firearms made him obsolete. A peasant with three weeks of training and an arquebus could, in volley fire formation, defeat a cavalry charge. The peasant was much cheaper. The aristocrat was much more expendable than he had been.

The political consequence — across Europe, the Islamic world, and East Asia — was the centralized state with infantry-heavy armies, salaried soldiers, and a tax base sufficient to pay for both. The Janissaries are an early version. Spanish *tercios*, Swedish reformed pike-and-shot infantry, Mughal infantry, Ming bannermen — these are all variations. The aristocracy, where it survived, became increasingly courtly rather than military. Where it did not survive, the change was traumatic; the European Wars of Religion of the next two centuries were partly about which kind of state would emerge from the gunpowder transition.

Third, and as a slow consequence of the previous two, gunpowder ended the era of the steppe nomad as a major political force. The pattern that had defined Eurasian history from the Xiongnu through the Mongols — a nomadic confederation organizing itself out of the steppe and conquering settled neighbors — became progressively harder to execute. The steppe nomad's traditional advantages were mobility and individual archery skill. Gunpowder wiped out the second; only the first remained. By the seventeenth century, the steppe peoples were either being absorbed into the new gunpowder empires (the Manchus, who became the Qing dynasty in 1644, are the great exception — they conquered China by adopting Han Chinese infantry and artillery) or pushed to the margins of the world economy (the western Mongols after the seventeenth century; the Plains Indians of North America in the nineteenth).

The Mongol and Turco-Mongol cavalry empires of Chinggis and Timur were the last of their kind. After 1500, the major land empires would be settled, gunpowder, and bureaucratic.

## Concept 8 — The Renaissance, briefly

One last thread, because the chapter started in 1453 and 1453 is also conventionally the start of the Italian Renaissance.

The Renaissance was already underway in northern Italy before Constantinople fell. Petrarch (1304–1374) had been advocating a return to classical Latin style for a century. Florentine humanists had been recovering Latin manuscripts since the early 1400s. The Medici had been patronizing artists in Florence since the 1430s. What 1453 added was a sudden influx of Greek-speaking scholars and Greek manuscripts.

Anna Notaras, a wealthy Byzantine noblewoman who had moved to Venice before the conquest, helped organize the relocation of Greek scholars and clergy into Italy. Greek manuscripts of Plato (largely unknown in the Latin West until then), of Plotinus, of Galen, of Aristotle in the original Greek (Aristotle had circulated in Latin via Arabic translations) reached Florence and Rome. Marsilio Ficino translated all of Plato's known works into Latin between the 1460s and his death in 1499. The Florentine Platonic Academy became a center for the synthesis of Plato, Christian theology, and Hermetic mysticism that would define late Renaissance humanism.

The art history of the Renaissance is its more famous story — Brunelleschi's perspective, Masaccio's frescoes, Donatello's sculpture, the synthesis in Leonardo and Michelangelo — but the intellectual story is at least as important and is more directly tied to the Ottoman event. The Aristotelian-Platonic synthesis that emerged from late-fifteenth-century Florence shaped the early scientific revolution. Copernicus was reading Plato and Plotinus alongside Ptolemy. Galileo's mathematical Platonism — the conviction that the universe is written in the language of mathematics — is recognizable as a downstream consequence of Ficino. The line from Mehmed's bombards to Galileo's telescope is not direct, but it runs.

## Synthesis

Pull back. Three states. Three answers to the same problem.

The Ottomans built a centralized empire on conquered territory by combining a non-hereditary cavalry tax-farming class (*timar*), a slave-soldier infantry corps and bureaucracy (*devshirme* and Janissaries), and a sultan whose authority was constrained by neither nobility nor *ulama*. They took Constantinople with gunpowder, absorbed the Byzantine institutions that survived, and ruled a multi-confessional Mediterranean empire for four hundred years.

The Mamluks ran an Egyptian-Syrian state by inverting the relationship: rather than slave-soldiers serving the dynasty, the slave-soldiers *were* the dynasty. The system was extractive, violent, and unstable, but it survived two and a half centuries of constant succession turmoil because no other faction could displace the slave-soldier class. The Ottomans finally did so in 1517.

The Ming restored Han Chinese rule with a Confucian-bureaucratic civil service whose authority came from an examination system that nominally selected by merit. The military was deliberately subordinated. The state turned inward in the mid-fifteenth century, abandoning the Indian Ocean and concentrating on continental defense. The choice was not foreordained, but it stuck.

Each state's solution had costs. The Ottoman *devshirme* extracted Christian children under coercion. The Mamluk system burned through sultans at a rate of one every seven years on average. The Ming bureaucracy, though magnificent at its best, became increasingly paralyzed when emperors disengaged, and the resulting institutional decay would contribute to the Ming collapse in 1644.

What unites them is what unites all three with the gunpowder-revolutionary states of Western Europe: by 1500 the basic political architecture of the early modern era was visible. Centralized states with paid standing armies, gunpowder weapons, salaried bureaucracies, and revenue extraction systems sufficient to pay for all of the above. The medieval order — the world of feudal kingdoms, *ghazi* warlords, hereditary nobility, walled cities — was over, or in obvious decline. Something else had replaced it.

Now come back to the morning of May 29, 1453. Urban's bombard at the Saint Romanus Gate. The Theodosian Walls breaching. Constantine XI dying somewhere among the bodies. Mehmed II walking into the Hagia Sophia. From the perspective of the people in the city, this was a discrete catastrophe — the end of an empire, the loss of a city, the beginning of a captivity for some and a new political loyalty for others. From a longer perspective, it is the moment when one early modern state demonstrated that it could decisively defeat a late medieval one. The technology was newly mature. The institutions were newly assembled. The combination was new.

A last sentence, then. The medieval Mediterranean ended, more or less, on a May morning in 1453. The early modern Mediterranean — and the early modern world — began that afternoon.

## Chapter summary

The Ottoman Turkish state grew from Osman's late-thirteenth-century *beylik* in northwestern Anatolia, expanded into the Balkans in the fourteenth century (Edirne 1362, Kosovo 1389), and was nearly destroyed by Timur at the Battle of Ankara in 1402. Recovery under Mehmed I and Murad II led to the conquest of Constantinople by Mehmed II on May 29, 1453, ending the Byzantine Empire. Ottoman institutional innovations — the *timar* tax-farming cavalry, the *devshirme* slave-soldier conscription, the Janissary infantry corps, and the *millet* system of religious-community governance — produced an institutionally distinctive state.

Timur (Tamerlane), a Turco-Mongol conqueror from the Chagatai Khanate, assembled an empire from the 1370s to 1405 stretching from Anatolia to Delhi, but built no durable administrative structure beyond Persia and Transoxiana. His empire fragmented after his death.

The Mamluk Sultanate (1250–1517), founded when slave-soldier troops overthrew the Ayyubid dynasty in Egypt, was administered as a self-perpetuating military oligarchy. Mamluks defended Egypt and Syria against Mongol invasions (Ain Jalut 1260) and finished off the Crusader states (Acre 1291). Cairo became the dominant Sunni Muslim cultural and religious center. The Ottomans conquered the Mamluks in 1516–1517, retaining mamluks as local administrators.

The Ming dynasty was founded by Zhu Yuanzhang (Hongwu Emperor, r. 1368–1398), a former peasant and Buddhist novice, after expelling the Mongol Yuan dynasty. Hongwu's reign emphasized Confucian restoration, agricultural smallholders, civil service examinations, and centralized imperial authority. His son the Yongle Emperor (r. 1402–1424) moved the capital to Beijing, built the Forbidden City, commissioned the *Yongle Encyclopedia*, and dispatched Admiral Zheng He on seven oceanic voyages between 1405 and 1433. After Zheng He's death and Yongle's, the Ming withdrew from oceanic projection and focused on continental defense, repairing and extending the Great Wall.

Gunpowder weaponry, originally Chinese, transformed warfare across all three empires and Western Europe in the fifteenth century, ending the era of the impregnable medieval city, the cavalry aristocracy, and (more slowly) the steppe nomadic confederation as a major political force.

The fall of Constantinople accelerated the Italian Renaissance through the migration of Greek scholars and manuscripts to Italy, with consequences for European art, philosophy, and the early scientific revolution.

## Key terms

*Beylik* — Anatolian Turkish principality, typical political unit after the collapse of the Sultanate of Rum.

*Ghazi* — frontier warrior fighting on behalf of Islamic expansion; foundational identity of the early Ottoman state.

*Timar* — non-hereditary fief held by Ottoman cavalry officers in exchange for military service.

*Timariot* — holder of a timar.

*Devshirme* — Ottoman levy of Christian boys from Balkan subjects, conscripted, converted to Islam, and trained for military and administrative service.

*Janissary* — elite Ottoman infantry corps, drawn from devshirme conscripts; the sultan's personal slave-soldiers.

*Millet* — Ottoman system of recognized religious communities (Greek Orthodox, Armenian, Jewish, etc.) governed internally by their own leaders and law.

*Mamluk* — slave-soldier of Turkic or Caucasian origin, central to Ayyubid and Mamluk Sultanate military and political organization.

*Bahri* and *Burji* dynasties — successive ruling factions within the Mamluk Sultanate (1250–1382 and 1382–1517 respectively).

*Hongwu Emperor* — Zhu Yuanzhang, founder of the Ming dynasty (r. 1368–1398).

*Yongle Emperor* — Zhu Di, Ming dynasty's third emperor (r. 1402–1424); commissioned Forbidden City, Yongle Encyclopedia, Zheng He voyages.

*Zheng He* — Muslim eunuch admiral who commanded the Ming Treasure Fleets between 1405 and 1433.

*Forbidden City* — imperial palace complex in Beijing, completed 1420; political, ceremonial, and residential center of the Ming and Qing.

*Tumu Crisis* — 1449 capture of the Zhengtong Emperor by Oirat Mongols, the only Chinese emperor in the Ming-Qing era to be taken prisoner.

*Bombard* — large-bore early cannon used in the Ottoman conquest of Constantinople and other late medieval sieges.

*Trace italienne* — angled-bastion fortification design developed in late-fifteenth-century Italy in response to gunpowder artillery.

*Renaissance* — European cultural and intellectual movement, 14th–16th centuries, characterized by recovery of classical antiquity and humanist learning.

*Humanism* — fourteenth-century-Italian intellectual movement emphasizing study of human nature and classical antiquity over scholastic theology.
