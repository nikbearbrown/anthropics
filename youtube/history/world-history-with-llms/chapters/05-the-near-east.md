## Chapter overview

By the end of this chapter you will know how the Near East — the region from the eastern Mediterranean through Mesopotamia and into the Iranian plateau — was reshaped by a sequence of empires across roughly 1,500 years, from Hammurabi's Babylon (eighteenth century BCE) through Cyrus the Great's Persian Empire (sixth century BCE). You will know the major Bronze Age powers (Babylon, the Hittites, New Kingdom Egypt) and the diplomatic system that bound them — including the world's first surviving peace treaty, signed at Kadesh in 1259 BCE. You will know the catastrophic Late Bronze Age Collapse (c. 1200 BCE) and the Iron Age that followed it. You will know the Neo-Assyrian Empire's "calculated frightfulness" — the largest empire the Near East had ever seen, sustained by ruthless military discipline. You will know its destruction in 612 BCE, the brief Neo-Babylonian succession under Nebuchadnezzar II, and Cyrus's bloodless capture of Babylon in 539 BCE. You will know the Persian Empire's administrative innovations — satrapies, royal roads, religious tolerance — and how they enabled an empire stretching from the Aegean to the Indus. And you will meet a small Iron Age people, the Hebrews, who produced no large empire but produced the Bible — a religious-literary tradition that outlived every empire in the chapter and continues to shape three world religions.

## Opening case — Kadesh, May 1274 BCE

In May 1274 BCE, near the city of Kadesh in what is now central Syria, two armies the size of small modern divisions met at the largest chariot battle the ancient world had yet seen. On one side stood the army of Ramesses II of Egypt, fresh from his Year 5 campaign into the northern Levant — perhaps twenty thousand troops and two thousand chariots. On the other side stood the army of Muwatalli II of the Hittite Empire — perhaps thirty thousand troops and three thousand chariots. The Hittites ambushed the Egyptian column. Ramesses, very nearly captured, rallied his bodyguard and held the line until reinforcements arrived. The two armies fought for most of the day. Neither side could claim a clear victory.

For the next fifteen years, Egypt and the Hittite Empire skirmished in the Levant. Then, in 1259 BCE, Ramesses II and the Hittite king Hattusili III signed a peace treaty. It was inscribed in cuneiform on silver tablets. Two copies survive — one in Hittite, recovered from the Hittite capital at Hattusas; one in Egyptian, carved on the walls of Ramesses's Karnak temple. They are the world's earliest peace treaty for which we have both sides' texts. They mutually recognize Hittite authority over Syria and Egyptian authority over the Levantine coast. They include extradition clauses, mutual defense provisions, and a renunciation of further claims. A copy of the treaty hangs in the United Nations headquarters in New York today, as a reminder that diplomacy is older than nations.

What the treaty actually expresses is the shape of the Bronze Age Near East. By 1259 BCE, the region was organized into a small number of large powers — Egypt, the Hittite Empire, the rump kingdom of Mitanni, Assyria, Babylonia, Elam — that conducted a continuous diplomacy through royal correspondence, dynastic intermarriage, gift exchange, and the occasional war. Surviving cuneiform letters from the period show kings addressing each other as "brother," dispatching ambassadors with elaborate gifts, complaining about insufficient gold shipments, and arranging marriages between their houses to seal alliances. Diplomats called the system the **Great Powers club**. Modern historians call it the Late Bronze Age **international system**.

Within fifty years, the system had collapsed. Within a hundred, every great power named in the treaty was either gone or transformed beyond recognition. The Hittite Empire vanished entirely; Hittite cities were burned and abandoned. Egypt staggered through invasions and political fragmentation. Mesopotamia entered a long decline. The cause is debated and probably plural — drought, migrations, internal revolts, the breakdown of trade. The result was the **Late Bronze Age Collapse** of around 1200 BCE, the same event that closed the Mycenaean palaces in Greece (Chapter 8) and disrupted the Levantine coast.

This chapter is about the empires that rose, the empires that fell, and the empires that learned from both. It runs from Hammurabi's eighteenth-century BCE Babylon through Cyrus the Great's sixth-century BCE Persia. It includes the Iron Age remaking of the Mesopotamian world after the Bronze Age Collapse — the brutal Neo-Assyrian Empire, the brief Neo-Babylonian succession, and the durable Persian Empire that grew larger than any Near Eastern empire before it. It also includes a small kingdom on the Mediterranean coast that never threatened anyone militarily but produced a religious tradition still alive in the twenty-first century: the Hebrews.

## Concept 1 — The Late Bronze Age system

After Hammurabi's death (c. 1750 BCE), Babylon's empire fragmented quickly. Within two centuries the city had been sacked by **Hittites** descending from Anatolia (1595 BCE) and then occupied by **Kassites** from the Zagros Mountains, who would rule southern Mesopotamia for nearly five hundred years.

The Hittites who sacked Babylon were Indo-European speakers — the first major Indo-European-speaking power in the Near East. Emerging in central Anatolia (modern Turkey) by 1600 BCE, with their capital at **Hattusas**, they expanded through the early sixteenth century BCE under the king Mursili I (the conqueror of Babylon). The empire weakened temporarily but revived dramatically in the fourteenth century BCE under **Suppiluliuma I**, who built the Hittite Empire into the dominant power of Anatolia and Syria. By the thirteenth century BCE the Hittites were the principal rival of New Kingdom Egypt for control of the Levant.

Below the great powers were a constellation of smaller kingdoms, vassal states, and trading entrepôts. The kingdom of **Mitanni** in upper Mesopotamia was a regional power for several centuries before being squeezed by Egypt and the Hittites. The Levantine coast was a string of city-states (Ugarit, Byblos, Tyre, Sidon) that traded with everyone and sometimes paid tribute to whoever was strongest at the moment. **Cyprus** was the source of much of the system's copper. **Crete** and **Mycenaean Greece** were on the system's western edge.

The system worked through diplomacy. Royal correspondence was conducted in **Akkadian cuneiform**, even between kings who spoke other languages — Akkadian was the international language. The most famous archive of this correspondence is the **Amarna letters**, a cache of about 380 clay tablets discovered in Akhenaten's short-lived capital in middle Egypt. The letters include exchanges between the Egyptian pharaohs Amenhotep III, Amenhotep IV (Akhenaten), and Tutankhamun on one side, and the kings of Mitanni, Babylonia, Assyria, the Hittites, and various Levantine vassals on the other. They reveal a system of ritualized gift exchange, dynastic intermarriage proposals, complaints about delayed gold shipments, requests for medical specialists, and petty quarrels over the proper protocols for ambassadors.

The system collapsed around 1200 BCE. The exact mix of causes is still being argued: a multi-decade drought identified in pollen and lake-sediment cores; a wave of migrations called the **Sea Peoples** (Egyptian inscriptions name several groups, including the *Peleset* who would become the biblical Philistines); peasant revolts; the disruption of long-distance trade by piracy and famine; cascading state failures in which the collapse of one power destabilized its neighbors. Within a few decades, Mycenaean Greece had fallen, Hittite Anatolia was burning, Levantine cities were destroyed, and Egypt — though it survived — entered a long decline that ended pharaonic central control. By 1100 BCE, the Bronze Age system was gone. The Iron Age began.

## Concept 2 — New Kingdom Egypt

While the Hittites were rising in the north, Egypt was experiencing the most dramatic period of its three-thousand-year pharaonic history. The **New Kingdom** (c. 1570–1069 BCE) is the era of the empire-building pharaohs whose names everyone knows.

It began with the expulsion of the **Hyksos** — Semitic-speaking peoples from the Levant who had ruled the Nile delta during the Second Intermediate Period. Around 1550 BCE, the Theban pharaoh **Ahmose I** drove the Hyksos out, reunified Egypt, and reasserted Egyptian control of Nubia (modern Sudan) to the south. His successors built the empire. **Thutmose I** marched Egyptian armies to the Euphrates. **Thutmose III**, the most successful military pharaoh in Egyptian history, fought seventeen campaigns in the Levant over twenty years and brought the region as far north as Syria under Egyptian control.

Between Thutmose I and Thutmose III came the unusual reign of **Hatshepsut** (c. 1479–1458 BCE) — Thutmose II's principal wife and Thutmose III's stepmother. When Thutmose II died leaving an infant heir, Hatshepsut declared herself co-regent and then proclaimed herself pharaoh. Egyptian iconography had no convention for a female pharaoh, so her statues depict her in male regalia (false beard, masculine torso) while her inscriptions occasionally describe her with feminine grammatical endings ("His Majesty, Herself"). Her twenty-one-year reign was prosperous. She conducted military campaigns, built her three-tiered mortuary temple at Deir el-Bahari (cut into the Theban cliffs and still one of the most striking architectural sites in Egypt), and dispatched an expedition to the land of **Punt** — probably modern Somalia or eastern Sudan — that returned with myrrh, gold, ivory, and exotic animals. Thutmose III, who succeeded her, had her name removed from many monuments after her death — an attempt to write her out of dynastic memory that mostly failed.

The most ideologically dramatic New Kingdom pharaoh was **Akhenaten** (Amenhotep IV, reigned c. 1353–1336 BCE). He attempted a religious revolution: rejecting the traditional Egyptian pantheon, he proclaimed the worship of a single god, the **Aten** (the visible sun-disk), and promoted himself as the sole intermediary between Aten and humanity. He moved the capital from Thebes to a new site in middle Egypt called Akhetaten (modern Tell el-Amarna), which he built from scratch in a few years. The art of the period broke from rigid pharaonic conventions and depicted Akhenaten and his family — including his queen **Nefertiti** — in unusually intimate, almost informal poses. The reform did not survive him. His son **Tutankhamun** (originally Tutankhaten) restored the old religion within a few years, moved the capital back to Thebes, and died young — leaving behind the small, intact tomb that Howard Carter discovered in 1922 and that turned the boy-king into the most famous Egyptian pharaoh in modern memory.

The New Kingdom climaxed with **Ramesses II** (reigned c. 1279–1213 BCE) — Ramesses the Great — whose sixty-six-year reign saw him build more monumental architecture than any other pharaoh, father somewhere between fifty and a hundred children, and fight the Battle of Kadesh that opened this chapter. After Ramesses II, the New Kingdom slowly declined. Late-thirteenth-century invasions by the Sea Peoples damaged Egypt. The **Third Intermediate Period** (c. 1069–525 BCE) saw the country fragment, fall briefly under Libyan and then Nubian rule, and ultimately, in 525 BCE, become a province of the Persian Empire.

## Concept 3 — The Neo-Assyrian Empire: calculated frightfulness

The Iron Age (c. 1100 BCE onward) saw the rise of the most ruthless empire the Near East had yet produced. The **Neo-Assyrian Empire** emerged from northern Mesopotamia around 900 BCE under **Ashurnasirpal II** and reached its full extent in the mid-seventh century BCE under **Ashurbanipal**.

At its height, the Neo-Assyrian Empire stretched from the Persian Gulf to Egypt, from the Mediterranean to the Iranian plateau. **Tiglath-Pileser III** (reigned 745–727 BCE) reorganized the empire into provinces governed by appointed officials, professionalized the army into a year-round fighting force, and pioneered the policy of **deportation** — relocating entire conquered populations to other parts of the empire to break their political coherence. It was Tiglath-Pileser's successor **Sargon II** who, in 722 BCE, conquered the northern Hebrew kingdom of Israel and deported much of its population — the disappearance of the so-called **ten lost tribes**. **Sennacherib** (reigned 704–681 BCE) destroyed Babylon in 689 BCE for repeated rebellion. **Esarhaddon** (reigned 681–669 BCE) invaded and conquered Egypt in 671 BCE — making the Neo-Assyrian Empire the first to control both Mesopotamia and the Nile. **Ashurbanipal** (reigned 669–631 BCE) was the empire's last great king and a literate one: his library at Nineveh, recovered by nineteenth-century British excavators, included some 30,000 cuneiform tablets — the most complete ancient Mesopotamian library ever found, including the most complete surviving copy of the *Epic of Gilgamesh*.

The Assyrian war machine was the empire's defining institution. Year-round professional infantry organized into specialist units: spear fighters, archers, slingers, charioteers, cavalry. The composite bow — wood, horn, and sinew laminated together — could shoot accurately at four hundred feet, three or four times the range of a self-bow. Two-wheeled horse-drawn chariots, deployed in squadrons of fifty, could break almost any infantry line. Siege engines — battering rams, mobile towers, sapping operations — let the Assyrians take fortified cities that earlier armies would have abandoned.

The Assyrians' most distinctive policy was what historians call **calculated frightfulness**. Captured rebel leaders were impaled on stakes, flayed alive, blinded, or decapitated, often with the punishments depicted in stone reliefs on palace walls so foreign visitors could see what awaited resistance. Whole populations were deported to other regions and replaced with peoples deported from elsewhere — making rebellion practically impossible because no community had local roots, infrastructure, or networks. The biblical accounts of the Assyrian conquests — the destruction of Israel in 722 BCE, the near-destruction of Judah in 701 BCE — are among the most vivid ancient testimonies to this brutality. The Hebrew prophet Nahum's celebration of Nineveh's eventual fall in 612 BCE has the spluttering joy of a victim watching his torturer finally collapse.

The fall came faster than the rise. Internal succession crises weakened the late seventh-century empire. In 626 BCE the **Chaldean** general **Nabopolassar** rebelled in Babylon and made it independent. In 614 BCE the Median king **Cyaxares** sacked the old Assyrian capital of **Assur**. Nabopolassar and Cyaxares allied. In 612 BCE their combined armies sacked **Nineveh**, the imperial capital. The Assyrian remnant fled west and was destroyed at Carchemish by **Nebuchadnezzar II** of Babylon in 605 BCE. Within a generation, the empire that had terrorized the Near East was gone — its cities depopulated, its language dying out, its art surviving mostly in the museums of the cultures it had crushed.

The successor was the **Neo-Babylonian Empire** under Nebuchadnezzar II (reigned 605–562 BCE), the most famous Babylonian ruler in popular memory. Nebuchadnezzar conquered the Hebrew kingdom of Judah, sacked Jerusalem in 586 BCE, destroyed Solomon's Temple, and deported the Judean elite to Babylon — the **Babylonian Exile** that produced much of the Hebrew Bible's later writing. He rebuilt Babylon as the greatest city of its age, with the famous **Hanging Gardens** (one of the Seven Wonders of the ancient world, though the historicity of the gardens themselves is debated) and the magnificent **Ishtar Gate** with its blue glazed-brick reliefs of bulls and dragons. The Neo-Babylonian Empire was rich, cultured, and short-lived — within forty years of Nebuchadnezzar's death, it would fall to a new power rising in the east.

## Concept 4 — Persia: the empire that lasted

In 559 BCE, a Persian noble named **Cyrus** inherited the small kingdom of Anshan in southwestern Iran. By 530 BCE, when he died in battle on the empire's eastern frontier, he had conquered most of the Near East. His successors held what he had built and added more. The **Achaemenid Persian Empire** lasted until Alexander the Great destroyed it in 330 BCE — over two centuries of mostly stable rule across an area larger than any previous empire.

Cyrus's first major conquest was his own overlord. The Persians had been vassals of the **Median Empire**, an Iranian power that had partnered with the Babylonians in destroying Assyria. In 550 BCE, when Cyrus revolted, the Median army went over to him. He absorbed Media without significant fighting. He then turned west. In 547 BCE he defeated the wealthy Anatolian kingdom of **Lydia** under King Croesus (whose name became proverbial for fabulous wealth). In 539 BCE he defeated the Neo-Babylonian army at Opis and entered Babylon to popular acclaim — the city's elite, alienated by the eccentric last Babylonian king Nabonidus, welcomed Cyrus as a liberator.

Cyrus's distinctive contribution was his approach to ruling diverse peoples. The **Cyrus Cylinder** — a clay artifact inscribed with cuneiform and discovered in 1879 — records Cyrus's account of his conquest of Babylon. It announces religious tolerance: the gods of Babylon are honored; the temples are not destroyed; deported peoples are allowed to return home. Among the deported peoples sent home were the Jews, who had been in Babylonian exile since Nebuchadnezzar's destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BCE. Persian policy permitted them to return to Judah and rebuild the Temple — a fact memorialized in Hebrew tradition (the books of Ezra and Nehemiah celebrate Cyrus by name) and in the unusual designation of Cyrus, in the book of Isaiah, as the *messiah* — the anointed one — chosen by Yahweh to liberate his people. The Cyrus Cylinder has been read by some moderns as the world's first declaration of human rights, which is an overreach (Cyrus was a conqueror restoring local cults to keep the conquered loyal, not a Jeffersonian rights theorist), but the contrast with Assyrian frightfulness is real and consequential.

Cyrus died on the empire's eastern frontier in 530 BCE. His son **Cambyses II** added Egypt to the empire in 525 BCE. When Cambyses died in 522 BCE on his way back from Egypt to put down a rebellion, the Persian throne went, after a year of confused succession struggles, to **Darius I** (reigned 522–486 BCE) — possibly a distant relative of Cyrus, possibly a usurper who invented his royal genealogy. Darius is the empire's most consequential administrator.

### Darius's institutional architecture

Darius reorganized the empire into about twenty large provinces called **satrapies**, each governed by a **satrap** — usually a Persian or Median noble — appointed by and answerable to the king. Satraps had their own courts and substantial regional autonomy. They collected taxes, raised troops, and administered justice within their satrapies. They reported to the king and, when appropriate, were audited by traveling royal officials called the **Eyes and Ears of the King**, who arrived unannounced, gathered intelligence, and reported back. A satrap whose audit went badly could be removed or executed. The system gave the empire's component regions enough local autonomy to function and enough royal oversight to keep them loyal.

Communication was carried by the **Royal Road**, a network of post stations linking the imperial capitals — **Susa**, **Persepolis**, **Pasargadae**, **Ecbatana** — to provincial centers. Stationed couriers changed horses at each stop; a message could travel from Susa to Sardis in western Anatolia (about 1,700 miles) in under a week. The system was fast enough that Herodotus, writing in the fifth century BCE, marveled at it: *"There is nothing that travels faster, and yet is mortal, than these couriers."* The architects of the United States Postal Service later carved a paraphrase of this passage into the front of the Eighth Avenue post office in Manhattan: *Neither snow nor rain nor heat nor gloom of night stays these couriers from the swift completion of their appointed rounds.*

Darius standardized weights and measures across the empire and minted **gold darics** and **silver sigloi** — coins that became the international currency of the eastern Mediterranean. He commissioned a canal between the Red Sea and the Nile (whether it was actually completed is debated). He built the ceremonial palace at **Persepolis**, whose carved staircases depict tribute-bearers from every satrapy of the empire — a visual catalog of the empire's diversity. He commissioned the **Behistun Inscription**, a trilingual relief carved into a cliff face along the road from Babylon to Ecbatana, in which Darius narrates his rise to power in Old Persian, Elamite, and Akkadian; the inscription was the Rosetta Stone for cuneiform, allowing nineteenth-century scholars to decipher the script.

Persian religion was distinctive. The religion of the Achaemenid royal house was **Zoroastrianism** — a faith founded (probably) by the prophet **Zarathustra** (Greek: Zoroaster) somewhere in eastern Iran, perhaps in the second millennium BCE. Zoroastrianism is **dualist**: the universe is the battleground between the good god **Ahura Mazda** and the evil spirit **Angra Mainyu**, and humans must choose sides through right thought, right speech, and right action. The faith is monotheistic-leaning (Ahura Mazda is the primary deity) but acknowledges other divine beings. Many ideas familiar from Judaism, Christianity, and Islam — angels and demons, the cosmic battle of good and evil, the final judgment, resurrection of the dead — entered those traditions through Zoroastrian influence during the Persian and Hellenistic periods. The Persian kings, however, did not impose Zoroastrianism on their subjects. Religious tolerance was the empire's policy.

### Persia's collision with Greece

The Persian Empire's western expansion brought it into conflict with the Greek city-states. Darius's invasion of Greece was repulsed at the Battle of Marathon in 490 BCE; his son Xerxes's much larger invasion was repulsed at the Battles of Salamis (480 BCE) and Plataea (479 BCE). These wars are covered in Chapter 8. The Persian Empire continued to dominate the Near East for another 150 years before falling to Alexander.

## Concept 5 — The Hebrews and the development of monotheism

In a small region between Egypt and Mesopotamia — the Levant, specifically the highlands of modern Israel and the West Bank — a Semitic-speaking people called the **Hebrews** developed a religious tradition that, alone among the religions of the ancient Near East, has continuous adherents today. The Hebrews never built a great empire. They produced a great book.

### History

The early history is contested between the biblical narrative and the archaeological record. The **Hebrew Bible** (the *Tanakh* in Jewish tradition; the Old Testament in Christian tradition) tells the story of the patriarch **Abraham** migrating from Ur in Mesopotamia to Canaan around 2150 BCE; of his descendants going down to Egypt during a famine; of the Hebrews' enslavement under an unnamed pharaoh; of their **Exodus** under **Moses** and acceptance of the covenant with **Yahweh** at Sinai; of the conquest of Canaan under **Joshua**; of the period of the **Judges**; of the establishment of a united monarchy under **Saul**, **David**, and **Solomon** in the tenth century BCE; of the kingdom's split into **Israel** in the north and **Judah** in the south; of the destruction of Israel by Assyria in 722 BCE and Judah by Babylonia in 586 BCE; and of the **Babylonian Exile** and **Return** under Cyrus.

The archaeological record supports some elements of this narrative and not others. There is no archaeological evidence for Abraham, the Egyptian sojourn, or the Exodus on the scale the Bible describes; what may have happened was a much smaller migration of a Semitic group from Egypt that joined an already-present Canaanite population. The conquest of Canaan as the Bible describes it does not match what archaeologists find at the major sites — Jericho was not destroyed at the time the Bible says, and the settlement of the Israelite highlands appears to have been a gradual process rather than a sudden invasion. The united monarchy of David and Solomon is debated: some scholars find evidence for a small but real tenth-century BCE kingdom centered at Jerusalem, others see the biblical accounts as late embellishments. The Assyrian and Babylonian conquests, by contrast, are well-attested in non-biblical sources — Assyrian and Babylonian chronicles describe the same events from the conqueror's perspective.

What is clear is that, by the eighth century BCE, the Hebrews had developed a distinctive religion. Their god, **Yahweh**, demanded exclusive worship — not the Mesopotamian or Egyptian pattern of devotion to a city's patron god while acknowledging the existence of others, but a more radical position that the other gods of the surrounding peoples were either powerless or non-existent. The prophets of the eighth and seventh centuries BCE — **Amos**, **Hosea**, **Isaiah**, **Jeremiah**, **Ezekiel** — denounced not only Hebrew worship of Canaanite gods but also social injustice, ritual emptiness, and political corruption. The prophetic tradition's combination of strict monotheism with a social-justice critique of unjust kingship became one of the religion's distinctive features.

### The Babylonian Exile and the formation of the Bible

The exile under Nebuchadnezzar II was the formative crisis. The Judean elite — priests, scribes, court officials — were deported to Babylon between 597 and 586 BCE. Solomon's Temple was destroyed. The kingdom was annihilated. For about fifty years, the exiled community survived as a religious minority in a foreign empire. Many of them adapted: they took Babylonian names, learned Aramaic, prospered in trade and finance. But a remnant kept the religious tradition alive, and during the exile much of what we now call the Hebrew Bible was edited, compiled, and given its enduring form. The **Torah** (the first five books) was probably substantially compiled during and after the exile, drawing on older traditions. The historical books (Joshua, Judges, Samuel, Kings) were edited into a continuous narrative that read the kingdom's destruction as divine punishment for its kings' infidelity to Yahweh. The prophetic books were collected and copied. The exile transformed Hebrew religion from the cult of a small kingdom into a portable book-based tradition that could survive the loss of the kingdom — a structural innovation that would matter when the kingdom was lost again, definitively, in 70 CE under Roman rule.

When Cyrus permitted the exiles to return in 539 BCE, only a fraction did. Those who returned rebuilt a smaller version of the Temple in Jerusalem and re-established Yahweh worship in the homeland. Those who stayed in Babylon (and elsewhere in the Persian Empire and beyond) became the **diaspora** — a Jewish community scattered across multiple kingdoms, held together by Torah, Sabbath, dietary law, circumcision, and the synagogue. The diaspora pattern would prove durable: Jewish communities outside the Land of Israel have existed continuously for the 2,500 years since.

### Why Hebrew religion mattered

Several features distinguished Hebrew religion from its contemporaries.

**Strict monotheism.** No other surrounding religion of the period denied the existence of other gods so categorically. This was both a religious claim and a political one: Yahweh was the king's only legitimate sovereign, which limited the king's claim to divine status (David and Solomon were anointed kings, not gods).

**An ethical god.** Yahweh demanded not just ritual but justice — care for the poor, the widow, the orphan, the foreigner. The prophets wielded this demand as a critique of unjust kings and corrupt elites. The combination of religion and social ethics is now familiar but was unusual in the ancient Near East.

**Scripture.** Other ancient religions had sacred texts; few invested as much in producing, copying, and interpreting them as the Hebrews did. The post-exile development of a textual tradition that could be carried, taught, and reinterpreted across generations made the religion portable.

**A covenant.** Yahweh's relationship with the Hebrews was framed as a contract — a *covenant* — with mutual obligations. This conceptual frame would shape later political theology in the Christian and Islamic traditions.

These features made Hebrew religion the seed of three of the world's largest religious traditions. **Christianity** emerged from first-century CE Judaism in Roman Palestine and spread through the Roman world over the next three centuries (Chapters 9 and 13). **Islam** emerged in seventh-century CE Arabia, drawing on Jewish and Christian traditions, and spread across the Near East and beyond in the next century (Chapter 14). All three — Judaism, Christianity, Islam — claim Abraham as a common ancestor and Yahweh / God / Allah as the same deity.

The thing to watch for, going forward, is the temptation to treat the empires of this chapter as natural or inevitable. They were institutional inventions that solved specific problems and created new ones. The next chapter follows the rise of comparable civilizations in East Asia, where similar problems were solved through different inventions.

## Key terms

- **Akhenaten.** New Kingdom pharaoh (reigned c. 1353–1336 BCE) who attempted to replace Egyptian polytheism with monotheistic worship of the sun-disk Aten. His reforms did not survive his death.
- **Amarna letters.** A cache of about 380 clay tablets recovered from Akhenaten's capital, recording diplomatic correspondence between Egypt and the other Late Bronze Age powers.
- **Babylonian Exile.** The deportation of the Judean elite to Babylon by Nebuchadnezzar II between 597 and 586 BCE, ended by Cyrus the Great's permission to return in 539 BCE.
- **Calculated frightfulness.** The Neo-Assyrian policy of public torture, deportation, and brutal reprisal designed to deter rebellion.
- **Cyrus the Great.** Founder of the Achaemenid Persian Empire (reigned 559–530 BCE). Famous for his policy of religious tolerance.
- **Cyrus Cylinder.** A clay artifact inscribed with Cyrus's account of his conquest of Babylon and his policy of religious tolerance. Discovered in 1879.
- **Darius I.** Persian king (reigned 522–486 BCE) who reorganized the empire into satrapies and built the institutional architecture that sustained Persian rule for two centuries.
- **Hebrew Bible.** The collection of religious-historical texts that became the foundational scripture of Judaism, edited substantially during and after the Babylonian Exile.
- **Hittites.** Indo-European-speaking people who built an empire centered in Anatolia (c. 1600–1200 BCE) and were the principal rival of New Kingdom Egypt.
- **Iron Age.** The period beginning c. 1200 BCE during which iron replaced bronze as the dominant metal for tools and weapons.
- **Late Bronze Age Collapse.** The breakdown of the Bronze Age international system around 1200 BCE, in which the Hittite Empire vanished, Egypt declined, and Levantine cities were destroyed.
- **Monotheism.** Belief in a single god. Hebrew religion developed an unusually strict version in which other peoples' gods were denied existence.
- **Neo-Assyrian Empire.** The Iron Age Mesopotamian empire (c. 911–612 BCE) that conquered most of the Near East, including Egypt.
- **Neo-Babylonian Empire.** The Chaldean dynasty that succeeded the Neo-Assyrians (626–539 BCE), most famous for Nebuchadnezzar II.
- **Persian Empire.** The Achaemenid Empire (c. 550–330 BCE), the largest the Near East had yet seen, founded by Cyrus and administered by Darius's satrapy system.
- **Pharaoh of the Exodus.** The (unnamed) Egyptian ruler whom the Hebrew Bible identifies as the antagonist of the Exodus story. Modern scholars have proposed several candidates; the question is unsettled.
- **Royal Road.** The Persian Empire's network of post stations and roads connecting the imperial capitals to the satrapies.
- **Satrapy.** A province of the Persian Empire, governed by an appointed satrap.
- **Sea Peoples.** A group of migrating peoples whose attacks contributed to the Late Bronze Age Collapse.
- **Vassal state.** A nominally independent kingdom required to provide tribute and military support to a dominant empire.
- **Yahweh.** The god of the Hebrews, who in the developed Hebrew religion is the only true god.
- **Zoroastrianism.** The dualist religion of pre-Islamic Iran, founded by the prophet Zarathustra. Influenced later Jewish, Christian, and Islamic theology.
