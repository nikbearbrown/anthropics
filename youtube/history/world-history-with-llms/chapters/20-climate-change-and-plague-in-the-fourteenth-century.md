# Chapter 20 — Climate Change and Plague in the Fourteenth Century

**Suggested titles**

- Climate Change and Plague in the Fourteenth Century
- The Calamitous Fourteenth Century: Famine, Plague, and the End of an Order
- Yersinia pestis: How a Bacterium Reorganized Eurasia

**TL;DR.** Between 1315 and 1400 a sequence of overlapping shocks — a sustained climatic cooling we now call the Little Ice Age, a Great Famine across northern Europe, two protracted wars in Eurasia, and a bubonic plague pandemic that killed roughly a third of the population from China to Iceland — disrupted nearly every state and church in the Old World north of the equator. The Mongol Empire fragmented; the Yuan dynasty fell to the Ming; the Mamluk Sultanate began its long decline; the European serf-feudal order broke; the Latin Christian Church entered a legitimacy crisis it would not fully recover from. The combined chapter is a study in how connected systems amplify shocks — and how the institutions that emerge from such a century are often unrecognizable from those that entered it.

---

## Overview

In October 1347, twelve Genoese galleys put in at the harbor of Messina, on the eastern coast of Sicily. They had been blown off course while running west from Caffa, a Genoese trading post on the Crimean coast of the Black Sea. The Sicilian harbor authorities boarded the ships to inspect cargo. What they found, the Franciscan friar Michele da Piazza wrote a few years later, was that the sailors "carried such a disease in their bodies that if anyone so much as spoke to them, he was infected with the deadly illness and could not avoid death."

The galleys were ordered out of port. They went to Genoa. Genoa expelled them. They went to Marseille. Marseille became one of the worst-hit cities in southern France within months. By the spring of 1348, the disease was in Florence, Pisa, Avignon, and Paris. By 1349 it was in London and Bergen and Cairo. By 1351, when the first wave finally burned itself out, perhaps thirty-five percent of the European population was dead. In Cairo, perhaps one in three. In some Tuscan villages, perhaps half. In Iceland, when the disease finally arrived in 1402, perhaps sixty percent.

The bacterium responsible — *Yersinia pestis* — has been on earth, we now know from genetic analysis, for at least 5,500 years. It had caused at least one earlier pandemic, the so-called Plague of Justinian in the sixth century. But the fourteenth-century pandemic, the Black Death, was the moment when this particular bacterium acquired a permanent place in human collective memory. We have, in the centuries since, used "plague" as a metaphor for almost any catastrophe.

The chapter is about that catastrophe, and about everything around it. Because the Black Death did not arrive in a stable world. It arrived in a world that had already been shaken — by climate change, by famine, by political collapse, by religious crisis, by war. The plague hit weakened populations on top of a slow-motion crisis that had been building for half a century. What we owe ourselves is not the Hollywood image of medieval people falling dead in the street, but the harder, more interesting history of why the systems that should have absorbed the shock did not, and what came next.

A note on sources before we start. The fourteenth century is exceptionally well-documented for parts of it (Italy, England, Egypt) and very thinly documented for others (most of sub-Saharan Africa, much of central Asia, most of the Americas). Recent decades have added a new evidential layer: paleoclimatology (tree rings, ice cores, varves), ancient-DNA recovery from medieval mass graves, and isotopic analysis of skeletal remains. These tools have repeatedly overturned earlier consensus — including, in the last fifteen years, much of the standard story of where the Black Death came from. Where the science has moved, the chapter follows it. Where it remains unsettled, the chapter says so.

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- Trace the chain of fourteenth-century crises across China, the Middle East, North Africa, and Europe, and identify the institutional weaknesses that amplified them.

- Compare Christian European, Muslim, and Chinese responses to the plague.

## Opening case — Caffa, 1346

Caffa, on the southeastern coast of the Crimean peninsula, was in 1346 a Genoese trading colony operating under license from the local Mongol khanate (the Golden Horde). It was a major node in the trade between the Mediterranean and the Silk Roads — slaves, grain, furs, salt, and Chinese silks all passed through. About a thousand Genoese lived inside the city walls. Many thousands of Mongol soldiers stood outside.

The siege was the result of a dispute. According to the Italian notary Gabriele de Mussis, who wrote his account in Piacenza a few years later, Italian merchants in Tana (a sister colony at the mouth of the Don) had brawled with local Mongols. The Mongol khan Jani Beg responded by expelling the Italians and besieging Caffa, where many had taken refuge. The siege went on through the winter and into 1346.

Then, de Mussis writes, the Mongol army began to fall ill. "An infinite number of Tartars died, daily and hourly, as if arrows were raining down from heaven to strike and crush the Tartars' arrogance." The disease worked through the besieging army. Jani Beg, before withdrawing, ordered the corpses of plague-dead soldiers loaded onto catapults and flung over the walls. The Genoese — de Mussis says — "ordered the corpses to be thrown into the sea, but soon the air became infected and the water poisoned, and such a stench arose that scarcely one in a thousand was able to flee the remains of the Tartar army."

Some of those who fled boarded ships home. Caffa is the door through which the Black Death entered the Mediterranean.

A few warnings about this opening. De Mussis was not an eyewitness; he wrote in Piacenza, hundreds of miles away. The story of the catapulted corpses may be true, may be exaggerated, may be invented entirely. The infection of Caffa probably had less to do with the catapults — flea-borne plague does not easily transmit via cadavers — and more to do with rats and fleas crossing the lines as they had been crossing the steppe for years. What is certain is that ships left Caffa in late 1346 and arrived in Mediterranean ports in 1347, and that the disease arrived with them.

Pull back. To understand why the disease was already on the steppe — and why it spread so far and so fast once it reached the trading networks — we have to start earlier. With the climate.

## Concept 1 — Reading the past climate, and what it says

How do we know the climate of seven hundred years ago? The simple answer is: we read it off proxies.

A *proxy* is something that responded to past climate and that has survived to be measured. The classic proxy is tree rings. A tree in a temperate forest grows a wider ring in a wet year and a narrower ring in a dry year. A core taken from a single live tree gives you about a century of climate history; matching cores from older living trees and ancient timbers — a method called crossdating, pioneered by the astronomer A. E. Douglass at Arizona in the 1920s — can extend the record back thousands of years. Bristlecone pine chronologies in the American West now run for over eight thousand years.

A second proxy is ice. Snow that falls and compacts into glacial ice traps tiny bubbles of atmospheric gas. The ratio of oxygen-18 to oxygen-16 in the water molecules themselves shifts with temperature; the gas in the bubbles records the chemistry of the atmosphere in the year the snow fell. Cores from Greenland and Antarctica give us continuous records of temperature, atmospheric carbon dioxide, and volcanic activity going back hundreds of thousands of years.

Volcanic activity matters because it forces climate. A large eruption — Tambora in 1815, Pinatubo in 1991 — injects sulfate aerosols into the stratosphere. Sulfate reflects sunlight back into space. The earth cools. The cooling shows up in tree rings (a narrow ring), in ice cores (a sulfate spike), in agricultural records (a bad harvest), and in chronicles (the "year without a summer" that followed Tambora).

Combine the proxies. What they tell us about the late thirteenth and fourteenth centuries is a coherent story.

From roughly 950 to 1250 CE, much of the Northern Hemisphere had experienced what historical climatologists call the Medieval Warm Period — annual mean temperatures perhaps 0.5°C above the long-term average. This was the climate in which Vikings settled Greenland and Iceland, in which the European population doubled, in which Champa rice doubled the Chinese harvest. It was, on net, a good time to be a farmer.

Beginning around 1250 — and accelerating through several large tropical volcanic eruptions identified in ice cores in the late 1200s — global mean temperatures began to drop. By 1310 they were perhaps 0.6°C below the medieval mean. The shift, gradual in absolute terms, was catastrophic in agricultural terms. The growing season in northern Europe shortened by two to three weeks. Wet summers — particularly the years 1314, 1315, and 1316 — could not ripen grain. Mountain glaciers in the Alps began to advance, and the advance continued, with fluctuations, into the seventeenth century. We now call this period the Little Ice Age, and although the dating of its start and end is debated, almost no one disputes that the early fourteenth century was its first climatic shock.

**Worked example: the harvest of 1315.** Consider what a wet, cold summer does to a wheat field in fourteenth-century England. Wheat needs about 1,400 to 1,800 growing-degree-days above 5°C to ripen. In a normal year, southern England gets about 1,600. In 1315, persistent rain and overcast cooled the season and left perhaps 1,300 — enough to grow plants but not enough to fully ripen the heads of grain. The chronicler Johannes de Trokelowe at St. Albans wrote that the grain "could hardly be gathered and used to bake bread . . . unless it was first put in vessels to dry. Bread did not have its usual nourishing power and strength because the grain was not nourished by the warmth of summer sunshine. Hence those who ate it, even in large quantities, were hungry again after a little while."

The price of a quarter of wheat in England rose from five shillings in 1313 to twenty shillings in 1315 — a fourfold increase, in a society where the typical agricultural laborer earned around two pence a day. A worker would have to spend two or three days' labor to buy bread for one day. Animal feed prices spiked even worse. Pigs starved. Plow oxen, too valuable to slaughter normally, were eaten. Salt — necessary to preserve any remaining meat — went from a normal price to thirty-five shillings a quarter, "which in former times was quite unheard of." Trokelowe says, with the characteristic medieval directness, "men and women in many places secretly ate their own children." The claim was probably true in some cases; it was certainly believed.

## Concept 2 — The Great Famine: a slow disaster, 1315–1322

The Great Famine of 1315–1317 was the first systemic shock of the fourteenth century. Across an area of roughly 400,000 square miles in northern Europe — England, the Low Countries, France north of the Loire, the German principalities, much of Scandinavia — harvests failed for three consecutive years. Population estimates are imprecise but roughly five to ten percent of the affected region died, mostly of malnutrition and the secondary diseases that follow it. Cattle and sheep died of murrain (epizootic disease) at unprecedented rates; one English manor lost forty percent of its sheep in a single year.

The economic consequences extended past the famine itself. Grain markets, which had been integrating across Europe over the previous century, fragmented as cities banned exports. Long-distance grain trade collapsed. Land that had been brought under cultivation during the medieval warm period — marginal hillside fields, recently cleared forests — proved unviable in the new climate and was abandoned. Some of this abandoned land was the foundation of new pastoral economies (the English wool industry, which would grow over the next century, was partly built on Famine-era pasture conversion). Some was simply lost.

Politically, the Famine eroded the legitimacy of European governments and the Church. Edward II of England, whose reign overlapped much of the famine, lost his throne to his wife Isabella and her ally Roger Mortimer in 1327 and was murdered shortly thereafter; the chroniclers tied his deposition partly to his perceived inability to manage the crisis. In France, repeated famines and the Mortmain edict (taxing donations to the Church) corroded the relationship between Crown and clergy. Across northern Europe, witch-trials began to rise — climatologists at Harvard a few years ago documented a strong correlation between cold periods and witch-trial intensity over the next four centuries, which is precisely what you would expect if a community under environmental stress turns inward and looks for blame.

The Great Famine was not, however, the only shock that tied up the early fourteenth century. The Mongol world was already coming apart. The Yuan dynasty was already losing China. The Mamluks were peaking in wealth but had no more credible succession arrangement than the Mongols had. We need to look east before we follow the plague west.

## Concept 3 — The Yuan dynasty cracks: from Kublai's grandson to the Red Turbans

We left the Yuan dynasty at the end of Chapter 18 with paper money inflation, failed Vietnamese and Japanese expeditions, and a four-tier ethnic class system that put ninety percent of the population (the Han Chinese) at the bottom. After Kublai's death in 1294, the Yuan went through eight emperors in forty years. Most reigned briefly. Several were assassinated. Two were children when they came to the throne.

In the 1320s and 1330s, several disasters compounded. The Yellow River — which had been moving north over the previous century — flooded catastrophically, repeatedly, killing perhaps hundreds of thousands and displacing millions. The Yuan response, ordering a forced labor levy of over a hundred thousand peasants to redirect the river, sparked the rebellion that would ultimately end the dynasty.

Plague reached northeastern China in the 1330s. (The precise dating is debated; some scholars now suggest a thirteenth-century origin in Mongol military zones, with the fourteenth-century European epidemic as a downstream consequence; we will come back to this in a moment.) Imperial records record an "unknown sickness" in 1331 in Hebei province that killed nine-tenths of the local population, although later records and modern epidemiological reconstruction suggest the figure is exaggerated and the cause may not have been bubonic plague specifically.

By 1351 the rebellions had coalesced. The most important was the Red Turban movement, led by a Buddhist millenarian named Han Shantong who claimed the imminent return of the Maitreya Buddha. The movement attracted peasants, displaced laborers, and former soldiers. After Han Shantong's execution, leadership passed to a former Buddhist novice named Zhu Yuanzhang. Zhu, born to a peasant family that had lost most of its members to plague and famine in 1344, had been a beggar, a soldier, and finally a rebel commander. By 1368 his armies had taken Beijing. He proclaimed the Ming dynasty and adopted the reign name Hongwu ("Vastly Martial").

The Yuan court fled north to the Mongolian steppe. The dynasty did not formally end — the so-called Northern Yuan persisted for another century — but the Mongol grip on China was finished.

The trade-off named. The Yuan collapse shows what happens when ecological shocks (Yellow River floods, plague), monetary mismanagement (paper inflation), and dynastic fragility (rapid succession crises) compound on top of an underlying legitimacy deficit (foreign rule, ethnic stratification). Each of these shocks alone might have been survivable. Together they were not. The Ming would learn from the Yuan collapse — Hongwu rebuilt the Confucian examination system, banned paper money, dismantled the foreign trade fleets, and turned China inward — and the resulting state would last 276 years.

## Concept 4 — Mongol successor states and the Mamluks

While the Yuan were collapsing, the western Mongol khanates were also dissolving. The Il-Khanate, which had ruled Persia and Mesopotamia, was held together — barely — by Abu Sa'id, Hulagu's great-great-grandson. Abu Sa'id had become *il-khan* at the age of twelve in 1316, was an effective if conventional ruler, and died in 1335, possibly from plague, certainly without an heir.

The Il-Khanate fragmented immediately. The Jalayirids took central Iraq and Azerbaijan. The Muzaffarids took central Iran. The Sarbadars took Khurasan. By the 1380s, when Timur (Tamerlane) — a Turco-Mongol warlord from Transoxiana with no Chinggisid ancestry but considerable military genius — began assembling his own empire on top of the wreckage, the Il-Khanate had been gone for fifty years. We will pick up Timur in Chapter 21.

The Chagatai Khanate fragmented similarly in the mid-fourteenth century. Only the Golden Horde, on the Pontic-Caspian steppe, held together — and then only because its core territory was less integrated into the urban systems of the Persian and Chinese economies, and so suffered less from the disruption. Even there, by the late fourteenth century, internal struggle and Timur's incursions had begun the process that would ultimately reduce the Horde to a series of smaller successor states.

The one Muslim state that strengthened during this period was the Mamluk Sultanate of Egypt and Syria. We met the Mamluks in Chapter 18 — slave-soldiers of Turkic origin who had overthrown their Ayyubid masters in 1250 and stopped Hulagu's army at Ain Jalut in 1260. Through the late thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries, the Mamluks under sultans like Baybars (r. 1260–1277) and al-Nasir Muhammad (r. 1293–1341, with two interruptions) consolidated their hold on Syria, finished off the surviving crusader states (Tripoli 1289, Acre 1291), repelled multiple Mongol invasions, and turned Cairo into the wealthiest, most populous Sunni Muslim city in the world. Cairo's population around 1340 was perhaps half a million — twice that of any contemporary European city outside Constantinople.

The Mamluk peak was al-Nasir Muhammad's third reign. His Cairo received Mansa Musa's gold (Chapter 19), supported the great chronicler Ibn Battuta, sponsored the architectural masterpieces of the Bahri Mamluk style — the Sultan Hassan complex, the Qalawun complex — and was the destination of refugees from every Mongol catastrophe to the east. Then al-Nasir Muhammad died in 1341. Within twenty years, eight of his sons and grandsons had succeeded him in turn, most reigning briefly, several deposed, and the plague had arrived.

The Mamluk plague experience is documented in detail by Ibn al-Wardi and Ibn Khaldun, two of the era's great Muslim chroniclers. (Ibn al-Wardi died of plague himself in Aleppo in 1349.) Egypt's losses are estimated at a third of the population. Cairo's losses may have been higher. The agricultural irrigation system that depended on dense rural labor was crippled — abandoned canals silted up, fields reverted to fallow, and Egyptian agricultural output did not return to its pre-plague level until well into the nineteenth century.

The Mamluk state survived, however. It would continue to govern Egypt and Syria until the Ottoman conquest in 1517. But the dynamism of the al-Nasir Muhammad period was over.

## Concept 5 — The bacterium and its routes

Now to the disease itself, and to a story that has been substantially rewritten by genetics in the last fifteen years.

*Yersinia pestis* is a Gram-negative bacterium. It evolved from a much milder relative, *Yersinia pseudotuberculosis*, perhaps 5,500 to 6,000 years ago. The genetic evidence for this comes from sequencing of ancient DNA recovered from skeletal remains across Eurasia. We now have *Y. pestis* genomes from Neolithic Sweden, Bronze Age Russia, sixth-century Bavaria (the Plague of Justinian), and dozens of fourteenth-century European mass graves. The genomes can be aligned into an evolutionary tree.

What that tree tells us, very approximately, is the following. There was a major branching event — what evolutionary biologists call the "Big Bang" of *Y. pestis* — somewhere in the Asian steppe, probably central Asia, in the thirteenth or early fourteenth century. From this branching event radiated several lineages. One reached Europe via Caffa in the late 1340s. Another reached the Middle East and North Africa around the same time, but perhaps via a different route through Anatolia. A third may have reached eastern Asia and contributed to the Yuan-era outbreaks. The medieval historian Monica H. Green has argued for what she calls the "Four Black Deaths" — four distinct major waves of *Y. pestis* expansion across the long thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, originating in Mongol military zones and following Mongol trade and migration routes outward.

The standard older story — that the plague originated at Lake Issyk-Kul in 1338 or 1339, where Nestorian Christian gravestones record an unusual death rate, and spread westward — is not so much wrong as incomplete. The Issyk-Kul evidence is real (recent ancient-DNA work confirmed *Y. pestis* in graves there from those years) but represents one branch of a more complex pattern.

The disease has three principal forms in humans, and their interaction is what made the medieval pandemic so devastating.

*Bubonic plague* is the most common. The bacterium enters the body through the bite of an infected flea (typically *Xenopsylla cheopis*, the oriental rat flea), travels via the lymphatic system to the nearest lymph node, and reproduces there. The infected node swells dramatically into the *bubo* — typically in the groin, armpit, or neck, depending on bite location. Mortality without antibiotic treatment is roughly thirty to sixty percent, with death usually following in three to five days.

*Pneumonic plague* occurs when *Y. pestis* establishes infection in the lungs — either secondarily, from bubonic plague that has spread, or primarily, by inhalation of bacteria coughed up by an already-infected human. Pneumonic plague is transmitted person-to-person through respiratory droplets, like tuberculosis or COVID-19. Mortality without treatment is essentially 100 percent, with death in one to three days. This is the form that allows the disease to spread between humans without rats or fleas, and it is the form that makes a plague epidemic catastrophic in winter, when fleas are dormant but coughs are not.

*Septicemic plague* occurs when the bacterium reaches the bloodstream directly. Mortality is total. Death often follows within hours of symptom onset, sometimes before the characteristic buboes have time to form. Contemporary accounts of people falling dead in the street, with no apparent prior illness, are likely septicemic.

The combination matters because the three forms make different things possible. Bubonic plague, alone, requires a flea-rat-human chain. The disease spreads at the speed of rats and trade caravans — perhaps a few miles a week, although faster on ships. Pneumonic plague spreads at the speed of human contact — household to household, town to town. Septicemic plague is essentially undetectable until people start dying suddenly. A pandemic with all three forms can move along trade routes by rats, jump from town to town by direct human contact, and decimate communities so fast that survivors have no time to organize a response.

The medieval Black Death had all three. So have all subsequent major outbreaks of the disease, including the late nineteenth-century pandemic that originated in Yunnan in 1855, killed perhaps fifteen million people across Asia, and was finally controlled in the early twentieth century after Alexandre Yersin and Shibasaburo Kitasato — independently, in 1894 — identified the bacterium that bears Yersin's name.

## Mid-chapter checkpoint

You have, so far, three things. First, a climate shift that began in the late thirteenth century and produced agricultural collapse and famine in northern Europe by the 1310s. Second, a series of Mongol successor states and Yuan-era political crises that dramatically weakened the institutional capacity of much of Eurasia. Third, a bacterium that had been radiating from a central Asian source through the thirteenth century, hidden inside Mongol military and trade movements, and that broke out into Europe and the Mediterranean in 1346–1347.

Hold those three. Now we trace what happens when the disease arrives in already-stressed societies.

## Concept 6 — The Black Death year by year

By any reasonable accounting, the year 1348 is the worst year in European history.

In Italy, Florence — population around 100,000 in 1347 — lost perhaps half its population in six months. Boccaccio, who survived the epidemic and lost his father and stepmother to it, wrote *The Decameron* a few years later as a frame for ten young Florentine survivors who flee the city to a country villa and pass their quarantine telling each other stories. The Introduction to *The Decameron* is the most-read eyewitness account of the plague in any language. He describes the *gavoccioli* (buboes), the rapid death of those infected, the breakdown of normal social bonds. "Brother forsook brother, uncle nephew, sister brother, and oftentimes wife husband; nay (what is yet more extraordinary and well-nigh incredible) fathers and mothers refused to visit or tend their very children."

In Avignon, where the papacy had been seated since 1309, Pope Clement VI commissioned his physician Guy de Chauliac to write a treatise on the disease. (Guy contracted plague himself, survived, and described his own symptoms.) Clement himself sat between two enormous fires — believed at the time to purify the air — for the duration of the epidemic. He survived. He also, in the years that followed, repeatedly issued papal bulls forbidding the harassment of Jews, who were being scapegoated across Europe as the source of the disease.

In Cairo, Ibn Khaldun — then a teenager — lost both his parents to the plague. The experience became the implicit moral background of his great work *al-Muqaddimah*, written some thirty years later, which is among other things a sustained meditation on the rise and fall of civilizations and the way that prosperous, settled urban populations are vulnerable in ways that nomadic populations are not.

In England, the plague arrived in Weymouth (Dorset) in June 1348 and reached London by autumn. Estimates of total English mortality range from thirty to fifty percent; the careful manorial record studies of Mark Bailey and others suggest the higher figure. The English chancellor's clerk William of Dene wrote that "where there had been twenty thousand sheep, scarce five hundred remained."

The geography of the disease is worth noticing. It moved, generally, along trade routes — by ship along the Mediterranean and Atlantic coasts, by river up the Rhône and Rhine, by road across Anatolia and the Levant. It reached Norway in 1349 (a single ship arriving at Bergen, plague-stricken), Russia in 1352, Iceland not until 1402. Some areas with thin connections to the trade networks were spared or only lightly hit — much of inland Poland, parts of the Pyrenees, the Atlas Mountains. Every other place was devastated.

The mortality numbers add up to something extraordinary. Across Europe, perhaps thirty-five percent. Across the Mamluk lands, perhaps thirty. Across the Yuan and post-Yuan Chinese world, harder to estimate, but plausibly twenty-five to thirty over the long fourteenth century if multiple waves are counted. Total Eurasian mortality from the fourteenth-century pandemic: perhaps seventy-five to two hundred million. The wide range reflects how uncertain the underlying counts are. Even at the low end, it is the worst pandemic in recorded history by absolute numbers, and almost certainly the worst by proportion of population killed.

**Worked example: the comparison with COVID-19.** It is tempting to compare the Black Death with recent pandemics. The comparison clarifies as much as it confuses. COVID-19 from 2020 to 2023 killed, by most credible estimates, around 7 million people directly and perhaps 15–20 million in total when excess mortality is counted. The world population today is around 8 billion. The proportional mortality is on the order of 0.2 percent. The fourteenth-century pandemic killed something like 30 percent of the affected population. The orders of magnitude are not comparable. Modern medicine — antibiotics for bacterial pneumonia, ventilators, oxygen supplementation — has changed the basic mathematics of pandemic mortality. The medieval pandemic shows what happens when those tools are not available. It is, in that sense, the baseline rather than the outlier.

## Concept 7 — Responses: religion, blame, social rupture

People did not just die during the Black Death. They tried to understand it, and the way different societies tried — religiously, intellectually, politically — tells us a great deal about the institutions they had to work with.

In the Latin Christian world, the church's first explanation was that the plague was God's punishment for human sin. Penitential processions formed in Avignon and Rome. Then the explanation began to fracture. If the plague were specifically a punishment for sin, why were the pious dying alongside the wicked? Why were monks and clergy dying at higher rates than the laity? (They were, because they ministered to the sick.) The traditional theology had no good answer.

A radical movement emerged. The flagellants — called the Brethren of the Cross in some sources — were lay penitents who traveled in disciplined groups from town to town, publicly whipping themselves with leather scourges studded with iron points until they drew blood. The performance was meant as collective atonement for the sins of humanity. The movement originated in central Europe, particularly the Rhineland and Flanders, and spread rapidly across the German lands, Poland, and the Low Countries. Crowds welcomed them. Some flagellant leaders began to claim direct divine authority — that their atonement, not the church's sacraments, would end the plague. Pope Clement VI condemned the movement in 1349, and most secular authorities suppressed it within the next year or two, but the underlying message — that the established church was failing — did not go away.

The other movement of 1348–1349 was anti-Jewish violence. In dozens of European cities — Strasbourg, Basel, Cologne, Erfurt, Mainz, Brussels, Frankfurt — Jewish communities were accused of poisoning wells to cause the plague, were tortured into confessions, and were burned alive. The Strasbourg massacre on Saint Valentine's Day 1349 killed perhaps two thousand Jews; the city's chronicle says they were locked into a wooden building set on fire. Pope Clement VI issued bulls in July and September 1348 specifically condemning the accusations and pointing out that Jews were dying of the plague in proportion to other populations — "they could not have done this thing, since they have likewise died." The bulls were widely ignored. By 1351, when the wave passed, the Ashkenazi Jewish presence in much of central Europe had been reduced or eliminated, and the survivors had moved east to Poland, Lithuania, and the early Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth — where they would form the demographic core of Eastern European Jewry until the twentieth century.

In the Islamic world, the response was very different. We have multiple Mamluk-era *plague treatises* — most prominently those of Ibn al-Wardi (d. 1349, of plague), Ibn al-Khatib (d. 1374, of strangulation in prison after dabbling in heresy), and Ibn Khatima of Almeria (d. 1369). These authors argued, against the prevailing religious view, for some form of contagion theory — Ibn al-Khatib most explicitly, writing that "the existence of contagion is proven by experience, deduction, the senses, observation, and trustworthy reports of transmission, and these are the elements of proof." This was, in retrospect, broadly correct and centuries ahead of the European medical consensus. But it sat awkwardly with the religious view that the plague was a divine visitation that the faithful should accept rather than flee, and Ibn al-Khatib's heterodoxy probably contributed to his eventual murder.

The anti-Jewish violence of Christian Europe had no clear analogue in the Mamluk lands. Cairo's Jewish community continued essentially undisturbed. Coptic Christians faced some heightened pressure but no systematic persecution. The Mamluks did, however, see a sharp increase in religious-architectural patronage — mosques, *madrasas*, mausoleums — funded by survivors, particularly merchants who had inherited the estates of plague victims. The Sultan Hassan complex in Cairo, begun in 1356, is the largest medieval mosque in the world. It is a plague monument.

In Yuan and early Ming China, the disease was woven into a broader narrative of Mandate withdrawal. The Yuan, already foreign rulers, were seen as having lost legitimacy when famine, flood, and plague compounded. The Red Turban movement that produced the Ming was explicitly millenarian — followers believed they were preparing for the imminent return of the Maitreya Buddha. The Ming founder Hongwu, when he had consolidated power, suppressed the millenarian elements that had brought him to power; the apocalyptic register of the rebellion period gave way to a deliberately conservative, Confucian-restoration register that would define the Ming.

## Concept 8 — Aftermath: a labor economy reborn

The plague ended, eventually. The first wave passed by 1351; subsequent waves in 1361, 1369, 1374, and roughly every decade through the late fifteenth century were progressively less devastating but kept the population suppressed. By 1400, Europe had perhaps two-thirds the population of 1347. Recovery to pre-plague levels would not occur until around 1500–1550 in most regions.

What happened in the meantime is one of the most studied transformations in economic history. The basic mechanics are simple. Land is fixed; labor is scarce. The price of labor rises; the price of land falls. Wages go up. Rents go down. The peasants who survive — particularly in Western Europe — are in a stronger bargaining position than their ancestors had been in centuries.

Landowners and governments tried to fight this. The English Statute of Laborers (1351) attempted to fix wages at pre-plague levels and required all able-bodied unemployed to take whatever work was offered. France issued similar edicts. Most failed. Workers simply moved to landlords who would pay more — sometimes secretly, sometimes openly. The serf-feudal labor regime that had organized European agriculture for centuries — peasants tied to a manor, owing labor to a lord in exchange for protection and a strip of land — eroded over the next century.

Where the regime did not erode, it broke. The Jacquerie in northern France in 1358 — *Jacques Bonhomme* was the contemptuous name for a French peasant — was an uprising of perhaps tens of thousands of peasants and townspeople against the local nobility, motivated by war taxes, banditry, and the perceived failure of the nobility to defend them. It was suppressed brutally. The English Peasants' Revolt of 1381, led by Wat Tyler and the radical priest John Ball, marched on London with demands including the abolition of serfdom, the seizure of church lands, and the reduction of all men to equal social standing. Ball's sermon — "When Adam delved and Eve span, who was then the gentleman?" — is still quoted. The revolt was suppressed; Tyler was killed by the Lord Mayor of London during a parley with the fourteen-year-old Richard II; Ball was hanged, drawn, and quartered. But the revolt did not end peasant assertiveness; it defined a new political vocabulary that would echo across late medieval Europe.

The plague did not directly cause the rise of the centralized state, the decline of feudalism, the growth of the merchant class, or the religious crises that produced the Reformation. All of these were already underway before 1347. But the plague accelerated all of them dramatically. A society that has lost a third of its labor force in three years has no choice but to reorganize. The reorganization that emerged — wage labor, more centralized monarchies, more powerful merchant cities, weaker nobility, a church that had visibly failed to deliver salvation in the moment of greatest need — is the ground from which the early modern Europe of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries grows.

In the Islamic world, the post-plague period was less revolutionary, but the consolidations of the Ottoman, Safavid, and (in the next generation) Mughal empires would not have been possible in the political fragmentation of pre-plague Asia. Each of these empires assembled itself on the ruins of older systems that the plague had hollowed out. The Ottomans, in particular — whom we will pick up in Chapter 21 — emerged as the principal Sunni power in Anatolia partly because the Byzantine and Mamluk competitors had been so badly weakened.

In China, the Ming consolidation had a different shape: a reactionary turn. Hongwu and his successors deliberately rebuilt the Confucian state model, expelled foreign influence, dismantled the Yuan-era Mongol-favoring social structures, and after the brief seven-voyage Treasure Fleet expeditions of Zheng He (1405–1433, under the Yongle Emperor), turned away from oceanic exploration entirely. This is the Ming policy of *haijin* — "sea ban" — that would define Chinese foreign engagement for the next two centuries and that would, by withdrawing China from the Indian Ocean exactly as European powers were arriving, leave the field to Vasco da Gama and his successors. Chapter 21 picks up the consequences.

## Synthesis

Pull back. The fourteenth century is a study in the way that connected systems amplify shocks. None of the individual catastrophes — the Little Ice Age, the Great Famine, the Yuan collapse, the Mongol fragmentation, the plague — was unprecedented. Climate had shifted before. Famines had occurred before. Empires had fallen before. Plagues had occurred before. What was unprecedented was the conjunction.

The conjunction was possible because Eurasia had become, by the early fourteenth century, more connected than it had ever been before. The Mongol Pax had knit together trade routes, postal systems, and population movements on a continental scale. That same connectivity carried climate-induced famine refugees, fugitive Mongol cavalry, and plague-bearing rats and fleas across distances that would have stopped them a century earlier. The plague that escaped Caffa in 1346 and reached Iceland by 1402 was riding on infrastructure that the Mongols had built.

A general principle hides here, and it is worth stating clearly. *The same connections that create wealth in good times propagate catastrophe in bad times.* The Pax Mongolica is not a separate historical phenomenon from the Black Death; it is the precondition. The interconnected Mediterranean economy that had produced Florentine banking and the Genoese galley fleets is the same economy that brought *Yersinia pestis* into every coastal city of Europe within thirty months of its first appearance in Caffa.

What does this teach? Several things, most of which apply with little modification to the present.

First, that climate matters to politics in the long run, and that the boundary between climatic and political history is artificial. The Little Ice Age helped to bring down the Yuan, helped to weaken the European feudal order, helped to push populations into denser and more disease-vulnerable urban settlements. The mechanisms are not mysterious; they are agricultural and demographic.

Second, that the response to a pandemic is governed less by biology than by institutions. Christian Europe's persecution of Jews, the Mamluks' architectural boom, the Ming's millenarian-then-reactionary turn — these are different responses to the same disease, and they reflect the institutional resources each society had to draw on. A society that has a tradition of intellectual contagion theory (the Mamluk *ulama*) will respond differently than a society that has a tradition of penitential atonement (Latin Christendom).

Third, that the post-shock world is not a return to the pre-shock world. It cannot be. The labor markets of post-plague Europe were structurally different from those before. The legitimacy of the Latin Christian Church was structurally different. The institutional confidence of the Mongol khanates was structurally different. Recovery is always to a new equilibrium, not the old one.

Now come back to Caffa, 1346. Twelve Genoese galleys, a fortified port besieged by a Mongol army, a disease moving through the besiegers and into the city. From the perspective of the people on the walls, this was a discrete event — a siege, then a flight, then home. From a longer perspective, it was one capillary node in a system that connected Mongol cavalry on the steppe to Florentine wool merchants to Cairo madrasas to English peasants. The bacterium found that system and used it.

A last sentence, then. We sometimes imagine that interconnection is a recent invention. The fourteenth century is a reminder that it is not, and that connection's costs are paid in the same currency as its benefits, just at different times.

## Chapter summary

A climatic shift beginning in the late thirteenth century — what we now call the Little Ice Age — produced cooler, wetter summers across the Northern Hemisphere. The result was the Great Famine of 1315–1317, which killed perhaps five to ten percent of northern Europe's population and destabilized the medieval agricultural order. Roughly contemporaneously, the Yuan dynasty in China entered a long crisis driven by Yellow River flooding, paper-money inflation, and dynastic instability. The Il-Khanate fragmented after Abu Sa'id's death in 1335.

The bacterium *Yersinia pestis* — which had been radiating from a central Asian source through the thirteenth century — reached the Mediterranean in 1346 via the Genoese trading post at Caffa and ports of the Black Sea. Within four years it had killed perhaps a third of the European population (and proportional shares in Mamluk Egypt and the Yuan Chinese world). The disease's combination of bubonic, pneumonic, and septicemic forms made it impossible to contain with the medical and institutional resources of fourteenth-century societies.

The response varied by region: penitential and anti-Jewish violence in Latin Christendom; quietist religious-acceptance and medical-contagion debate in the Mamluk world; millenarian peasant rebellion in China that produced the Ming dynasty in 1368. The Avignon papacy and the subsequent Great Western Schism (1378–1417) badly damaged the legitimacy of the Latin Christian Church. The Hundred Years' War (1337–1453) consumed France and England across the same period. The Holy Roman Empire fragmented further under the Golden Bull of 1356.

Long-term economic consequences in Europe included the breakdown of serfdom, the Statute of Laborers and analogous wage-control attempts, the Jacquerie (1358) and English Peasants' Revolt (1381), the rise of merchant cities, and the centralization of monarchical power. In the Islamic world, the post-plague reorganization opened space for the Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal consolidations of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. In China, the Ming dynasty turned inward, restoring the Confucian state model and ultimately abandoning oceanic exploration after the Zheng He voyages.

## Key terms

*Little Ice Age* — period of cooler global temperatures from roughly 1300 to 1850, with first severe phase in the early fourteenth century.

*Great Famine* — northern European food crisis of 1315–1317 produced by Little Ice Age weather.

*Yersinia pestis* — Gram-negative bacterium that causes plague in its bubonic, pneumonic, and septicemic forms.

*Black Death* — fourteenth-century plague pandemic, peaking in Europe 1347–1351.

*Bubonic plague* — flea-borne form of the disease, characterized by *bubo* (swollen lymph node), 30–60 percent mortality untreated.

*Pneumonic plague* — respiratory form, transmitted human-to-human, near-100 percent mortality untreated.

*Septicemic plague* — bloodstream form, often fatal within hours.

*Flagellants* — penitential lay movement of self-scourging that swept central Europe during the plague years.

*Statute of Laborers (1351)* — English law fixing wages at pre-plague levels; widely evaded.

*Jacquerie* — 1358 northern French peasant uprising against the nobility.

*English Peasants' Revolt* — 1381 English uprising led by Wat Tyler and John Ball.

*Avignon papacy* — period 1309–1376 when popes resided in Avignon rather than Rome.

*Great Western Schism* — 1378–1417 period of multiple competing claimants to the papacy.

*Hundred Years' War* — 1337–1453 series of conflicts between England and France.

*Golden Bull (1356)* — Holy Roman Empire constitutional document specifying seven prince-electors.

*Ming dynasty* — 1368–1644 Chinese dynasty founded by Zhu Yuanzhang (Hongwu Emperor).

*Mandate of Heaven* — Confucian doctrine that legitimate rule depends on divine favor, withdrawn from corrupt or incompetent dynasties.

## Discussion questions

1. The Black Death killed perhaps a third of the European population in three years. Why did European institutions — the Church, monarchies, urban councils — not collapse entirely? What kinds of institutions are robust to large mortality shocks, and what kinds are not?

2. Latin Christian Europe, the Mamluk world, and Yuan China experienced the same biological disease but produced very different cultural responses. What does this tell us about the relationship between biology and culture in pandemic experience?

3. The flagellants and the anti-Jewish massacres of 1348–1349 are sometimes treated as separate phenomena and sometimes as expressions of the same underlying social anxiety. Argue for one of these positions, with specific evidence.

4. The Statute of Laborers (1351) tried to fix wages at pre-plague levels and failed. What does the failure tell us about the limits of legal authority in the face of structural economic shocks?

5. Boccaccio's *Decameron* opens with a long, unflinching description of the plague in Florence and then turns to a hundred frame-stories told by ten young people in a country villa. What is the literary function of this structure? What does it suggest about how survivors processed catastrophe?

## Further reading

- Barbara Tuchman, *A Distant Mirror: The Calamitous 14th Century* (1978) — the classic narrative history; sometimes overdramatizes but enormously readable.
- John Aberth, *The Black Death: A New History of the Great Mortality, 1347–1500* (2021) — current scholarly synthesis.
- Bruce M. S. Campbell, *The Great Transition: Climate, Disease and Society in the Late-Medieval World* (2016) — quantitative environmental-economic analysis.
- Monica H. Green, "The Four Black Deaths" (*The American Historical Review*, 2014) — revisionist piece on the deeper history of *Yersinia pestis*.
- Giovanni Boccaccio, *The Decameron* (c. 1353) — primary source; the introduction is the most-read eyewitness account of the Florentine plague.
- Ibn Khaldun, *The Muqaddimah* (c. 1377), trans. Franz Rosenthal — primary source; Maghreb-Tunisian intellectual whose family was killed by the plague.
- Geoffrey Parker, *Global Crisis: War, Climate Change and Catastrophe in the Seventeenth Century* (2013) — covers a later climatic-catastrophic period but uses fourteenth-century precedents extensively.
- William Chester Jordan, *The Great Famine: Northern Europe in the Early Fourteenth Century* (1996) — the standard work on the famine that preceded the plague.
- Maria Kelly, *A History of the Black Death in Ireland* (2001) — case study of plague spread in a peripheral region.

## What would change my mind

If new ancient-DNA evidence demonstrated that fourteenth-century *Yersinia pestis* strains were biologically less virulent than current models assume — and that the catastrophic mortality of the Black Death was therefore more a consequence of social conditions (malnutrition, poor sanitation, crowding) than of the bacterium itself — much of this chapter's account of the disease as the dominant causal driver would need revision toward an account in which the climate-famine context did most of the work. The DNA evidence currently does not support this revision, but the question is open enough that the genomes recovered in the next decade may shift it.

## Still puzzling

I do not fully understand why the Mamluk medical tradition of contagion theory, which Ibn al-Khatib and others articulated in the 1340s and 1350s, did not become dominant in the Islamic world over the following centuries — and why, when European medicine eventually rediscovered contagion theory in the nineteenth century, it did so with no apparent awareness of the medieval Arabic precedents. The transmission failure between intellectual traditions is itself a historical question. I have not seen a fully convincing answer.
