## Chapter overview

By the end of this chapter you will know why some Neolithic settlements crossed a threshold and others did not. You will know what archaeologists and historians mean when they call a society a *civilization* — and why the term has problems we should name. You will know the structural features common to early urban societies: agricultural surplus, labor specialization, social stratification, organized government, monumental architecture, organized religion, and (in most cases) writing. You will know the three river-valley civilizations that emerged in the fourth and third millennia BCE — Mesopotamia (in the Tigris and Euphrates valley), Egypt (along the Nile), and the Indus Valley (along the Indus and now-vanished Sarasvati). You will know the names that anchor each civilization — Uruk, Sumer, Sargon of Akkad, Hammurabi, Narmer, Khufu, Harappa, Mohenjo-Daro — and the institutions they invented or perfected. You will know what cuneiform and hieroglyphics did for record-keeping and what the still-undeciphered Indus script may or may not have been. And you will know what each civilization tells you about the fundamental trade-offs of urban life: order at the cost of freedom, surplus at the cost of equality, durability at the cost of a more rigid hierarchy.

## Opening case — Uruk, c. 3000 BCE

Stand on the ramparts of Uruk in southern Mesopotamia, around three thousand years before our era, and look at what you would see. A wall of mud-brick about six miles long, enclosing about two square miles of city. Inside, perhaps fifty thousand people — at the time, possibly the largest concentration of human beings anywhere on Earth. A central temple complex called the **ziggurat**, a stepped tower of sun-dried brick climbing toward a flat platform on which stood a small shrine to the goddess Inanna, patroness of the city. Around the temple, the residences of priests and the workshops of artisans. Below them, neighborhoods of homes, markets, granaries. Beyond the wall, fields irrigated by a network of canals dug into the alluvium between the Tigris and the Euphrates. In those fields, men and women bent over rows of barley and emmer wheat, the same crops their ancestors had been domesticating for the previous five or six thousand years.

Uruk was new. It was new in the same way that, twelve thousand years before, agriculture had been new. Agriculture had been the first major change in how humans fed themselves. Uruk was the first major change in how humans organized themselves. A hunter-gatherer band of forty people negotiates everything by face-to-face conversation. A village of two hundred people manages by extended family. A city of fifty thousand people cannot. It needs procedures. It needs writing — to record who owes whom how much grain. It needs taxes — to support the people who do not farm. It needs priests — to manage the relationship between the city and its patron god, on whose favor the harvest is believed to depend. It needs a king — or a council, or some other arrangement that can decide things faster than fifty thousand people can decide them by consensus.

Uruk had all of these. The first cuneiform tablets were inscribed in Uruk around 3300 BCE. The first ziggurat was built there. The first epic of which a written version survives — the *Epic of Gilgamesh*, attributed to a legendary king of Uruk — has its hero walk on those same ramparts.

This chapter is about the threshold Uruk crossed and about the three civilizations that crossed it in the third and second millennia BCE: Mesopotamia, Egypt, and the Indus Valley. Each made a different version of the same bargain. Each invented institutions we are still living with.

## Concept 1 — What does "civilization" mean?

The word *civilization* is contested. Older usages treated it as a value judgment, suggesting that civilized peoples were "advanced" while non-civilized peoples were "primitive." That usage has been correctly abandoned. Hunter-gatherers, pastoralists, and small-scale farmers are not less sophisticated than urban civilization-builders; they have made different choices, often well-suited to different environments, and they have produced their own complex social and religious systems.

The technical use of *civilization* in modern archaeology and history is descriptive, not evaluative. It names a particular configuration of features that some societies developed in particular places at particular times. The features:

**Agricultural surplus.** Civilization requires that some people grow more food than they need themselves, so that other people can do other work. In hunter-gatherer bands and most early Neolithic villages, almost everyone is involved in food production. In early civilizations, perhaps half the population is. The other half are doing something else.

**Specialization.** When food becomes a collective resource that can be redistributed, specialists emerge. Potters, weavers, smiths, scribes, soldiers, priests, traders, builders, musicians, singers. Each specialist depends on the surplus produced by farmers; the farmers depend on the goods and services produced by the specialists. The division of labor — the source of much of civilization's productivity — follows from the surplus.

**Stratification.** Specialization is not neutral. Some specialists — priests with control of temple lands, kings with control of armies, merchants with control of trade routes — accumulate wealth. They pass that wealth to their children. Wealth becomes inherited social position. Social classes emerge: a small elite, a larger artisan and merchant class, the great mass of farmers, and (in most early civilizations) enslaved people at the bottom. The egalitarianism of hunter-gatherer life and early Neolithic village life becomes impossible. There is too much that can be owned, too much that can be inherited.

**Organized government.** A village of two hundred people can be governed by elders meeting at the well. A city of fifty thousand cannot. Decisions about war, taxation, public works, justice, and ritual must be made by people authorized to make them. In the early civilizations, the authorization usually came from religion: kings ruled because the gods had chosen them. The god-king or priest-king is the most common political form of the early Bronze Age across all three regions this chapter covers.

**Monumental architecture.** Cities accumulate the labor of thousands and channel it into structures that no smaller community could build. Ziggurats in Mesopotamia. Pyramids in Egypt. Citadels and great baths in the Indus Valley. Monumental architecture is partly religious — the buildings honor the gods — and partly political — they advertise the king's command of labor on a scale that no rebel could match.

**Writing.** Most early civilizations developed some form of writing. Mesopotamia produced cuneiform; Egypt produced hieroglyphics; the Indus Valley produced a script we still cannot read. Writing did several things: it made record-keeping at city scale possible (granary inventories, tax receipts, contracts), it preserved religious traditions across generations, and it allowed the state to communicate with itself across distance. Where writing was not invented — much of sub-Saharan Africa before later contact, the Andes before the Inca *quipu*, much of Oceania — civilizations on equally complex scales developed using oral tradition and other mnemonic systems instead. Writing is common in early civilization but not strictly necessary.

The three civilizations this chapter covers all developed all of these features. They did so independently, in roughly the same window of time, by different pathways. Their parallels are telling. Their differences are also telling.

## Concept 2 — Mesopotamia: city-states, empires, and the rule of law

Mesopotamia means, in Greek, "the land between the rivers." The Greek historian Herodotus probably coined the term in the fifth century BCE. The land in question is the alluvial plain between the Tigris and the Euphrates in modern Iraq, stretching from the mountains of southeastern Turkey to the Persian Gulf. The two rivers flooded unpredictably. They deposited rich soil. They required, and rewarded, irrigation. Mesopotamian civilization was, from its beginning, a story of cooperative water management.

### Sumer and the city-state era (c. 4500–2300 BCE)

By the middle of the fourth millennium BCE, southern Mesopotamia — the region the Sumerians called Sumer — had developed a network of urban centers. **Uruk** was the largest and most influential, with a population that may have approached fifty thousand. **Ur** controlled the southern delta. **Nippur** was the religious center, sacred to the storm god Enlil. **Eridu** was the oldest. **Lagash**, **Kish**, **Umma**, **Larsa** rounded out the system. By the end of the fourth millennium BCE, archaeologists count more than 120 villages of about a hundred people each, twenty towns of around two thousand, twenty small cities of about five thousand, and Uruk by itself with fifty thousand. The transition from village to city had happened, and it had happened across a region.

The Sumerians invented or perfected several technologies that would define civilization for the next three thousand years. **Bronze**, an alloy of copper and tin much harder than either metal alone, became available around 4000 BCE; the Bronze Age would last until about 1100 BCE. **The plow** and **the wheel** revolutionized agriculture and transport. **Irrigation** through canals and dikes turned the floodplain into the most productive farmland on Earth. **Mathematics** based on the number 60 — the source of our 60-minute hour, our 360-degree circle, and our division of the day into twelve hour-segments — emerged here. So did the **twelve-month calendar**.

Above all, the Sumerians developed **cuneiform**, the world's first known full writing system. Cuneiform began around 3300 BCE as pictographic accounting marks pressed into clay with a reed stylus. Within a few centuries, the marks had abstracted into wedge-shaped (Latin *cuneus*, "wedge") symbols representing syllables. The system became flexible enough to record laws, religious hymns, royal proclamations, mathematical tables, contracts, school exercises, and personal letters. It also became the first writing system to be borrowed: the Akkadians, Babylonians, Assyrians, Hittites, and others would adapt cuneiform to languages from at least three different language families.

Each Sumerian city was a **city-state** — a politically independent unit consisting of the city and its surrounding agricultural land. Each was ruled by a king (Sumerian *lugal*), who was simultaneously a war leader, a chief priest, and a manager of the central temple-and-palace complex that redistributed the agricultural surplus. The city-states fought each other constantly, mostly over land and water rights. The early dynastic period (c. 2900–2350 BCE) is a long story of shifting alliances, occasional dominance by one city over its neighbors, and constant warfare. The most famous king of this era was **Gilgamesh** of Uruk, around 2700 BCE, whose semi-legendary deeds were the basis for the *Epic of Gilgamesh* — the world's oldest surviving extended literary work, written in its classic form around 2100 BCE.

### Sargon and the first empire (c. 2334–2154 BCE)

Around 2300 BCE, a king named **Sargon of Akkad** conquered Sumer and built the world's first known **empire** — a state in which one ruler controlled multiple regional powers across an area larger than any single city-state could administer. Sargon's empire stretched from the Persian Gulf to the upper Euphrates, encompassing Sumer, Akkad (the central Mesopotamian region around the city of Kish), and parts of what are now Syria and southwestern Iran. He installed his daughter **Enheduanna** as high priestess of the moon god in Ur — making her, incidentally, the world's first author known by name; her hymns to the goddess Inanna survive and bear her signature.

Sargon's empire lasted about 150 years. After it collapsed under invasions by mountain peoples from the Zagros (the Guti) around 2154 BCE, Sumer regrouped under the **Third Dynasty of Ur** (c. 2112–2004 BCE). The most famous king of this period, **Ur-Nammu**, issued the earliest surviving law code — predating Hammurabi by some three centuries.

### Hammurabi and Babylon (c. 1792–1750 BCE)

Around 1900 BCE, **Amorite** peoples — speakers of a Semitic language, originally pastoralists from the Syrian desert — settled in Mesopotamia and built new dynasties. The city of **Babylon**, on the middle Euphrates, became their capital under a series of kings, the most famous of whom was **Hammurabi** (reigned c. 1792–1750 BCE). Hammurabi conquered the rival cities of Mari and Larsa, building an empire that covered nearly all of Mesopotamia.

Hammurabi's claim to lasting fame is his **law code**, the most complete surviving from the ancient Near East. The code was inscribed in cuneiform on stone pillars erected throughout his realm — a physical, public broadcast of the king's authority and the rules under which his subjects would be judged. About 282 individual laws survive, covering criminal punishment, family law, commercial disputes, slavery, property, agricultural rights, and dozens of other matters.

The principle most often cited from Hammurabi's code is **lex talionis**, "an eye for an eye, a tooth for a tooth" — punishment that fits the crime in the literal sense. But the principle was qualified by class. An aristocrat who killed another aristocrat paid with his life. An aristocrat who killed a commoner paid a fine. A commoner who killed an aristocrat paid with his life and then some. Mesopotamian society was rigidly stratified, and the law made the stratification explicit.

Hammurabi's empire fragmented after his death. Babylon was sacked by Hittites from Anatolia in 1595 BCE, then ruled by Kassites from the Iranian highlands for nearly five hundred years. The pattern would repeat: Mesopotamia's openness to invasion from the surrounding mountains and deserts meant that political structures rose, stabilized, fell, and were reconstituted by new dynasties — all while the underlying urban civilization continued.

### Mesopotamian religion: pessimistic gods, fickle weather

Mesopotamian religion was **polytheistic** — many gods, each associated with a force of nature or aspect of life. **Inanna** (Akkadian Ishtar) was the goddess of love and war. **Enlil** was the storm god, ruler of the divine assembly. **Anu** was the god of the sky. **Utu** (Akkadian Shamash) was the sun. Each city had a patron god whose temple — the ziggurat — was the city's tallest structure. The gods were anthropomorphic in their behavior: they ate, fought, lusted, slept, and sometimes lashed out with floods or plagues when annoyed.

The character of Mesopotamian religion has often been described as **pessimistic**. The unpredictability of the rivers (which could flood catastrophically), the openness of the land to invasion, and the hard work of irrigation all reinforced a sense that human existence was fragile and the gods unreliable. The afterlife in Mesopotamian belief was a shadowy underworld — "the land of no return" — where the dead ate dust and clay and longed pitifully for the living world. The contrast with the Egyptian afterlife, with its more elaborate rewards and punishments, is striking. Both traditions emerged from similar river-valley environments. Egypt's river was reliable. Mesopotamia's was not. The religions reflected the difference.

## Concept 3 — Egypt: the gift of the Nile

The Greek historian Herodotus, writing in the fifth century BCE, called Egypt "the gift of the Nile." The phrase has stuck because it is accurate. Without the Nile, Egypt would be desert. With the Nile, Egypt was, for three thousand years, the most stable and continuously prosperous civilization in the Mediterranean world.

### The river and the unification

The Nile flows north for over four thousand miles, from sources in central Africa to the Mediterranean. The Egyptian section, from modern Aswan to the delta, runs through one of the driest deserts on Earth. The river's annual flood — caused by summer monsoons in the Ethiopian highlands far to the south — was, in pre-modern times, almost perfectly regular. It rose every July, peaked in August or September, and receded in October, leaving a layer of fresh silt across the floodplain. Egyptian agriculture worked on this rhythm. Crops were sown after the flood receded and harvested before the next one rose. The river was reliable. The desert protected Egypt on both flanks from invasion. The geography made Egypt simultaneously fertile and isolated — the perfect conditions for a stable, conservative civilization to develop in.

For most of its early history, Egypt was divided into two kingdoms — **Lower Egypt** in the Nile delta and **Upper Egypt** along the river to the south. Around 3150 BCE, a king from Upper Egypt — variously called **Narmer** or **Menes**, possibly the same person — unified the two kingdoms under a single crown. The Narmer Palette, a carved slate plaque from this period, shows the king in the act of conquest, wearing the white crown of Upper Egypt on one side and the red crown of Lower Egypt on the other. The double crown of unified Egypt would symbolize the pharaoh's authority for the next three thousand years.

### The pharaoh

The Egyptian king was called **pharaoh** (Egyptian *per-aa*, "great house" — originally a reference to the palace, eventually transferred to its occupant). The pharaoh was simultaneously a political ruler, a high priest, and a god. As a god, the pharaoh was identified with **Horus**, the falcon-headed god of justice and truth, and after death with **Osiris**, the god of the underworld. The pharaoh's divine status meant that the maintenance of *Ma'at* — the cosmic principle of order, truth, justice, and balance — depended on him. When *Ma'at* was maintained, the Nile flooded, the harvest came in, and the kingdom prospered. When *Ma'at* failed, disaster followed.

The pharaoh ruled a population that, at unification, may have been two million. (For comparison, the largest Sumerian *lugals* ruled perhaps thirty thousand.) Egyptian government scaled up accordingly. A bureaucracy of **scribes**, regional governors (**nomarchs**), and temple priests administered the kingdom. Tax was collected in kind — grain, cattle, cloth, labor — and stored in royal granaries.

### The Old Kingdom and the pyramids (c. 2613–2181 BCE)

The Egyptian periodization runs through eight major eras: Pre-Dynastic, Early Dynastic, Old Kingdom, First Intermediate, Middle Kingdom, Second Intermediate, New Kingdom, Third Intermediate. The **Old Kingdom** is the era of the great pyramids.

The first pyramid was built around 2630 BCE for the pharaoh **Djoser** by his architect **Imhotep**. It was a stepped pyramid — six progressively smaller mastabas (rectangular flat-roofed tombs) stacked on top of each other. The form became smoother over the next century. By the reign of **Khufu** (c. 2589–2566 BCE), the form had reached its final expression: the **Great Pyramid of Giza**, 481 feet tall at construction, 756 feet on each side at the base, containing 2.3 million stone blocks averaging 2.5 tons each. Two smaller pyramids were built nearby for Khufu's successors Khafre and Menkaure. The Sphinx — a gigantic stone lion with a pharaoh's head — was carved out of bedrock at the Giza complex during the same period.

The pyramids were tombs. Egyptian religion held that a person consisted of multiple components — a physical body, the *ka* (a spiritual double that remained with the body), the *ba* (a wandering spirit), the *akh* (a transformed afterlife self) — and that all of them needed the body to be preserved. **Mummification** preserved the body. The pyramid kept it safe from grave robbers and protected the *ka* in its eternal home. The wealth of objects buried with the pharaoh — gold, jewelry, weapons, food — were what the *ka* needed to enjoy the afterlife.

The Old Kingdom pyramids were not, as is sometimes claimed, built by enslaved Israelites. They were built by Egyptian peasant labor, organized by the state, working in shifts during the months when the Nile flood made agricultural work impossible. The workers lived in dedicated cities near the construction sites, with their own bakeries, breweries, butchers, and medical staff. They were paid in beer, bread, and grain. The Greek historian Herodotus's claim that 100,000 men worked for twenty years on the Great Pyramid is probably an exaggeration; modern archaeologists estimate a workforce of around twenty thousand at peak.

### The Middle and New Kingdoms

The Old Kingdom collapsed around 2181 BCE in what is called the **First Intermediate Period** — a hundred-and-fifty years of weakened central authority during which the pharaohs lost effective control to regional governors. The **Middle Kingdom** (c. 2040–1782 BCE) saw centralization restored under a series of strong pharaohs, the most powerful of whom was **Senusret III**. The Middle Kingdom was a cultural high point — its literature, art, and architecture set the classical standard for later Egyptian civilization.

The **Second Intermediate Period** followed, during which the **Hyksos** — Semitic-speaking peoples from the Levant — established their own dynasty in the Nile delta. The **New Kingdom** (c. 1570–1069 BCE) saw the Egyptians expel the Hyksos, conquer Nubia (modern Sudan) for its gold, and project power into the Levant. We will follow the New Kingdom and its decline in Chapter 5.

### Egyptian religion and writing

Egyptian religion, like Mesopotamian, was polytheistic, and the gods were associated with natural forces. **Re** (or **Ra**) was the sun. **Isis** was earth-mother and goddess of fertility. **Osiris** was the Nile and the underworld. **Horus** was the pharaoh. **Seth** was the desert wind, brother of Osiris and his murderer. **Anubis** was the jackal-headed god of mummification. **Thoth** was the god of writing and wisdom.

The annual cycle of the Nile shaped Egyptian theology in a specific way. **Osiris**, killed by his brother Seth and resurrected by his wife Isis, was a god who died and came back. His resurrection was the Nile's flood. The myth of Osiris was the Nile's own story, retold as theology. Egyptian religion was therefore considerably more optimistic about the afterlife than Mesopotamian religion: the dead, judged by Osiris in the underworld, could be granted blessed eternity if their hearts were as light as the feather of *Ma'at*.

Egyptian writing — **hieroglyphics** — emerged around 3000 BCE. Hieroglyphs were a complex mixed system: some symbols represented sounds (like alphabetic letters), some represented syllables, some represented whole words, some were determinatives that clarified meaning. Literacy was a specialist skill, the property of trained scribes. A simplified cursive form, **hieratic**, was used for everyday administrative writing. Centuries later, an even more cursive form called demotic emerged. Egyptian writing was preserved on stone (for monuments and tombs) and on **papyrus**, a paper-like material made from the pith of the Nile's reeds. The dry climate preserved papyri remarkably well; modern scholars have many thousands of Egyptian texts to read, ranging from religious hymns to royal proclamations to school exercises to a young man's letter complaining about a delayed shipment of beer.

Hieroglyphics were lost to literate use after the fourth century CE, when Egypt converted to Christianity and Coptic (a form of Egyptian written in a Greek-derived alphabet) replaced them. Modern Europeans could not read hieroglyphics until 1822, when **Jean-François Champollion**, working from the trilingual **Rosetta Stone** discovered by Napoleon's expedition in 1799, deciphered the script. After Champollion, three thousand years of Egyptian record became readable again.

## Concept 4 — The Indus Valley civilization

The third river-valley civilization of the Bronze Age developed along the **Indus River** in what is now Pakistan and northwestern India, with extensions along the now-dry Sarasvati. It emerged around 2800 BCE, reached its height around 2000 BCE, and collapsed mysteriously around 1800–1500 BCE. We know less about it than we know about Mesopotamia or Egypt — chiefly because we cannot read its writing. The script remains undeciphered.

### Cities of remarkable planning

The two largest cities of the Indus Valley civilization are **Harappa** in the north and **Mohenjo-Daro** ("Mound of the Dead") in the south. Both had populations of around thirty thousand at their peak. Many other large urban centers — Dholavira, Ganeriwala, Rakhigarhi, Lothal — and over a thousand smaller settlements made up the broader civilization.

What distinguishes the Indus Valley cities from their Mesopotamian and Egyptian counterparts is the *uniformity of city planning*. Harappa, Mohenjo-Daro, and the other major sites were laid out on a similar grid plan, with streets at right angles and standardized brick sizes (Indus bricks were made in a 1:2:4 ratio, the same ratio used in modern bricks). Each city had a separate **citadel** — an elevated, walled section, usually to the west — that probably housed administrative and ceremonial buildings. Below the citadel was a **lower city** of residential and commercial neighborhoods.

The standout feature of Indus Valley urbanism is **drainage and water management**. Houses had private bathrooms with toilets connected to a covered citywide sewer system. The Great Bath at Mohenjo-Daro — a forty-by-twenty-three-foot pool, lined with bitumen-sealed brick, accessible by stairs — is the earliest known public bathing facility. Wells were distributed throughout the cities. The level of sanitary engineering at Mohenjo-Daro in 2000 BCE was not matched in Europe until Roman times two thousand years later, and not matched in the rest of the world until the nineteenth century CE.

### Trade and writing

The Indus Valley civilization was extensively connected to Mesopotamia. Cuneiform tablets refer to a place called **Meluhha** — the Mesopotamian name for the Indus Valley region — from which were imported **lapis lazuli**, **carnelian**, **ivory**, **timber**, and certain marine shells. Indus Valley merchants used distinctive stamp seals, often carved with a bull or other animal and a short line of script, to mark goods. These seals have been found in Mesopotamian sites, and Mesopotamian goods have been found in Indus Valley sites. The Indian Ocean trade route, from the Indus to the Persian Gulf, was operating by 2500 BCE.

The **Indus script** appears on these seals and on a small number of other artifacts. It is not deciphered. About four hundred distinct signs are known. The script is too short on individual artifacts to allow easy decipherment by traditional methods (no Indus equivalent of the Rosetta Stone has been found). Scholars have argued for decades over whether the script encodes a language at all — and if so, whether it represents an early **Dravidian language** (related to the languages of southern India), an **Indo-European language** (related to Sanskrit), or something else entirely. The Indian-American computer scientist **Rajesh Rao** and his collaborators have used statistical methods on the script's symbol frequencies, comparing them to known languages and to non-linguistic sign systems. Their work suggests the script does encode a language. Beyond that, the question remains open. If the Indus script is ever deciphered, our knowledge of the civilization will deepen dramatically — but for now, it remains the great undeciphered system of the ancient world.

### Religion, art, and the absence of grand monuments

The most striking thing about the Indus Valley civilization, by comparison with its neighbors, is what is missing. There are no royal tombs like the Egyptian pyramids or the royal cemeteries of Ur. There are no great temples like the Mesopotamian ziggurats. There are no inscriptions celebrating the deeds of named kings. There are no military scenes in the surviving art. There are no obvious palaces.

What there is: small clay figurines that may represent deities, possibly including a goddess associated with fertility (sometimes connected to the later Hindu goddess Durga). There are seals that depict a horned figure surrounded by animals, sometimes interpreted as a precursor to the Hindu god Shiva in his role as Lord of the Animals. There are shaped stones — *lingams* and *yonis* — that may represent the male and female sexual organs and may have been ritual objects. Burials are typically modest, with a few clay pots; there is no Egyptian-style elaborate funerary ritual. The implications: the religion of the Indus Valley civilization probably had distant continuities with later Hindu practice but operated without the kind of grand state cult typical of Mesopotamia and Egypt.

The political organization is similarly opaque. The uniformity of planning across the Indus Valley sites suggests some kind of central authority. The absence of palaces, royal tombs, and royal inscriptions suggests that the central authority — whatever it was — did not advertise itself the way Mesopotamian and Egyptian kings did. Theories include: a federation of city-states with shared standards; an elite priestly class (suggested by the prominence of the Great Bath as a possibly ritual structure); a more egalitarian, less hierarchical society than its contemporaries. The honest answer is that we do not know.

### Decline

Around 1800 BCE, the Indus Valley cities began to be abandoned. By 1500 BCE, the urban civilization had collapsed. The cause is debated. Possibilities:

- **Climate change.** The monsoon weakened and shifted. The Sarasvati River, which seems to have been a major water source for the western part of the civilization, dried up almost entirely. The agricultural base may have shrunk past sustainable.
- **Tectonic change.** Earthquakes and river-course changes disrupted both irrigation and trade. Mohenjo-Daro shows evidence of repeated flood damage in its later layers.
- **Disease.** Skeletal evidence at Harappa suggests that the late population may have suffered from a wave of infectious disease.
- **Indo-European migration.** Around 1500 BCE, peoples speaking Indo-European languages — calling themselves Aryans, in their own term — migrated from the Eurasian steppe through Iran into northwestern India. The early Indo-European religious texts, the *Rigveda*, describe attacks on walled cities. An older theory held that the Aryans destroyed the Indus civilization. Modern scholarship is more cautious: the Aryan arrival probably overlapped with an already-declining urban system. The Aryans may have been the final blow rather than the primary cause.

A combination of these factors is the most defensible answer. What is clear is that, by 1500 BCE, the cities were empty, the script had stopped being inscribed, and the civilization that had built Mohenjo-Daro was gone. Its successor — the early Vedic culture that would produce the Hindu tradition — emerged in the upper Ganges valley several centuries later. Chapter 6 picks up the thread.

## Concept 5 — Patterns and divergences across the three civilizations

The three civilizations this chapter has tracked emerged independently, in different river valleys, on different continents, in roughly the same window of time (the third and second millennia BCE). They share the structural features named in Concept 1: agricultural surplus, specialization, stratification, organized government, monumental architecture, writing (or, in the Indus case, a script that may or may not be writing). They differ in important ways that the comparison reveals.

**Political form.** Mesopotamia was a system of competing city-states for most of its early history, occasionally unified into short-lived empires (Akkad, Ur III, Babylon). Egypt was a unified kingdom from very early in its history (c. 3150 BCE) and remained unified for most of the next three thousand years, broken only by occasional intermediate periods of weakened central control. The Indus Valley was probably a federation of cities sharing standards but lacking the visible centralized monarchies of its contemporaries — or at least lacking monarchies that advertised themselves through inscription and monumental tomb.

**Geography.** Egypt was geographically isolated by deserts on both sides; Mesopotamia was open to invasion from the surrounding mountains and deserts; the Indus Valley was bounded by the Himalayas to the north and the Indian Ocean to the south but open to the steppe to the northwest. The political-organization differences track the geographic ones. Egypt could sustain a single unified state because it was hard to invade. Mesopotamia could not, because every century brought a new wave of invaders. The Indus Valley's relative isolation lasted until the late second millennium BCE, when steppe invaders finally reached it.

**Religion.** All three were polytheistic, with anthropomorphic gods associated with natural forces. Egyptian religion was relatively optimistic, with an elaborate afterlife and a beneficent cosmic order (*Ma'at*); Mesopotamian religion was relatively pessimistic, with a shadowy afterlife and capricious gods. The Indus Valley religion is opaque to us because we cannot read its texts; what survives suggests a goddess focus and possible continuities with later Hinduism.

**Writing.** Cuneiform and hieroglyphics were both highly sophisticated systems used by specialist scribes. Cuneiform's adaptability to multiple languages made it the lingua franca of the ancient Near East for two thousand years. Hieroglyphics remained Egypt-specific. The Indus script is undeciphered.

**Stratification.** All three were stratified societies, with kings or priestly elites at the top, free commoners (farmers, artisans, merchants) in the middle, and enslaved people at the bottom. Hammurabi's code makes Mesopotamian stratification visible in legal text. Egyptian stratification is visible in burial elaboration and access to the afterlife. Indus Valley stratification is harder to see — possibly because it was less extreme, possibly because we lack the texts that would reveal it.

What the comparison teaches is that *civilization is a category, not a single thing*. The structural features are common because they emerge from the same underlying engineering — agricultural surplus enables specialization, which enables stratification, which enables organized government, which enables monumental architecture and writing. But the specific forms taken by each feature in each region depend on the specific environment, the specific neighbors, the specific moments of conquest and contact, and the specific ideas that happened to be available. Three river valleys; three different solutions to the same fundamental problem of how to organize a city of fifty thousand people.

You can now do five things you could not do before this chapter.

You can name the structural features that distinguish early civilizations from earlier village societies — agricultural surplus, specialization, stratification, organized government, monumental architecture, and (usually) writing — and explain why agricultural surplus is the hinge that makes the others possible.

You can place the three river-valley civilizations of the Bronze Age — Mesopotamia (Tigris-Euphrates), Egypt (Nile), Indus Valley (Indus and Sarasvati) — on a map and a chronology, and you can name the major cities, kings, and dynasties of each.

You can trace Mesopotamian political history from Sumerian city-states (Uruk, Ur, Lagash) through the Akkadian empire of Sargon, through the Third Dynasty of Ur, to Hammurabi's Babylon, and you can describe the writing system (cuneiform) and the religious worldview (polytheism, pessimistic afterlife) that ran through all of them.

You can describe Egyptian civilization as the unified state Mesopotamia mostly was not — pharaonic, geographically isolated, religiously optimistic, and architecturally astonishing — and you can place the major Egyptian eras (Old Kingdom pyramids, Middle Kingdom recovery, intermediate periods of fragmentation, New Kingdom expansion) in their rough chronological windows.

You can describe what we know and what we don't know about the Indus Valley civilization — its remarkable urban planning, its drainage engineering, its undeciphered script, its quiet political organization, its mysterious decline — and you can name the contestable theories about why it collapsed.

The thing to watch for, going forward, is the temptation to treat these three civilizations as the only ones that mattered. They were not. Sub-Saharan Africa, Mesoamerica, the Andes, and East Asia were developing parallel civilizations on their own timetables. The next several chapters tell those stories.

## Key terms

- **Akkadian Empire.** The world's first known empire, founded by Sargon of Akkad around 2334 BCE. Lasted about 150 years.
- **Bronze Age.** The period from about 3500 BCE to 1100 BCE during which bronze (an alloy of copper and tin) was the dominant metal for tools and weapons.
- **City-state.** A politically independent unit consisting of a city and its surrounding agricultural land.
- **Code of Hammurabi.** The Babylonian law code inscribed on stone pillars during Hammurabi's reign (c. 1792–1750 BCE). One of the most complete surviving law codes from the ancient Near East.
- **Cuneiform.** The Sumerian writing system, characterized by wedge-shaped marks pressed into clay tablets. Adapted by many subsequent Mesopotamian peoples.
- **Empire.** A state in which one ruler controls multiple regional powers across a large area.
- **Harappan.** Of or related to the Indus Valley civilization (named for the city of Harappa).
- **Hieroglyphics.** The Egyptian writing system, which combined alphabetic, syllabic, and pictographic signs. Deciphered by Champollion in 1822.
- **Lugal.** The Sumerian word for king.
- **Ma'at.** The Egyptian principle of cosmic order, truth, justice, and balance. Personified as a goddess.
- **Mesopotamia.** The land between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers in modern Iraq.
- **Mohenjo-Daro.** Major Indus Valley city in the south of the civilization. Famous for its drainage system and Great Bath.
- **Nile.** The river that made Egyptian civilization possible. Annual flooding deposited fertile silt across the floodplain.
- **Nomarchs.** Regional governors in ancient Egypt.
- **Papyrus.** Egyptian writing material made from the pith of Nile reeds.
- **Pharaoh.** The Egyptian king. Simultaneously a political ruler, a high priest, and a god.
- **Pyramid.** Massive Egyptian royal tomb. The largest, the Great Pyramid of Giza, was built for Khufu around 2580 BCE.
- **Stratification.** The organization of society into hierarchical classes based on wealth and status.
- **Sumer.** The southern Mesopotamian region where the world's earliest known civilization emerged. Home to Uruk, Ur, Nippur, and other early cities.
- **Uruk.** The largest Sumerian city, with a population that may have approached 50,000 around 3000 BCE.
- **Ziggurat.** A stepped tower of mud-brick that served as the central temple in a Sumerian city.

## Further reading

- **Kriwaczek, Paul.** *Babylon: Mesopotamia and the Birth of Civilization.* 2010. A readable narrative of Mesopotamian history that takes the long view from Sumer to the Achaemenid conquest.
- **Schmandt-Besserat, Denise.** *How Writing Came About.* 1996. The classic argument that cuneiform evolved from Neolithic accounting tokens. Read alongside Glassner's counter-argument for a sense of the debate.
- **Jacobsen, Thorkild.** *The Treasures of Darkness: A History of Mesopotamian Religion.* 1976. Still the best single-volume account of the Mesopotamian religious world.
- **Wilkinson, Toby.** *The Rise and Fall of Ancient Egypt.* 2011. A modern narrative history of pharaonic Egypt by one of the leading current Egyptologists.
- **Ikram, Salima.** *Death and Burial in Ancient Egypt.* 2003. A clear account of mummification, tombs, and the Egyptian afterlife by an Egyptian-trained Egyptologist.
- **Possehl, Gregory.** *The Indus Civilization: A Contemporary Perspective.* 2002. The standard archaeological reference on the Indus Valley civilization, written for a general readership.
- **Wright, Rita P.** *The Ancient Indus: Urbanism, Economy, and Society.* 2010. A focused study of how the Indus cities actually functioned.
