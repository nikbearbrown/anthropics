# Chapter 10 — The Americas in Ancient Times
*Three Continents, Twelve Thousand Years, No Wheel, No Horse, Civilizations on Their Own Schedule.*

## Three suggested titles

- The Americas in Ancient Times: From Beringia to Tenochtitlán
- Civilizations Without Wheels: The Pre-Columbian World
- Olmec, Maya, Aztec, Inca, Cahokia — Five Worlds the Old World Did Not Know

## Chapter overview

By the end of this chapter you will know how the Americas were peopled, how indigenous peoples developed agriculture independently of the Old World, and how a series of complex civilizations rose and fell across Mesoamerica, the Andes, and North America between roughly 1500 BCE and 1500 CE. You will know the **Olmec** as Mesoamerica's earliest complex civilization (c. 1500–400 BCE) — the source of much of the religious and artistic vocabulary later civilizations would inherit. You will know the **Maya** (c. 250–900 CE classical period) — astronomers, mathematicians, calendar-keepers, builders of the great stone cities of Tikal, Palenque, and Copán. You will know **Teotihuacán** — the largest city in the Americas at its height (perhaps 125,000 inhabitants around 500 CE), built on a grid plan whose rulers we still cannot identify. You will know the **Aztec Empire** under the Triple Alliance from Tenochtitlán (c. 1428–1521 CE), and what Cortés actually saw when his army first walked into the Valley of Mexico. You will know the Andean sequence — Chavín, Moche, Tiwanaku, Wari, and finally the **Inca Empire** (c. 1438–1533 CE), the largest indigenous American empire, organized without writing or wheeled transport. And you will know the major North American civilizations — the Adena, Hopewell, and Mississippian mound-builders, including **Cahokia**, the largest city north of Mexico, larger than London at its height. You will know what these civilizations had in common and what made them irreducibly different from the Old World ones we have studied so far.

## Opening case — November 8, 1519

On November 8, 1519, the Spanish conquistador **Hernán Cortés** and his small army — roughly four hundred Spanish soldiers, supplemented by thousands of indigenous allies who hated their Aztec overlords — walked across a long stone causeway over the lake that surrounded the Aztec capital. The capital was **Tenochtitlán**. It had been built on an island in **Lake Texcoco** in the **Valley of Mexico**. It was, at that moment, perhaps the fifth-largest city in the world — population estimates range from 150,000 to 300,000, larger than any European city of the same year except Paris and Constantinople. Its causeways were lined with cypress trees. Its temples rose from terraced platforms to heights that startled the Spaniards. Its market — the great market at **Tlatelolco**, which Cortés's lieutenant Bernal Díaz del Castillo described in his memoirs decades later — held perhaps 60,000 buyers and sellers on any given day.

What followed is one of history's most-recounted destructions. The Aztec emperor **Moctezuma II** received Cortés peacefully. Within months, Cortés had taken Moctezuma hostage. Within two years, Tenochtitlán had been besieged, starved, plagued, and destroyed; perhaps two hundred thousand of its inhabitants were dead by warfare, smallpox, and starvation; the Aztec Empire had been replaced by Spanish colonial administration. The Spaniards built Mexico City on top of the ruins. The temples were dismantled and their stones used for cathedrals. The lake was drained over the next four centuries. Almost everything visible in Tenochtitlán in 1519 was gone within a generation.

This chapter is about the world Cortés saw and helped destroy.

The Americas in 1492 were not, as European writers from Columbus to nineteenth-century historians sometimes implied, an empty continent inhabited by scattered tribes. They were home to two empires (Aztec and Inca) larger than most contemporary European states, dozens of substantial complex societies, hundreds of distinct languages, an estimated population somewhere between 50 and 100 million people. The civilizations they hosted had been developing for at least 3,500 years at the time of contact, in some places much longer. They had agriculture, monumental architecture, mathematics, astronomy, written and quasi-written record-keeping (Maya glyphs, Aztec codices, Inca *quipu* knot-string accounting), complex religion, statecraft, long-distance trade. They did not have certain Old World features we sometimes think of as universal: they did not have **wheeled transport** (the wheel was known but used only as a toy), they did not have **draft animals** (no horses, oxen, or camels — the llama and alpaca were too small for plowing), they did not have **iron metallurgy** (most American metalworking was in copper and gold). These absences mattered for what their civilizations could and could not do. They did not, however, mark these civilizations as primitive. They marked them as different.

This chapter walks the major civilizations of the Americas in their own terms, from the peopling of the continent through the empires Cortés and Pizarro destroyed.

## Concept 1 — Peopling the Americas

The Americas were the last major landmass that humans reached. The traditional model: around 15,000 to 20,000 years ago, during the last glacial maximum, sea levels were 100 meters lower than today; the **Bering Strait** was a broad land bridge — **Beringia** — connecting Siberia to Alaska. Big-game hunters who had been pursuing mammoths and other megafauna across the Eurasian steppe crossed Beringia and entered North America. As the ice sheets melted and an ice-free corridor opened through what is now western Canada, they spread south. Within a few thousand years they had reached the southern tip of South America.

The traditional model is now under revision. Several archaeological sites push the dates back. **Monte Verde** in southern Chile has been dated to at least 14,800 years ago — too early to be reached by an ice-free-corridor crossing. The **Cerutti Mastodon Site** in California has been controversially dated to 130,000 years ago, though most archaeologists are skeptical of this date. **Coastal migration** routes — small groups moving down the Pacific coast by raft and on foot, around the edge of the ice — are now considered likely; the coastlines they would have used are now under water due to post-glacial sea-level rise. **Multiple migrations** are also probable — at least one early wave from northeastern Asia, a later **Athabaskan** wave that became the speakers of Athabaskan languages (ancestors of the Apache and Navajo), and possibly a separate **Inuit-Aleut** migration that brought the peoples of the Arctic.

The **Kennewick Man** — a 9,000-year-old skeleton recovered from the Columbia River in Washington State in 1996 — produced anatomical features more consistent with Asian Pacific Rim populations than with the populations historically presumed to have peopled the Americas. The interpretation generated decades of legal controversy between archaeologists and Native American tribes claiming Kennewick Man as an ancestor. DNA analysis eventually showed that Kennewick Man was genetically related to modern Native Americans (the Umatilla in particular). The bones were repatriated and reburied in 2017. The episode dramatized both the complexity of the peopling story and the political sensitivity of how ancient American remains are studied and treated.

The earliest widespread North American culture was the **Clovis culture** (c. 13,000 years ago), named for the distinctive fluted stone projectile points first identified at Clovis, New Mexico. Clovis hunters pursued mammoth, bison, and other megafauna with these points hafted onto spears. The mammoths and other large American megafauna went extinct around the same time the Clovis people arrived. The role of human predation versus climate change in the extinction is debated and probably plural — likely some of both.

After Clovis, indigenous peoples adapted to every environment in the hemisphere — Arctic tundra, boreal forest, eastern woodland, plains, deserts, tropical rainforest, mountain highlands, coastal mangroves. They developed regionally distinct cultures, languages, technologies, and subsistence strategies. By the time agriculture began to emerge in the Americas, the continents already supported hundreds of distinct hunter-gatherer cultures.

## Concept 2 — The American Neolithic Revolution

The Americas developed agriculture independently of the Old World. The processes were similar: long-term harvesting of wild plants gradually shifted toward deliberate planting; selected individuals of preferred species produced larger and more reliable crops; a few thousand years of selection produced the major domesticated American crops.

The two major centers were **Mesoamerica** (modern Mexico and Central America) and the **Andes** (modern Peru, Bolivia, Ecuador). Smaller independent centers operated in the **Eastern Woodlands** of North America and possibly in **Amazonia**.

**Mesoamerican domestications**: maize (corn), squash, beans, chili peppers, tomatoes, avocados, cacao (chocolate), amaranth, vanilla. Maize is the most consequential. Its wild ancestor, **teosinte**, has small ears with hard inedible kernels; it took perhaps 6,000 years of selective cultivation to produce the maize we recognize today. Once developed, maize spread northward into what is now the United States and southward into the Andes, becoming a core staple across two continents. The combination of maize, beans, and squash — known to the Iroquois as **the Three Sisters** — provides a nutritionally complete diet (maize provides carbohydrate, beans provide protein and lysine, squash provides vitamins) and grows well together in mixed plantings.

**Andean domestications**: potatoes (over 4,000 varieties developed by Andean farmers), quinoa, beans, peppers, peanuts, pineapples, sweet potatoes, manioc (cassava). The potato is the most consequential. After the Columbian Exchange, potatoes would feed the population growth of early modern Europe.

**Animal domestications** were limited and very different from the Old World. The Andes domesticated the **llama** and **alpaca** (small camelids, used for wool, meat, and pack-carrying — but too small for riding or plowing). The Andes also domesticated the **guinea pig** for meat. The Mesoamerican world domesticated **turkeys** and **dogs**. That was essentially it. There were no horses, cattle, sheep, goats, pigs, donkeys, water buffalo, or other large draft animals. The reason was supply: most candidate species had gone extinct in the late Pleistocene, in the same wave of megafauna extinctions that wiped out mammoths, mastodons, ground sloths, and giant short-faced bears. The American civilizations that emerged from the Neolithic Revolution had to work without the animal labor that powered Eurasian agriculture, transport, and warfare. They did. But the absence shaped what was possible.

## Concept 3 — Mesoamerica: Olmec to Aztec

Mesoamerican civilization developed across a continuous region from central Mexico through Guatemala and Honduras. The civilizations that occupied this region across nearly 3,000 years shared a common cultural inheritance: maize-based agriculture, pyramid-temple architecture, a 260-day ritual calendar combined with a 365-day solar calendar, a religious pantheon centered on rain gods, sun gods, and a feathered-serpent figure that recurs under different names across cultures, and the ritual ball game that nearly every Mesoamerican society played in special stone-walled courts.

### The Olmec (c. 1500–400 BCE)

The earliest complex Mesoamerican civilization was the **Olmec**, in the gulf lowlands of modern Veracruz and Tabasco. Their two principal sites were **San Lorenzo** (active c. 1350–900 BCE) and **La Venta** (c. 1200–400 BCE). The Olmec were not the first agricultural society in Mesoamerica — that distinction goes to earlier cultures whose names we do not know. They were the first to produce **monumental art** at scale. The most famous artifacts are the seventeen surviving **colossal stone heads**, basalt sculptures of male faces wearing helmet-like headgear, the largest weighing 50 tons. The basalt was quarried at the Tuxtla Mountains, perhaps ninety miles from the sites where the heads stood; how the Olmec moved 50-ton stone blocks ninety miles without wheels or draft animals is itself a substantial puzzle (probably partial floating on rivers, then rollers and human labor).

The Olmec religious vocabulary — the **were-jaguar**, the **feathered serpent**, deities of maize, rain, sun, fertility — would be inherited by later Mesoamerican civilizations. Their art and architectural conventions would shape the Maya, Teotihuacán, Toltec, and Aztec traditions over the next 1,500 years. The Olmec are sometimes called Mesoamerica's "mother culture" — a useful term, though scholars have refined it: not all later Mesoamerican civilization derives from the Olmec, but enough does that the term is defensible.

### Teotihuacán (c. 100 BCE–600 CE)

In the Valley of Mexico, north of the modern Mexico City, the city of **Teotihuacán** rose to become the largest city in the Americas — perhaps 125,000 inhabitants at its peak around 500 CE. (The population estimate is contested; some recent estimates go higher.) The city was built on a strict grid plan oriented to a particular astronomical alignment. Its main avenue, the **Avenue of the Dead**, was framed by two of the largest pyramids ever built in the New World: the **Pyramid of the Sun** (the third-largest pyramid in the world by volume) and the **Pyramid of the Moon**. The city contained a multistory residential complex housing perhaps 2,000 apartment buildings, a great market, multiple temple districts, and craft quarters specialized in pottery, obsidian-working, and other crafts.

What we do not know about Teotihuacán is, paradoxically, very little. We do not know what its inhabitants called themselves or their city — the name *Teotihuacán* is what the Aztecs called the abandoned ruins almost a thousand years later, in their language: "the place where the gods were created." We do not know who ruled it; the city has produced no surviving images of named individual kings, no royal tombs, no inscriptions identifying rulers. This absence is striking: every other Mesoamerican civilization was loud about its kings. Teotihuacán was either ruled in some way we cannot reconstruct — by a council, by anonymous bureaucrats, by religious authorities who declined personal display — or its rulers' names and images have all been deliberately erased. The city's collapse around 600 CE was at least partly violent — burning of the temple precincts is visible in the archaeological record. Whether the violence came from outside invaders or internal revolt is debated. The city was abandoned over the following century, never resettled.

Teotihuacán's influence radiated across Mesoamerica during its peak. Diplomats and settlers from Teotihuacán reached Maya cities; in 378 CE, a Teotihuacán-affiliated leader appears to have engineered a coup at the major Maya city of Tikal. Teotihuacán pottery has been found across the region. Even after its collapse, the city's architectural and religious vocabulary persisted in later cultures.

### The Classic Maya (c. 250–900 CE)

The **Maya** civilization occupied the lowland forests of the Yucatán Peninsula, Belize, Guatemala, and parts of Honduras and El Salvador. The Maya were not a single political unit. They were a cultural area of dozens of small to medium city-states that shared a language family, a religion, a calendar system, a writing system, and architectural conventions. The major Classical Maya cities included **Tikal**, **Calakmul**, **Palenque**, **Copán**, and **Caracol**. Each had its own dynasty. Each fought wars against its neighbors. They ranged in population from a few thousand at smaller sites to perhaps 100,000 at the largest. Together they may have supported a regional population of millions.

The Maya developed a **writing system** that, after decades of decipherment work in the late twentieth century, can now be read. Maya inscriptions name kings, record the births and deaths of rulers, document wars, mark astronomical events, and proclaim the political genealogies that legitimated dynastic succession. The decipherment, led principally by Yuri Knorozov, Tatiana Proskouriakoff, and Linda Schele, was one of the great intellectual achievements of twentieth-century archaeology and transformed our understanding of the Maya from a vague cosmic priestly civilization (the older view) into a recognizably political culture of competing kingdoms.

The Maya developed sophisticated **mathematics** and **astronomy**. They independently invented the concept and notation of zero (a few centuries after India did, but on the other side of the world, with no possibility of contact). They mapped the cycles of Venus accurately enough to predict its appearances and disappearances centuries in advance. Their **calendar system** combined the **Tzolkin** (a 260-day ritual cycle) with the **Haab** (a 365-day solar year) to produce a 52-year **Calendar Round**; longer time was tracked through the **Long Count**, a numbering system that started from a mythological zero date corresponding to August 11, 3114 BCE and could project unique dates centuries or millennia into the future. (The Long Count cycle of 144,000 days × 13 *baktuns* = 5,125 years was completing on December 21, 2012 CE — the basis of the popular but groundless "Maya prediction of the world's end" of that year.)

The **Classic Maya collapse** (c. 800–900 CE) is one of the great archaeological puzzles. Within a century or two, dozens of major Maya cities of the southern lowlands were abandoned, their populations dispersed. Causes proposed include drought (recent climate research finds evidence of severe multidecadal droughts in this period), warfare, soil exhaustion, peasant revolt against an increasingly demanding elite, and disease. As with most ancient collapses, the answer is probably multiple causes interacting. The Maya did not disappear; their descendants live throughout southern Mexico and Central America today, and their language and religious traditions survived the collapse. What collapsed was the Classic-period system of dynastic city-states, monumental construction, and inscribed stelae. The Postclassic period that followed saw smaller cities, less elaborate inscriptions, and a shift of cultural energy north to the Yucatán peninsula.

### The Toltec and the Aztec

After the collapse of Teotihuacán and the Classic Maya, central Mexico saw the rise of the **Toltec** civilization, centered at **Tula** (active c. 950–1150 CE). The Toltec were warriors and traders; their religious-political culture would be claimed by later Mesoamerican rulers — including the Aztecs — as the source of legitimate kingship. The exact relationship between historical Toltec society and the **Toltec mythology** that the Aztecs and others elaborated is unclear; the Toltecs of legend were ancestors of all kings, the Toltecs of archaeology were a particular post-Teotihuacán society in central Mexico.

The **Aztecs** entered the Valley of Mexico as one of several semi-nomadic groups from the north in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. They were initially mercenaries serving more established city-states. In 1325 CE, by tradition, they founded the city of **Tenochtitlán** on an island in Lake Texcoco at a spot indicated by a divine sign (an eagle eating a serpent on a cactus — still the symbol on the Mexican flag). Within a century they had become the dominant power of the Valley.

In 1428, three Aztec city-states — Tenochtitlán, **Texcoco**, and **Tlacopan** — formed the **Triple Alliance** that historians usually call simply the **Aztec Empire**. Under the Triple Alliance, especially under its dominant member Tenochtitlán, the Aztecs conquered most of central and southern Mexico over the next ninety years. The empire was not a unified administrative state on the Roman or Han model. It was a tribute empire — conquered city-states retained their internal governance but paid tribute (food, textiles, precious goods, war captives, and human sacrifices) to the Triple Alliance.

Aztec religion required **human sacrifice** on a substantial scale. The chief god, **Huitzilopochtli**, was the patron of war and the sun; the sun's daily journey across the sky required human blood to sustain it. Sacrificial victims were captured in war (the so-called **Flower Wars** between Aztec and rival cities were partly performative, designed to capture victims for sacrifice without producing decisive military outcomes); some were obtained as tribute; some were slaves bought at market. Estimates of annual sacrifice numbers vary widely — Spanish accounts give numbers in the tens of thousands per year, which most modern historians regard as inflated; the actual scale was probably much smaller but still considerable. The practice was central to Aztec religious culture and was one of the most disturbing aspects of the Aztec world for the Spanish observers who first encountered it.

When Cortés arrived in 1519, the Aztec Empire was at its territorial maximum and at the same time politically vulnerable. Many subject peoples — especially the **Tlaxcala** confederation, which had managed to remain independent — hated their Aztec overlords; Cortés recruited them as allies. **Smallpox**, introduced by the Spanish, killed perhaps a third of the population during the conquest. By August 1521 CE, Tenochtitlán had fallen. The Triple Alliance was dissolved. Mexico became a Spanish colony.

## Concept 4 — The Andean civilizations

In the Andes — the long mountain range running down the western edge of South America — civilization developed along a different rhythm than in Mesoamerica. The combination of high altitude, sharp altitudinal zonation (each 1,000 meters of elevation produces a different climate and a different set of crops), narrow Pacific-coastal valleys, and difficult overland transport produced civilizations organized around **vertical archipelagos**: a single political unit might control patches of land at multiple altitudes, with the high-altitude pastoralists trading with valley farmers and coastal fishers, all under one polity.

### Chavín de Huántar (c. 900–200 BCE)

The earliest widespread Andean civilization was **Chavín de Huántar**, named for a major ceremonial site in the highlands of central Peru. Chavín produced a distinctive art style — fanged jaguar deities, eagles, snakes, complex mythological scenes — that spread across the Andean region from roughly 900 BCE to 200 BCE. The Chavín influence was probably religious more than political; it spread through pilgrimage and shared ceremonial practice rather than conquest.

### Moche (c. 100–700 CE)

On the northern Peruvian coast, the **Moche** civilization produced some of the most sophisticated metalwork, ceramics, and adobe architecture of the pre-Columbian Americas. Moche pottery is famously realistic — portrait heads, scenes from daily life, sometimes startlingly explicit erotic scenes. Their two great pyramids, the **Huaca del Sol** and **Huaca de la Luna**, are massive adobe structures whose construction required organized labor on a scale comparable to Mesopotamian ziggurats. The Moche were probably organized as a confederation of local lords rather than a unified state.

### Tiwanaku and Wari (c. 500–1000 CE)

In the high altiplano around Lake Titicaca, the city of **Tiwanaku** (in modern Bolivia) emerged as the religious-ceremonial center of a substantial polity. Its monumental architecture — the **Gateway of the Sun**, the great platform of **Akapana** — combines megalithic stonework with iconographic conventions that would influence later Andean cultures including the Inca. To the north, the city of **Wari** (in modern Peru) built a more clearly imperial state, with a network of roads, administrative centers, and storage facilities that prefigured the Inca system. Tiwanaku and Wari coexisted and influenced each other for several centuries; both declined around 1000 CE for reasons probably involving climate change.

### The Inca Empire (c. 1438–1533 CE)

The Inca emerged as a small kingdom around Cuzco in the Andean highlands in the early fifteenth century CE. Under **Pachacuti** (reigned c. 1438–1471 CE) and his successors, they conquered an empire that would eventually stretch from southern Colombia to central Chile — roughly 2,500 miles north to south, the largest indigenous American empire ever, encompassing perhaps 12 million people.

The Inca state was highly centralized. The empire was divided into four administrative quarters meeting at Cuzco; the official name was **Tawantinsuyu**, "the four quarters together." The emperor (the **Sapa Inca**) ruled with semi-divine authority. A class of nobles and priests administered the empire. Conquered peoples were absorbed through a combination of military force, religious incorporation (their local gods were brought into the Inca pantheon, often as subordinates of the Inca sun-god **Inti**), and population resettlement (entire communities could be moved to new regions to break local political networks and serve specific imperial functions).

The Inca had no writing in the conventional sense. They had **quipu** — knotted cords used for accounting, census-taking, and possibly historical narrative. Quipu were worked by specialist record-keepers (*quipucamayocs*) who could encode and read them. Modern decipherment of quipu has made progress (the system is partly numeric, partly possibly linguistic) but is far from complete. The Inca state managed an empire of millions of people without alphabetic writing, using quipu records, an oral bureaucratic culture, and the **royal road system** that connected the empire.

The **royal road** (*Qhapaq Ñan*) was the Inca state's most distinctive infrastructure. It ran along the Andean spine from one end of the empire to the other — perhaps 25,000 miles in total when the network's branches are included. Along it were rest stations (*tampu*) at one-day intervals, suspension bridges across river gorges, and storehouses (*qollqa*) full of food, weapons, and trade goods. **Royal messengers** (*chasquis*) ran the road in relays, carrying messages or quipu records faster than any other premodern communication system in the Americas — perhaps 250 miles per day across mountainous terrain.

The Inca state was famously well-organized. Imperial granaries held food reserves to feed populations during droughts. The state managed agricultural labor through a system called *mita*, in which each community owed a labor levy to the state in exchange for state-provided goods and services. The Inca road system enabled the rapid movement of armies. The agricultural terraces that the Inca built across the steep Andean slopes — many still in use today — increased the productive area of mountainous land enormously.

The Inca Empire was also vulnerable. Its centralization concentrated power in the Sapa Inca and his closest court; a succession dispute could paralyze the empire. In the 1520s, smallpox — introduced into the Americas by Spanish contact in Mesoamerica and Central America — reached the Andes ahead of the Spanish themselves and killed the reigning Inca **Huayna Capac** along with much of his family. A civil war between his sons **Atahualpa** and **Huascar** weakened the empire just as **Francisco Pizarro** arrived with 168 Spanish soldiers in 1532. Atahualpa was captured at Cajamarca; the Spanish demanded a ransom of gold and silver to fill a room (the Aztec ransom story is here, reversed: the Inca actually paid the ransom before being killed anyway); the empire was conquered over the following several years. The pattern of indigenous civilization, smallpox, civil war, and Spanish conquest that had played out in Mexico played out again in the Andes.

## Concept 5 — North America: Adena, Hopewell, Mississippian

North America beyond Mesoamerica was, until contact, occupied by hundreds of distinct indigenous societies. Most were not state-level civilizations in the Mesoamerican or Andean sense. But several substantial complex societies developed, particularly in the **Eastern Woodlands** (the forested region of the eastern United States) and the **Southwest**.

### The mound-builders

In the **Ohio River valley** and surrounding regions, a series of cultures from roughly 1000 BCE to 1500 CE built thousands of earthwork mounds for ceremonial, burial, and probably astronomical purposes. The earliest were the **Adena culture** (c. 1000 BCE–200 CE), who built relatively small burial mounds and elaborate burial objects (effigy pipes, copper jewelry). They were succeeded by the **Hopewell culture** (c. 200 BCE–500 CE), whose monumental earthworks at sites in modern Ohio (the Newark Earthworks, Hopewell Mound Group, Mound City) include geometric enclosures (squares, circles, octagons) covering hundreds of acres, often aligned with astronomical events including the major lunar standstill that occurs every 18.6 years.

After Hopewell came the **Mississippian culture** (c. 800–1500 CE), the most politically complex of the North American mound-building traditions. The Mississippians built large urban centers throughout the Mississippi and Ohio river valleys and the Southeast. Their economic base was intensive maize agriculture (a crop that had moved north from Mesoamerica over the previous millennium and had been adapted to North American climates).

### Cahokia

The largest Mississippian city, and the largest pre-contact city north of Mexico, was **Cahokia**, located across the Mississippi River from modern St. Louis. At its peak around 1100 CE, Cahokia covered six square miles and held perhaps 10,000–20,000 inhabitants — at that time, larger than London. Its central feature was **Monks Mound**, a stepped earthen pyramid 100 feet tall, covering 14 acres at its base — the largest earthwork ever built in the Americas north of Mexico. Around it were dozens of smaller mounds, plazas, residential neighborhoods, and a large wooden astronomical observatory (modern archaeologists call it "Woodhenge"). The city's economy combined intensive maize agriculture, long-distance trade (Cahokia goods have been found from the Great Lakes to the Gulf), and substantial labor mobilization for monumental construction.

Cahokia's collapse began around 1200 CE and was complete by 1350 CE. The reasons probably include drought, deforestation, soil exhaustion from intensive maize agriculture, political factionalism, and possibly conflict with neighbors. By the time European explorers reached the region in the seventeenth century, Cahokia had been abandoned for centuries; the indigenous populations they encountered were not the descendants of Cahokia's builders in any direct political sense, though they may have included Cahokia-descended communities.

### The Southwest

In what is now the American Southwest, a different set of complex societies developed. The **Ancestral Puebloans** (formerly called the Anasazi) built remarkable stone settlements in the Four Corners region — most famously **Chaco Canyon** in northwestern New Mexico (active c. 850–1150 CE) and the **cliff dwellings** of Mesa Verde in southwestern Colorado. Chaco Canyon's major buildings — multi-story masonry "great houses" of hundreds of rooms — were connected to outlying sites by a system of straight roads cut into the desert. The Ancestral Puebloans were sophisticated farmers in an extremely dry environment, using terracing and water management to grow maize, beans, and squash in a region that should not have supported their population. A multidecadal drought beginning around 1130 CE may have triggered their dispersal; their descendants are the modern Pueblo peoples of New Mexico and Arizona.

These North American complex societies were smaller in scale than the Mesoamerican and Andean civilizations and did not develop comparable bureaucratic state structures. They were nonetheless substantial — millions of people living in organized agricultural societies, with monumental architecture, long-distance trade, complex religion, and political institutions that European explorers in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries struggled (and often failed) to understand.

You can explain how the Americas were peopled — the Beringia crossing, the spread south, the early Clovis culture, the megafauna extinctions — and discuss the recent revisions to the standard model based on coastal-migration evidence and sites like Monte Verde.

You can characterize the American Neolithic Revolution as a parallel to the Old World one (independent domestication of distinctive crop packages — maize, beans, squash in Mesoamerica; potatoes and quinoa in the Andes) but with sharply different animal-domestication outcomes (no large draft animals, with downstream consequences for transport, agriculture, and warfare).

You can describe the major institutions of the Aztec Triple Alliance — the tribute empire, the Flower Wars, the practice of human sacrifice, the Tlaxcala alliance that helped the Spanish destroy it — and explain how the empire's structure both supported and ultimately threatened it.

You can describe the Inca Empire — *Tawantinsuyu*, the *Sapa Inca*, the *quipu* record-keeping system, the *Qhapaq Ñan* royal road network, the *mita* labor system, the imperial granaries — and explain how an empire of 12 million people was administered without alphabetic writing.

You can describe the major North American complex societies — the Adena and Hopewell mound-builders, the Mississippian Cahokia, the Ancestral Puebloans of Chaco Canyon and Mesa Verde — and explain how their political and material organization differed from the Mesoamerican and Andean state systems while still constituting substantial civilizations.

The thing to watch for, going forward, is the temptation to treat the Americas as a single area or to read pre-Columbian history through the lens of European contact. The civilizations of this chapter were not preludes to European arrival; they were complete worlds with their own histories. The contact that ended most of them is itself the subject of later chapters.

## Key terms

- **Aztec Empire (Triple Alliance).** The tribute empire of central and southern Mexico (c. 1428–1521 CE), led by Tenochtitlán.
- **Beringia.** The land bridge between Siberia and Alaska that existed during the last glacial maximum, allowing the peopling of the Americas.
- **Cahokia.** The largest Mississippian city (peak c. 1100 CE) and the largest pre-contact city north of Mexico.
- **Chavín de Huántar.** Early Andean civilization (c. 900–200 BCE) whose religious art spread widely.
- **Clovis culture.** The earliest widespread North American hunting culture (c. 13,000 years ago).
- **Hopewell culture.** Mound-building tradition of the Eastern Woodlands (c. 200 BCE–500 CE), known for elaborate geometric earthworks.
- **Huitzilopochtli.** The Aztec patron god of war and the sun.
- **Inca Empire (Tawantinsuyu).** The Andean empire (c. 1438–1533 CE), the largest indigenous American empire.
- **Maize.** The staple grain of Mesoamerica, domesticated from wild teosinte; the key crop of the American agricultural revolution.
- **Maya civilization.** The Mesoamerican cultural area of city-states (Classical period c. 250–900 CE), known for writing, mathematics, and astronomy.
- **Mississippian culture.** North American mound-building civilization (c. 800–1500 CE) centered on Cahokia.
- **Olmec.** Mesoamerica's earliest complex civilization (c. 1500–400 BCE), known for the colossal stone heads.
- **Pachacuti.** The Inca emperor (c. 1438–1471 CE) who began the imperial expansion.
- **Quipu.** The Inca knotted-cord record-keeping system.
- **Qhapaq Ñan.** The Inca royal road network.
- **Sapa Inca.** The supreme ruler of the Inca state, considered semi-divine.
- **Teotihuacán.** Major central Mexican city (c. 100 BCE–600 CE), the largest in the pre-Columbian Americas at its peak.
- **Tenochtitlán.** Aztec capital, on an island in Lake Texcoco; one of the largest cities in the world at its 1519 peak.
- **Three Sisters.** The maize, beans, and squash polyculture system of the Americas.
- **Tlaxcala.** Mesoamerican confederation that allied with Cortés against the Aztecs.
- **Toltec.** Central Mexican civilization (c. 950–1150 CE) that became the legendary ancestor culture of later Mesoamerican kingships.

## Discussion questions

1. The Americas had no large draft animals. The Old World had horses, oxen, water buffalo, and donkeys. How much of the difference between American and Eurasian civilizational trajectories can be attributed to this single factor? Build the case for a strong "yes" and a strong "no." Decide which case is more compelling.

2. The Maya developed sophisticated mathematics, astronomy, and writing without alphabetic script. The Inca governed an empire of 12 million people without writing of any conventional kind. What does this tell you about the relationship between literacy and complexity? Are there ways in which the Maya and Inca solutions had advantages over alphabetic systems?

3. Aztec human sacrifice has been used by some authors to delegitimize the Aztec civilization in popular accounts. Build the strongest case for understanding the practice within Aztec religious-political logic. Then build the strongest case against treating the practice as morally equivalent to comparable Old World atrocities (Roman gladiatorial games, Inquisition burnings, slave-empire violence).

4. The American civilizations developed independently of Old World civilizations and arrived at many similar institutions (cities, monumental architecture, agriculture, religion, hierarchy, imperial conquest). Build the case that these convergences support a universal logic of civilization. Then build the case that the convergence is exaggerated and that the differences are more important.

5. *Cross-chapter synthesis.* The Spanish conquests of the Aztec and Inca empires are the subject of Chapter 19 in some periodizations of this book and a topic this chapter has only briefly mentioned. Based on the structures and vulnerabilities of the Aztec and Inca empires this chapter has described, predict the specific weaknesses Cortés and Pizarro would exploit.

**Project:** *A Commodity's Journey*.

**What you're building this chapter:** Section 8 — whether your commodity existed at all in the pre-Columbian Americas, what filled its role if it didn't, and what its absence might explain.

**The prompt:**

```
Section 8 — The Commodity in the Pre-Columbian Americas.

Chapter 10 covered Olmec, Maya, Aztec, Inca, Cahokia, and the long

Old World forms; rich evidence from Maya glyphs, Inca quipu,
Mexica codices; the constraints of geography (no east-west

Write Section 8 of the commodity biography (~600–1,000 words):

1. **Did my commodity exist in the Americas?** Be honest. Many Old
   World commodities did not — wheat, rice, sugar, coffee, horses,

   Old World analog — corn, potatoes, tomatoes, cacao, tobacco,
```
