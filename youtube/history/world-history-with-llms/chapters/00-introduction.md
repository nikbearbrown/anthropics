## A Note about AI

World history is a discipline that is both expansive and contested. The model is fluent in the broad narratives and casual about whose perspective is doing the narrating. The note examines what that means for a course built to broaden the student beyond a single national or civilizational frame.

The model has read a wide range of world-history scholarship — Eurocentric textbooks, decolonial critiques of those textbooks, regional histories, comparative histories, and contemporary global histories. The fluency is real and useful for breadth. The problem is that the model produces aggregated narratives in which the perspective is invisible — text about, say, the Mongol expansion may read as descriptive while making interpretive choices the field has been actively contesting for fifty years.

Where the model genuinely helps: producing structural overviews of major civilizations and periods (Tang China, the Abbasid Caliphate, the Mughal Empire, the Mali Empire, the Inca state, the Atlantic system), explaining the canonical methods of world history (comparative, connective, world-systems, environmental), surveying major scholarly debates about transitions and turning points, and producing the chronological scaffolding students need before drilling into specific regions or themes.

Where the model does damage: characterizing the conquests, colonizations, and encounters that constitute much of world history in language that subtly carries the inflection of the source whose voice was dominant in its training. The model will produce descriptions of European colonialism that read as descriptive and contain interpretive choices about agency, consequence, and causation that are still actively debated. Its narratives of non-Western histories may flatten internal complexity in ways the field has been working to correct.

A specific failure mode worth naming: the model is particularly fluent in *causal narratives* — explanations of why something happened — that paper over the multi-causal, contested, often unresolved nature of historical explanation. World-historical causation is a field's ongoing argument; the model produces explanations that have the texture of consensus when consensus does not exist.

The rule that covers all three: events and structural overviews from the model; interpretations from named historians with positions you can identify and place; primary sources from voices inside the cultures and periods being described. The discipline's modern practice is to attend to who is speaking. The model has no who, and its prose should be read with that absence in mind.
