# Chapter 19 — States and Societies in Sub-Saharan Africa

**Suggested titles**

- States and Societies in Sub-Saharan Africa
- Salt for Gold: The African Empires of the Niger and the Limpopo
- Mansa Musa's Empire: West Africa, the Swahili Coast, and the Zimbabwean Plateau

**TL;DR.** Between roughly 500 and 1500 CE, three regions of sub-Saharan Africa built state systems whose wealth came from controlling specific commodity flows: West Africa moved Saharan salt for sub-Saharan gold and grew the empires of Ghana, Mali, and Songhai; the East African coast wove Arab and Bantu cultures together into the Swahili city-states that exchanged African gold and ivory for Indian and Chinese goods; the Zimbabwean plateau built drystone capitals at Mapungubwe and Great Zimbabwe on the inland end of that same Indian Ocean network. The trans-Saharan trade in particular reshaped North African politics, carried Islam south, and produced rulers — most spectacularly Mansa Musa — whose wealth was legible from Cairo to Majorca.

---

## Overview

In July 1324 a caravan rode out of West Africa into Cairo. Sixty thousand men, five hundred enslaved attendants each carrying a four-pound gold staff, eighty camels each loaded with three hundred pounds of gold dust. At its head was Mansa Musa, ninth ruler of the Mali Empire, on his way to Mecca for hajj. He stopped in Cairo for three months. He distributed gold to officials, to charities, to ordinary people in the markets. The Mamluk Sultan al-Nasir Muhammad treated him as an equal sovereign. The Syrian scholar al-Umari, working in the sultan's court, would later write that Mansa Musa "is the greatest of the Muslim kings of the Sudan. He rules the most extensive territory, has the most numerous army, is the bravest, richest, the most fortunate, the most victorious over his enemies, and the best able to distribute benefits."

He was also too generous. The gold Mansa Musa gave away in Cairo flooded the Egyptian economy. Inflation in the city — by some accounts — did not return to its previous level for twelve years. A monarch from a region most Europeans had never heard of had moved enough precious metal in a single visit to break a major Mediterranean economy.

That is one image of medieval West Africa. Hold it. Now hold the second image. In the fifteenth century, Portuguese sailors began making landfall on the southern African coast. When they pressed inland to the Zimbabwean plateau, they found stone walls thirty-five feet high, an enclosure 820 feet around, a tower that none of them could explain. The site is what we now call Great Zimbabwe — *dzimba dzamabwe*, "stone buildings" in Shona. The Portuguese decided it must have been built by Phoenicians or Arabs, because they could not believe Africans had done it. They were wrong. Bantu-speaking Shona had quarried, dressed, and stacked the granite, course on course, without mortar, between the eleventh and fifteenth centuries. The capital had been the inland anchor of a trade network that ran from the goldfields of the western plateau to Sofala on the coast, on through the Swahili port of Kilwa, across the Indian Ocean, and into the markets of Gujarat and southern China.

This chapter is about how Africa got from one image to the other — and about all the things going on in between. It covers about a thousand years and an area larger than the United States, China, and Europe combined. Some constraints up front. The written sources for sub-Saharan Africa before the European arrival are mostly external — Arab geographers, Persian travelers, Chinese chroniclers — supplemented by African oral traditions written down centuries later and by the archaeological record. We know things, but our confidence is uneven, and you should expect the chapter to flag where the evidence thins. The mechanisms — geography, trade routes, religious diffusion, succession politics — are clearer than the personalities. We will work the mechanisms.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- Describe the major environmental zones of Africa (Sahara, Sahel, savanna, rainforest) and explain how each shaped the movement of people and goods.
- Trace the Bantu migrations and the diffusion of ironworking across sub-Saharan Africa.
- Explain how Christianity reached North Africa and Ethiopia and why it took different institutional forms there than in Latin Europe.
- Account for the spread of Islam across North Africa and into the Sahel, and identify the role of the trans-Saharan trade in that diffusion.
- Compare the Ghana, Mali, and Songhai empires as a sequence — what each took from its predecessor, what each added, and what brought each down.
- Describe Mapungubwe and Great Zimbabwe as parts of the Indian Ocean trade system, and identify the evidence we have for their internal organization.
- Explain the Almoravid and Almohad movements as cases of the Maghreb-Sahel political feedback loop.

## Opening case — A king who broke an economy by accident

Step into Cairo in late July 1324. The city is the largest in the eastern Mediterranean — perhaps half a million people. The Mamluk sultanate of Egypt is the most powerful Sunni Muslim state in the world; it has just defeated the Mongol II-Khanate at Wadi al-Khazandar and is ruling Syria and the Hijaz. Cairo's economy, a sophisticated mix of Mediterranean trade, Nile agriculture, and Red Sea commerce, runs on a stable gold-silver bimetallic standard. The gold *dinar* is the unit of account.

Mansa Musa arrives. Even for a city accustomed to wealthy pilgrims, the procession is striking. Al-Umari, who interviewed Cairenes who had seen it, wrote that Musa did not at first wish to call on the sultan, fearing he would be required to kiss the sultan's hand. The protocol was eventually negotiated. Mansa Musa agreed to prostrate to God rather than to the sultan, satisfying both monarchs.

What al-Umari is most interested in, however, is the gold. Musa pays for everything in gold. He gives gold to the Cairenes who hosted him; gold to the sultan; gold in alms in the streets. He borrows from local moneylenders during his stay to keep up the spending — and pays them back at extravagant rates on his return.

Twelve years later, al-Umari is still finding people in Cairo who remember the impact. The price of gold in Cairo had fallen by twenty-five percent or more — exactly what you would expect from a sudden injection of gold into the Egyptian money supply — and the Egyptian gold *dinar* had not fully recovered its value when al-Umari was writing.

Now ask the obvious question. Where did Mansa Musa get all that gold? The answer is the Bambuk and Bure goldfields, on the upper Senegal and Niger rivers, controlled — if you take the word loosely — by the Mali Empire. And how did Mansa Musa become the ruler of Mali? The answer involves a young prince named Sundiata Keita who in 1235 defeated a sorcerer-king named Soumaoro Kanté at the Battle of Kirina. And how did Sundiata's empire get its trade routes? The answer involves the older Ghana Empire, which had been controlling salt-for-gold exchange across the Sahara since the eighth century. And how did the Sahara become a trade route in the first place? The answer involves a camel.

That is the chapter. Let's start with the geography that made it all possible.

## Concept 1 — Geography as the first cause

Africa is the second-largest continent. Its environmental zones are arranged, very approximately, in latitudinal bands.

In the north, a strip of Mediterranean climate — wet winters, dry summers, suitable for wheat and barley — runs from Morocco through Tunisia and Libya into Egypt. This is the part of Africa that is, climatically and culturally, part of the Mediterranean world. Phoenicians settled here. Romans ruled here. Latin and later Arabic spread here.

South of that strip is the Sahara — about 3.6 million square miles of desert, almost the size of the United States. It runs from the Atlantic to the Red Sea. For most of its area, settled life is impossible; the desert can be crossed only at oases, and only with an animal capable of moving long distances without water. Until that animal was available, the Sahara worked as a barrier.

The animal arrived in the early centuries CE. Camels — the one-humped *Camelus dromedarius* — had been domesticated in Arabia. Romans, possibly from contacts in Egypt, introduced them into North Africa between the third and fifth centuries. A camel can carry 400 to 600 pounds, walk thirty miles a day, and go a week between drinks. The Sahara stopped being a barrier and became a road.

South of the Sahara is the Sahel — a transition zone of semi-arid grassland about 3,300 miles long and a few hundred miles wide. The word comes from Arabic *sahil*, "shore" — the shore of the sand-sea to the north. The Sahel is where caravans coming south first met agricultural societies, and where the great medieval West African empires built their capitals. Cities like Koumbi Saleh, Timbuktu, Gao, and Djenné are Sahelian cities.

South of the Sahel is the savanna — wetter, more wooded, more biodiverse, the densest belt of human settlement in pre-modern Africa. The Niger River flows through both Sahel and savanna; its annual flood is the agricultural foundation of the empires we are about to discuss. Sorghum, millet, and African rice are the staples.

South of the savanna in West Africa is the rainforest — tropical, dense, and historically the limit of horse cavalry, which could not survive the trypanosomiasis carried by tsetse flies. This is one reason the Mali and Songhai empires expanded north and east rather than south. Their military advantage stopped at the forest line.

In southern Africa, the geography is different. The Kalahari and Namib are real deserts but smaller. The bulk of southern Africa is savanna and grassland, with rivers — the Zambezi, the Limpopo — running east to the Indian Ocean. This is the geography the Bantu would settle into.

A worked example, then, of geography producing politics. The Sahel runs east-west. The Niger flows from the western highlands east toward Lake Chad and then turns south. Goldfields lie south of the Sahel, in the savanna. Saltworks lie deep in the desert. Any state that can sit at the join — controlling the southern end of the Saharan crossings and the northern end of the gold flow — can tax both. Ghana sat there. Mali sat there. Songhai sat there. The geography made the position; the position made the empires.

## Concept 2 — The Bantu migrations: a long, slow takeover

While West African empires were forming in the Sahel, a much older and slower process was reshaping the rest of sub-Saharan Africa. Bantu speakers — originally a small population in the area of modern Cameroon and Nigeria — had been expanding outward for thousands of years.

The mechanism is debated in detail, but the broad outline is clear. Around 3000 BCE, proto-Bantu populations began moving east and south. They carried with them a language family (today, more than 500 Bantu languages from Swahili to Zulu), an agricultural package centered on sorghum and millet (and later, after contact with the Indonesian-influenced east coast, banana and yam), and — crucially — ironworking, acquired around 500 BCE from the Nok culture of central Nigeria.

Iron is the multiplier. An iron axe clears bush three times faster than a stone axe. An iron hoe makes heavier soils cultivable. Iron weapons defeat stone weapons in any extended conflict. By the second century CE, Bantu-speaking groups had reached the Indian Ocean coast in what is now Tanzania and Kenya. By the fifth century they were in southern Africa. The hunter-gatherer populations they encountered — ancestors of today's San and other Khoisan-speaking peoples — were partly displaced, partly absorbed, partly pushed into less hospitable terrain like the Kalahari.

The early Bantu societies were organized along kinship lines without strong centralization. Villages of a few dozen round houses surrounded a livestock pen. Authority was diffuse. Then, beginning around the eighth century, in places where the land could support concentration, things changed. Cattle herds grew. Surplus accumulated. Hierarchies hardened. By the tenth century there were Bantu kingdoms — small, mostly — across the savannas of Central and southern Africa. By the twelfth century there was the Leopard's Kopje culture in what is now southwestern Zimbabwe. By the thirteenth there was Mapungubwe.

We will come back to those polities. First we need to deal with religion.

## Concept 3 — Christianity in Africa: an old and somewhat lost story

Christianity was an African religion before it was a European one. By tradition Saint Mark founded the church of Alexandria in the mid-first century. By the third century, Alexandria was one of the great centers of Christian theology — the home of Origen, of Athanasius, of the school whose allegorical readings of scripture would shape Christian interpretation for a thousand years.

The patterns are worth noticing. When Roman persecution intensified — under Decius in 250, peaking under Diocletian in 303 — Egyptian Christians invented the institution of monasticism. Antony of the Desert (c. 251–356) lived as a hermit in the western desert. Pachomius (c. 290–346) organized the first cenobitic (communal) monasteries in southern Egypt. By 400 there were three distinct monastic traditions in Egypt — eremitic in the north, cenobitic in the south, and a hierarchical abbot-led form in the northwest — and the Egyptian model would be exported, through John Cassian and others, into Latin Europe. Every Benedictine, every Trappist, every cloistered monk in the Western tradition is using a system that was assembled in Egypt before it was assembled anywhere else.

Christianity moved south from Egypt. In the fourth century, the missionary Frumentius — shipwrecked on the Eritrean coast and brought to the royal court — converted King Ezana of Aksum. Aksum, which we met in Chapter 11, would be a Christian kingdom for over a thousand years. When Aksum collapsed in the tenth century, its Zagwe successor under King Lalibela (r. 1181–1221) carved a new capital out of the ground itself: eleven churches at Lalibela, hewn directly from the basalt below the surface, linked by tunnels and trenches. Lalibela renamed the local stream the River Jordan and styled the city "the new Jerusalem." It is among the most ambitious surviving works of medieval architecture anywhere on the planet.

Christianity moved west across the Maghreb. Roman North Africa produced some of Christianity's most influential thinkers. Tertullian (c. 155–220) coined the Latin theological vocabulary. Cyprian of Carthage (d. 258) was martyred during the Decian persecution. Augustine of Hippo (354–430) — born in what is now Algeria, schooled in Carthage, converted in Milan after reading the *Life of Antony* — produced *Confessions*, *The City of God*, and the controversial-but-victorious arguments against the Donatists, the schismatic North African Christians who held that priests who had renounced the faith under persecution could no longer perform valid sacraments. The doctrine that emerged from Augustine's anti-Donatist writings — that the validity of sacraments depends on Christ rather than on the moral state of the priest — is built into Catholic and most Protestant theology to this day. It was forged in Algeria.

In Egypt, Christianity also produced a major schism. The Council of Chalcedon in 451 decreed that Christ was both fully divine and fully human in two natures (the Dyophysite position). The Egyptian church, which had held that Christ's humanity and divinity were inseparable in one nature (Monophysite, or more accurately Miaphysite), rejected the council. The result was the Coptic Church — which has continued in unbroken succession to the present day — and a parallel break that produced the Ethiopian Orthodox Church and the Armenian and Syrian Orthodox Churches. By the eighth century, Coptic Christianity was the established church of Egypt and Nubia.

Then Islam arrived.

**Worked example: Augustine and the Donatists.** This dispute is worth a moment because it shows the African church doing serious institutional thinking. The Donatists asked: if a bishop hands over scripture to Roman persecutors to save his life, and is later restored to his see, are the baptisms and ordinations he performs valid? Their answer was no — a polluted vessel cannot transmit grace. Augustine's answer was that grace flows from God through the office, not the man, and that the alternative would make Christian sacraments perpetually uncertain, since the moral state of any minister is hidden. This is, if you look at it carefully, a structural argument: which model of authority produces a stable institution? Donatism produced a purer church but one where every sacrament was contestable. Catholic doctrine produced a less pure church but one with stable transmission. The Catholic answer won. North African Christianity stayed unified — until the seventh century — and a doctrinal solution invented in Hippo became the Latin West's standard answer for the next sixteen hundred years.

## Concept 4 — Islam in Africa: the second monotheistic wave

The Muslim conquest of Egypt began in 639 and was complete by 642. Carthage fell in 698. By 709 the Umayyad caliphate controlled the entire North African coast. In 711, an Umayyad-Berber army under Tariq ibn Ziyad crossed the Strait of Gibraltar (named for him — *Jabal Tariq*, "Mount of Tariq") and within a few years had conquered most of Iberia.

Conversion was slower than conquest. Coptic Christians remained the majority of Egypt's population well into the tenth century, paying the *jizya* — the tax on protected non-Muslims — and continuing to dominate Egyptian administration even under Muslim rulers. The Berbers (who call themselves Imazighen, "free people") of North Africa converted within a century or so, but their version of Islam was inflected by their own institutions — and they revolted often.

A pattern emerged that would repeat for centuries. A reformist movement would rise in the Atlas Mountains or the desert oases, claiming that the urban rulers of the Maghreb had become decadent and impure. The reformers would unite the desert and mountain Berbers, march on the cities, conquer them, and rule. Within a generation or two the new dynasty would itself become urban and decadent, and a new reformist movement would rise in the desert.

We see this pattern in the Almoravids (eleventh century) and the Almohads (twelfth century). The Almoravid movement began with an Islamic jurist, Ibn Yasin, who settled among the Sanhaja Berbers around 1039 and preached a strict literalist Sunni Islam (the Maliki legal school). His followers — the *al-Murabitun*, "those of the fortified place" — captured the trans-Saharan trade route between Sijilmasa and Awdaghost by 1054, took Fez in 1069, founded Marrakesh as their capital in 1070, and crossed into Iberia in 1086 to support the embattled Andalusian Muslim states against Christian advance. By 1090 they ruled from southern Morocco to Zaragoza on the Ebro. They also, in the same period, captured Awdaghost from the Ghana Empire — the same Awdaghost we will meet again in a moment — and tilted the conversion of Ghana to Islam.

The Almoravids were superseded by the Almohads — *al-Muwahhidun*, "those of the unity" — under Ibn Tumart, a more cosmopolitan reformer from the Masmuda Berbers of the High Atlas. By 1147 the Almohads had taken Marrakesh, by 1160 they ruled the entire Maghreb to Tripoli, and by 1200 they controlled Muslim Iberia. But — as we saw in Chapter 16 — they were broken at the Battle of Las Navas de Tolosa in 1212 by a coalition of Iberian Christian kings, and within decades the empire had crumbled.

The crucial point for sub-Saharan Africa is that the same trade routes that carried these reform movements also carried Islam south. Berber merchants on the trans-Saharan route — Sanhaja from the western Sahara, Tuareg from the central Sahara — were Muslim, and their Muslim networks of credit, contract, and hospitality were extremely useful for trade. African rulers who wanted reliable access to that trade had strong reasons to convert, or at minimum to tolerate.

By the eleventh century, the kings of Ghana were welcoming Muslim merchants in a separate Muslim quarter of their capital, with twelve mosques, salaried imams, and Muslim administrators in the royal court. By the thirteenth century, the rulers of Mali were Muslims themselves. By the fourteenth century, the kings of Songhai were Muslim — though, as we will see, with varying enthusiasm.

The pattern in East Africa was different. Muslim traders settled along the Indian Ocean coast from the eighth century onward, intermarried with the local Bantu population, and produced a hybrid culture: the Swahili. The word comes from Arabic *sawahil*, "coasts." Swahili is genuinely a Bantu language — its grammar and most of its vocabulary are Bantu — but it has absorbed substantial Arabic loanwords, especially in commerce, religion, and abstract thought. The Swahili city-states (Kilwa, Mombasa, Mogadishu, Zanzibar, Sofala) were Muslim cities, and Islam in this region was less an imposition than an ingredient.

In the African interior — the East African Great Lakes, the Congo basin, the Zimbabwean plateau — Islam barely penetrated before the modern era. The trade goods that came inland did so without the religious package that came with them on the coast.

## Concept 5 — Ghana: the first of the Sahelian empires

Now to West Africa proper. The Ghana Empire emerged sometime around the fifth century, founded — according to the oral tradition recorded centuries later — by Diabe Cisse, a leader of the Soninke people of what is now eastern Senegal and western Mali. The kingdom's actual name was Wagadu; "Ghana" was originally one of the king's titles. (Note: this is not the modern nation-state of Ghana, which sits much further south, in what was then the Akan forest.)

Ghana sat at the join. To its north was the Sahara, with the Saharan saltworks at Taghaza producing two-pound slabs of salt mined by enslaved laborers. To its south was the Bambuk goldfield, on the upper Senegal River, where miners sank shafts up to sixty feet deep into gold-bearing seams. Ghana traded salt for gold, in both directions, taking a cut on each transaction. By the eleventh century, Ghana's army numbered around 200,000. Its capital, Koumbi Saleh, was actually two cities: a Muslim quarter for the merchants, with twelve mosques and Quranic schools, and a royal city six miles away, where the king received audiences in a court protected by guard dogs wearing collars of gold and silver.

We have a vivid eyewitness account from al-Bakri, an Andalusian geographer writing in 1067–1068 from his home in Almería. He never visited Ghana, but he interviewed merchants who had. The picture is rich. The king dresses "like a woman" in necklaces and bracelets, with a high cap of gold and a fine cotton turban. His subjects greet him by falling to their knees and sprinkling dust on their heads. Muslims, exempted from this prostration, greet him by clapping. The king's heir is "the son of his sister" — al-Bakri is observing a matrilineal succession system, in which kingship passes to the king's sister's son rather than to his own son. This is not unusual in West African politics; it solves a paternity problem that bothered Mongol khans (see Chapter 18).

Most strikingly, al-Bakri tells us that the king's interpreters, treasurer, and most ministers are Muslims, even though the king himself is not. Ghana's policy was tolerance and integration without conversion: a king who keeps his ancestral religion and the political legitimacy that came with it, while staffing his administration with Muslim functionaries who could read, calculate, and interface with the trans-Saharan trade. It was a good arrangement until it wasn't.

What broke it was the Almoravid expansion. In 1055 the Almoravids took Awdaghost — Ghana's northernmost trading post — and the resulting religious-and-economic conflict pushed Ghana's elite into Islam. By the late eleventh century the king of Ghana was a Muslim. By the twelfth, the empire was producing its own Muslim scholars and pilgrims. But the trade routes had moved. New goldfields opened at Bure on the upper Niger, well beyond Ghana's reach. New Saharan crossings ran through Oualata rather than Awdaghost. Ghana's central position was no longer central.

In the early thirteenth century, the southern Soninke chiefdom of Sosso, under a sorcerer-king named Soumaoro Kanté, broke away and conquered most of what had been Ghana, sacking Koumbi Saleh around 1224. That was the end of the Ghana Empire.

The trade-off named. Ghana shows the costs and limits of broker capitalism. A state can grow rich by sitting between two flows of goods and taxing both. But the position depends on the flows continuing to pass through. When the goldfields move and the desert routes shift, the broker's wealth disappears and the state has nothing else to fall back on. Ghana never built a deep agricultural surplus the way the Nile or the Yangzi did. Its capital was a function of its position. When the position eroded, so did the capital.

## Concept 6 — Mali and Mansa Musa: the mountain peak

What replaced Ghana was Mali — and the transition runs through one of West Africa's great oral epics. The story is preserved in the Mande tradition (where it is called the Sundjata epic), in the seventeenth-century *History of the Sudan* by al-Sadi, and in the work of modern *jeli* (griot) singers who carry it still.

Sundiata Keita was the son of a Mande chief who, the epic says, was lame as a child and not expected to lead. He was sheltered during a Sosso raid and grew up in exile. When Soumaoro Kanté's raids reached the Mande, Sundiata returned, raised an army from the small Mande kingdoms, and at the Battle of Kirina in 1235 — by tradition, after a sorcerous duel in which he discovered the secret of how to kill the otherwise invulnerable Soumaoro — defeated the Sosso. Five years later he took Koumbi Saleh, and the kingdom of Mali was founded.

Sundiata convinced the Mande kings to surrender their title *mansa* (king) to him. He thus became the *mansa* — the religious and secular leader of all the Mande, with the implicit understanding that he held that title by their consent rather than by sacred right. The administrative capital was placed at Niani, in the southern savanna, near the Bure goldfields. The empire ran north to absorb the Sahel trading towns: Oualata, Gao, Timbuktu, and Djenné.

The economy was deeper and more diverse than Ghana's had been. Mali had the trans-Saharan trade — gold and salt and enslaved captives and copper and ivory — but it also had agricultural rainfall. The southern savanna grew sorghum and millet. The Niger and Gambia floodplains grew rice. The Sahelian grasslands grazed cattle, sheep, and goats. The empire produced food and surplus on top of taxing trade.

The Malian *mansas* converted to Islam — but with a striking practical compromise documented by al-Umari. When a *mansa* conquered a gold-producing region and tried to convert the miners, the gold supply collapsed. The miners — practitioners of older religious systems in which gold was tied to land spirits — would simply stop working. The Malian solution: leave the goldfields semi-independent, demand only tribute, and let the producers worship whatever they wanted. The empire tolerated what it could not afford to convert.

By 1312, Mansa Musa came to the throne. He had inherited an empire at its peak. His pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324–1325 was the public announcement of that fact. We have already looked at what it did to the Egyptian gold market. What we have not yet looked at is what it did at home.

Mansa Musa returned to Mali with a retinue of Muslim scholars and architects — most notably the Andalusian polymath al-Saheli, who designed mosques in Gao and Timbuktu. The Djinguereber Mosque in Timbuktu, built in 1327 from mudbrick reinforced with palm-wood beams, still stands. Timbuktu became, over the next two centuries, a center of Islamic scholarship comparable in its way to Córdoba or Cairo. By the sixteenth century, the city had three universities (Sankore, Djinguereber, Sidi Yahya), tens of thousands of manuscripts, and a manuscript trade that was — according to Leo Africanus, writing in 1526 — "more profitable than any other branch of business."

So firmly was Mansa Musa's reputation established that, half a century after his death, the Catalan mapmaker Abraham Cresques included him in *The Catalan Atlas* of 1375, depicting an enthroned African monarch holding a gold nugget the size of a fist, with the caption: "This king is the richest and most noble lord of all this country by reason of the abundance of gold taken out of his land." The map is now in the Bibliothèque nationale de France. It is the first reasonably accurate European depiction of the West African interior, and it is, essentially, a Mansa Musa portrait.

Mali declined in the late fourteenth century through a combination of weak rulers, succession civil wars, raids by the Tuareg from the north, and revolts by the Mossi from the south. In 1438, the *mansa* abandoned Gao and Timbuktu. The empire had broken into pieces. The pieces were soon picked up by Songhai.

## Mid-chapter checkpoint

You have, so far, three things. First, a geography that arranges environmental zones in latitudinal bands, with the Niger river system serving as the agricultural and commercial spine of West Africa and the camel turning the Sahara into a navigable sea. Second, a slow Bantu expansion from West-Central Africa that, by the second millennium CE, has filled most of sub-Saharan Africa with iron-using agricultural societies organized first by kinship and later, in places, by hierarchical chiefdoms. Third, two waves of monotheistic religion — Christianity from the Mediterranean and Islam from the same — each finding particular forms in African contexts and each interacting with African polities differently in different regions.

Hold those. We turn now to the southern continent and the Indian Ocean trade.

## Concept 7 — Mapungubwe and Great Zimbabwe: the inland end of the Indian Ocean

While the West African empires were running their salt-for-gold trade across the Sahara, an entirely different trade system was operating across the Indian Ocean. The east African coast — the Swahili coast — had been linked to Arabia, India, and (less directly) China for centuries. The system worked on the monsoon: the southwest monsoon (April to September) carried ships from East Africa and Arabia toward India; the northeast monsoon (November to March) carried them back. A merchant in Mogadishu or Kilwa could time a voyage to Gujarat to take advantage of both winds and be home within the year.

By the tenth century, Arab geographers like al-Masudi (writing 915) were describing a Swahili coast that exported gold, ivory, ambergris, and leopard skins, and imported pottery, glass, and finished cloth. By the thirteenth century, the Swahili city-states had taken on a distinctive form: Bantu-speaking populations using the Swahili language, Muslim in religion but with significant African ancestor-veneration practices retained, oriented toward the sea rather than the interior, organized as independent city-states (Mogadishu, Lamu, Mombasa, Pemba, Zanzibar, Kilwa, Sofala) that competed with each other for trade.

What the Swahili exported, in part, came from the deep interior. And the deep interior, it turns out, had been building states.

Mapungubwe — at the confluence of the Limpopo and Shashe rivers, near the modern boundary of South Africa, Zimbabwe, and Botswana — flourished from roughly 1075 to 1300. It grew out of an earlier Iron Age culture called Leopard's Kopje. The site had cattle (the traditional southern African form of wealth), terraced agriculture on the hillsides, cotton cultivation, weaving, and — beginning in the tenth century — gold mining. Miners sank shafts up to ninety feet deep, cracking rock with alternating fire and water and breaking the gold-bearing fragments out with iron wedges.

Archaeology gives us one of the clearest pictures of an African early state we possess. The settlement is hierarchical: commoners in mudbrick-and-thatch houses on the lower ground, district leaders on small hilltops at the periphery, and the king in a stone enclosure on top of Mapungubwe Hill itself, a sandstone outcrop 98 feet high and 328 feet long. Royal wives lived in separate dwellings. The whole royal complex was surrounded by a wooden palisade. This is the first clearly class-stratified society we can identify south of the Zambezi.

The trade evidence is striking. At Mapungubwe, archaeologists have found Indian glass beads, fragments of brightly colored Indian textiles, and Chinese celadon pottery. The most famous artifact is the Mapungubwe gold rhinoceros — a small wooden carving covered in beaten gold sheets, recovered from a royal grave. The rhinoceros went into the Mapungubwe royal burial. Chinese porcelain came in via Sofala, on the Indian Ocean coast. The connections were transcontinental.

Mapungubwe declined sharply at the end of the thirteenth century, very likely due to a regional cooling-and-drying episode that historians have associated with the early phase of what would become the Little Ice Age. As pasturage and crops failed, the population dispersed. The center of southern African civilization shifted north, onto the Zimbabwean plateau, where rainfall was more reliable. There, the Shona — Bantu-speaking pastoralists who had been on the plateau for centuries — began building Great Zimbabwe.

Great Zimbabwe is the inland anchor of the Indian Ocean trade for the period from roughly 1250 to 1550. It is enormous. The site covers about 1,700 acres. The Hill Complex, perched on a granite outcrop, is the oldest part. The Great Enclosure, in the valley below, has drystone walls thirty-five feet high and seventeen feet thick, an outer perimeter of 820 feet, and an interior conical tower thirty feet high whose purpose is unknown — possibly a granary, possibly a ritual structure, possibly both. Between the two are the Valley Ruins, residential areas with circular mud houses up to thirty-two feet across and twenty feet high. The total population, at peak, was perhaps eighteen thousand.

It was built by the Shona. There is no archaeological mystery about this, despite a long colonial-era literature that tried, against evidence, to attribute Great Zimbabwe to Phoenicians, Sabaeans, or some other non-African builder. The pottery sequences, the burial practices, the linguistic evidence, and the architectural continuity with smaller Shona stone constructions across the plateau all point unambiguously to a Bantu, Shona-speaking civilization. The colonial denial — Cecil Rhodes's Rhodesia made this denial more or less official policy — was a political choice, not an archaeological one.

What was Great Zimbabwe doing? Trading. The capital sat at the head of the Sabi River valley, the natural route from the western goldfields of the plateau to Sofala on the coast. Zimbabwean gold reached Kilwa, where it underwrote that city's wealth (Kilwa, by the fourteenth century, was striking its own coinage). Ibn Battuta, when he visited Kilwa in 1331, was impressed enough to write that it was "one of the most beautiful and well-constructed towns in the world." Kilwa's wealth came from Zimbabwean gold passing through Sofala. Great Zimbabwe's wealth came from selling that gold.

The internal organization of Great Zimbabwe is harder to reconstruct. We have no written sources from the inhabitants. The Shona oral tradition, recorded centuries later, identifies a chiefly hereditary ruler advised by senior men. Cattle herd size correlated with status, and herd size correlated in turn with the number of wives and dependents a man could support — which meant labor, which meant productive capacity, which meant more cattle. The king had no standing army, which suggests rule by consensus and ritual authority rather than coercion. The grandeur of the stone buildings tells us there was political authority capable of organizing labor at scale; the absence of an army tells us that authority was not principally military.

Great Zimbabwe declined in the mid-fifteenth century, again likely due to ecological pressure — overgrazing, soil exhaustion, possibly drought — and trade routes that had begun to favor more northerly capitals. Its successor, the Mutapa state under Mwene Mutapa Nyatsimba Mutota, built its own stone capital further north on the plateau and continued the gold trade until Portuguese disruption in the sixteenth century broke it.

## Concept 8 — Songhai: the third Sahelian empire

Back to West Africa for the conclusion. As Mali declined in the early fifteenth century, the Songhai people of the middle Niger took the opportunity to assert independence. Their rebellion was led by Sunni Ali (r. 1464–1492), who built the Songhai Empire from the rubble of Mali.

Sunni Ali was an exceptional military commander. He inherited a small Songhai kingdom centered on Gao and, over twenty-eight years, expanded it to control the entire middle Niger from the Atlantic-facing forest line in the southwest to deep into the Saharan north. He took Timbuktu in 1468 and Djenné in 1473. His army combined Songhai cavalry with a fleet of war canoes that could move troops along the Niger faster than rival armies could move on land. He campaigned almost continuously.

He also alienated the Muslim establishment. Sunni Ali was a nominal Muslim — he had to be, to rule a Muslim merchant economy — but he was not pious in any way the Timbuktu *ulama* could recognize. He seems to have continued to practice older Songhai religious rites in private. He treated rebellious religious scholars, particularly in Timbuktu, with public violence; al-Sadi's *History of the Sudan*, written in the seventeenth century by a Timbuktu chronicler who hated him, calls him "a great oppressor and notorious evildoer" who "tyrannized the scholars and holy men, killing them, insulting them, and humiliating them."

Sunni Ali drowned in 1492 — possibly assassinated, possibly accidentally — and his son was overthrown a year later by his general Muhammad Touré, known to history as Askia Muhammad. Askia Muhammad reversed Sunni Ali's religious policy. He went on hajj in 1496, returned with the title of caliph of Sudan from the Abbasid shadow caliph in Cairo, restored the Timbuktu *ulama* to influence, and made Songhai a formally Islamic state on the model of Mali under Mansa Musa.

Songhai under Askia Muhammad and his successors lasted until 1591, when a Moroccan army equipped with arquebuses crossed the Sahara and defeated the Songhai cavalry at the Battle of Tondibi. Cavalry beats infantry, in general, but cavalry does not beat firearms. The Sahelian empire system — Ghana to Mali to Songhai — ended that day.

The trade-off named. The whole West African imperial sequence (Ghana → Mali → Songhai, 800–1591) is built on controlling the trans-Saharan trade. As long as gold and salt move primarily through the Sahara, the empires of the Niger bend can capture rents on the flow. When that flow shifts — because Atlantic trade routes from West African ports to European ships become competitive in the late fifteenth century, because firearms change the military balance, because the goldfields move — the system breaks. By the seventeenth century, sub-Saharan gold goes to Lisbon and Amsterdam by ship, not to Cairo by camel. The imperial Sahel falls apart. What replaces it is something quite different — a coastal trade system that includes, increasingly, a transatlantic slave trade — and that we will pick up in Chapter 21 and beyond.

## Synthesis

Pull back. What did medieval Africa actually do?

It assembled state systems on top of three different economic foundations. In West Africa, salt-for-gold trans-Saharan trade. In southern Africa, gold-for-luxuries Indian Ocean trade. In northeast Africa, a Christian state system anchored in the Ethiopian highlands and the long Coptic continuity in Egypt. Each system had its own internal logic, its own characteristic political form, its own relationship with the wider Afro-Eurasian world.

It produced, in the process, some of the medieval world's largest cities (Koumbi Saleh, Timbuktu, Djenné, Kilwa, Mapungubwe, Great Zimbabwe), most ambitious architecture (the rock-hewn churches of Lalibela, the Djinguereber Mosque, the Great Enclosure), and most far-flung trade networks (gold from the Bambuk to Egypt; Chinese celadon to Mapungubwe; Indian beads to the Limpopo).

It carried two world religions through their own internal evolutions — Coptic and Ethiopian Christianity diverging from the Latin and Byzantine forms after Chalcedon, Maliki Sunni Islam working out its relation to West African political custom under successive *mansas* and *askias*. African religious history is not a footnote to other people's; it is the main text in its own right.

It also did not produce — and it is worth being clear about this — a unified African state, a continental writing system, or a transcontinental military power that mattered outside its own region. The West African empires were big but did not interfere with North African politics. The Swahili city-states were rich but did not consolidate into a single Swahili polity. Great Zimbabwe was monumental but did not export its model. Africa in the medieval period is a story of regional florescence with limited integration across regions.

What set the stage for what came after — the dramatic and brutal transformation of African economic geography from the sixteenth century onward — was the combination of three things: (1) the partial atrophy of the trans-Saharan trade as Atlantic shipping routes opened, (2) the growing European demand for African goods and labor, and (3) the breakdown of the Sahelian empire system after Tondibi. Each is a story we will return to in later chapters.

For now, hold the image we started with. Mansa Musa in Cairo, distributing gold so freely that he broke the local economy. Pull the camera back. Behind him are the Bambuk goldfields. Behind the goldfields are the Soninke and Malinke villages whose miners pull the metal from the ground. Behind the villages is the Niger River, flooding once a year, carrying sorghum and rice from the savanna to the Sahel. Behind the Niger is the geography of a continent, arranged in bands. The wealth in the *mansa*'s saddlebags is not personal. It is the visible upper layer of an enormous, mostly invisible economic system that linked at least three continents and that ran for several hundred years before anyone in Cairo, Cordoba, or Catalonia knew quite what they were dealing with.

A last sentence. Medieval Africa was not waiting to be discovered. It was already discovered, by itself.

## Chapter summary

Africa's geography (Mediterranean coast, Sahara, Sahel, savanna, rainforest in the north; Kalahari and savanna in the south) and its great rivers (Niger, Limpopo, Zambezi, Nile) shaped both its agriculture and its trade. The introduction of the camel in the early centuries CE turned the Sahara from a barrier into a road and allowed the Sahelian-Saharan exchange system to develop.

Bantu-speaking peoples expanded from a homeland in modern Cameroon-Nigeria across most of sub-Saharan Africa over several thousand years, carrying ironworking and an agricultural package that supported, eventually, Iron Age states across central, southern, and East Africa.

North Africa was Christianized between the second and seventh centuries, producing major theological centers at Alexandria, Carthage, and Hippo, the institution of monasticism, and the Coptic and Ethiopian Orthodox traditions that survived later Islamic conquest. Islam reached North Africa with the Arab conquests of the seventh century, spread south across the Sahara via Berber merchants, and was adopted by the elite classes of Ghana (eleventh century), Mali (thirteenth century), and Songhai (Askia Muhammad onward), often with significant accommodation to existing African religious practices.

The Ghana Empire (c. 500–1240) controlled the western trans-Saharan trade in gold and salt and was succeeded by the Mali Empire (c. 1235–1438), founded by Sundiata Keita and most famously ruled by Mansa Musa, whose 1324 hajj demonstrated Mali's gold wealth across the Mediterranean. Mali was succeeded by the Songhai Empire (c. 1464–1591), built by Sunni Ali and Islamized under Askia Muhammad, ending at the Battle of Tondibi when Moroccan firearms defeated Songhai cavalry.

In southern Africa, Mapungubwe (c. 1075–1300) and its successor Great Zimbabwe (c. 1250–1550) built drystone capitals on the gold trade running from the Zimbabwean plateau through Sofala to the Swahili city-states of the Indian Ocean. Both fell, probably, to ecological pressure as much as political conflict. Their successors continued the gold trade until Portuguese disruption in the sixteenth century.

In the Maghreb, the Almoravid (1040s–1140s) and Almohad (1120s–1260s) reform movements illustrated a recurring pattern of mountain or desert reformist movements toppling urban dynasties, ruling for a generation or two, and being toppled in turn. Their main effect on sub-Saharan Africa was the further Islamization of the western Sahel.

## Key terms

*Sahel* — semi-arid grassland belt south of the Sahara; from Arabic *sahil*, "shore."

*Trans-Saharan trade* — caravan-based exchange of West African gold and enslaved captives for Saharan salt and North African manufactures; possible only after camels arrived.

*Mansa* — Mande title for the kings of Mali, used in the imperial sense from Sundiata onward.

*Askia* — Songhai title for emperors after Askia Muhammad; the dynastic name of Songhai's Islamic period.

*Coptic* — the Egyptian Christian church; specifically the Miaphysite tradition that rejected the Council of Chalcedon (451).

*Almoravid* — Sanhaja Berber reformist movement (1050s–1140s) that ruled the Maghreb and Iberia and pressed south on Ghana.

*Almohad* — Masmuda Berber reformist movement (1120s–1260s) that displaced the Almoravids; broken at Las Navas de Tolosa (1212).

*Drystone* — masonry without mortar, characteristic of Mapungubwe and Great Zimbabwe.

*Swahili* — Bantu language and culture of the East African coast, with significant Arabic borrowings; from Arabic *sawahil*, "coasts."

*Mapungubwe* — c. 1075–1300 southern African polity at the Limpopo-Shashe confluence; first known class-stratified state south of the Zambezi.

*Great Zimbabwe* — c. 1250–1550 Shona capital on the Zimbabwean plateau; largest pre-modern stone construction in sub-Saharan Africa.

*Bambuk and Bure goldfields* — Senegal River and upper Niger goldfields supplying the Sahelian empires.

*Donatist controversy* — North African Christian schism over the validity of sacraments performed by clergy who had renounced the faith under Roman persecution; resolved against the Donatists by Augustine of Hippo.

## What would change my mind

If new archaeological evidence demonstrated that the gold trade through Mali in the fourteenth century was substantially smaller than current estimates suggest — say, an order of magnitude less than the volumes implied by Mansa Musa's Cairo distributions — much of this chapter's account of Mali's relative weight in the world economy would need revision. Such evidence might come from refined analysis of West African gold isotopes in surviving Mediterranean coinage.

## Still puzzling

I do not fully understand why Great Zimbabwe declined. The dominant explanations — drought, soil exhaustion, shifting trade routes — are plausible but underdetermined; we have no chronicles, very few datable artifacts from the final phase, and the kingdom's transition into its Mutapa successor is still being worked out by archaeologists. The structural argument that gold-trade capitals on the inland end of long supply chains are inherently fragile when those chains shift seems right to me, but I do not yet have a clean test case.
