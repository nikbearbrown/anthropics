## Chapter overview

By the end of this chapter you will know how the African continent — the world's second-largest, the cradle of *Homo sapiens*, the source of much of what later civilizations elsewhere borrowed — developed in the period between roughly 3000 BCE and 1000 CE. You will know the geographic diversity that shaped that development: Mediterranean coast and Saharan desert in the north, Sahel grasslands on the desert's southern edge, equatorial rainforests in the center, savanna across most of the south, and the Ethiopian highlands rising above it all. You will know how African agriculture developed in multiple independent centers and how a millennia-long set of population movements — the **Bantu migrations** — spread Bantu-speaking peoples and their farming, ironworking, and language across most of sub-Saharan Africa. You will know the **Kingdom of Kush** in Nubia, which traded with and was sometimes conquered by Egypt and which, in the eighth century BCE, briefly conquered Egypt itself. You will know how **Carthage** dominated the Mediterranean coast of North Africa until the Romans destroyed it. You will know the **trans-Saharan trade routes** that, by the early centuries CE, were already moving gold, salt, and ideas across the world's largest desert. The chapter is the corrective to a long-standing distortion: the notion that "African history before colonization" was somehow brief, simple, or unimportant. It was none of those things.

## Opening case — Napata, 728 BCE

In the year 728 BCE, a Nubian king named **Piye** marched his army north from his capital at **Napata**, in what is now northern Sudan. Within months, he had defeated the rival Egyptian dynasties of the Nile delta and entered the city of Memphis, the ancient Egyptian capital. He founded what later Egyptian historiography would call the **25th Dynasty** — the dynasty of the so-called "Black Pharaohs." Piye and his successors **Shabaka**, **Shebitku**, **Taharqa**, and **Tantamani** would rule Egypt for nearly a century, until the Assyrian invasion of 671 BCE finally drove them back south.

The 25th Dynasty's existence has been embarrassingly underrepresented in popular accounts of ancient Egypt. The reasons are partly historiographic — nineteenth-century European Egyptologists were uninterested in the African southern frontier — and partly material: most of the Nubian capitals lay in modern Sudan, in regions less accessible to European archaeology and, for much of the twentieth century, less politically stable. The Nubians themselves preserved a tradition that distinguished sharply between the periods when they ruled Egypt and the periods when Egypt ruled them; they viewed their period of dominance as the moment when the gods favored Kush over its northern neighbor.

Piye's reign matters for this chapter because it overturns the most common popular distortion of African ancient history: that Egypt was the only African civilization, that the rest of the continent was peripheral, that history south of the Sahara begins with the European arrival in the fifteenth century CE. Each of these claims is wrong. Egypt's southern neighbor was its frequent rival, occasional conqueror, and continuous trading partner for nearly three thousand years. Bantu-speaking peoples spread agriculture and ironworking across most of the continent's southern half across two thousand years of migration. Carthage, on the North African coast, was for several centuries the dominant power of the western Mediterranean. Trans-Saharan trade routes operated from at least the early centuries CE. The kingdoms of Aksum (in Ethiopia), Ghana (in West Africa), and others would emerge in the first millennium CE and shape the later African story.

This chapter is the African ancient world on its own terms.

## Concept 1 — The geographic diversity of Africa

Africa is enormous. The continent spans about 5,000 miles north to south and nearly as much east to west. It is large enough to contain China, India, the United States, and most of Europe combined and still have room left over. The continent's climate zones run roughly horizontally, in symmetric bands above and below the equator: temperate Mediterranean coast, hyperarid desert, semi-arid Sahel, tropical savanna, equatorial rainforest, tropical savanna, semi-arid Kalahari/Namib, temperate Cape coast.

The major regions:

**Mediterranean coast and Atlas Mountains**: the strip from modern Morocco to Egypt. Mediterranean climate. Linked culturally and economically to the rest of the Mediterranean world for the entire historical period. Hosted Carthage, Roman provinces, the Vandals, the Byzantine Exarchate, and eventually the Islamic conquest.

**The Sahara Desert**: the world's largest hot desert, about 3.5 million square miles. The Sahara was greener several thousand years ago — during the **African Humid Period** (roughly 11,000–5,000 years ago), the region supported lakes, rivers, and grazing populations, with rock art across what is now the central Sahara depicting cattle, giraffes, and human figures. Around 5,000 years ago, the African monsoon weakened and the Sahara dried. The desiccation forced populations to retreat to the Nile valley (where they fed the rise of Egyptian civilization) or south into the Sahel and savanna.

**The Sahel**: the semi-arid grassland strip just south of the Sahara, running from Senegal to Sudan. The Sahel was the long-distance trade route that connected the Sahara's southern edge to West Africa and East Africa; the great medieval West African empires of Ghana, Mali, and Songhai (Chapter 19) would arise here.

**The equatorial rainforest**: centered on the Congo Basin, with a similar belt extending through West Africa. Dense tropical forest. Difficult terrain for state-building (no large rivers running through cleared landscape, dense undergrowth, endemic diseases including malaria and trypanosomiasis that kept large livestock out). But productive for the small-scale agricultural and foraging societies that adapted to it.

**The savanna**: vast grasslands across much of the southern half of the continent. Productive for both pastoralism and agriculture. Most of the major sub-Saharan civilizations of the historical period have been on or near savanna lands.

**The East African Rift Valley**: the great geological tear running from the Red Sea south through East Africa. Volcanic, mountainous, lake-strewn. The rift includes the Ethiopian highlands (whose elevation produces a temperate climate within the tropics) and the lakes (Tana, Victoria, Tanganyika, Malawi) that have been population centers for thousands of years.

**The Ethiopian highlands**: high-elevation plateau rising from the Rift Valley. Temperate climate. Home to one of Africa's earliest centers of independent agriculture (teff, ensete, coffee), the kingdom of Aksum (covered in Chapter 13), and the Ethiopian Christian tradition.

**Southern Africa**: the temperate-zone band south of the savanna. Includes the Kalahari Desert (home of the San), the Drakensberg Mountains, and the Cape region (where Khoisan peoples lived for millennia before the Bantu arrival from the north).

This diversity matters for the chapter's argument. African societies were not a single thing because African environments were not a single thing. The civilizations that grew up along the Nile and on the Mediterranean coast were directly continuous with the civilizations of the rest of the Mediterranean basin. The civilizations that grew up in the savanna and rainforest had their own logics, shaped by their own environments, their own crops, and their own constraints. A history of "Africa" that lumps all of these together is a history of geography, not a history of human societies.

## Concept 2 — African origins of agriculture and the Bantu migrations

Africa was one of the world's centers of independent agricultural origin. Several distinct sub-Saharan agricultural systems developed without obvious diffusion from the Near East:

- **The West African Sahel**, where peoples domesticated **pearl millet**, **sorghum**, and **African rice** by perhaps 5,000 BCE.
- **The Ethiopian highlands**, where peoples domesticated **teff** (the grain still used for the Ethiopian flatbread *injera*), **ensete** (a banana-relative whose corm provides starch), and **coffee** (whose wild ancestor still grows in highland Ethiopia).
- **The forest-savanna boundary** of West Africa, where peoples domesticated **yams**, **oil palm**, and several other tubers and tree crops.

By around 3,000 BCE, agriculture was widely practiced across most of Africa where the environment permitted. Iron metallurgy emerged in sub-Saharan Africa earlier than was once thought — sites in modern Nigeria, Cameroon, and the African Great Lakes region show iron production by 1,000 BCE or earlier. The traditional model of iron technology spreading from the Near East has been substantially revised; sub-Saharan iron production may have been independently invented.

### The Bantu migrations

The defining demographic event of African ancient history was the **Bantu migrations** — a slow, multi-millennial expansion of Bantu-speaking peoples from a homeland in what is now the Nigeria-Cameroon border region across most of sub-Saharan Africa.

The original Bantu speakers were Neolithic farmers who, around 3,000 BCE, lived in the equatorial forest-savanna boundary of West Central Africa. They cultivated pearl millet, yams, and oil palm. They had iron technology. As climate change opened a corridor of grassland through what had been dense rainforest (the **Sangha River Interval**), Bantu-speaking groups began moving south and east. The expansion proceeded in waves over the next 3,000 years. By the early centuries CE, Bantu speakers had reached the East African Rift; by 1000 CE, southern Africa.

The mechanism was not conquest in the conventional sense. It was **gradual demographic expansion**. Small groups of Bantu farmers moved into territories occupied by hunter-gatherer or pastoralist populations. The Bantu had agriculture; they could support denser populations than the foragers they encountered. Over generations, the Bantu population in any region grew faster than the foragers'; intermarriage gradually absorbed the original populations into the Bantu cultural-linguistic system. In some regions — particularly the Kalahari and the southern Cape — pre-Bantu populations (the ancestors of the Khoisan) survived as distinct cultures into recent times.

The evidence for the migrations is principally **linguistic**. The roughly 600 Bantu languages spoken today across sub-Saharan Africa share a common ancestor (proto-Bantu) and have diverged in patterns that linguists can map back through time. Comparative reconstruction of vocabulary related to agriculture, ironworking, and material culture reveals the timing and direction of various migration waves. **Archaeological evidence** — pottery styles, iron-smelting sites, agricultural remains — provides supporting confirmation.

By the time Bantu-speaking peoples reached southern Africa around 1000 CE, they were carrying agriculture, cattle herding, ironworking, and a recognizably Bantu cultural-religious complex. The southern African civilizations that would emerge in the next several centuries — culminating in **Great Zimbabwe** (1100–1450 CE, covered in Chapter 19) — were Bantu polities. The ethnographic-linguistic map of modern Africa, in which Bantu languages dominate everything south of the Sahel, is the long aftermath of the Bantu migrations.

## Concept 3 — The Kingdom of Kush

The longest-running African civilization outside Egypt was the **Kingdom of Kush**, in what is now northern Sudan along the upper Nile. Kush emerged around 2400 BCE and survived in various political forms until 350 CE. For three thousand years it traded with Egypt, was sometimes ruled by Egypt, sometimes ruled Egypt, and developed a distinctive civilization that combined Egyptian influences with sub-Saharan ones.

### Kerma (c. 2400–1500 BCE)

The earliest Kushite capital was **Kerma**, in modern northern Sudan just south of the Nile's third cataract. Kerma was a substantial city — its central religious complex, the great mud-brick temple called the **Western Deffufa**, was three stories tall and the largest pre-modern mud-brick structure in Africa. The kingdom traded with Egypt's Old and Middle Kingdom in gold, ivory, ebony, ostrich feathers, incense, and exotic animals. **Tutankhamun's funerary mask** and many other Egyptian royal artifacts used Nubian gold.

Egyptian-Nubian relations were not always peaceful. The Egyptian Middle Kingdom built fortifications at Buhen and other sites to control the trade and the population. During the Hyksos period in Egypt (the Second Intermediate Period, c. 1782–1570 BCE), Kerma reached its zenith, briefly threatening Egyptian holdings. With the New Kingdom restoration after 1570 BCE, Egypt invaded Nubia and conquered Kerma. From around 1500 to 1000 BCE, the upper Nile was a province of the Egyptian empire, with Egyptian temples, Egyptian governors, and a substantial Egyptian cultural overlay.

### Napata and the 25th Dynasty (c. 1000–656 BCE)

Egyptian control over Nubia weakened after the New Kingdom collapse around 1100 BCE. By 1000 BCE, Nubian rulers had reasserted local control, with their capital at **Napata**, near the fourth cataract. Napatan culture combined Egyptian religious and architectural conventions (pyramids, temple complexes, hieroglyphic inscriptions in the Egyptian language) with Nubian elements.

In the eighth century BCE, the Napatan king **Piye** invaded a politically fragmented Egypt and unified it under his rule. The 25th Dynasty (the Kushite or "Black Pharaoh" dynasty) ruled Egypt from c. 730 to 656 BCE. Its kings — Piye, Shabaka, Shebitku, Taharqa, Tantamani — built temples in Egypt, restored Egyptian religious institutions that the Late Period rulers had let lapse, and explicitly positioned themselves as more authentically Egyptian than the Egyptians. The dynasty ended in 656 BCE when Assyrian armies invaded Egypt and drove the Kushites back south.

### Meroe (c. 590 BCE–350 CE)

After the Assyrian intervention, the Nubian capital moved south to **Meroe**, on the upper Nile between the fifth and sixth cataracts. Meroe became the capital of a kingdom that lasted nearly a millennium. The city was a major trade center: caravans connected it to the Red Sea ports (and thus Indian Ocean trade), to sub-Saharan Africa via Sahel routes, to the Mediterranean via the Nile, and to the African interior via Nile branches.

Meroe's signature institutions distinguished it from earlier Kushite culture and from Egypt:

- **Iron production**. Meroe was a major iron-smelting center; mounds of iron slag still mark the city's industrial quarter. The proximity of iron ore, charcoal-producing forests, and skilled smiths made the city an industrial hub.
- **Meroitic script**. By the third century BCE, Meroitic scribes had developed their own writing system, partially based on Egyptian hieroglyphics but encoding the Meroitic language (still imperfectly deciphered today). The Meroitic script is one of the major undeciphered writing systems of antiquity.
- **Female rulers**. The Meroitic Kingdom had a tradition of powerful queens, called **kandakes** (the term gives English the word "Candace"). Several Meroitic queens led armies — one famously fought a Roman incursion under Augustus to a stalemate.
- **Pyramids**. Meroe is surrounded by hundreds of small steep-sided pyramids — Nubian royal tombs. There are more pyramids in modern Sudan than in Egypt, though they are smaller and more numerous than the Egyptian giants.

Meroe declined in the fourth century CE under pressure from the rising Christian-Aksumite kingdom to its east (Chapter 13). The capital was sacked around 350 CE. The Meroitic civilization's successors split into smaller Christian kingdoms (Nobatia, Makuria, Alodia) that would persist along the upper Nile until the Islamic period.

## Concept 4 — North Africa: Carthage and the Berbers

The Mediterranean coast of Africa west of Egypt was settled, by the Iron Age, by Berber-speaking peoples (the local name for themselves is **Imazighen**, "free people"). Berber populations had been present in the region for thousands of years and had their own distinct cultural and political organization.

The transformative event for Mediterranean North Africa was the arrival of **Phoenician** colonists from Tyre and Sidon in the late ninth century BCE. The Phoenicians, the great traders of the Iron Age Mediterranean (Chapter 8), founded a colony at **Carthage** (in modern Tunisia) around 814 BCE. Carthage grew steadily through the next several centuries, eventually displacing the eastern Phoenician homeland as the wealthiest and most powerful Phoenician city.

By the fifth century BCE, Carthage controlled an empire that included the North African coast from Cyrenaica (modern eastern Libya) to the Atlantic, the major Mediterranean islands (Corsica, Sardinia, the western half of Sicily), the southern Iberian peninsula (modern Spain), and the Phoenician trading colonies that dotted the Mediterranean from Cyprus to the Strait of Gibraltar. Carthage's wealth came from Mediterranean trade, from the silver mines of Iberia, and from its agricultural hinterland in modern Tunisia.

The Berber populations of the North African interior alternately allied with and resisted Carthaginian authority. **Numidia**, in modern Algeria-Tunisia, became a substantial Berber kingdom under King **Massinissa** (c. 238–148 BCE), an ally of Rome during the Second Punic War.

The end of Carthaginian power came with the **Punic Wars** against Rome (264–146 BCE), covered in Chapter 8. Rome destroyed Carthage in 146 BCE and absorbed its North African territories as the Roman province of **Africa**. Roman North Africa would remain prosperous for the next six centuries, providing a substantial portion of Rome's grain and producing a series of significant Latin authors (Tertullian, Augustine of Hippo, Apuleius). Numidia and Mauretania (the western Berber kingdoms) became Roman client states and eventually Roman provinces.

The Berber populations survived under Roman, Vandal (after 429 CE), Byzantine (after 533 CE), and finally Arab-Islamic (after 647 CE) rule. Berber languages and cultural identity persist across North Africa today.

## Concept 5 — The Sahara as connector

The Sahara Desert is sometimes described as a barrier separating "North Africa" from "sub-Saharan Africa." This framing is historically misleading. The Sahara was — and is — also a connector. Trade routes crossed the desert from at least the early centuries CE, possibly earlier; by the late first millennium CE, the **trans-Saharan trade** was a major force shaping both North African and West African societies.

The trade depended on the **camel**. Camels had been domesticated in Arabia by perhaps 3000 BCE; they entered North Africa through Egypt around the fourth century BCE and spread west across the Sahara over the next several centuries. By the first century CE, camel caravans were the standard means of crossing the desert. A trans-Saharan caravan took roughly two months to cross from the Sahel south of the desert to the Mediterranean coast, traveling in stages between oases and welcomed at established stopping points.

The goods moving north included:
- **Gold**. West African mines in the upper Niger and Senegal basins produced enormous quantities of gold, much of which entered the Mediterranean economy and the Islamic world via the trans-Saharan trade.
- **Salt**. Salt was scarce in West Africa and abundant in the Sahara (deposits at Taghaza, Bilma, and other sites). Salt traveled south.
- **Slaves**. The trans-Saharan slave trade is older than the more famous Atlantic slave trade and continued into the modern period. Estimates of total slaves transported across the Sahara across the entire period are substantial.
- **Ivory**, **ostrich feathers**, **kola nuts**, and other items.

Going south from the Mediterranean: **horses** (highly valued by West African states for cavalry warfare), **textiles**, **glass**, **manufactured goods**, **books and ideas** (Islamic scholars and missionaries crossing the Sahara would shape West African religious life from the eighth century onward).

The trans-Saharan trade was the economic backbone of the West African Sahel kingdoms — Ghana (c. 400–1200 CE), Mali (c. 1230–1670 CE), Songhai (c. 1340–1591 CE) — that we will encounter in Chapter 19. By 1000 CE these Sahel kingdoms were major participants in a Mediterranean-Saharan-Sahel trading system that stretched from the Atlantic to the Red Sea and connected Africa to the wider Islamic world and beyond.

The image of the Sahara as an isolating barrier was partly a product of nineteenth-century European geography (which arrived in Africa from the coasts and saw the desert as the obstacle between northern and southern halves of "the dark continent"). To Africans, then and now, the Sahara was a road, not a wall.

## Chapter summary

You can now do six things you could not do before this chapter.

You can describe the geographic diversity of Africa — Mediterranean coast, Sahara, Sahel, equatorial rainforest, savanna, East African Rift, Ethiopian highlands, southern temperate zone — and explain how each region's environment shaped the human societies that developed there.

You can describe the multiple independent centers of African agricultural origin — West African Sahel, Ethiopian highlands, the forest-savanna boundary — and the crops that defined each.

You can describe the **Bantu migrations** as a multi-millennial demographic expansion that spread Bantu-speaking peoples and their farming-ironworking complex across most of sub-Saharan Africa, and identify the linguistic and archaeological evidence used to reconstruct the process.

You can place the **Kingdom of Kush** in its long history — Kerma, Napata, Meroe — and explain its complex relationship with Egypt, including the period of Nubian rule over Egypt under the 25th Dynasty.

You can describe **Carthage** as the dominant Phoenician colonial power of the western Mediterranean and the principal rival of Rome before its destruction in 146 BCE; and you can place Berber-speaking peoples in their continuous history under Phoenician, Roman, Vandal, Byzantine, and Arab rule.

You can describe the **trans-Saharan trade** as the connective tissue between Mediterranean Africa, the Sahara, and the West African Sahel — a trade in gold, salt, slaves, and ideas that operated for at least 1,500 years and was the economic foundation of the medieval West African kingdoms.

The thing to watch for, going forward, is the temptation to read pre-colonial African history through the lens of European arrival. The kingdoms and trade networks this chapter has covered were operating long before European contact and continued to operate after it. The modern image of "Africa" as a single backward space is a colonial construction. The actual African past is plural, deep, and inseparable from the broader history of Eurasia.

## Key terms

- **Bantu migrations.** The multi-millennial expansion of Bantu-speaking peoples from a homeland in the Nigeria-Cameroon border region across most of sub-Saharan Africa.
- **Berber.** The ethnolinguistic identity of the indigenous peoples of North Africa west of Egypt; their own term for themselves is *Imazighen*.
- **Carthage.** Phoenician colonial city in modern Tunisia (founded c. 814 BCE), the dominant power of the western Mediterranean before Rome.
- **Deffufa.** A monumental mud-brick temple structure characteristic of Kerma-period Nubia.
- **Imazighen.** "Free people," the Berber name for themselves.
- **Kandake.** Title of female rulers of the Meroitic Kingdom of Kush.
- **Kerma.** The earliest Nubian capital (c. 2400–1500 BCE), in modern northern Sudan.
- **Kush.** The long-lasting Nubian civilization (c. 2400 BCE–350 CE) along the upper Nile in modern Sudan.
- **Meroe.** The later Nubian capital (c. 590 BCE–350 CE), known for iron production, pyramids, and queens.
- **Meroitic script.** The (still imperfectly deciphered) writing system developed at Meroe, partly based on Egyptian hieroglyphics.
- **Napata.** Nubian capital (c. 1000–656 BCE), site of the 25th Dynasty's origin.
- **Numidia.** Berber kingdom in modern Algeria-Tunisia, ally of Rome during the Punic Wars.
- **Piye.** Nubian king who conquered Egypt around 728 BCE and founded the 25th Dynasty.
- **Sahel.** The semi-arid grassland strip on the southern edge of the Sahara.
- **Trans-Saharan trade.** The system of long-distance trade routes across the Sahara, connecting North Africa to West Africa and the wider Mediterranean-Indian Ocean economy.

## LLM Exercise — Chapter 11: The Commodity in Ancient Africa

**Project:** *A Commodity's Journey*.

**What you're building this chapter:** Section 9 — how your commodity moved through ancient Africa beyond Egypt: Kush, the Bantu expansion, the Saharan trade.

**Tool:** Claude Project (your *Commodity Biography* project).

**The prompt:**

```
Section 9 — The Commodity in Ancient Africa.

Chapter 11 covered Africa as a continent of continents — Sahara,
savanna, rainforest, highland, coast — beyond the Egypt-centric
historiography. Key concepts: the Kingdom of Kush as a parallel
Nile civilization that conquered and was conquered by Egypt; the
Bantu agricultural and linguistic expansion across the continent;
the deep-time Saharan trade in salt, copper, and (later) gold;
the iron metallurgy of Nok and the Bantu diaspora; the
geographic-ecological constraint that shaped African
civilizational forms differently from Eurasian ones.

Write Section 9 of the commodity biography (~600–1,000 words):

1. **Locate my commodity in the Egypt-Kush axis.** Egyptian tomb
   inventories and tribute lists (often from Kush, Punt, or
   southern Egypt) included many commodities. Did mine appear?
   Cite a specific tomb, temple inscription, or Egyptian text.

2. **Trace it through the Saharan trade.** The trans-Saharan trade
   in salt, copper, and gold predates Islam by centuries. The
   Garamantes ran routes from the Mediterranean coast deep into
   the central Sahara. Did my commodity move on these routes?

3. **Locate it in the Bantu expansion.** Iron-using, banana-and-
   yam-cultivating Bantu-speaking peoples spread across most of
   sub-Saharan Africa over the first millennium BCE and CE. What
   did they bring? What did they take up? Where does my commodity
   sit in this expansion?

4. **Examine the African iron metallurgy story.** Nok iron from
   the first millennium BCE is among the earliest worldwide, and
   African iron production was sustained through pre-modern
   times. Did my commodity intersect with iron production?

5. **Identify the regional specificity.** The Ethiopian highlands,
   the Niger bend, the Great Lakes region, the southern African
   plateau — each had distinct material cultures. If my commodity
   was regional, which region carried it?

6. **End with the constraint.** Africa's east-west geography is
   broken up by deserts, rainforests, and the rift valley far more
   than Eurasia's. Movement was harder. What does this constraint
   explain about how my commodity (or its analog) moved or
   didn't move?

Save to running draft under "Beyond Egypt: The Commodity in
Ancient Africa." Cite at least one specific African region, one
piece of archaeological or textual evidence, and one
Saharan-trade configuration. Flag verification needs.
```

**What this produces:** A section that locates your commodity in the African civilizational story without flattening it into Egyptian-or-trans-Atlantic-slavery framing.

**How to adapt this prompt:**

- *For your own project:* African archaeology and historiography have been growing rapidly. Cross-check against recent scholarship; older textbooks under-cover this.
- *For ChatGPT / Gemini:* Both work but verify African archaeology citations carefully — date ranges and trade-route maps shift with new discoveries.
- *For Claude Code:* Not the fit.
- *For a Claude Project:* Native fit.

**Connection to previous chapters:** Adds the fourth major civilizational track. The biography now has Mediterranean, Near Eastern, Asian, American, and African threads.

**Preview of next chapter:** Chapter 13 (Empires of Faith) covers Late Antiquity — Christian Rome, Sasanian Persia, Aksum, Himyar. Section 10 will ask how your commodity fared in the religious-empire matrix.
