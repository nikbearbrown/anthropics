# Chapter 8 — Mediterranean Peoples
*From the Aegean Bronze Age to Augustus, in One Connected Sea.*

## Three suggested titles

- Mediterranean Peoples: How a Sea Became a Single World
- From Knossos to Augustus: The Long Mediterranean
- The Greek-Roman Synthesis and the Civilizations It Absorbed

---

## Chapter overview

By the end of this chapter you will know how the Mediterranean became a single connected world over roughly 3,500 years. You will know the Bronze Age civilizations that began the connection — Minoan, Mycenaean, Egyptian, Hittite — and what destroyed them. You will know the Iron Age peoples who picked up the pieces — Phoenicians, who invented the alphabet that almost every Western script descends from; Greeks, who invented the *polis* and through it the political vocabulary the rest of the chapter uses; Etruscans, who taught Rome what a city should look like. You will know how Sparta and Athens organized themselves so differently, how the Persian Empire failed twice to conquer them, and how they then destroyed each other in the Peloponnesian War. You will know how Alexander of Macedon spent thirteen years undoing the Persian Empire and how his successors fragmented his conquests into the Hellenistic world. You will know how Rome rose from a small Italian city-state, swallowed Carthage in three wars, absorbed the Hellenistic kingdoms, then nearly tore itself apart in civil war before Augustus turned the Republic into an empire by leaving its forms intact and emptying them of meaning. The mechanisms — colonization, alphabetic literacy, citizen armies, debt politics, the patron-client system, client armies loyal to generals rather than to the state — recur. By the end, a single sea is one shared world.

## Opening case — a Pompeii floor in 79 CE

In a wealthy house in Pompeii, sometime in the first century CE, a Roman family commissioned a floor mosaic of an event that had happened in modern Turkey three hundred years earlier. The mosaic shows the Battle of Issus in 333 BCE — Alexander the Great of Macedon charging the Persian king Darius III, who is turning his chariot in retreat. The figures are nearly life-size. The mosaic uses about a million tiny tiles called *tesserae*. The patron paid for one of the most expensive floors in the empire, in a town that was about to be buried by Vesuvius and preserved for two thousand years.

Why?

Pompeii in 79 CE was a Roman town. The patron was Roman. Italy in the first century CE was the political center of an empire that ruled the entire Mediterranean basin. Alexander had been Macedonian. The battle had been fought in what is now southeastern Turkey. The defeated Persian king's grandfathers had ruled an empire that stretched from the Aegean to the Indus. The Romans of 79 CE had no direct stake in any of these powers. None of them existed any longer.

The answer is that, by 79 CE, the Mediterranean was a single shared cultural space. Greek colonists had founded settlements on the Italian peninsula seven hundred years before the mosaic was laid. Romans had absorbed Greek myths, Greek architecture, Greek philosophy, and Greek political vocabulary as the foundation of their own. Alexander, conquering the Persian "barbarians" in the name of Greek civilization, was a hero in a tradition the Roman patron considered his own. The mosaic was not foreign decoration. It was family history.

This chapter is the story of how that single shared culture came to exist. The story runs from approximately 3,500 years before our era to roughly 14 CE — from the Bronze Age palaces of Minoan Crete to the death of Augustus Caesar. The mechanisms are concrete. Trade routes that needed protection. Alphabets that simplified record-keeping. Colonies that exported language with the cargo. Wars that absorbed one civilization into another and forced the absorbed civilization's vocabulary onto the conqueror's tongue. The Romans of Pompeii thought of Alexander as one of their own because, by their time, Greekness had been built into the foundations of Roman education, religion, and self-image so thoroughly that the seam was invisible.

Reading the mosaic tells you the destination. The chapter walks the route.

## The Late Bronze Age world, c. 1600–1200 BCE

By 1600 BCE, the eastern Mediterranean was knit together by an interlocking trade system that the modern term *Late Bronze Age world* captures only loosely. Egypt was the dominant power. The Hittite Empire ruled most of Anatolia (modern Turkey). The Mitanni and Assyrians held northern Mesopotamia. The Kassites held Babylon. Minoan Crete and Mycenaean Greece held the Aegean. These polities were not allies. They were not enemies. They were, more accurately, *participants in the same system* — a system organized around redistributive economies (taxes collected as agricultural surplus, redistributed by palace bureaucracies), elite warrior castes fighting from horse-drawn chariots, dynastic intermarriage to seal alliances, and a shared diplomatic language. Official correspondence between rulers was conducted in Akkadian cuneiform, even when neither the sender nor the recipient spoke Akkadian as a first language. Vassal states paid tribute to dominant ones to avoid invasion.

The trade goods moved across long distances. Wine and olive oil from Greece. Cedar from the Levantine coast. Copper from Cyprus. Tin from sources as far away as Cornwall in modern Britain. Bronze, the metal that gave the age its name, was an alloy of copper and tin; its supply chain was the system's circulatory network.

**Minoan Crete.** A unique culture had developed on the Aegean island of Crete by 2000 BCE, reaching its height around 1600 BCE. The Minoans built large unfortified palaces — the largest at Knossos — which historians read as evidence that the island was generally peaceful and politically unified. They were sea traders. They had their own writing system, which scholars call Linear A and have not yet deciphered. Their art shows leaping bulls, sea creatures, and a goddess associated with snakes and fertility. The classical Greeks remembered Minoan Crete dimly — the legend of King Minos and the Minotaur in the Labyrinth preserves a memory of the bull-leaping rituals, the palace at Knossos, and a sea power that demanded tribute from the Greek mainland.

**Mycenaean Greece.** Around 1600 BCE, Greek-speaking peoples on the Greek mainland — descendants of migrants from the Balkans — adopted Minoan civilization. Their largest city was Mycenae, which lent the era its name. They wrote in Linear B, derived from Linear A and now decipherable as an early form of Greek. Their kingdoms were militarized, often surrounded by stone fortifications so massive that later Greeks called them *Cyclopean* — the work of giants. Their kings bore a title preserved in Linear B tablets: *Wanax*, "lord." The same word appears in the *Iliad*, attributed to Homer, applied to King Agamemnon of Mycenae, who in the poem leads the Greek armies against Troy. Archaeologists have excavated Bronze Age Troy at Hisarlik in northwestern Turkey. The *Iliad* is not history in the modern sense, but the underlying memory it preserves of Mycenaean kingdoms is a real one.

### The Collapse, c. 1200–1100 BCE

The system ended badly. Beginning around 1200 BCE, much of the eastern Mediterranean fell apart in a wave of wars, invasions, and migrations. Mycenaean palaces burned. The Hittite Empire collapsed. Levantine kingdoms were destroyed. Egyptian inscriptions describe attacks by groups they called the *Sea Peoples* — Akawasha, Lukka, Shardana, Tursha, Peleset, and others. The Peleset settled the southern Levantine coast and became the people the Bible calls the Philistines. Other peoples were also on the move: Libyans pushing into Egypt's Nile Delta, Phrygians migrating from the Balkans into Anatolia, Aramaeans moving from the Arabian Desert into Syria and Mesopotamia.

The causes are still debated. Drought, earthquakes, peasant revolts, technological obsolescence, and pure migration cascade have all been proposed; the truth is probably some combination. What matters for our story is that the disruption broke the tin trade. Without reliable tin, bronze became scarce, and metalworkers across the eastern Mediterranean experimented for centuries with iron ore. They learned to add carbon to harden the metal (steeling), to cool it suddenly with water (quenching), and to heat-treat it (tempering). By around 900 BCE, the Iron Age had begun. The tools and weapons were better. The supply chain was simpler — iron ore is abundant almost everywhere. The Bronze Age system had been an interlocking elite. The Iron Age would be more democratic, in the sense that more people could afford the tools of war and the tools of work.

## The Iron Age — Phoenicians, Greeks, Etruscans

Three peoples emerged from the Iron Age dark to remake the Mediterranean.

**Phoenicians.** The Phoenicians were descended from the Bronze Age Canaanites of the Levantine coast. They lived in independent city-states — Tyre, Sidon, Byblos — each ruled by a king. They were the great sailors and traders of the Iron Age. They established trading posts in Cyprus, North Africa, Sicily, Sardinia, and Spain, and they sailed (the records suggest) along the west coast of Africa and to the British Isles in search of tin. By 700 BCE, Phoenician merchants had founded the city of Carthage in modern Tunisia as a stopping place on the long voyage to Spain. Carthage grew into the dominant power of the western Mediterranean and would eventually fight Rome for control of the sea.

The Phoenician contribution that mattered most was the alphabet. Around 1100 BCE, Phoenician scribes invented a writing system in which symbols stood for consonants. Strung together, the consonants spelled words. Vowels were inferred by context. The alphabet had perhaps two dozen characters, an order of magnitude smaller than Egyptian hieroglyphs or Mesopotamian cuneiform. Anyone could learn it in weeks. Literacy stopped being a specialist profession and started being a widely accessible skill. The Phoenicians' commercial dominance was at least partly an instruction-budget effect: their record-keeping was cheaper. Aramaeans, Israelites, and others adapted the script to their own languages within a few centuries. By the eighth century BCE, the Greeks had adopted it and added vowels — the innovation that turned the consonant alphabet into the full alphabet from which most Western scripts (including the one this textbook is set in) descend.

**Greeks.** After the Mycenaean collapse, Greece entered a period of population decline, lost literacy, and reduced material culture that historians call the Greek Dark Ages (1100–800 BCE). Around 800 BCE, a recovery began. Population grew. Settlements organized themselves into independent city-states, each called a *polis* — a town and its surrounding agricultural land, each with its own government, religion, and local identity, each politically separate from its neighbors. The Greeks called themselves *Hellenes* (after a mythical king Hellen) and their language Hellenic, but they did not unify politically. Their shared culture coexisted with constant rivalry between cities. The most famous shared sites were Delphi (the oracle of Apollo) and Olympia (the games dedicated to Zeus, held every four years from 776 BCE).

The Archaic period (800–500 BCE) was an era of expansion. Greek population growth outran the islands' farmland, and Greeks colonized aggressively — the Black Sea coast, North Africa (Cyrene), southern France (Massalia, today's Marseille), Sicily, and southern Italy (so densely settled that the Romans called it *Magna Graecia*, "Greater Greece"). When a colony was founded, the free adult male settlers divided the land into equal lots. From this practice grew the most consequential idea of the period: that citizenship in a community was associated with equality in landholding and participation in the community's government. The idea was taken back to mainland Greece. By the sixth century BCE, the older aristocracies, the *aristoi*, were in conflict with the rising commoner class, the *kakoi*, who demanded land and political voice. In some cities, the conflict produced the rule of *tyrants* (a Greek word meaning, originally, populist strongman, with no necessarily negative connotation). In others, including Athens, it produced experiments in broader citizen governance.

**Etruscans.** In central Italy, a civilization called the Etruscans developed in the Iron Age. They organized themselves into independent city-states like the Greeks. They adopted Greek artistic and architectural conventions, then modified them. They modified the Greek alphabet to write their own language, which scholars have not yet fully deciphered. By 600 BCE, the Etruscans had expanded south and colonized a small Latin city-state on the Tiber River called Rome. Etruscan kings ruled Rome for the next century. Roman religion, including the priestly practice of reading omens in the entrails of sacrificed animals, came from the Etruscans. So did the *fasces*, the bundle of rods and an axe that symbolized the authority of Roman magistrates and centuries later was adopted by twentieth-century Italian fascism, from which the political movement took its name. The Roman alphabet, still used across Western Europe and much of the world, was Etruscan modifications of Greek modifications of Phoenician.

Three peoples, one transmission chain. Phoenicians invented the alphabet. Greeks added vowels and exported it across the Mediterranean. Etruscans adapted it for Italian use and passed it to Rome. Rome carried it to Gaul, to Britain, to Iberia, and eventually — much later — across an ocean that the Phoenicians had already sailed.

## Sparta and Athens — two answers to the same problem

By 500 BCE, the Greek world contained dozens of independent *poleis*, but two dominated the political imagination: Sparta and Athens. They had emerged from the same general Archaic problem — how should the *aristoi* and the *kakoi* share power? — and produced opposite answers.

**Sparta** was a collection of villages in Laconia, in southern Greece. In the eighth and seventh centuries BCE, Sparta solved its land shortage by conquering its neighbors, especially the Messenians, and reducing them to forced agricultural labor as *helots*. Helots were not the kind of enslaved people who could be bought and sold. They were attached to the land, generation after generation. Their forced labor freed the Spartan citizen class — the *Spartiates* — to dedicate their lives to the military.

The Spartan political system was an oligarchy. Two hereditary kings drawn from rival royal families served limited functions: priestly, ceremonial, occasionally military. A Council of Elders (the *Gerousia*) and an Assembly of all adult Spartiates passed laws and approved treaties. Five annually elected officials called *ephors* administered the state. Tradition attributed the system to a lawgiver named Lycurgus around 800 BCE; modern historians think it evolved.

The defining feature was the *agoge*, the Spartan educational system. At seven, Spartan boys left their families and were raised by the state. They were systematically underfed, on the theory that learning to steal food would make them resilient. From twelve, they wore one cloak year-round and bathed once a year. They were beaten ritually to harden them. From eighteen they trained intensively in arms; at twenty their education was complete. Spartan women trained physically as well, on the assumption that strong mothers produced strong sons; they ran their family estates while their husbands were away at war and were unusually independent for Greek women. Sparta produced what was in its own time the most feared infantry in the Mediterranean, paid for by the labor of an enslaved population whose memory of conquest never faded. Helot revolts haunted Sparta throughout its history.

**Athens** was a different solution to the same problem. Larger than most *poleis* — its territory of Attica enclosed several mountain valleys — Athens experimented with progressively broader political participation across the seventh and sixth centuries BCE. The legislator Draco codified harsh laws around 620 BCE (giving us the modern adjective *draconian*). When the city's political system staggered toward civil war near the end of the seventh century, the Athenians appointed Solon, an aristocrat with literary reputation and a sense for compromise, to draft a new constitution in 594 BCE. Solon canceled outstanding debts, banned debt-slavery for Athenian citizens, opened government offices to wealth (no longer just to birth), and created jury courts in which any citizen could appeal a magistrate's ruling. His reforms did not end conflict; a tyrant named Pisistratus seized power in 545 BCE and ruled, popularly enough, until his death.

Around 509 BCE, the aristocrat Cleisthenes — having lost a struggle for control to a pro-Spartan faction — appealed to the common people of the Athenian *demes* (villages), proposing a *demokratia*, "rule by the people." His reforms reorganized Athens into ten new tribes, each composed of villages drawn from across Attica, breaking the old regional aristocratic blocs. Each tribe sent fifty members chosen by lot to a Council of Five Hundred. The Assembly of all citizens (free adult males) met at least ten times a year. It declared war and approved treaties. It passed laws. Citizens served on juries. By the early fifth century BCE, Athens had a functioning direct democracy — the most participatory political system the ancient world had yet produced — though one whose participation excluded women, enslaved people, foreign residents, and the vast majority of the human population.

## The Persian Wars and the Athenian century

In the mid-sixth century BCE, the Persian Empire under Cyrus the Great conquered the Greek city-states of Ionia (the Aegean coast of modern Turkey). When the Ionian Greeks rebelled in 499 BCE, Athens sent help. The Persians crushed the rebellion by 494 BCE. The Persian king Darius decided that Athens would be punished.

In 490 BCE, a Persian fleet landed at Marathon on the Athenian coast. The Athenians, vastly outnumbered, charged. The Persian draftees broke. The Persians sailed for Athens itself; the Athenian soldiers ran the twenty-six miles back across the peninsula and arrived in time to defend the city. The Persians went home. (The modern marathon footrace commemorates the run, though the historical evidence for any single runner is thin.)

Ten years later, in 480 BCE, Darius's son Xerxes launched a much larger invasion. Most Greek city-states united in the Hellenic League. At Thermopylae, a narrow pass in northeastern Greece, a small force led by the Spartan king Leonidas held the Persians for three days before being outflanked and killed to the last man. The delay let the Greeks regroup. At Salamis, the Athenian fleet trapped the Persian navy in a narrow strait and destroyed it. Without his fleet, Xerxes withdrew most of his army; the remainder was crushed at Plataea in 479 BCE.

The Persian Wars set up the Athenian fifth century. The Delian League, formed in 477 BCE under Athenian leadership, was originally an anti-Persian alliance: members contributed ships, troops, or cash. Over time, Athens turned the league into an empire. The treasury moved to Athens. Recalcitrant members were forced to remain. Tribute funded an enormous building program on the Athenian Acropolis, including the Parthenon, dedicated to Athena. The democracy itself was paid for by the tribute — Athenian jurors and assembly attendees received per-diem payments funded by the empire's revenues.

The classical Athenian Golden Age happened in this window. Sophocles and Euripides composed the tragedies that the Western theatrical tradition is built on. Aristophanes wrote the comedies. Herodotus wrote his *Histories* of the Greco-Persian wars; Thucydides wrote his *History of the Peloponnesian War*. Empedocles theorized that the universe was composed of four elements in tension; Democritus theorized that it was composed of indivisible *atoms*. Socrates questioned everyone in public until an Athenian jury sentenced him to death in 399 BCE for impiety and corrupting the youth. Plato wrote dialogues featuring his teacher and founded the Academy. Aristotle, Plato's student, founded the Lyceum and built systematic frameworks for biology, logic, ethics, and political theory. The art evolved from Egyptian-influenced rigid forms to lifelike, idealized human figures whose conventions European Renaissance artists would copy two thousand years later.

The empire was paid for with Greek subjugation, however, and the Spartans noticed. In 431 BCE, Sparta and its Peloponnesian League invaded Attica. The Peloponnesian War lasted twenty-seven years. The Athenian leader Pericles withdrew the population behind the city walls and let the navy supply them. A plague erupted in the crowded city in 426 BCE and killed thousands, including Pericles. A fragile peace held briefly. The young politician Alcibiades persuaded Athens to invade Sicily in 415 BCE; the expedition was annihilated in 413 BCE; Sparta, with Persian financial backing, eventually built a fleet capable of beating Athens at sea. In 405 BCE, the Athenian navy was destroyed at Aegospotami. In 404 BCE, Athens surrendered. The empire dissolved.

Sparta took over. Within a generation, Thebes had broken Spartan supremacy at Leuctra in 371 BCE. The 350s BCE found the Greek world exhausted, decentralized, and vulnerable to a new power rising in the north.

## Macedon, Alexander, and the Hellenistic world

The kingdom of Macedon straddled what is today northern Greece and southern North Macedonia. The Macedonians had adopted Greek culture in the Archaic period; their royal family claimed descent from the Greek hero Heracles. King Philip II (reigned 359–336 BCE) reformed the Macedonian army around a deep-ranked infantry phalanx armed with long pikes, with aristocratic cavalry on the wings. He used silver from Thrace to pay his troops year-round, turning what had been seasonal levies into a professional force. In 338 BCE, his army crushed the combined armies of Athens and Thebes at Chaeronea. Greece's independence ended. Philip began organizing a Greek-Macedonian invasion of the Persian Empire as a war of vengeance for the Persian Wars of 150 years earlier. In 336 BCE, before he could launch the invasion, he was assassinated.

His twenty-year-old son **Alexander** carried out the plan. Within thirteen years, Alexander's army had crossed the Hellespont, defeated the Persian field armies at Issus (333 BCE) and Gaugamela (330 BCE), occupied Egypt and been crowned pharaoh, sacked the Persian ceremonial capital Persepolis, taken the Persian throne after the assassination of Darius III, and pushed east into central Asia and the Indus valley. In 326 BCE, his exhausted army mutinied on the banks of the Hyphasis River and refused to march further. He led them back to Babylon, where in 323 BCE he died at thirty-three, probably of fever complicated by accumulated wounds and excessive drinking.

He left no plan for succession. His Bactrian wife Roxane was pregnant. His top generals — the *Successors* or *Diadochi* — fought over the empire for forty years. By the middle of the third century BCE, three major dynasties had emerged from the wars: the Antigonids in Macedon and Greece, the Seleucids ruling from Syria across the Iranian plateau to central Asia, and the Ptolemies in Egypt. Each was Macedonian by origin and Greek-speaking; each ruled vastly larger non-Greek populations through mostly Greek administrators.

The result is what historians call the **Hellenistic world** (323–31 BCE) — neither Greek nor non-Greek but *Hellenistic*, "Greek-like." Alexander and his successors founded Greek cities across the former Persian Empire (Alexandria in Egypt was the largest, with a population eventually approaching one million). These cities had Greek temples, theaters, marketplaces, and gymnasia. They imported Greek language as the lingua franca of administration and commerce. Over time, the Greek-speaking populations became integrated with the indigenous populations of Egypt, Syria, Mesopotamia, Persia, and central Asia. The Hebrew Bible was translated into Greek (the *Septuagint*) under Ptolemaic Egyptian rule, because so many Jews had immigrated to Alexandria. A Bactrian king named Menander I converted to Buddhism and became known in Indian sources as Milinda. Greek and Indian sculptural traditions met and produced the Gandhara school, which influenced Buddhist art for centuries.

The classical Greek city-state ideal — citizens governing themselves — did not survive in most of the Hellenistic world. Most cities were under royal control. Many Greeks turned from politics to philosophies of personal happiness. **Stoicism**, founded by Zeno around 300 BCE and named for the *stoa poikile* (painted porch) where he taught in Athens, held that the universe was governed by divine reason (*Logos*) and that happiness came from accepting fate while avoiding extreme emotions. **Epicureanism**, founded by Epicurus, held that the path to happiness was the avoidance of pain and the pursuit of moderate pleasure. Both schools survived for centuries. New mystery religions, including the worship of the Egyptian goddess Isis and the Persian sun god Mithras, promised eternal life through secret rituals. Roman elites would later adopt many of these religious and philosophical practices, and through them they would shape the religious vocabulary in which Christianity later articulated itself.

## The Roman Republic — from city-state to Mediterranean power

According to the Roman tradition, the city of Rome was founded in 753 BCE by twin brothers Romulus and Remus, sons of the war god Mars. Romulus killed Remus when his brother mocked the new city's wall. The story emphasizes Roman respect for boundaries. Modern archaeology suggests there really was a settlement on the Palatine Hill in the eighth century BCE and that the Romans of the Iron Age were trading with Greek merchants who had sailed up the Tiber.

Rome was an Etruscan city-state from around 600 BCE. The last Etruscan king, Tarquin the Proud, was overthrown in 509 BCE after his son raped the noblewoman Lucretia, who killed herself in front of her family and demanded vengeance. The story is partly legendary; the underlying fact that Rome expelled its Etruscan ruling dynasty and declared itself a republic is real. The word *republic* derives from the Roman term *res publica*, "the public thing," distinguishing the new system from monarchical *res privata* — the private thing of a king.

The early Republic was an aristocratic state. The two classes were the *patricians* (the elite, who alone could hold office and sit in the Senate) and the *plebeians* (the common people). The political conflict between them is called the **Struggle of the Orders**. The plebeians' leverage was military: when Rome was under threat from neighboring Italic peoples, the plebeians could refuse to fight. They first did so in 450 BCE, demanding that Rome's customary laws be written down so patrician judges could not interpret them at will. The result was the **Twelve Tables**, posted in the Roman Forum — Rome's first written code of law, accessible to every literate citizen.

After 450 BCE, the plebeians formed their own assembly, electing ten *tribunes* annually. Tribunes had the power of *veto* — Latin for "I forbid" — over any government action they considered harmful to the plebs. By 367 BCE, one of Rome's two senior magistrates (the *consuls*) had to be a plebeian. By 287 BCE, laws passed by the Plebeian Assembly applied to all Roman citizens. The Struggle of the Orders ended with formal legal equality. It did not end social hierarchy: well-off plebeians joined a new combined Roman elite. Poor Romans remained poor.

The political system that emerged was not a pure democracy and not a pure oligarchy. It was layered. Three public assemblies (Plebeian, Tribal, Centuriate) elected officials annually. The Senate, composed of former magistrates serving for life, controlled foreign policy and finance. Elected officials advised by the Senate ran the day-to-day administration. The Centuriate Assembly was weighted toward the wealthy: voting blocs were defined by the number of military *centuries* (units of one hundred soldiers) that the bloc could equip. Those who paid for the army got more votes. Underlying everything was the **patron-client system**, in which a wealthy citizen (the *patron*) provided legal and financial assistance to less wealthy clients in exchange for their political loyalty in the assemblies. Patronage descended through families. A patron with many clients wielded enormous quiet power.

### The Punic Wars and the Mediterranean conquest

In the third century BCE, Rome unified the Italian peninsula and then collided with Carthage, the Phoenician-founded Mediterranean trading power that controlled Sicily, Sardinia, North Africa, and parts of Spain. The three **Punic Wars** (264–241 BCE, 218–201 BCE, 149–146 BCE) shaped the Mediterranean for centuries.

The First Punic War was fought largely at sea, mostly around Sicily. Rome had no significant navy at the start; it built one. The Roman innovation was a hooked boarding plank that turned naval combat into a deck-by-deck infantry fight, which the Roman legions won. Sicily became Rome's first overseas province in 241 BCE.

The Second Punic War is the famous one. The Carthaginian general Hannibal marched an army — including war elephants — from Iberia across southern Gaul and over the Alps into Italy. He won three crushing victories: at the Trebia (218 BCE), Lake Trasimene (217 BCE), and Cannae (216 BCE). At Cannae, his army of about 50,000 destroyed a Roman army of perhaps 80,000 in a single afternoon. Hannibal expected Rome's Italian allies to defect. They mostly did not. Rome refused to negotiate. While Hannibal raided Italy, Rome sent a counter-invasion to Africa under Scipio Africanus. At Zama in 202 BCE, Scipio defeated Hannibal. Carthage surrendered. It lost all overseas territory.

The Third Punic War was an extermination. Rome, fearing a Carthaginian commercial revival, declared war on the city in 149 BCE and besieged it for three years. In 146 BCE, Rome destroyed Carthage and sold the surviving population into slavery. In the same year, Rome sacked the Greek city of Corinth and absorbed Greece itself. By the mid-second century BCE, Rome had no peer in the Mediterranean.

## The Republic of troubles

Conquest broke the Roman political system that had produced it.

The traditional Roman citizen had been a small farmer who served in the army when called. The Punic Wars and the Mediterranean conquests required armies in the field for years. While soldier-farmers were away, their farms were neglected; many returned to find their land lost or sold. Large plantations — *latifundia* — bought up the land and worked it with enslaved foreigners, who flooded into Italy after each new conquest. (After Rome defeated Macedon in 168 BCE, 150,000 people from Epirus were enslaved in a single transaction. The slave market on the Aegean island of Delos reportedly sold 10,000 people a day at its peak.) Dispossessed Roman farmers migrated to the city of Rome and joined the **proletariat**, the landless urban poor. By the first century BCE, Rome had perhaps a million inhabitants, most of them poor.

The patron-client system, which had run on rural farmers needing the help of urban patrons, broke down. The new urban poor needed grain, jobs, and entertainment more than they needed legal patronage. Politicians who provided these things — *populares* — built power bases independent of the old elite. Politicians who defended the old elite called themselves *optimates* — "the best men."

In 133 BCE, the tribune **Tiberius Gracchus** proposed redistributing public land to landless Romans. Many senators stood to lose property. Tiberius pushed his bill through over senatorial opposition. Senators declared a state of emergency and beat him to death. Ten years later, his brother **Gaius Gracchus** as tribune passed laws providing free grain to the poor, public works for jobs, and a court system that could try senators for corruption. In 121 BCE, Gaius died in another senatorial coup. The pattern was set: the Republic now solved political disputes by killing its tribunes.

By the end of the second century BCE, the Roman army had been reformed by the general **Gaius Marius** (consul 107 BCE) into a professional force. Marius opened the legions to the landless poor and promised land grants on retirement. The result was a **client army** — soldiers loyal to the general who fed them and would deliver their pension, not to the Roman state that had recruited them. In 88 BCE, the optimate general **Sulla** led his army on Rome itself to seize political control from the populares. In 87 BCE, Marius did the same in reverse. Sulla returned in 83 BCE, made himself dictator, executed thousands of political enemies, and retired in 79 BCE. He believed he had saved the Republic. He had instead demonstrated that a determined general with a loyal army could simply take Rome.

The lesson was learned. In 60 BCE, three generals — **Pompey** (who had cleared the Mediterranean of pirates and conquered Syria and Judea), **Crassus** (Rome's richest man, victor over Spartacus's slave revolt), and **Julius Caesar** (a populist and member of Marius's family) — formed the **First Triumvirate**, an informal alliance to run the Republic together. Caesar took command in Gaul (modern France) for ten years and conquered the entire territory. Crassus invaded Parthia in 54 BCE in pursuit of his own military reputation; he died at the battle of Carrhae. Pompey, jealous of Caesar's popularity, allied with the optimates against him. In 49 BCE, the Senate ordered Caesar to disband his army. Instead, he marched his legions across the Rubicon — the river that marked Italy's northern boundary, which armies were forbidden to cross — and invaded his own country. *Crossing the Rubicon* still means: passing the point of no return.

Caesar defeated Pompey at Pharsalus in 48 BCE. Pompey fled to Egypt and was murdered by the Egyptian regime hoping to ingratiate itself with the winner. Caesar had himself appointed dictator, then dictator for ten years, then dictator for life. His former optimate enemies, now offered offices in his government, viewed him as a tyrant. In March of 44 BCE, a conspiracy led by Brutus and Cassius assassinated him in the Senate house.

Caesar's eighteen-year-old grandnephew and adopted heir **Octavian** allied with two of Caesar's lieutenants, Marc Antony and Lepidus, in the **Second Triumvirate** of 43 BCE. The alliance crushed Caesar's assassins at Philippi in 42 BCE and divided the Roman world among the three. Predictably, the three quarreled. Lepidus was forced into retirement. Antony allied with the Ptolemaic queen Cleopatra VII of Egypt and married her. In 31 BCE, Octavian defeated Antony and Cleopatra at the naval battle of Actium. Antony and Cleopatra killed themselves the following year. Octavian executed Cleopatra's son by Caesar (Ptolemy XV Caesarion) and made Egypt his personal property.

He was thirty-two years old. He was the sole ruler of the Roman world. He had no model for what to do next.

## Augustus and the principate

The genius of the political system Octavian invented was that, on its surface, nothing changed.

In 27 BCE, Octavian formally returned all his powers to the Senate and the Roman people. The Senate, now stuffed with his supporters, immediately appointed him **proconsul** of every frontier province where Rome's armies were stationed — making him commander of the Roman army. They named him **Princeps Senatus**, "first citizen of the Senate." They voted him the religious-political title **Augustus**, "revered one," previously reserved for gods and heroes. He held one of the two consulships every year so he could sit in the Senate. In 23 BCE, the Senate gave him the powers of a tribune — the right to veto any government action and to introduce legislation directly to the Plebeian Assembly.

Each grant was within Roman constitutional tradition. The combination was unprecedented. Augustus held the army, the highest ceremonial office, the supreme religious-political authority, and a permanent veto. The Republic's institutions — Senate, consuls, tribunes, assemblies — continued to meet and to vote. They did so, increasingly, on what Augustus had already decided.

Augustus solved problems that had broken the Republic. He reduced the army from 600,000 to 200,000 and gave 100,000 discharged veterans land in conquered territories. He created a treasury (the *aerarium militare*) to fund future veterans' pensions, ending the dependence on individual generals. He started a permanent program of free grain for the urban poor, using Egypt's harvest as the supply. He built public works on a vast scale (he reportedly boasted that he had transformed Rome from a city of brick to a city of marble). He created the Praetorian Guard as a permanent imperial police force. He extended Roman citizenship to provincial elites who completed long army service, accelerating the cultural integration of the empire.

He could not solve succession. There was no formal office of emperor. There was no republican tradition of hereditary rule. Augustus's preferred heirs kept dying before him. Eventually he settled on his stepson Tiberius. The transition in 14 CE went smoothly. The next several transitions did not. Tiberius (14–37 CE) became paranoid in his old age. His successor Caligula (37–41 CE) showed signs of mental illness and was assassinated by the Praetorian Guard. Claudius (41–54 CE) was an effective administrator; his successor Nero (54–68 CE) executed his mother, then his critics, then himself. The year 68–69 CE saw four different emperors take and lose the throne. Vespasian (69–79 CE) restored stability and founded the Flavian dynasty; his son Domitian (81–96 CE) was assassinated in his palace.

The principate was a system in which legitimate succession depended on the personal charisma or institutional grip of a man at the top. When the man was capable — Augustus, Vespasian, later Trajan and Hadrian — the system worked. When he was not, the army and the Praetorian Guard intervened and the system survived through a brief period of chaos. The Republic's old fears of one-man rule had been justified. They had also been displaced. The empire that resulted lasted, in the West, for nearly five hundred years.

The Pompeii floor of the Battle of Issus was laid down somewhere in this stretch of imperial stability. Three centuries after Alexander, in a Roman town under a Roman emperor, a Roman family thought of a Macedonian king as their own. They were not wrong. The cultural fusion the chapter has tracked — Phoenician alphabet to Greek alphabet to Etruscan alphabet to Roman alphabet, Greek philosophy to Hellenistic synthesis to Roman absorption, Greek polis to Roman *res publica* to Augustan principate — is the floor under their floor.

You can read Augustus's "restoration of the Republic" as a political fiction that left the institutional shells intact while transferring real power to a single man, and you can name the components of the principate that he assembled to make this work.

The thing to watch for, going forward, is the temptation to treat the Roman Empire as the natural endpoint of Mediterranean history. It was not. It was an unstable solution to a specific set of problems — debt politics, client armies, succession crises — that the Republic had failed to solve. The next chapter follows the Empire forward and shows how its solutions then broke down in their own way.

## Key terms

- **Augustus.** Title meaning "revered one," voted by the Senate to Octavian in 27 BCE and held by every subsequent Roman emperor.
- **Client army.** A professional army loyal to its commanding general (who delivered land and pay) rather than to the state. Marius's reform after 107 BCE.
- **Hellenistic.** "Greek-like." Refers to the period 323–31 BCE in which Greek culture and language spread across the former Persian Empire under the kingdoms of Alexander's successors.
- **Hellenes.** The Greeks' name for themselves, derived from a mythical king Hellen.
- **Helots.** Forced agricultural laborers in Sparta, especially Messenians; bound to the land rather than owned individually.
- **Levant.** The eastern Mediterranean coastal region, today Israel, Jordan, Lebanon, Palestine, and Syria.
- **Linear A.** The Minoan script. Not yet deciphered.
- **Linear B.** The Mycenaean script, derived from Linear A. Deciphered as an early form of Greek.
- **Optimates.** "The best men." Politicians who defended the traditional senatorial elite in late-Republican Roman politics.
- **Polis.** A Greek city-state, consisting of a town and its surrounding agricultural land.
- **Populares.** Politicians who built power bases by appealing to discontented groups in late-Republican Rome.
- **Principate.** The political system Augustus established after 27 BCE, in which republican forms continued to function over the substance of one-man rule.
- **Proletariat.** The landless urban working class in late-Republican Rome.
- **Punic Wars.** Three wars between Rome and Carthage (264–241 BCE, 218–201 BCE, 149–146 BCE) that ended with the destruction of Carthage and Roman supremacy in the western Mediterranean.
- **Roman Republic.** The Roman political system from approximately 509 BCE to 27 BCE, ruled by elected magistrates and the Senate without a king.
- **Spartiates.** The Spartan citizen class. Trained from childhood in the *agoge* and obligated to military service.
- **Struggle of the Orders.** The political conflict between Roman patricians and plebeians, c. 494–287 BCE, that ended with formal legal equality.
- **Twelve Tables.** Rome's first written law code, posted in the Forum c. 450 BCE.

## Further reading

- **Cline, Eric H.** *1177 B.C.: The Year Civilization Collapsed.* 2014. The current best single-volume treatment of the Late Bronze Age Collapse, organized around its multi-cause structure.
- **Cartledge, Paul.** *The Spartans: An Epic History.* 2002. A working classicist's account of Sparta as a real society, not the mythologized military caricature.
- **Hansen, Mogens Herman.** *The Athenian Democracy in the Age of Demosthenes.* 1991. The standard reference on how Athenian democracy actually functioned day-to-day.
- **Beard, Mary.** *SPQR: A History of Ancient Rome.* 2015. The most readable single-volume history of Rome from foundation to the third century CE; pay particular attention to her treatment of the late Republic.
- **Holland, Tom.** *Rubicon: The Last Years of the Roman Republic.* 2003. Narrative history of the period from the Gracchi to Augustus. Strong on the politics and personalities.
- **Goldsworthy, Adrian.** *Augustus: First Emperor of Rome.* 2014. A military historian's biography that takes seriously the institutional architecture of the principate.
- **Green, Peter.** *Alexander to Actium: The Historical Evolution of the Hellenistic Age.* 1990. The major one-volume history of the Hellenistic world.
