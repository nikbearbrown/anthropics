## Chapter overview

By the end of this chapter you will know how the Roman Empire actually worked at the level of ordinary lived experience. You will know the structure of Roman family life — the *paterfamilias*, the *domus*, the legal subordination of women, the marriage age, the position of children, the Roman ideology of *mos maiorum* (the way of the ancestors). You will know how Roman slavery functioned — the scale of the enslaved population, the legal framework, the routes by which a person became enslaved or was freed, the use of enslaved labor in agriculture, mines, households, and the gladiatorial games. You will know how the empire was financed — long-distance trade routes that knit the Mediterranean into a single market, the tax system that supported the legions and the bureaucracy, and the cycle of conquest that fed the system with enslaved people and tribute. You will know the religious world — the public cults, the imperial cult, the diversity of mystery religions, the role of the Vestal Virgins, and the rise of Christianity from a small Jewish sect to the official religion of the empire. By the end, you will have a sense of what it would have been like to actually live in the Roman world — as elite citizen, as urban poor, as enslaved laborer, as provincial farmer, as Christian convert.

## Opening case — Pompeii, August 24, 79 CE

In late August of the year 79 CE, sometime around midday, the volcanic mountain that loomed over the Bay of Naples — Vesuvius — exploded. A column of pumice and ash rose perhaps twenty miles into the sky. Hot stone rained on the towns at the volcano's foot. Pyroclastic surges (avalanches of superheated gas and ash, traveling at hundreds of miles per hour, with internal temperatures perhaps reaching 700°F) rolled down the slopes. The Roman city of **Pompeii**, fifteen miles to the southeast, was buried under twenty-five feet of ash. The smaller town of **Herculaneum**, closer in, was buried under up to seventy-five feet of pyroclastic material. Their inhabitants — perhaps fifteen thousand at Pompeii, four thousand at Herculaneum — had a few hours' warning at most. Some fled. Some hid. Some died where they were caught. Their bodies, gas-flash-cooked and then encased in cooling ash, left voids in the hardened material that nineteenth-century archaeologists would discover and fill with plaster, casting the people of Pompeii where they fell — a baker covering his face with his hands, a child asleep on her mother's lap, a dog still chained to a wall.

The catastrophe is the historian's gift. Pompeii and Herculaneum were ordinary Roman towns whose ordinariness was preserved by the very disaster that destroyed them. We have shop counters with carbonized loaves of bread still on them. We have election graffiti scratched on walls supporting candidates whose campaigns were never finished. We have brothel walls with painted price lists. We have a pet dog's collar with the dog's name engraved on it. We have lunch counters, latrines, gymnasiums, an amphitheater, the tomb of a baker who insisted that his epitaph commemorate the size of his bread oven.

Pompeii is the chapter's destination. The previous chapter (Chapter 8) followed the political shape of the Roman world from its origins through the establishment of the Empire under Augustus. This chapter goes inside the world that political shape produced. What did a Roman family look like? What did slavery actually mean in this empire? How did the empire feed itself? What did the people of the empire believe? The political history is the bones. The lived experience is the flesh.

We pick it up with the family.

## Concept 1 — The Roman family

The Roman family — *familia* — was a larger and more legally articulated unit than a modern family. It included not only spouses and children but also the extended kin descended through the male line, all the household's enslaved people, and the freedmen and freedwomen (former slaves) and clients who depended on the household. The term *domus* was used both for the physical house and for the family that lived in it; the two concepts were not really distinguished.

The head of the household was the **paterfamilias** — the eldest male in the direct line of descent. His legal authority over his dependents, called *patria potestas*, was extraordinary by modern standards. In principle, he could sell his children into slavery, disown them, arrange their marriages, dispose of their property, or — in the most extreme reading of Roman law — order them killed. In practice, the absolute powers were rarely exercised. Family decisions were typically made by consensus. But the patriarchal framework remained the household's organizing principle, and Roman political language constantly drew on it. Senators were called *patres conscripti* ("conscript fathers"). The emperor was, formally, *pater patriae* ("father of the country"). The Roman Republic and Empire never escaped the conceptual gravity of the Roman family.

### Women, children, and marriage

Roman women were legally subordinate to their fathers and (if married) their husbands. They could not vote, hold political office, or — in most periods — appear in court without a male representative. The legal framework was strict; the social practice was more varied. Roman women, especially in elite families, owned property, ran businesses, managed estates, and exerted political influence through their husbands, sons, and brothers. The empress Livia, wife of Augustus, shaped imperial politics for decades; the noblewoman Cornelia, mother of the Gracchi tribunes, became a model of Roman maternal virtue; women named Julia, Agrippina, and Helena recur as powerful figures in the imperial story.

Marriage was usually arranged. Girls married young — typically between fourteen and eighteen — to men often a decade or more older. The bride brought a **dowry** that became her husband's property during the marriage but was returnable to her family upon divorce or her husband's death. Marriages were classified by whether the wife passed under her husband's *patria potestas* (the older form, *cum manu*) or remained under her father's (the later, more common form, *sine manu*). The *sine manu* form gave a wife some legal independence and made divorce simpler.

Children were raised at home in their early years and — in elite families — sent to schools at age seven. Education centered on Latin, Greek, and rhetoric. A boy received the *toga virilis* (the toga of manhood) at fifteen, formally entering Roman public life. A girl was prepared for marriage. Children of enslaved people received whatever education their owners chose to give them — typically, none.

### The domus and the insula

The Roman residential landscape divided sharply by class. Elite families lived in **domus** houses — single-family townhouses organized around an *atrium* (a courtyard with a partial roof open to the sky, with an *impluvium* pool to catch rainwater). The atrium was a public space within the private home: it was where the *paterfamilias* received his clients in the morning ritual called the *salutatio*, where family business was conducted, where ancestor masks were displayed in niches called *alae*. Behind the atrium lay the *tablinum* (the master's office and family records), beyond it a peristyle garden, and surrounding both a series of dining rooms (*triclinia*), kitchens, bedrooms, and storerooms. Wall paintings, mosaics, and small sculptures filled the rooms. Pompeii has dozens of intact *domus* houses; the most luxurious — the House of the Faun, the House of the Vettii — convey the scale of elite Roman domestic display.

Outside the city walls, wealthy Romans owned **villas** — country residences attached to large agricultural estates called **latifundia**. Latifundia were worked by enslaved labor; they produced the wine, olive oil, and grain that fed the empire's cities and made fortunes for senatorial families. The emperor Hadrian's villa at Tivoli, just outside Rome, included a small replica of the Egyptian city of Canopus and pavilions inspired by every region of the empire — a country residence built to remember an emperor's travels.

The non-elite majority lived in different conditions. Roman cities were dense — Rome itself may have reached one million inhabitants in the first century CE — and the urban poor lived in **insulae** ("islands"), apartment buildings of three to five stories, often poorly built, frequently catching fire. The juvenalian poet Juvenal complained about the impossibility of sleep in a Roman insula: the carts rattling at night, the shouting, the constant fear of collapse, the smoke from a downstairs apartment threatening to suffocate the upper floors. The lower classes ate from public food shops (*thermopolia*) — fast-food counters that served cooked stews and breads — because most insula apartments lacked kitchens. They drank in *tabernae*. They washed at public baths. The infrastructure of Roman urban life was substantially designed to compensate for what private apartments couldn't provide.

Rural life was different again. Most of the empire's population were farmers — free smallholders, tenant farmers, or enslaved agricultural laborers. They lived in small villages; their lives were rhythmed by the agricultural year; they paid taxes to the local Roman authorities and saw little of the empire that ruled them.

## Concept 2 — Slavery and gladiators

Roman slavery was massive in scale and central to the empire's economy. Estimates of the enslaved fraction of the population vary; reasonable modern estimates place it at perhaps 10–25% in the early imperial period (10% empire-wide, perhaps 25% in Italy and the wealthier provinces). Some forty percent of the population of Rome itself may have been enslaved or formerly enslaved at the height of the empire. The empire was, in raw demographic terms, one of history's largest slave societies.

### Becoming enslaved

A person became enslaved by several routes. **Capture in war** was the most common. Roman conquests reliably produced thousands or tens of thousands of new enslaved people; the conquest of Epirus in 168 BCE produced 150,000 enslaved Greeks in a single transaction. **Birth into slavery** was the second route — the children of enslaved mothers were enslaved at birth, regardless of who the father was. **Piracy and kidnapping** flooded the slave markets when wartime supply was low; the Greek island of Delos became one of the largest slave markets of the late Republic, reportedly trading 10,000 people a day at its peak. **Debt** could enslave a free person, though debt-slavery for Roman citizens was abolished in the early Republic. **Sentencing to slavery** could result from criminal conviction — most famously, those condemned to the gladiatorial schools or to the mines.

### Living enslaved

Enslaved people performed almost every kind of labor in the Roman world. **Agricultural slavery** on the *latifundia* was probably the worst — long hours, harsh discipline, bad food, short lives. **Mine slavery** was worse still — sentences to the silver mines of Spain or the marble quarries of Italy were often effective death sentences. **Urban household slavery** was, by comparison, less harsh: enslaved cooks, servants, tutors, clerks, doctors, secretaries, stewards lived in the homes of their owners and could rise to positions of substantial responsibility. **Skilled slavery** — Greek-educated tutors, accountants, scribes — was sometimes profitable for the owner and tolerable for the enslaved person. The conditions varied enormously by occupation, era, and the individual character of the enslaver.

### Manumission and freedmen

Roman slavery had a feature unusual among ancient slave systems: a relatively well-developed mechanism for **manumission** — the freeing of enslaved people. Owners freed enslaved individuals for many reasons: as a reward for long service, as a gesture of personal favor, in their wills, or — crucially — because manumission could be financially advantageous for the owner. A freed slave became a **freedman** (*libertus*) or **freedwoman** (*liberta*), gained Roman citizenship in many cases (though with some restrictions), and remained socially attached to their former owner as a *patron-client* relationship that often included continued work and gifts.

The manumission system created a class of formerly enslaved people who could rise economically and (over generations) socially. Freedmen ran businesses, owned property, accumulated wealth, and occasionally rose to positions of considerable influence. The emperor Claudius's chief financial minister, **Pallas**, was a freedman; he became one of the richest men in the empire. The novelist Petronius's *Satyricon* features the boorish, newly rich freedman Trimalchio as the central comic figure — a character whose vulgarity is also a sign of upward mobility.

The system did not soften slavery's brutality at the level of daily life. It provided an exit for some individuals while leaving the institution intact.

### Spartacus

Slave revolts were rare in the Roman world, despite the scale of the institution. The most famous, the revolt led by **Spartacus** from 73 to 71 BCE, was an unusual case. Spartacus was a Thracian gladiator, trained at the gladiatorial school at Capua. In 73 BCE he and about seventy other gladiators escaped, armed themselves, and were soon joined by tens of thousands of other enslaved people across southern Italy. The rebel army defeated several Roman legions before being crushed by an army under **Marcus Licinius Crassus** in 71 BCE. Spartacus died in battle. His surviving followers — perhaps 6,000 of them — were crucified along the Appian Way from Capua to Rome, the bodies left for months as a warning. The revolt did not change Roman slavery. It did become — much later — one of history's most-cited symbols of resistance to enslavement.

### Gladiators

The gladiatorial games were the most visible institution of Roman slavery for the audience that watched them, and one of the most peculiar institutions of Roman culture. **Gladiators** were professional fighters — overwhelmingly enslaved people, condemned criminals, or (occasionally) free men who chose the life — who fought each other and wild animals in public arenas. The largest such arena was the **Colosseum** in Rome (formally the Flavian Amphitheater, completed around 80 CE under the emperor Titus, with a capacity of about 50,000 spectators). The games were public spectacles; they were free or low-cost to attend; emperors and wealthy patrons paid for them as gifts to the populace, advertising their generosity.

The cultural meaning of the gladiatorial games is contested. They provided entertainment to a population that had lost the political agency of the Republic and had fewer outlets for civic engagement. They displayed Roman martial values — courage, skill, endurance under extreme conditions — in stylized form. They were, the modern viewer cannot help but notice, **mass executions for entertainment**. Some gladiators became celebrities; their names appeared in graffiti across Pompeii, with women's romantic boasts about them on tavern walls. Most gladiators died in the arena or in training, in their twenties.

The games persisted for centuries. They became less common (though not abolished) under the Christian emperors of the fourth and fifth centuries CE; the last recorded gladiatorial combats date to the early fifth century. Animal fights persisted longer.

## Concept 3 — Trade, taxes, conquest

The Roman Empire was the largest economic unit the Mediterranean world had yet produced. At its height, it included perhaps 50–70 million people. Its economy depended on three institutions that worked together: long-distance trade, systematic taxation, and a continuous (if increasingly performative) cycle of conquest.

### Trade

Roman trade routes connected the empire to itself and to the worlds beyond it. Within the empire, the Mediterranean was a Roman lake — the saying *mare nostrum* ("our sea") was, by the first century CE, accurate. Wheat from Egypt and North Africa fed the city of Rome. Wine from Italy and Spain was shipped to Gaul, Britain, and Germany. Olive oil from Spain fueled the lamps of the empire. Pottery from Gaul reached Britain. Marble from Greece and Italy faced public buildings across the empire.

Beyond the empire's borders, Roman trade reached startling distances. **Indian Ocean** trade, conducted from Egyptian Red Sea ports through monsoon-driven sailing routes, brought Indian pepper, cotton, gemstones, and ivory to Rome; Roman gold coins from this period have been found at archaeological sites in southern India. **Silk Road** trade reached the empire from China, with silk reaching Rome via Parthian or Indian intermediaries; Pliny the Elder complained about the empire's silver and gold draining east in payment. **Baltic** trade brought amber. **African** trade brought ivory, lions for the games, and people.

The trade was supported by Roman infrastructure. **Roads** — perhaps 50,000 miles of paved highways — connected the empire's cities. **Bridges**, **aqueducts**, **ports**, and **lighthouses** kept the network functional. Roman engineering of these public works has been admired ever since; many Roman roads and aqueducts are still in service today.

### Taxes

The empire's expenses — the legions, the bureaucracy, the public works, the *annona* (subsidized grain for the poor of Rome), the games — were paid for by tax. Taxation came in several forms. **Land tax** was the largest revenue source, levied on agricultural production across the empire. **Poll tax** was paid by individuals. **Customs duties** were collected at provincial borders and major harbors. **Sales taxes** were collected on slaves and certain other goods. **Inheritance taxes** funded the army's pension fund.

Tax collection was conducted, until the early imperial period, largely through **publicans** — private contractors who bid for the right to collect taxes in a region and kept whatever they could squeeze beyond the bid amount. The publican system was famously corrupt. Augustus and his successors gradually replaced it with direct administration through Roman officials, which improved efficiency but did not eliminate corruption. The empire's tax burden was real but not overwhelming for most farmers under most circumstances; tax revolts were rare, though they did occur in periods of stress.

### Conquest

The Roman Republic had been built by conquest, and the early Empire continued the pattern. Augustus added Egypt, parts of Spain, and the Alpine regions. Tiberius and Claudius added Britain, Mauretania, and Thrace. Trajan (reigned 98–117 CE) reached the empire's maximum extent with the conquest of Dacia (modern Romania) and a brief occupation of Mesopotamia.

Conquest paid for itself. New territories provided land for veterans, tribute for the treasury, slaves for the markets, and prestige for the emperor. By the second century CE, however, the limits of profitable expansion had been reached. Hadrian (reigned 117–138 CE) abandoned Trajan's Mesopotamian conquests as too expensive to defend. The empire shifted to a defensive posture, building **frontier walls** — most famously, **Hadrian's Wall** across northern Britain (built starting 122 CE) and the **Limes Germanicus** along the Rhine and Danube — to mark and defend the empire's edges. The shift to defense changed the empire's economic logic. Without continued conquest, the supply of new enslaved people slowed, the ideological justification of expansionist emperors weakened, and the army became a permanent fiscal burden rather than a self-financing engine.

The third century CE would expose the consequences. **The Crisis of the Third Century** (235–284 CE) saw fifty different emperors in fifty years, civil wars, plague, and barbarian incursions across the frontiers. The empire survived — Diocletian (reigned 284–305 CE) and Constantine (reigned 306–337 CE) restructured it for survival — but it never fully recovered the easy expansion of the first two centuries. ## Concept 4 — Religion in the Roman Empire

The Roman Empire was religiously plural in a way that no monotheistic empire would later be. Romans worshipped a vast pantheon of gods — Jupiter (sky-king), Juno (queen of the gods), Neptune (sea), Mars (war), Venus (love), Minerva (wisdom), Mercury (commerce), Diana (hunt), Apollo (sun and arts), Vesta (hearth), Ceres (grain), Bacchus (wine) — many adapted directly from Greek originals (Zeus → Jupiter, Hera → Juno, etc.). Below the high gods were the *lares* and *penates* (household gods) and the *genius* (the divine spirit of a person, place, or family).

Roman religious practice was heavily ritualistic. Public ceremonies were conducted by **priestly colleges** — the most important being the **pontifex maximus** (chief priest of Rome, an office Augustus took as part of his concentration of power). State religion focused on correct ritual rather than personal belief; the gods were placated by accurate performance of sacrifice and ceremony. The political and the religious were not distinguished — Roman magistrates conducted religious functions; Roman religious officials had political authority.

### The Vestal Virgins

Among the most distinctive Roman religious institutions was the **Vestal Virgins** — six priestesses dedicated to the goddess **Vesta**, the deity of the hearth and the protective fire of the city. The Vestals lived in the *Atrium Vestae* in the Roman Forum. They tended the sacred fire of Vesta, which was believed to embody the safety of Rome itself; if it went out, the city was in mortal danger. The Vestals took thirty-year vows of chastity (ten years of training, ten of service, ten of teaching) and were the only Roman women with substantial public authority. They could own property, make wills, give testimony in court without male representation, sit in privileged seats at games, and travel with lictors. A Vestal who broke her vow of chastity was buried alive — a punishment that was rarely (but not never) carried out. The Vestals served as a kind of sacralization of Roman femininity: the women whose public role embodied the virtues that elite Roman matrons were expected to perform privately.

### The imperial cult

After Augustus, emperors were progressively divinized. **Augustus** himself accepted divine honors in the eastern provinces (where Greek tradition was already comfortable with divine kings) and the title *divi filius* ("son of the divine [Caesar]") in Rome. After his death, he was officially deified by the Senate. The pattern continued: dead emperors were typically deified by senatorial decree; the cult of the dead emperor became part of the official state religion. The **imperial cult** — temples to the deified emperors, sacrifices on their birthdays, oaths sworn in their names — was a unifying religious institution across the empire's provinces. It was also a political loyalty test. To refuse to participate in the imperial cult was, in effect, to refuse loyalty to the empire.

### Religious diversity and the mystery cults

The empire absorbed and tolerated a wide range of religions. **Egyptian gods** — Isis, Osiris, Anubis, Serapis — had popular followings across the empire; the cult of Isis was especially strong among urban women. The **cult of Mithras** — a male-only mystery religion of Persian origin, particularly popular in the Roman army — built underground temples (*mithraea*) at military bases from Britain to Syria. The **cult of Magna Mater** (the Phrygian Cybele) was officially imported to Rome during the Punic Wars. The **cult of Bacchus / Dionysus** offered ecstatic ritual experience. **Druidic** religion in Gaul and Britain was suppressed (in part because of human sacrifice, in part because the druids were political organizers for resistance to Roman rule). **Judaism** was tolerated as an ancient ancestral religion of an entire people, though Roman authorities clashed repeatedly with Jewish national-religious aspirations — the Jewish revolts of 66–73 CE, 115–117 CE, and 132–135 CE all ended in mass destruction and dispersal. After the third revolt, Jews were largely expelled from Jerusalem and the city was rebuilt as a Roman colony.

### Christianity

Christianity emerged in the early first century CE as a small Jewish sect in Roman Palestine. Its founder, **Jesus of Nazareth**, was a Galilean Jewish preacher; he was crucified by the Roman governor Pontius Pilate around 30 CE on charges that mixed religious blasphemy (in Jewish terms) with political sedition (in Roman terms — "King of the Jews" was the formal charge nailed to the cross). His followers — initially a small Jewish-Christian community in Jerusalem under his brother James — were transformed by the missionary work of **Paul of Tarsus** (c. 5–65 CE), a Hellenized Jewish convert who carried the message to Gentile (non-Jewish) audiences across the empire and detached the new religion from Jewish ritual law (kosher food, circumcision, Sabbath observance). By the late first century CE, Christian communities existed in most major cities of the eastern empire; by the third century CE, in nearly every province.

Roman authorities periodically persecuted Christians. The reasons were political, not theological. Christians refused to participate in the imperial cult; they were suspect as a secret society; their refusal to acknowledge any of Rome's gods looked, to Roman observers, like atheism. Persecutions under **Nero** (after the 64 CE fire of Rome, blamed on Christians), **Domitian**, **Decius** (mid-third century), and **Diocletian** (early fourth century) produced famous martyrs but did not significantly slow the religion's spread.

The decisive change came in 312 CE. The Roman emperor **Constantine**, on the eve of the Battle of the Milvian Bridge against his rival Maxentius, reportedly experienced a vision of a Christian symbol with the words *In hoc signo vinces* — "in this sign you will conquer." He won the battle; he attributed the victory to the Christian God; he issued the **Edict of Milan** in 313 CE, ending Christian persecution and granting the religion legal status equal to other religions; he eventually converted personally and began favoring Christian institutions with imperial patronage. Within a century, Christianity had moved from persecuted minority to official religion (under Theodosius I in the late fourth century CE). The Empire that had crucified Jesus had become his church's principal patron.

The transition mattered enormously. It changed the religion (Christianity in 200 CE was a movement of urban poor, women, and social marginals; Christianity in 400 CE was the religion of emperors, bishops, and aristocrats). It changed the empire (Roman institutions absorbed Christian theology and Christian institutions absorbed Roman administrative structure). It changed the world (the Christianity that spread north and west into post-Roman Europe across the next millennium was a Roman-imperial Christianity, complete with Latin liturgy, hierarchical church structure, and the absorbed legal traditions of Roman law).

## Concept 5 — Roman culture and the people of the empire

The Roman Empire at its height included people speaking dozens of languages, descending from dozens of ancestral cultures, practicing dozens of religions. The empire's policy was usually accommodation: provincial elites retained their local cultures while adopting enough Roman institutions and Latin to participate in imperial administration. Provincial languages — Greek in the east, Aramaic in the Levant, Egyptian and Coptic in Egypt, Punic in North Africa, Celtic languages in Gaul and Britain — survived alongside Latin for centuries. Roman citizenship spread gradually through the imperial period, and was finally extended to all free male inhabitants of the empire by the **Edict of Caracalla** in 212 CE.

Roman culture was famously syncretic — the empire absorbed what it conquered. Greek philosophy, literature, and art remained the empire's dominant intellectual reference points; Roman education was conducted bilingually in Latin and Greek; Roman gods were equated with Greek gods. Egyptian religion, Persian mystery cults, Jewish ethics all flowed into the cultural mix. The Roman literary canon — Virgil's *Aeneid*, Horace's odes, Ovid's *Metamorphoses*, Tacitus's *Annals*, Marcus Aurelius's *Meditations* — drew on Greek models while producing distinctively Roman work. Roman engineering — concrete construction, the dome, the aqueduct, the road network — was the empire's most enduring material legacy.

Roman law was the empire's intellectual achievement that would most profoundly shape later history. Roman jurists across centuries compiled, classified, and refined legal principles. The **Corpus Juris Civilis** of the emperor Justinian I (compiled in the sixth century CE in the eastern empire, after the western half had fallen) preserved Roman legal thinking and became the foundation of the civil law tradition that still governs most of continental Europe and Latin America today. American common law, while differently structured, was substantially influenced by Roman legal concepts as well.

The peoples of the empire experienced Roman rule in many ways. For the urban elites of provincial cities, the empire was an opportunity — Roman citizenship, Roman law, Roman roads, Roman trade. For frontier farmers, the empire was a presence the local garrison reminded them of. For enslaved laborers in the *latifundia*, the empire was the system that owned them. For Christian congregations in the second and third centuries, the empire was the persecutor. For Christian congregations in the fifth century, the empire was the increasingly fragile patron whose collapse would leave them on their own. The story of Roman rule does not have a single point of view.

The thing to watch for, going forward, is the temptation to treat the Roman Empire as a single experience. It was experienced very differently by the urban elites of Rome, by frontier farmers in Britain, by enslaved miners in Spain, by Christian congregations in Antioch, by Jewish villagers in Galilee. ## Key terms

- **Christianity.** The religion that emerged from first-century CE Judaism and became the official religion of the Roman Empire by the late fourth century.
- **Domus.** The Roman single-family townhouse and, by extension, the family that lived in it.
- **Edict of Caracalla.** The 212 CE proclamation that extended Roman citizenship to all free male inhabitants of the empire.
- **Edict of Milan.** The 313 CE proclamation by Constantine and Licinius that ended the persecution of Christians and granted the religion legal recognition.
- **Gladiator.** A trained fighter — usually enslaved or condemned — who fought in public games.
- **Imperial cult.** The religious worship of deified emperors, a major unifying institution of the Roman state religion.
- **Insula.** A multi-story Roman urban apartment building, typically housing the urban poor.
- **Latifundia.** Large agricultural estates worked by enslaved laborers.
- **Manumission.** The legal freeing of an enslaved person.
- **Mos maiorum.** "The way of the ancestors," the Roman ideal of conformity to ancestral custom.
- **Paterfamilias.** The eldest male in a Roman family, holder of legal authority over his dependents.
- **Patron-client system.** The Roman institution of formal mutual obligations between a wealthy patron and dependent clients.
- **Persecution.** Roman state action against Christians, especially intense under Nero, Decius, and Diocletian.
- **Salutatio.** The morning ritual at which a Roman patron received his clients.
- **Spartacus.** Thracian gladiator who led a major slave revolt in Italy from 73 to 71 BCE.
- **Vestal Virgins.** Six priestesses dedicated to Vesta who tended Rome's sacred fire and held unusual public authority.

## Discussion questions

1. The Roman family's *paterfamilias* held extraordinary legal authority but rarely used the most extreme powers. What does this gap between formal authority and practical use tell you about how to read Roman legal texts as evidence of Roman life?

2. Roman slavery was both massive in scale and equipped with relatively well-developed manumission and freedman institutions. Build the case that the manumission system softened the institution. Then build the case that it strengthened it (by providing an exit valve that reduced pressure for abolition).

3. The gladiatorial games are difficult to read for modern viewers. Build the strongest case for understanding them on their own terms — what they meant to Roman audiences and why an otherwise sophisticated civilization tolerated them.

4. Christianity's rise from small persecuted sect to official religion happened over roughly three centuries. Identify the institutional features that made the religion portable, persuasive, and resistant to suppression. Compare it to the spread of Islam (Chapter 14) — what features did the two religions share, and where did they diverge?

5. *Cross-chapter synthesis.* Chapter 13 (Empires of Faith) follows the Christian world after the fall of the Western Roman Empire. Based on what you know about Roman institutions, predict which features of Roman administration, law, and culture will be carried into post-Roman Christian institutions and which will be lost.
