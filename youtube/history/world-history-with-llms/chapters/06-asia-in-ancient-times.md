# Chapter 6 — Asia in Ancient Times
*Four Civilizations, Four Solutions to the Problem of Order.*

## Three suggested titles

- Asia in Ancient Times: From Shang Oracle Bones to the Gupta Dynasty
- China, the Steppe, Korea, Japan, India — Four Worlds in One Continent
- Confucius, the Buddha, Ashoka, and the Long Asian Bronze and Iron Ages

## Chapter overview

By the end of this chapter you will know how four broadly distinct ancient civilizations developed across Asia: in China, on the Eurasian steppe, on the Korean peninsula and Japanese archipelago, and on the Indian subcontinent. You will know the major Chinese dynasties of the ancient era — Shang (c. 1600–1050 BCE), Zhou (c. 1046–256 BCE), and Qin (221–206 BCE) — and how each one solved the political problem of governing a large agricultural population. You will know the **Hundred Schools of Thought** that emerged during the Warring States period, including Confucianism, Daoism, and Legalism, and how each gave rulers a different theory of how to maintain order. You will know **Qin Shi Huang** and the political revolution of imperial unification in 221 BCE. You will know the steppe nomadic civilizations that lived alongside settled China and the constant interchange between the two. You will know how Korea and Japan developed in the long shadow of Chinese influence while preserving their own institutions. You will know the **Aryan migration** into northern India around 1500 BCE, the development of **Vedic religion** into **Brahmanism**, the *varna* (caste) system that grew out of it, the life and teachings of the **Buddha** around 563 BCE, the **Mauryan Empire** under **Ashoka**, and the **Gupta Dynasty** that produced India's classical age. By the end, you will see four parallel solutions to the universal problem of how a large, diverse population can be governed.

## Opening case — Confucius among the warring states, c. 500 BCE

In the year 497 BCE, a fifty-five-year-old Chinese teacher left his home state of Lu in eastern China and began a fourteen-year wandering. His name was **Kong Fuzi** — Master Kong — known to the West by the Latinized form of that name, **Confucius**. He had served briefly as a minister of justice in Lu and was widely respected as a scholar of the ancient Zhou dynasty's rituals and texts. He believed that those rituals and texts contained the formula for restoring order to a Chinese world that was, in his time, falling apart.

It was falling apart in a specific way. The **Zhou dynasty** had ruled most of what is now central and northern China since 1046 BCE. Around 771 BCE, after a nomadic raid sacked the Zhou capital, the dynasty had relocated east and lost effective control of its own territories. By Confucius's time the Zhou king was a ceremonial figure. The real power lay with seven or eight large regional states that fought each other constantly. Armies grew. Death tolls grew. The chivalric codes that had once constrained Bronze Age warfare were abandoned. The era is called the **Warring States** for a reason. Confucius walked from court to court — Wei, Song, Chen, Cai, Chu — offering rulers a vision of stable governance built on ritual, virtue, and the cultivation of moral character. None of them adopted his program. He returned to Lu in 484 BCE and died there in 479 BCE without having held real political office for more than a few years total.

Three centuries later, his teachings would become the foundational ideology of Chinese imperial government. They would shape the political and educational institutions of China for the next 2,400 years and spread to Korea, Vietnam, and Japan as the basis of those countries' state systems too. Confucius failed in his own lifetime. He may have been the most successful failure in human history.

This chapter is the long story of how civilizations across Asia — the Chinese world, the steppes north of it, Korea and Japan to its east, the Indian subcontinent to its southwest — solved (or partly solved) the problem of organizing large populations under stable institutions. The solutions were different in each region. The problems were the same. Governing a million people requires institutions that fifty hunter-gatherers do not need. Ancient Asia produced four substantially different answers.

## Concept 1 — Early Dynastic China: Shang and Zhou

Chinese civilization developed in the river valleys of the **Yellow River** in the north and, somewhat later, the **Yangtze River** in the south. By 8500 BCE, Neolithic cultures along the Yellow River were cultivating millet; by 8000 BCE, cultures along the Yangtze were cultivating rice. Over the next several thousand years, these cultures developed pottery, jade-working, walled settlements, and increasingly stratified social organization. By 2000 BCE, the late Neolithic cultures of the central plain were producing bronze, building palaces of rammed earth, and constructing elaborate burial sites. Chinese tradition names a first dynasty — the **Xia** — said to have ruled from around 2070 BCE to 1600 BCE. There is no Xia writing, and archaeologists are divided about which excavated sites should be identified with the Xia. What is clear is that, by around 1600 BCE, the Yellow River valley was producing organized states with kings, armies, and complex urban centers.

### The Shang (c. 1600–1050 BCE)

The first Chinese dynasty for which we have indisputable evidence is the **Shang**. Shang power was concentrated along the lower Yellow River. The dynasty's capital cities (the most fully excavated is Anyang, occupied from about 1300 BCE) included palaces, temples, royal cemeteries, and bronze foundries.

The Shang produced two innovations that defined Chinese civilization for the next three thousand years.

**Writing.** Shang scribes inscribed Chinese characters on **oracle bones** — turtle plastrons and ox shoulder blades that diviners used in religious ritual. The diviner inscribed a question (about the harvest, a coming battle, an heir's birth, a hunting trip), heated the bone until it cracked, and read the cracks as the gods' or ancestors' answer. Then he inscribed the answer next to the question. Tens of thousands of inscribed oracle bones have been recovered. They are the world's earliest extensive Chinese writing. The Shang script was **logographic** — each character represented a word or concept, not a sound — and the system was already complex by the time it appears in the archaeological record. The Chinese writing system has been used continuously from the Shang to the present, making it the oldest continuously used writing system in the world.

**Bronze.** The Shang produced bronze ritual vessels of extraordinary technical and artistic sophistication. These were used in ancestor-worship ceremonies; food and wine offerings to royal ancestors were placed in them. The largest weighed hundreds of pounds. Their casting required organized labor, mining, transport, and metallurgical knowledge on a scale that prefigured later imperial bureaucracy.

Shang religion centered on the worship of royal ancestors and a high god called **Di**. The king was the chief priest, the only person authorized to communicate with the most distant ancestors. **Human sacrifice** was practiced on a large scale at royal funerals; tomb excavations have recovered the bones of sacrificed servants, prisoners, and (in one case) more than 9,000 sacrificial victims attributed to the reign of King Wu Ding. Among the most extraordinary Shang individuals is **Lady Fu Hao**, a wife of Wu Ding who was a general and high priestess; her tomb at Anyang, untouched by looters, contained over 1,500 grave goods including bronze vessels, jade carvings, weapons, and the bones of sixteen sacrificed servants.

### The Zhou (c. 1046–256 BCE) and the Mandate of Heaven

In 1046 BCE, the Shang's western vassals — the **Zhou**, originally based in the Wei River valley in modern Shaanxi province — rebelled and overthrew the dynasty. The Zhou inherited Shang script, bronze technology, and ritual organization, but they introduced a major theological innovation that would shape Chinese political thought for the next three thousand years: the **Mandate of Heaven** (*tianming*).

The Mandate of Heaven held that **Heaven** (*Tian*), a higher cosmic power than the personal gods of the Shang, granted the right to rule to a virtuous ruler and withdrew it from a corrupt one. The Zhou justified their overthrow of the Shang by arguing that the late Shang kings had become despotic, that Heaven had withdrawn its mandate, and that Heaven had bestowed it on the Zhou. This was, formally, a theological doctrine. In practice, it served as both a legitimating ideology (the current dynasty rules because Heaven approves) and a built-in justification for revolt (a dynasty that no longer rules well no longer has the mandate). It made every Chinese dynasty's political legitimacy contingent on its actual performance — natural disasters, peasant rebellions, military failures, all could be read as signs that the mandate was being withdrawn. The doctrine was uniquely durable: it survived dozens of dynastic transitions, including the conquests by non-Chinese steppe peoples (Mongols, Manchus) who claimed the mandate as conquerors.

The early Zhou ruled through a decentralized **feudal system** in which the king granted lands to relatives and trusted nobles, who served as regional lords in exchange for tribute and military service. The system worked well in the Western Zhou period (c. 1046–771 BCE). It worked less well after 771 BCE, when a nomadic raid sacked the Zhou capital and the dynasty relocated east to Luoyang. The **Eastern Zhou** (771–256 BCE) saw the Zhou king's authority gradually evaporate while the regional states grew larger, more militarized, and more autonomous. The era divides into two halves:

- The **Spring and Autumn period** (770–476 BCE), named for the *Spring and Autumn Annals*, a chronicle of the period. Many small states; chivalric warfare; the gradual erosion of Zhou central authority.
- The **Warring States period** (475–221 BCE). Seven or eight large regional states; conscript armies of hundreds of thousands; iron weapons; the crossbow; the abandonment of chivalric rules; constant total war until one state — Qin — won everything.

## Concept 2 — The Hundred Schools of Thought

The Warring States period was disastrous as politics. As intellectual history, it was an axial age. The collapse of Zhou central authority forced rulers, scholars, and administrators to ask basic questions about the nature of order and the legitimate basis of government. The traditions that emerged from this competition — the **Hundred Schools of Thought** — shaped East Asian political thought for the next two thousand years.

### Confucianism

**Kong Fuzi** (Confucius, traditionally 551–479 BCE) was a teacher and minor official from the state of Lu. His teachings were collected by his disciples, generations after his death, in the **Analects**. The central Confucian claim: order in society follows from *moral cultivation* in individuals, especially in rulers. A ruler who lives virtuously and observes proper rituals draws the loyalty of the population the way (in Confucius's metaphor) the polestar draws the orbits of the other stars. A ruler who governs only by laws and punishments will produce subjects who avoid punishment without acquiring virtue.

Confucian social philosophy is built on **five relationships**: ruler and subject, father and son, husband and wife, elder brother and younger sibling, friend and friend. In four of the five, one party is the authority and the other is the subordinate; both have obligations. The authority owes the subordinate **ren** — generosity, empathy, benevolence; the subordinate owes the authority obedience and respect. When all five relationships are properly maintained, society functions. When any of them is corrupted, disorder follows.

Later Confucians extended the system. **Mengzi** (Mencius, c. 372–289 BCE) argued that human nature is fundamentally good and that the state's job is to cultivate the goodness already present. **Xunzi** (c. 310–235 BCE), writing during the worst of the Warring States violence, argued the opposite — that human nature is fundamentally selfish and that ritual education must impose order on a population that would otherwise tear itself apart. The two views remain in productive tension within the Confucian tradition.

### Daoism

**Daoism** (sometimes Taoism) is the philosophical-religious tradition associated with the texts attributed to **Laozi** (the *Dao De Jing*, "Classic of the Way and Virtue") and **Zhuangzi**. The historicity of Laozi himself is debated; the texts are probably anthologies compiled over centuries.

The central Daoist claim is that the *dao* — "the way" — is the underlying current of the universe, present in all things. Human beings live well by aligning themselves with the *dao* rather than by imposing artificial structures on the world. The political implication is **wuwei** — "non-action" or "effortless action" — a doctrine of minimalist government in which the wise ruler intervenes as little as possible and lets natural patterns work themselves out. Daoism celebrates simplicity, humility, the cultivation of inner stillness, and a deep skepticism of grand human projects. It is in many ways the opposite of Confucianism — Confucius wanted to remake the world through ritual and moral effort; Laozi wanted to step out of the world's way.

Daoist religious institutions developed later, during the Han dynasty and after. As philosophy, Daoism was already widely taught in the late Zhou period and influenced everyone from poets to court ministers.

### Legalism

The third major school was **Legalism**, represented most influentially by **Han Feizi** (c. 280–233 BCE) and the earlier statesman **Lord Shang Yang** (c. 390–338 BCE). The Legalist claim was that governance should be based on **clear written laws**, **strict rewards and punishments**, and **the maximization of state power** — not on moral cultivation, not on the *dao*, not on inherited aristocratic privilege. The Legalist's ideal subject was obedient and productive; the Legalist's ideal state was a "rich country and a strong army." Legalism distrusted both Confucian ethics (too soft) and Daoist quietism (too disengaged). It was the philosophy of bureaucrats and military reformers.

Legalism became state ideology in the western state of **Qin** under Lord Shang's reforms in the fourth century BCE. The Qin reorganized society around merit and law, creating a centralized state that proved more efficient at war than its rivals. Legalism is the philosophical background of the unification of China by the Qin in 221 BCE.

The three schools — Confucian moral cultivation, Daoist quietism, Legalist coercive efficiency — would coexist, compete, and intermix in Chinese governance for the next two millennia. No major Chinese dynasty was purely one. Most were Confucian in their public ideology and Legalist in their administrative practice, with Daoism providing the intellectual escape valve for individuals tired of court life.

## Concept 3 — Qin unification and the first emperor

In 221 BCE, the western state of **Qin** completed a decade-long campaign of conquest that had eliminated all six of its rival states. The Qin king **Ying Zheng** declared himself **Qin Shi Huang** — "First Sovereign Emperor of Qin" — and inaugurated the Chinese imperial era that would last (with interruptions) until 1912 CE.

The Qin's success had been built on Legalist reforms. Lord Shang's program in the fourth century BCE had abolished hereditary aristocracy in favor of bureaucratic appointment by merit; standardized weights, measures, and law; rewarded military service with state-allocated land; punished failure ruthlessly; and concentrated military and economic power in the king's hands. These reforms produced a state that was less elegant culturally than its rivals (the Qin were widely considered "barbaric" by the eastern states) but more efficient at every measurable function of war.

Once unified, **Qin Shi Huang** applied Legalist principles to the entire Chinese world. He divided the empire into thirty-six **commanderies**, each administered by a centrally appointed governor rather than by hereditary lords. He standardized **the Chinese script**, mandating that all official documents use the Qin form (a script reform that ensured a literate Chinese could read documents from anywhere in the empire — and, crucially, that scholars two thousand years later could still read those documents). He standardized **weights and measures**, **coinage**, **axle widths** for carts (so wheel ruts were uniform across the empire's roads), and **legal codes**. He confiscated weapons from the conquered aristocrats and melted them down into ceremonial bronze statues. He launched mass deportations of defeated noble families to the new capital near modern Xi'an, weakening their regional power bases.

He built infrastructure on a massive scale. **Roads** radiated from the capital across the empire; tens of thousands of miles were built or improved. **The Great Wall** — actually a connection and extension of earlier defensive walls — was constructed across the northern frontier as protection against the steppe nomads, requiring hundreds of thousands of conscript laborers (popular memory holds that countless workers died in the construction; the historicity of the specific death tolls is debated but the scale of forced labor is not).

He ordered, in 213 BCE, a notorious **burning of books** — a campaign to suppress philosophical texts (especially Confucian ones) that were used to criticize his rule, sparing only technical works on agriculture and medicine. He is also said to have buried Confucian scholars alive, though some modern scholars see this as later anti-Qin propaganda by the Han dynasty that succeeded his.

He spent the last years of his reign hunting an elixir of immortality. The hunt failed. He died in 210 BCE, on a tour of the empire, possibly of mercury poisoning from the alchemical preparations he had been consuming in pursuit of the elixir. His tomb complex near Xi'an — discovered in 1974 when farmers digging a well happened upon part of it — covers nearly forty square miles and contains the **Terracotta Army**: more than 8,000 life-sized clay soldiers, hundreds of horses, dozens of chariots, all arranged in military formation to guard the emperor in the afterlife. The central tomb chamber, said by ancient sources to contain rivers of mercury and an inlaid map of the empire, has not been excavated.

The Qin dynasty did not survive its founder. **Qin Shi Huang's** son was a weak ruler. The empire's harsh laws had built up grievances among peasants, conscripts, and former aristocratic families. By 206 BCE — only fifteen years after the unification — peasant rebellions had toppled the dynasty. The general who emerged as the new emperor, **Liu Bang**, founded the **Han dynasty** (206 BCE–220 CE), which kept the Qin's bureaucratic centralization but softened its laws and adopted Confucianism as the state ideology. The Han dynasty would last over four hundred years and become the Chinese civilization's classical age. The ethnic group still called the **Han Chinese** today takes its name from this dynasty. But the unification — the political form, the script, the road system, the bureaucratic state — that the Han inherited and refined was the Qin's invention.

## Concept 4 — The steppe nomads

North of China lay a vast, open landscape — the **Eurasian Steppe** — running from Manchuria across Mongolia, Siberia, central Asia, and into eastern Europe. The steppe is too dry and too cold for productive agriculture across most of its area, but its grasslands are excellent for pastoralism. Beginning around 1500 BCE, climate change reduced rainfall in the eastern steppe and pushed the peoples there from settled mixed agriculture toward nomadic livestock herding.

The result was a series of nomadic peoples whose lives revolved around herds of sheep, goats, horses, camels, and cattle. They lived in portable felt tents (later called yurts or *gers*). They moved seasonally between summer and winter pastures. They drank fermented mare's milk (*kumiss*) and ate dried meat. The technological development that defined them was **mounted archery**: a steppe warrior could shoot a composite bow from horseback at full gallop, accurately, while carrying enough food and water to remain in the field for weeks. Steppe armies were therefore small relative to the populations of settled civilizations but enormously mobile and difficult to engage on favorable terms.

The first organized nomadic confederation that engaged systematically with China was the **Xiongnu**, who emerged in the third century BCE as a unified power on the northern steppe. The Xiongnu confederation extended from Manchuria to central Asia; its leader, the *chanyu*, commanded perhaps several hundred thousand mounted warriors. Qin Shi Huang's Great Wall was built specifically against the Xiongnu and their predecessors. The Han dynasty fought the Xiongnu for centuries, alternating between military campaigns into the steppe and a system of "**heqin**" diplomacy in which the Han sent princesses and silk to the *chanyu* in exchange for non-aggression. The relationship was always asymmetric in style — the Chinese saw the steppe as barbarian periphery; the Xiongnu saw the Chinese as a wealthy resource pool to be raided when convenient and traded with otherwise.

The interchange went both ways. Chinese silk, lacquerware, and grain reached the steppe as tribute, plunder, and trade. Steppe horses, furs, and military techniques (especially mounted archery) reached China. The **Silk Road** — the complex of trade routes connecting China through central Asia to the Mediterranean — depended on the cooperation of steppe powers who controlled long stretches of it. Buddhism would travel along the Silk Road from India into China beginning in the first century CE, carried by traders and missionaries crossing the steppe.

The pattern of mounted nomadic civilizations on the steppe interacting with settled agricultural civilizations to their south would shape Eurasian history for the next 2,500 years. The Xiongnu's distant relatives the **Huns** would push into Europe in the fourth century CE. The **Mongols** would conquer most of the known world in the thirteenth century CE (Chapter 18). The **Manchu** would conquer China in the seventeenth century CE. The ancient Asian story includes both halves of this relationship: the cities of the Yellow River valley and the riders of the open grasslands, in tension and exchange across two thousand years.

## Concept 5 — Korea and Japan

The **Korean peninsula** is mountainous, with cold snowy winters in the north and hot wet summers in the south. The earliest humans reached Korea perhaps 30,000 years ago. By the early second millennium BCE, communities along the Daedong, Han, and Yalu rivers were practicing agriculture (rice, millet, barley) and producing bronze.

The first Korean political entity attested in written sources is **Gojoseon** ("Old Joseon"), in northern Korea and southern Manchuria. Chinese sources from the Han dynasty describe Gojoseon as a kingdom; its founding is wrapped in mythology (the legendary king Dangun, descended from a god and a bear-turned-woman, said to have founded Joseon in 2333 BCE). What is verifiable is that, by the first millennium BCE, the northern Korean peninsula was politically organized; that it traded with both China and the steppe peoples; and that in 108 BCE the Han Chinese emperor Wu invaded northern Korea, destroyed Gojoseon, and established four Chinese commanderies (the **Four Han Commanderies**) on the peninsula. These would last for nearly four centuries before being driven out by reviving Korean kingdoms.

The post-Han period saw the rise of **three Korean kingdoms** — **Goguryeo** in the north (founded c. 37 BCE), **Baekje** in the southwest (founded c. 18 BCE), and **Silla** in the southeast (founded c. 57 BCE) — that contested the peninsula for the next seven hundred years (the **Three Kingdoms** period). Buddhism arrived from China in the fourth century CE and was adopted by all three kingdoms. Chinese script, Confucian institutions, and Chinese administrative models were absorbed selectively. In 668 CE, Silla — with help from the Tang dynasty in China — defeated the other two kingdoms and unified the peninsula. The unified Korean state would persist, in various forms, until the Japanese colonization of 1910.

The **Japanese archipelago** developed on a different rhythm. The earliest known culture is the **Jōmon** (c. 14,000–300 BCE) — Paleolithic and Neolithic foragers and incipient agriculturalists who produced some of the world's earliest pottery, characterized by the cord-marked decorations from which the period takes its name. Around 300 BCE, a wave of migrants from the Korean peninsula introduced wet-rice agriculture, bronze and iron tools, and a new social organization. The **Yayoi** period (c. 300 BCE–300 CE) saw the spread of rice cultivation across western and central Japan, the formation of small chiefdoms, and the gradual development of a more stratified society.

By the third century CE, the **Yamato** clan in the Nara basin had begun consolidating power over rival chiefdoms. The Yamato kings claimed descent from the sun goddess Amaterasu (a claim the Japanese imperial family still makes today, making the line in some sense the world's oldest continuous monarchy). Buddhism arrived from Korea in the sixth century CE. Chinese script, Confucian institutions, and the Chinese imperial model were systematically imported. The early Japanese state organized itself on a Chinese model while preserving the local Shinto religion and its own social structures — a pattern of selective borrowing that would characterize Japanese cultural development through the modern era.

## Concept 6 — Ancient India

The **Indus Valley** civilization that we met in Chapter 4 collapsed around 1800 BCE. What followed is contested. The traditional account is that **Indo-European** speakers from the Eurasian steppe — calling themselves *Aryans* in their own term — migrated south through Iran into northwestern India around 1500 BCE, bringing with them a new language (Sanskrit), a new religion (the Vedic religion), and a new political order. Whether the Aryan migration was a violent invasion (the older view), a gradual filtering of pastoralists (the more common modern view), or, as some Indian scholars argue, not a migration at all but an indigenous cultural development is disputed. What is clear is that, after the Indus Valley collapse, northern India was producing Sanskrit-language religious texts — the **Vedas** — by around 1500–1200 BCE.

### Vedic religion and Brahmanism

The early Vedic religion was a polytheistic sacrificial cult. **Indra** was the warrior-god of storms; **Varuna** was the cosmic ruler; **Agni** was the fire of sacrifice; **Soma** was the personified ritual elixir. Religious ritual centered on elaborate fire sacrifices conducted by a priestly class, the **Brahmans**. Hymns were transmitted orally for centuries before being committed to writing.

By the late Vedic period (c. 800–500 BCE), the religion had developed in a more philosophical direction. The texts called the **Upanishads** introduced two ideas that would shape Indian religious thought through the modern era: **samsara**, the cycle of rebirth in which the soul moves from body to body across lifetimes, and **karma**, the principle that one's actions in this life determine the conditions of the next. The ultimate religious goal — *moksha* — was release from the cycle of rebirth through union with **Brahman**, the universal cosmic principle.

The social order that grew up around this theology was the **varna system** — the four-tier ranking that became the basis of the **caste system**. At the top stood the **Brahmans** (priests and scholars), who controlled the sacrificial rituals. Below them stood the **Kshatriyas** (warriors and rulers), the **Vaishyas** (merchants, farmers, artisans), and the **Shudras** (laborers and servants). Outside the system entirely stood the **Dalits** ("untouchables"), barred from religious participation and confined to ritually impure occupations. Karma justified the system: those born high deserved their status because of past-life merit; those born low deserved their status because of past-life failures. The system was hereditary, was reinforced by detailed rules about marriage, food, and contact, and would prove enormously durable. Caste continues to shape Indian social life today, though formal legal discrimination is prohibited and the oldest categories have splintered into thousands of subcastes.

### The Buddha and Buddhism

In the sixth century BCE, in the kingdom of Magadha in what is now northeastern India, a prince named **Siddhartha Gautama** (traditionally c. 563–483 BCE, though some scholars now favor a slightly later date) abandoned a privileged life to seek understanding of human suffering. After years of meditation and ascetic practice, he experienced an awakening under a bodhi tree and began teaching what he had learned. He came to be called the **Buddha** — "the awakened one."

The Buddha's teaching is summarized in the **Four Noble Truths**: (1) life involves suffering; (2) suffering arises from desire; (3) suffering ends with the cessation of desire; (4) the path to the cessation of desire is the **Eightfold Path** of right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration. The goal was **nirvana** — the extinction of desire and release from the cycle of rebirth.

Buddhism shared with Brahmanism the assumptions of *samsara* and *karma*. It departed sharply on social organization and ritual. The Buddha denied the religious authority of the Brahman caste. He taught that any person — high-born or low, male or female — could pursue enlightenment through the Eightfold Path. He rejected animal sacrifice. He challenged the legitimacy of the caste system itself.

Early Buddhism organized itself around monasteries (**sanghas**) of monks and (controversially, but eventually) nuns, supported by lay donations. The religion spread rapidly across northern India and, beginning in the third century BCE, into Central Asia and East Asia along the Silk Road. Two major branches developed: **Theravada Buddhism**, which preserved what it considered the Buddha's original teachings and spread to Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia; **Mahayana Buddhism**, which developed elaborate cosmologies and bodhisattva traditions and spread to China, Korea, and Japan. Buddhism would eventually decline in India itself (by the medieval period it was largely replaced by reformed Hindu traditions and, in some regions, by Islam) but would become the dominant religion across most of East and Southeast Asia.

### The Mauryan Empire (c. 322–185 BCE)

The first large-scale political unification of the Indian subcontinent came shortly after the Buddha's death. Around 322 BCE, **Chandragupta Maurya** seized power in the kingdom of Magadha and built an empire that, by the time of his grandson **Ashoka** (reigned c. 268–232 BCE), included most of the subcontinent except the southern tip.

Chandragupta's empire was centralized, bureaucratic, and militarily powerful. His chief minister **Kautilya** (also called **Chanakya**) produced the *Arthashastra*, a comprehensive manual of statecraft that included detailed advice on espionage, diplomacy, taxation, law, and military strategy. The *Arthashastra*'s ruthlessness rivals that of Han Feizi's Legalism in China; its assumption is that the ruler's primary duty is the preservation and expansion of state power, by whatever means.

**Ashoka** is the more famous of the dynasty. After a brutal conquest of Kalinga (modern Odisha) in around 261 BCE, in which by his own count 100,000 people were killed and 150,000 deported, Ashoka publicly converted to Buddhism. He renounced military expansion. He committed himself, in his words, to the propagation of *dhamma* (Buddhist teaching, broadly understood — non-violence, truthfulness, generosity, respect for all life). He had **rock and pillar edicts** carved across the empire, written in local languages, announcing his policies and encouraging his subjects to ethical behavior. The edicts — recovered, deciphered, and dated by nineteenth-century European scholars — are some of the earliest dated South Asian written sources. He sponsored Buddhist missionary expeditions that carried the religion to Sri Lanka, Southeast Asia, central Asia, and possibly as far west as the Hellenistic Mediterranean.

The Mauryan Empire fragmented within fifty years of Ashoka's death. The next several centuries saw a return to regional kingdoms.

### The Gupta Dynasty (c. 320–550 CE)

A second great Indian empire arose in the early fourth century CE under **Chandragupta I** (no relation to the Mauryan founder) of the **Gupta dynasty**. The Gupta Empire, centered in the Ganges valley, produced what is often called India's **Classical Age**.

The Gupta period saw flowering across multiple domains. **Mathematics**: the decimal place-value system, the concept and notation of zero, and the early development of algebra all reached full form during the Gupta period. **Astronomy**: the mathematician-astronomer **Aryabhata** (476–550 CE) produced sophisticated calculations of the Earth's circumference and a heliocentric model of planetary motion that was, for a brief moment, ahead of contemporary Greek and Chinese astronomy. **Literature**: the Sanskrit playwright **Kalidasa** wrote dramas and epic poems still studied today. **Visual arts**: the Gupta sculptural style — graceful, idealized, profoundly serene — became the canonical model for Buddhist and Hindu art across Asia.

The Gupta dynasty fragmented under nomadic invasions (the **Hephthalites**, or "White Huns") in the late fifth and sixth centuries CE. India then entered a period of regional dynasties that would last until the Islamic conquests of the medieval era (Chapter 14).

## Key terms

- **Analects.** The collected sayings of Confucius, compiled by his disciples after his death.
- **Aryans.** Indo-European-speaking peoples who migrated into northern India around 1500 BCE, bringing the Sanskrit language and Vedic religion.
- **Ashoka.** Mauryan emperor (reigned c. 268–232 BCE) who converted to Buddhism after the conquest of Kalinga and promoted *dhamma* across his empire.
- **Brahman.** (1) The hereditary priestly caste of Vedic / Hindu society; (2) the cosmic principle into which the individual soul is reabsorbed.
- **Buddha.** Siddhartha Gautama (traditionally c. 563–483 BCE), founder of Buddhism.
- **Confucianism.** Philosophical-ethical tradition based on the teachings of Kong Fuzi (Confucius), centered on moral cultivation, ritual, and the five relationships.
- **Daoism.** Philosophical-religious tradition based on the texts attributed to Laozi and Zhuangzi, centered on the *dao* and the principle of *wuwei* (non-action).
- **Eightfold Path.** The Buddhist program for the cessation of desire and the attainment of nirvana.
- **Eurasian Steppe.** The vast grassland belt running from Manchuria across central Asia into eastern Europe.
- **Four Noble Truths.** The Buddha's diagnosis of human suffering and the path to its cessation.
- **Gupta Dynasty.** Indian dynasty (c. 320–550 CE) whose reign is called India's Classical Age.
- **Han Chinese.** The majority ethnic group of China; the name derives from the Han dynasty (206 BCE–220 CE).
- **Han Feizi.** Legalist philosopher (c. 280–233 BCE) whose work systematized the school's doctrines.
- **Hundred Schools of Thought.** The diverse philosophical-political traditions that emerged in China during the Warring States period.
- **Karma.** The principle that one's actions in this life determine the conditions of the next. Shared by Brahmanism and Buddhism.
- **Kong Fuzi (Confucius).** Chinese teacher (traditionally 551–479 BCE) whose ideas became foundational to East Asian political and ethical thought.
- **Legalism.** Chinese school of political thought that argued for clear laws, strict punishments, and the maximization of state power.
- **Mandate of Heaven.** The Chinese doctrine that Heaven grants the right to rule to virtuous rulers and withdraws it from corrupt ones.
- **Mauryan Empire.** Indian empire (c. 322–185 BCE) that united most of the subcontinent under Chandragupta and Ashoka.
- **Nirvana.** The Buddhist goal of the extinction of desire and release from the cycle of rebirth.
- **Oracle bones.** Turtle plastrons and ox shoulder blades inscribed with Chinese characters, used in Shang divination.
- **Qin Dynasty.** The first imperial Chinese dynasty (221–206 BCE), founded by Qin Shi Huang.
- **Qin Shi Huang.** "First Sovereign Emperor" of Qin, founder of the imperial Chinese state.
- **Samsara.** The cycle of rebirth through which the soul moves from body to body. Shared by Brahmanism and Buddhism.
- **Sangha.** A Buddhist monastic community.
- **Shang Dynasty.** The first historically attested Chinese dynasty (c. 1600–1050 BCE).
- **Terracotta Army.** The thousands of life-sized clay soldiers buried as guardians of Qin Shi Huang's tomb.
- **Upanishads.** Late Vedic philosophical texts that introduced the doctrines of *samsara* and *karma*.
- **Varna.** The four-tier hierarchical ranking — Brahman, Kshatriya, Vaishya, Shudra — that became the basis of the Indian caste system.
- **Vedas.** The earliest Sanskrit religious texts of India, including the *Rigveda*.
- **Warring States.** The era of total war between Chinese regional powers (475–221 BCE) that ended with Qin unification.
- **Xiongnu.** A confederation of steppe peoples north of China, the principal northern adversary of the Qin and Han dynasties.
- **Yamato.** The dominant clan in early Japan, founders of the imperial line that still occupies the throne.
- **Zhou Dynasty.** Chinese dynasty (c. 1046–256 BCE) that introduced the Mandate of Heaven and ruled (in declining centralized form) for nearly eight centuries.

## Further reading

- **Lewis, Mark Edward.** *The Early Chinese Empires: Qin and Han.* 2007. The first volume of Harvard's *History of Imperial China* series; the standard accessible synthesis.
- **Pines, Yuri.** *The Everlasting Empire: The Political Culture of Ancient China.* 2012. A close study of why Chinese political institutions proved so durable.
