*From Mecca to Córdoba in a Hundred Years.*

## Chapter overview

By the end of this chapter you will know how Islam emerged in early seventh-century Arabia, how it grew into the dominant religion of the Near East within a single lifetime, and how the **Rashidun**, **Umayyad**, and **Abbasid** caliphates built and administered the largest single empire of premodern history. You will know **Muhammad** (c. 570–632 CE) as a person — a Meccan merchant who, beginning around 610 CE, received what he believed to be revelations from God, preached strict monotheism in a polytheistic society, gathered a community of followers, fled persecution to Medina in 622 CE (the **Hijra**), and returned to take Mecca eight years later. You will know the **Quran** as the central scripture of Islam, the **hadith** as its supplementary tradition, and the **five pillars** as the practical framework of Islamic devotion. You will know the **Arab-Islamic conquests** that followed Muhammad's death in 632 CE — within thirty years, they had absorbed the Sasanian Persian Empire and most of the Byzantine East; within a hundred years, the empire stretched from Spain to the Indus. You will know the **Umayyad** dynasty (661–750 CE) that turned the religious community into an Arab-led empire, and the **Abbasid** dynasty (750–1258 CE) that opened the empire to non-Arab Muslims and produced the cosmopolitan, learning-centered Islamic world of Baghdad's golden age. You will know the **Sunni-Shia** division that opened with the assassination of the fourth caliph Ali in 661 CE and has shaped Islamic history ever since. You will know the **translation movement** that preserved much of Greek philosophy and science by translating it into Arabic. By the end, you will see Islam as both a religion and a civilization — a single tradition that, by 900 CE, had reshaped the world Late Antiquity left behind.

## Opening case — Mecca to Yathrib, 622 CE

In September of the year 622 CE, a fifty-two-year-old merchant from the Arabian city of **Mecca** slipped out of town under cover of darkness with a small group of his followers and his closest companion, **Abu Bakr**. They were fleeing a planned assassination. The merchant — **Muhammad ibn Abdullah** — had spent the previous twelve years preaching strict monotheism and a coming day of judgment to a polytheistic Meccan elite that had come, finally, to view him as a serious political threat. The elite of the **Quraysh** tribe controlled the **Kaaba**, the central sanctuary of Mecca and the centerpiece of an Arab pilgrimage economy that supported the city's wealth. Muhammad had told his followers that the gods of the Kaaba were idols and that the One God required exclusive worship. The Quraysh had decided to kill him before his preaching cost them their position.

He escaped. He and his small community walked north for about ten days across the Hijaz desert and arrived at the oasis city of **Yathrib**, two hundred miles to the north — a city that had invited him to come and arbitrate its tribal disputes. Yathrib was renamed shortly thereafter as **Medina** — *Madinat al-Nabi*, "the City of the Prophet" — and became Muhammad's base for the next ten years.

The journey is called the **Hijra** — "the migration." It is the moment from which Islamic time is reckoned. The Islamic lunar calendar, used continuously from the seventh century to today by Muslims around the world, begins with the year of the Hijra, designated **AH 1** (*Anno Hegirae*). The Hijra year of 622 CE is, in Islamic chronology, year zero. By this calendar, the Battle of Saratoga (1777 CE) is AH 1191. The death of the Sasanian emperor Khusrau II (628 CE) is AH 6. The Council of Trent (1545 CE) is AH 952.

The choice of the Hijra as the chronological starting point is itself revealing. Muhammad's birth (c. 570 CE), his first revelation (610 CE), or his death (632 CE) might have served as candidates. Each is a major event in Islamic sacred history. The early Muslim community chose the Hijra because it was the moment when Islam moved from being a small persecuted minority in Mecca to being a self-governing community — an *ummah* — with political institutions, a constitution (the **Constitution of Medina**), and an army. The transformation from religious movement to political-religious community is what made everything that followed possible. Muhammad's preaching might have died with him in Mecca. After the Hijra, it could not.

This chapter is about that community and what it built. Within a hundred years of the Hijra — by AH 100 / 720 CE — Muslim armies had conquered Persia, most of the Byzantine East, North Africa, and Spain. The empire was the largest the world had yet seen. Within another hundred years — by AH 200 / 820 CE — Baghdad was the cultural and intellectual center of the world. The story is one of the fastest civilizational ascents in human history.

We start with Arabia.

## Concept 1 — Arabia on the eve of Islam

The Arabian Peninsula in 600 CE was, mostly, desert and oasis, populated by a mixture of nomadic Bedouin tribes and settled merchants. The peninsula's interior — the Hijaz (western Arabia along the Red Sea coast), the Najd (central plateau), and the great deserts (Rub' al Khali, the Empty Quarter) — was sparsely populated and politically fragmented into hundreds of tribes and confederations. The peripheries were richer: the southern Arabian highlands of **Yemen** had supported substantial agricultural civilizations for over a thousand years (the Sabaeans, the Himyarites of Chapter 13); the eastern Arabian coast had Persian-influenced trading communities; the northern fringes were occupied by Arab client kingdoms of Rome (the **Ghassanids**) and Persia (the **Lakhmids**).

Religion in Arabia was plural. Most tribes were polytheistic, with regional pantheons that overlapped and varied. **Mecca** was the most important pilgrimage center: the city's central sanctuary, the **Kaaba**, was a black-stone cube that, by tradition, housed images of perhaps 360 deities revered across Arabia. Pilgrims came annually for trade and devotion. **Jewish** communities had been present in Arabia for centuries, particularly in the oasis settlements of Medina and Khaybar; some entire tribes were Jewish. **Christian** communities had grown in northern and southern Arabia, with substantial presence in Najran (in modern southern Saudi Arabia) and along the Red Sea coast. **Hanifs** — Arab monotheists who acknowledged a single supreme god (often called *Allah*, "the God") without affiliating with Judaism or Christianity — were a small but recognized presence in pre-Islamic Mecca and elsewhere.

Trade was the economic backbone of the western Arabian cities. The **Quraysh** tribe of Mecca had developed a sophisticated long-distance caravan trade carrying spices, incense, and luxury goods between southern Arabia, Syria, and Persia. The Quraysh's role as guardians of the Kaaba reinforced their commercial position: pilgrimage and trade routes converged on Mecca, and the sanctuary's status as a place where bloodshed was forbidden made the city a safe waypoint for caravans. Mecca prospered.

The peninsula in 600 CE was thus structurally well-positioned for a unifying religious-political movement. It had widely intermarried tribes, recognized pilgrimage centers, established trade routes, contact with the major monotheisms of the surrounding world, and a sense of cultural identity (the Arabic language, shared poetic and oral traditions) that crossed tribal lines. What it lacked was a political unity that could bind the tribes together. Muhammad would supply that.

## Concept 2 — Muhammad

**Muhammad ibn Abdullah** was born in Mecca around 570 CE — the exact year is uncertain. His father died before he was born; his mother died when he was six. He was raised by his uncle **Abu Talib**, a respected Meccan elder. As a young man he joined the family caravan business and earned a reputation for honesty, gaining the nickname **al-Amin** ("the trustworthy"). At twenty-five, he married **Khadija bint Khuwaylid**, a wealthy widow some years older than him who employed him in her trading enterprises. The marriage was monogamous for as long as Khadija lived — Muhammad would not take another wife until after her death — and the two had multiple children, of whom the daughter **Fatima** would become especially prominent in later Islamic history.

According to Islamic tradition, in the year 610 CE, Muhammad — by then around forty — was meditating in a cave outside Mecca when he received the first of what he believed to be divine revelations, conveyed through the angel **Gabriel** (*Jibril* in Arabic). The first message instructed him to **Recite!** — *Iqra!* — the same word from which the eventual scripture would take its name (the **Quran**, "the recitation"). Subsequent revelations came over the next twenty-three years and were memorized by Muhammad's followers as he received them. The collected revelations, organized in 114 chapters (*surahs*) of varying length, became the Quran, which Muslims regard as the literal word of God transmitted to Muhammad.

Muhammad began preaching publicly around 613 CE. His message was strict monotheism: there is no god but God; God is one, eternal, uncreated, and demands exclusive worship; idols are false; humans face a final judgment in which their actions will be weighed; ethical conduct, charity, and submission to God are the path to paradise. His preaching threatened the polytheistic religious-economic system the Quraysh elite controlled. Persecution of his small community of followers intensified. Some Muslims took refuge with the Christian king of Aksum in Ethiopia (the so-called **first Hijra**). In 619 CE, both Khadija and Abu Talib died — what Islamic tradition calls "the Year of Sorrow" — leaving Muhammad without his most influential supporters.

In 622 CE came the migration to Medina (the **Hijra**) we opened the chapter with. Muhammad's community in Medina, now substantial, included a mix of Meccan refugees, local Arabs who had converted, and the city's existing Jewish tribes. Muhammad served first as a religious teacher and arbiter, then as the city's effective political leader. The **Constitution of Medina** — a document preserved (in fragmentary form) in the early biographical sources — established a federation of tribes including the Muslims and the Jewish tribes, mutual defense obligations, and a framework for resolving disputes by reference to Muhammad's authority.

The relationship with the Jewish tribes deteriorated over the next several years, as did the relationship with the Meccan Quraysh. A series of military encounters between the Muslim community and the Meccans — the **Battle of Badr** (624 CE, a Muslim victory), the **Battle of Uhud** (625 CE, a Muslim defeat), the **Battle of the Trench** (627 CE, a successful Muslim defense of Medina) — ended with the **Treaty of Hudaybiyya** in 628 CE and the eventual conquest of Mecca in 630 CE. Muhammad entered Mecca with a large army and minimal violence; the Meccan elite mostly converted; the idols of the Kaaba were removed; the Kaaba itself was kept as a sanctuary, now dedicated to the worship of God alone. Within the next two years, the rest of the Arabian Peninsula had submitted to Muhammad's authority.

He died in Medina in **June 632 CE**, at about sixty-two. He had unified Arabia, established an institutional religion, produced (with his community's help) a recognized scripture, and left behind perhaps 100,000 followers organized into a self-governing political community. He had not designated a successor.

## Concept 3 — The Quran, the hadith, and Islamic teaching

The **Quran** is Islam's central scripture. Muslims regard it as the literal, eternal word of God transmitted through Muhammad in Arabic. It was composed orally; Muhammad's followers memorized it as he received it. After his death, the third caliph **Uthman** (reigned 644–656 CE) commissioned an authoritative written compilation, organized into 114 *surahs* roughly by length (longer surahs first, with the major exception of the brief opening *al-Fatiha*). The Uthmanic recension is the basis of the standard Quran in use today.

The Quran's themes overlap substantially with the Hebrew Bible and the New Testament. **Adam**, **Noah**, **Abraham** (*Ibrahim*), **Moses** (*Musa*), **David** (*Dawud*), **Solomon** (*Sulayman*), **Mary** (*Maryam*), **Jesus** (*Isa*) are all prophets in the Quranic tradition. The Quran tells stories about them that are recognizable to readers of the Bible while sometimes diverging in detail. The Quranic theological framework holds that all of these prophets received revelations from the same God; that Jews and Christians are *Ahl al-Kitab* (the "People of the Book") whose monotheism was authentic but whose scriptures had been corrupted in transmission; and that Muhammad is the final prophet sent to restore the original message in its complete form. This framework shaped early Islamic relations with Jewish and Christian communities — they were treated as religiously legitimate but theologically corrected.

The **Five Pillars of Islam** are the practical framework of Islamic devotion:

1. **Shahada** — the profession of faith: "There is no god but God, and Muhammad is the messenger of God." (*La ilaha illa Allah, Muhammadun rasulu Allah.*)
2. **Salat** — ritual prayer five times a day, facing Mecca.
3. **Zakat** — almsgiving, calculated as a percentage of accumulated wealth, owed to the poor and the community.
4. **Sawm** — fasting during the daylight hours of the lunar month of Ramadan.
5. **Hajj** — pilgrimage to Mecca, required at least once in a lifetime for those physically and financially able.

The Five Pillars were not all institutionalized in Muhammad's lifetime in the form they later took, but each is rooted in his teaching and the Quran.

The **hadith** are the recorded sayings and actions of Muhammad and his earliest companions, transmitted orally for the first century or two of Islamic history before being collected and assessed in the great hadith compilations of the eighth and ninth centuries CE — most importantly the *Sahih al-Bukhari* and *Sahih Muslim* compilations. Hadith provide much of the practical guidance for Islamic life that the Quran does not specify. The science of hadith verification — assessing the chain of transmitters (*isnad*) and the content (*matn*) for reliability — became one of the major intellectual disciplines of the early Islamic world.

**Sharia** — Islamic religious law — emerged from the interpretive synthesis of Quran and hadith over the next several centuries. Major schools of legal interpretation developed (the four Sunni schools — *Hanafi*, *Maliki*, *Shafi'i*, *Hanbali* — and the Shia *Ja'fari* school) that differ in detail while sharing the basic source texts.

**Aisha bint Abi Bakr**, Muhammad's youngest wife and the daughter of his closest companion, became one of the most important early Islamic authorities. She had been a teenager when Muhammad died but lived for nearly fifty more years, during which she taught, transmitted hadith, and participated in early Islamic political life. Many of the most reliable hadith on domestic and ritual matters are transmitted from her. Khadija, his first wife, mattered as the religion's first convert and earliest supporter; Aisha mattered as the most important transmitter of Muhammad's teaching to subsequent generations.

## Concept 4 — The Rashidun caliphate and the early conquests

Muhammad's death in 632 CE produced a succession crisis. He had not named a successor. The community gathered and selected **Abu Bakr** — Muhammad's father-in-law, his closest companion, and the second person to convert to Islam — as the first **caliph** (*khalifa*, "successor"). Abu Bakr ruled for two years (632–634 CE) before dying.

The first four caliphs — **Abu Bakr** (632–634), **Umar** (634–644), **Uthman** (644–656), and **Ali** (656–661) — are remembered in Sunni Islamic tradition as the **Rashidun**, the "rightly guided." They had all been close companions of Muhammad. They governed from Medina. They oversaw the rapid initial expansion of the Islamic state. Their reigns — collectively the **Rashidun caliphate** — are the formative period of post-Muhammadan Islam.

### The conquests

In 633 CE, Arab Muslim armies began crossing into the territories of the exhausted Byzantine and Sasanian empires. The two empires had spent the previous quarter-century in mutual destructive warfare (Chapter 13); their treasuries were empty, their armies were depleted, the bubonic plague had cut their populations, their religious populations were divided over Christological and Zoroastrian disputes. The Arab armies were small but tightly organized, religiously motivated, and led by experienced commanders (notably **Khalid ibn al-Walid**, "the Sword of God").

The conquests proceeded with extraordinary speed:

- **634**: Damascus falls.
- **636**: Battle of Yarmouk — Khalid's army crushes the Byzantine field army; Syria is effectively conquered.
- **637**: Battle of al-Qadisiyyah — Sasanian field army crushed; the Sasanian capital Ctesiphon falls within months.
- **638**: Jerusalem surrenders to caliph Umar after a brief siege.
- **641**: Egypt conquered after the fall of Alexandria.
- **642**: Battle of Nahavand — final Sasanian field army destroyed.
- **651**: The last Sasanian emperor Yazdegerd III is killed; the Sasanian Empire ceases to exist.

By Umar's death in 644 CE, the Caliphate included Iraq, Syria, Palestine, Egypt, and most of Iran. By the end of the seventh century it would include North Africa, Central Asia, and parts of the Indian subcontinent. By the early eighth century it would include Spain (after the conquest of the Visigothic kingdom in 711 CE) and would attempt unsuccessful invasions of Frankish Gaul (defeated at Tours in 732 CE) and the Byzantine capital at Constantinople (twice, in 674–678 and 717–718 CE).

The conquests are sometimes described as "miraculous" in popular accounts. They were not. They were the convergence of several factors: the structural weakness of the post-war Byzantine and Sasanian states; the sympathy or indifference of much of the conquered population (Monophysite Christians in Egypt and Syria, who had been persecuted by Chalcedonian Byzantines, often welcomed Arab rule as more tolerant; Persian peasants saw the Arab conquest as relief from Sasanian taxation); the cohesion of the Arab armies under religious-tribal leadership; the willingness of conquered local elites to retain their positions under new overlords. The result was a vast, multi-ethnic, multi-religious empire administered, in its first decades, by a small Arab military elite over a population that remained mostly Christian, Zoroastrian, or Jewish for the first century or two.

### The Sunni-Shia split

The Rashidun period ended in civil war. **Uthman**, the third caliph, was assassinated in 656 CE by rebels who accused him of nepotism and corruption. His successor **Ali ibn Abi Talib** — Muhammad's cousin, son-in-law, and (in some traditions) designated successor — was unable to bring the assassins to justice and was opposed by a coalition led by Uthman's family, the **Umayyads**. The conflict — the **First Fitna** (656–661 CE) — included the **Battle of the Camel** (where Muhammad's widow Aisha led the opposition to Ali) and the **Battle of Siffin**. Ali was assassinated in 661 CE. His rival **Mu'awiya ibn Abi Sufyan** of the Umayyad family took the caliphate.

The civil war produced the most consequential division in Islamic history. **Shia** ("party of Ali") Muslims hold that the caliphate should have remained in Muhammad's family, beginning with Ali; that the Umayyad takeover was a usurpation; and that Ali's descendants — particularly his son **Husayn**, killed at **Karbala** in 680 CE in a continuing rebellion against Umayyad rule — are the rightful religious-political authorities (**Imams**) of the Muslim community. **Sunni** ("people of the *sunna* — the tradition") Muslims accept the legitimacy of all four Rashidun caliphs and the broader principle that the *ummah* selects its caliphs. The two traditions developed distinct theological emphases (Shia Islam attaches greater weight to the Imams as authoritative interpreters; Sunni Islam attaches greater weight to consensus and the four schools of jurisprudence) and, in many regions, distinct ritual practices. The split has persisted for thirteen centuries. About 85% of Muslims today are Sunni; about 15% are Shia, with concentrations in Iran, Iraq, Bahrain, Lebanon, and parts of South Asia.

## Concept 5 — The Umayyad caliphate, 661–750 CE

The **Umayyad caliphate** moved the political center of Islam from Medina to **Damascus** in Syria. Its founder **Mu'awiya** had been the Umayyad governor of Syria; he kept Damascus as his capital. Damascus was wealthier, better positioned for governing the new empire's territories, and closer to the Byzantine frontier.

The Umayyad period was the empire-building phase. The Arab conquests continued — into North Africa, Spain, Central Asia, the Indus valley. The empire became enormous in territory: by 720 CE, it stretched from the Atlantic coast of Morocco to the borders of China and India. Administering it required institutional innovation. The Umayyads preserved much of the Byzantine and Sasanian bureaucracies in the conquered territories, employing Greek and Persian administrators, using Greek and Persian as official languages of administration in their respective regions, and continuing the existing tax structures (lightly modified). They added Arabic as an administrative language gradually, particularly under **Abd al-Malik** (reigned 685–705 CE), who issued the first all-Arabic Islamic coinage, replacing the Byzantine and Sasanian coins that had circulated in the early conquest period.

The Umayyad relationship with non-Muslim subjects was mixed. The basic legal structure was the **dhimma** — non-Muslims who were "People of the Book" (Christians, Jews, Zoroastrians) were granted protected status as *dhimmi* in exchange for paying a special tax (the *jizya*) and accepting certain restrictions (no proselytizing to Muslims, no public displays that competed with Islamic institutions). *Dhimmi* status was generally tolerable by the standards of the period but was unequal: Muslims paid lower taxes, held higher legal status, and had access to political offices generally closed to non-Muslims. The system created a powerful structural incentive for conversion to Islam, which proceeded gradually across the Umayyad and Abbasid periods.

The Umayyad relationship with non-Arab Muslims was also problematic. Non-Arab converts to Islam — particularly Persians and the inhabitants of Central Asia and North Africa — found themselves in an awkward position: theologically Muslim (and therefore not subject to the *jizya*) but not Arab (and therefore not first-class members of the political community). They were called *mawali* — clients of Arab tribes — and were systematically excluded from the highest political and military positions. This second-class status produced grievances that would, eventually, fuel the Abbasid revolution.

Umayyad cultural production is significant. The **Dome of the Rock** in Jerusalem (completed 691 CE under Abd al-Malik) is one of the world's earliest surviving Islamic monuments, built atop the site of the Jewish Second Temple and adopting Byzantine architectural conventions in service of an Islamic religious-political program. The **Great Mosque of Damascus** (completed 715 CE) is another major Umayyad monument. Islamic art under the Umayyads developed its distinctive aniconic conventions (avoiding figural representation in religious contexts) while drawing extensively on Byzantine and Sasanian decorative traditions.

The Umayyad caliphate ended in 750 CE in a revolution. The grievances of *mawali* converts, particularly in **Khurasan** in eastern Iran, combined with anti-Umayyad sentiment among Shia Muslims and the long-running political opposition of supporters of the family of Muhammad. A coalition rose under the leadership of the **Abbasid** family — descendants of Muhammad's uncle al-Abbas — and overthrew the Umayyads in a swift campaign. Most of the Umayyad family was killed. One survivor, **Abd al-Rahman**, fled west to Spain and founded an independent Umayyad kingdom (later caliphate) at **Córdoba** that would last until 1031 CE.

## Concept 6 — The Abbasid caliphate, 750–1258 CE

The **Abbasid caliphate** transformed the Islamic world from an Arab empire into a more genuinely cosmopolitan civilization. Its first major decision was to move the capital eastward — first to a temporary site in Iraq, then to a newly built capital at **Baghdad** on the Tigris River, founded in 762 CE. Baghdad was a planned circular city, designed for administration. Within a century, it had grown into the largest city in the world outside China — perhaps a million inhabitants at its peak, with markets, mosques, palaces, libraries, and a polyglot population including Arabs, Persians, Turks, Indians, Africans, Greeks, and Jews.

The Abbasid political settlement formally recognized non-Arab Muslims as full members of the *ummah*. The court culture was Persian-influenced (the Abbasids drew heavily on Sasanian administrative tradition for their bureaucratic structures; the *vizier* office was a Persian-derived development), Arabic-language (Arabic was the language of religion, administration, and elite literature), and increasingly Turkish-influenced as Turkish soldiers came to dominate the army during the ninth century. The empire's elite was itself ethnically mixed; the caliph **al-Ma'mun** (reigned 813–833 CE) was the son of a caliph and a Persian concubine.

### The translation movement

The most consequential Abbasid intellectual project was the **translation movement** (roughly 750–1000 CE). The early Abbasid caliphs sponsored a massive effort to translate Greek, Syriac, Persian, and Sanskrit philosophical, scientific, mathematical, and medical works into Arabic. The center of the translation movement was the **Bayt al-Hikmah** (the **House of Wisdom**) in Baghdad, established or expanded by al-Ma'mun in the early ninth century. Translators — many of them Christians and Sabians who knew both Greek and Arabic — produced Arabic versions of Aristotle, Plato (in part), Galen, Hippocrates, Euclid, Ptolemy, Archimedes, the Indian mathematical works on astronomy and computation, and much more.

The translation movement preserved an extraordinary amount of Greek and other ancient learning that would otherwise have been lost. Many ancient works survive only in Arabic translation today — the original Greek manuscripts have been lost. The translation movement also did not stop at translation. Arabic-language scholars built on the inherited material:

- **Al-Khwarizmi** (c. 780–850 CE) developed algebra (the term comes from his Arabic *al-jabr*) and introduced the decimal place-value system from India to the Mediterranean world. The word "algorithm" comes from the Latinization of his name.
- **Ibn al-Haytham** (965–1040 CE), known in Latin as Alhazen, produced the *Book of Optics*, which used systematic experimentation to derive principles of vision that the Greeks had only theorized. His method anticipated the modern scientific method by half a millennium.
- **Al-Razi** (854–925 CE), known in Latin as Rhazes, was a major medical clinician whose works on contagious disease, smallpox, and measles were standard references in both Islamic and European medicine for centuries.
- **Al-Farabi** (c. 872–950 CE) and **Ibn Sina** (Avicenna, 980–1037 CE) developed Islamic philosophy, drawing on Aristotle and Plato while addressing distinctively Islamic theological questions. Ibn Sina's *Canon of Medicine* was a major medical reference in Latin Europe through the seventeenth century.
- **Ibn Rushd** (Averroes, 1126–1198 CE) — writing in Almoravid Spain rather than Abbasid Iraq, but in the same intellectual tradition — produced commentaries on Aristotle that profoundly influenced the medieval European universities.

The transmission ran in both directions over the long term. From the eleventh century onward, much of the Arabic philosophical and scientific corpus was translated from Arabic into Latin (often in Spain and Sicily, where Christian scholars worked with the Arabic-language inheritance) and became the foundation of the European medieval scientific revival and, eventually, the European Renaissance. The European Renaissance was, in important part, the rediscovery — through Arabic — of the Greek philosophical-scientific tradition that the early Islamic world had preserved and extended.

### Sect formation

The Abbasid period also saw the consolidation of the major Islamic theological and legal schools. The four Sunni schools of jurisprudence — **Hanafi** (founded by Abu Hanifa, d. 767), **Maliki** (Malik ibn Anas, d. 795), **Shafi'i** (al-Shafi'i, d. 820), **Hanbali** (Ibn Hanbal, d. 855) — all crystallized in this period. The Shia tradition similarly produced its own legal-theological school (the **Ja'fari** school, named for the sixth Shia Imam **Ja'far al-Sadiq**, d. 765). The mystical tradition of **Sufism** also developed institutional form during the Abbasid period, with figures like **Hasan al-Basri** (642–728 CE), **Rabi'a al-Adawiyya** (713–801 CE — the major early female Sufi mystic), and later **al-Junayd**, **al-Hallaj**, and **al-Ghazali** producing the mystical-theological literature that would shape Islamic spirituality for the next millennium.

### The fragmentation

The Abbasid caliphate did not maintain unified control of its enormous empire for very long. Distant provinces broke away in the ninth century: the **Aghlabids** in North Africa, the **Tahirids** and **Saffarids** in Persia, the **Samanids** in Central Asia. By the mid-tenth century, the Abbasid caliph in Baghdad had become a religious figurehead while real political power was held by Turkish military commanders (the **Buyids**, then the **Seljuk** Turks) and various regional dynasties. The **Fatimids** (a Shia dynasty) conquered Egypt in 969 CE and built a rival caliphate that would last until Saladin's reconquest in 1171 CE.

The formal Abbasid caliphate persisted until 1258 CE, when the **Mongols** under **Hulagu Khan** sacked Baghdad and killed the last Abbasid caliph (Chapter 18). By that point the Islamic world had long ceased to be a single political unit. It remained, however, a single religious-cultural civilization — bound together by Arabic-language religion, by the scholarly traditions the translation movement had built, and by the shared infrastructure of pilgrimage, scholarship, and trade that connected the Atlantic coast of Morocco to the Indonesian archipelago.

You can describe Muhammad's career: Mecca, the first revelation in 610 CE, the Hijra to Medina in 622 CE, the development of the *ummah*, the conquest of Mecca, his death in 632 CE.

You can explain the Quran as Islam's central scripture, the relationship of Quran and *hadith*, and the practical framework of the Five Pillars.

You can trace the Arab-Islamic conquests under the Rashidun caliphs from 632 to 661 CE and explain how a small religious community in Medina became, within thirty years, the ruling power across most of the Near East.

You can describe the Umayyad caliphate (661–750 CE) as the Arab-dominated phase of Islamic empire — and explain how its grievances produced the Abbasid revolution. You can describe the Abbasid caliphate (750–1258 CE) as the cosmopolitan phase, with Baghdad as its center, the translation movement as its great intellectual project, and the gradual political fragmentation that left the caliph a religious figurehead by the tenth century.

You can identify the Sunni-Shia division as originating in the seventh-century First Fitna over the succession to Ali, and trace its consequences for Islamic political and religious history.

The thing to watch for, going forward, is the temptation to read the entire later Islamic world as a continuation of Abbasid Baghdad. After 900 CE, Islamic civilization was multi-centered: Cairo, Córdoba, Bukhara, Damascus, Cairo again under the Mamluks, Constantinople under the Ottomans. Each was a major Islamic capital. None was the only one. The next chapters follow what happened.

## Key terms

- **Abbasid caliphate.** The dynasty that ruled the Islamic empire from 750 CE to 1258 CE, with capital at Baghdad.
- **Aisha.** Muhammad's youngest wife; major transmitter of hadith.
- **Bayt al-Hikmah.** The House of Wisdom in Baghdad, center of the Abbasid translation movement.
- **Caliph.** "Successor" — the political-religious leader of the Islamic community after Muhammad.
- **Dhimma / dhimmi.** The legal arrangement granting protected status to People of the Book (Christians, Jews, Zoroastrians) under Muslim rule, in exchange for payment of the *jizya*.
- **Five Pillars of Islam.** *Shahada* (profession of faith), *salat* (prayer), *zakat* (almsgiving), *sawm* (fasting), *hajj* (pilgrimage).
- **Hadith.** Recorded sayings and actions of Muhammad and his companions.
- **Hijra.** Muhammad's migration from Mecca to Medina in 622 CE; year zero of the Islamic calendar.
- **Imam.** In Shia Islam, a divinely appointed religious-political leader from the family of Muhammad.
- **Jizya.** Tax paid by *dhimmi* non-Muslims under Muslim rule.
- **Ka'ba.** The cube-shaped sanctuary in Mecca, the central shrine of Islam.
- **Khadija.** Muhammad's first wife and the first convert to Islam.
- **Mawali.** Non-Arab converts to Islam under the early caliphate; treated as second-class members during the Umayyad period.
- **Mecca.** Muhammad's birthplace and the site of the Ka'ba; the direction of Muslim prayer.
- **Medina.** "The City of the Prophet," Muhammad's base after the Hijra.
- **Muhammad.** The Prophet of Islam (c. 570–632 CE).
- **Quran.** The central scripture of Islam.
- **Rashidun.** The first four caliphs (Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman, Ali), 632–661 CE.
- **Sharia.** Islamic religious law derived from Quran and hadith.
- **Shia.** "Party of Ali," the branch of Islam holding that the caliphate should have remained in Muhammad's family.
- **Sufism.** The mystical-spiritual tradition of Islam.
- **Sunni.** "People of the *sunna*," the majority branch of Islam.
- **Translation movement.** The Abbasid-era project of translating Greek, Syriac, Persian, and Sanskrit works into Arabic.
- **Ulama.** The class of Islamic religious scholars who interpret the Quran and hadith.
- **Ummah.** The community of Muslim believers.
- **Umayyad caliphate.** The dynasty that ruled the Islamic empire from 661 to 750 CE, with capital at Damascus.
