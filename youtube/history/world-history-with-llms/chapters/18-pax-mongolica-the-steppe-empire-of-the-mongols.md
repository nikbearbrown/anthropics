# Chapter 18 — Pax Mongolica: The Steppe Empire of the Mongols

**Suggested titles**

- Pax Mongolica: The Steppe Empire of the Mongols
- The Felt-Walled Empire: How Nomads Connected Eurasia
- Chinggis Khan and the World He Made

**TL;DR.** Between 1206 and 1260, a federation of seminomadic tribes from the Mongolian grasslands assembled the largest contiguous land empire in human history, knitting together silk roads from Korea to the Black Sea under a single legal code and a horse-relay postal system. The empire conquered through ruthlessness and terror; it ruled, where it could, through tolerance, taxation, and trade — a contradiction that produced the Pax Mongolica, broke at four points into four khanates, and rewired Eurasia's economic and religious geography for centuries.

---

## Overview

In February 1258, an army from the eastern steppe stood inside the walls of Baghdad. The caliph al-Musta'sim, thirty-seventh successor to Muhammad in the Abbasid line, had refused to surrender on the terms he was offered. Now his city was on fire. The House of Wisdom — the institution where Banu Musa brothers had translated Greek mathematics, where al-Kindi had argued for the indivisibility of God, where the translation movement of the ninth century had transferred Aristotle and Euclid into Arabic — was being looted and burned. Manuscripts were heaped into the Tigris until the river ran black with ink. The caliph himself was wrapped in a carpet and trampled to death by horses, since Mongol custom forbade spilling royal blood on the ground. Five hundred years of caliphate, ended in a week.

The man who gave the order, Hulagu Khan, was the grandson of a steppe boy who had been briefly enslaved as a child and who, when his father was murdered, had no clan, no protection, no future anyone could see. That boy's name was Temujin. By the time he died in 1227 he had united the Mongol-speaking peoples, built the most disciplined cavalry the world had yet seen, and conquered an arc of territory from the Pacific to the Caspian. By the time his grandsons were finished — Hulagu in Baghdad, Kublai in China, Batu in the lands of the Rus — the Mongol Empire stretched from Korea to Hungary. About one in four people alive on earth lived inside it.

This chapter is about how that happened. How a million scattered nomads conquered four hundred million settled people. How the conquest, after the killing was done, produced something the Mongols called the *yassa* and the merchants of Venice and Persia called the Pax Mongolica — a span of decades when a courier could ride from Beijing to Kyiv carrying a *paiza* tablet and expect food, fresh horses, and protection at every relay station. And how the empire fractured along the seam between those who wanted to keep living in tents and those who had decided cities were better.

A warning before we start. The Mongol record is hard to read fairly. Persian chroniclers writing for new Mongol patrons exaggerated atrocities to flatter; later Russian historians did so to explain backwardness; later European writers split the difference and produced a Chinggis Khan who is alternately Chaucer's "noble king" and Montesquieu's destroyer of Asia. What we have closest to a Mongol voice — *The Secret History of the Mongols* — was written down after Chinggis's death and survives only through interpreters. We do the best we can with the sources, name what we don't know, and proceed.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- Explain how Song China combined economic and technological flourishing with military weakness, and why this combination produced the conditions for Mongol conquest.
- Describe the social structure of the steppe peoples and the specific innovations Temujin introduced that turned tribal raiding into imperial conquest.
- Trace the conquests of Chinggis, Ogedei, Hulagu, and Kublai, and identify where the empire reached its limits.
- Analyze the Pax Mongolica as a system: what it connected, who paid for it, who profited, and why it lasted.
- Account for the four-khanate split and the differential paths of Yuan China, the Il-Khanate, the Chagatai Khanate, and the Golden Horde.
- Connect events on the steppe to parallel developments in Latin Europe (Magna Carta, the Inquisition, the Fourth Crusade) and the Islamic world (Mamluk Egypt, the Delhi Sultanate, the Battle of Ain Jalut).

## Opening case — A boy with no clan

The grasslands north of the Gobi in the late twelfth century are not a kind place. Winters drop below minus thirty. Wolves take livestock; horse-falls take riders. A clan that loses its grazing range in a bad winter loses its children to starvation by spring.

Temujin was born around 1162 — the date is uncertain — to Hoelun, the principal wife of a Mongol clan leader named Yesugei. He had a normal childhood for the steppe, which is to say a hard one, and then it became extraordinarily harder. Yesugei was poisoned by Tatar enemies when Temujin was about nine. The clan, in the ruthless arithmetic of survival, decided that a widow with seven small children was a liability rather than an asset and abandoned the family. Hoelun and her children survived on roots, marmots, and what they could trap. In a fight over a fish, Temujin killed his half-brother. Later he was captured by a rival clan, fitted with a wooden cangue around his neck, and held as a slave until a sympathetic family helped him escape. Then his young wife Borte was kidnapped by Merkit raiders. He went to a more powerful patron, Ong Khan of the Kereit, called in a debt his father had once been owed, and got her back. By the time he was about twenty he was a clanless young man with a strong wife, a disputed first son (Borte was already pregnant when he found her, and the timing made paternity unclear), and an exact understanding of how the steppe broke its weak.

This is the man who, twenty years later, would call himself Chinggis Khan — Universal Ruler — and reorganize the world. The thing to notice is that almost nothing in his early life suggested this outcome. The steppe was full of dispossessed boys. What made the difference, when we get to it, will be a set of policies — about loyalty, about plunder, about who got promoted and how — that look very specifically designed by someone who remembered being abandoned.

But before we follow Temujin, we have to look south. Because the empire he was about to build needed something to conquer, and what it conquered was the richest, most populous, most technologically advanced civilization on the planet.

## Concept 1 — Song China: the near-revolution that wasn't

When the Tang dynasty fell apart in 907 CE, the political map of East Asia shattered. Generals carved out little kingdoms; steppe peoples like the Khitan and the Tangut moved south into former Tang territory and started building cities. The Zhao family eventually pulled the central and southern fragments back together in the 960s and announced the Song dynasty.

The Song never reconquered all of Tang's old territory. The Khitan Liao kept the north; the Tangut Xi Xia held the northwest. By the early eleventh century the Song had formally agreed to pay annual tribute to the Liao — 200,000 bolts of silk and 130,000 ounces of silver, worth roughly 2.7 million 2020 dollars — in return for peace. This was officially the Treaty of Shanyuan of 1004, and it set the pattern. Song China was wealthy. Its neighbors were militarily formidable. The Song would pay rather than fight.

This sounds like weakness, and in some ways it was. But what happened inside the smaller, peace-buying Song dynasty was extraordinary. The population doubled in a century — fifty-five million around 1000, about 120 million by 1100. At least three Song cities passed a million people. By comparison, no European city outside Constantinople would reach 100,000 again until well after the fall of Rome.

The mechanism was agricultural. Champa rice, imported from what is now Vietnam, ripened fast enough that two harvests a year became normal. Chain pumps — a device of buckets on a continuous loop, driven by a foot pedal or a water wheel — moved water uphill, turning hillsides into terraced paddies. Iron production in Song China rose from about 65 million pounds in 1000 to over 250 million by 1100 — roughly twice what Britain would produce seven centuries later at the start of its own industrial revolution. Water-powered textile machinery operated in larger cities. Movable type appeared. Paper money circulated. Gunpowder, originally a Daoist alchemical curiosity, was weaponized into flamethrowers, hand-held projectile launchers (the world's first guns), and shrapnel bombs hurled by catapult and later by rocket.

Some economic historians have argued that twelfth-century Song China was on the verge of an industrial revolution six hundred years before Britain's. The argument is contested — there are genuine differences in market structure, energy use, and labor mobility. But you should take seriously how close the Song came. The technological inputs were there. The capital was there. The population pressure was there. The market was there.

What was not there was a political class willing to use the empire's military potential. Song governance was dominated by *mandarins*, civilian officials selected by examinations on the Confucian classics. The Song reformed the exam system to be more genuinely meritocratic — a peasant boy with talent and a willing village patron could, in principle, rise — and for the next thousand years the Confucian-trained mandarin would be the central figure in Chinese statecraft. Mandarins distrusted generals. The Song founders, who had themselves been generals, had structured the state to keep generals weak. A saying of the era: "Do not waste good iron making nails; do not waste good men making soldiers."

So you have an empire with the world's most advanced military technology, ruled by men ideologically committed to not using it, paying tribute to less-developed neighbors who could field cavalry the Song could not match. This is not stable.

In 1120 the Song bankrolled a revolt by a steppe people called the Jurchen against their Khitan Liao overlords, hoping to weaken both. The Jurchen won, taking over the Liao state as the new Jin dynasty. Then they noticed the Song. In 1126–1127, in what became known as the Jingkang incident, the Jin sacked the Song capital, captured the imperial family — including the artist-emperor Huizong, whose painting *Auspicious Cranes* had assured him of a glorious reign — and dragged them north as hostages. The Song reconstituted south of the Huai River as the Southern Song, paid an even larger annual tribute, and held on. They held on, in fact, for another century. But they were now half a state.

**The trade-off named.** Song China shows what happens when economic and technological capacity outruns political-military will. You can build the most sophisticated society on earth, and if you cannot defend it, the people who can will help themselves to it. The Song's Confucian preference for civilian over military rule produced enormous internal goods — meritocracy, prosperity, urbanization — at the price of strategic vulnerability to whoever organized the steppe. The Jurchen organized it once. Then someone organized it harder.

## Concept 2 — Temujin's policy choices: how a clanless boy built an army

Now we go back to Temujin, last seen recovering his wife Borte and joining the *anda* — sworn-brother — bond with his friend Jamukha. By the late 1180s Temujin's following was larger than Jamukha's. Their friendship cracked over class. Jamukha represented the steppe aristocracy: ruling lineages, hereditary privileges, traditional clan structures. Temujin, who had been on the wrong side of all that, did something almost no steppe leader had ever done. He kept punishing aristocrats and kept rewarding commoners.

The mechanism is worth slowing down for. When Temujin defeated a rival clan, his earlier custom would have been to enslave the captured adult males and parcel out the women and children. Temujin changed it. He spared the commoners and integrated their fighters into his own army. He put the aristocratic leaders on trial for whatever offense had supposedly justified the war, found them guilty, and executed them. The clan's social hierarchy was decapitated; its workers, herders, and soldiers were absorbed.

He banned looting and rape during battle. The reason was tactical, not moral: troops who stop to loot stop pursuing fleeing enemies, and fleeing enemies regroup and come back. He ordered survivors hunted down and either captured or killed. He divided spoils after the battle, by formula, equally between fighters — and crucially, the share of any soldier killed in the fight went to his widow and orphans, not to whoever took them in. This last rule changed the incentives of his troops. A Mongol soldier whose family would be cared for if he died had less reason to desert under pressure than a soldier whose family would be at the mercy of the next clan to absorb them.

He reorganized the army on the decimal system: units of ten, joined into hundreds, into thousands, into ten-thousands (*tumens*). Each unit at every level swore loyalty to the others in it. Below ten thousand, units chose their own leaders. Only at the *tumen* level did Temujin appoint the commander, and he appointed on the basis of merit and personal loyalty rather than kinship. This is the structural innovation that mattered. It dissolved the clan as the basic military unit and replaced it with the squad. A Naiman soldier and a Kereit soldier and a Tatar soldier in the same unit of ten ate together, fought together, and were bound by oath to each other. Within a generation, "Mongol" stopped meaning a kinship group and started meaning everyone Temujin had absorbed — which is why he came to call them not Mongols but the People of the Felt Walls, after the *gers* (yurts) they lived in.

The aristocracy of the steppe, watching this, were appalled. Several confederations swung against Temujin in alliance with Jamukha and the aging Ong Khan. Temujin was nearly trapped, fled, regrouped at a remote watering hole called Baljuna, and made his closest companions — nine men from nine different clans, professing four different religions — swear renewed loyalty. The Baljuna Covenant became the founding myth of the unified Mongol nation. Within two years Temujin had defeated Ong Khan, defeated the Naiman, captured Jamukha (whom he reluctantly executed at Jamukha's own request), and won the steppe.

In 1206, he called a *kurultai* — a great assembly — on the Onon River. *The Secret History* says nearly the entire million-person People of the Felt Walls came. A shaman proclaimed him Chinggis Khan, Universal Ruler, and announced that Tengri, the sky god of the steppe, granted him authority. The empire had a name and a constitution.

That constitution was the *yassa*, and it is worth reading as a policy document. Theft and robbery — the centuries-old engines of inter-clan violence — were made capital offenses. Enslaving Mongols was outlawed. Adultery was capital. Killing more animals than your household could use was prohibited. Hunting during mating seasons was prohibited. Religious leaders of any faith were exempted from taxes and labor service. Discrimination on the basis of religion was forbidden, possibly the first such provision in any law code.

The *yassa* did not end violence. It reorganized it. The internal raiding economy of the steppe was abolished, which meant the only way to acquire wealth and status was by serving Chinggis. He did not let his armies sit idle. He pointed them outward.

**The trade-off named.** Temujin's reforms turned the steppe into a discipline machine. The cost was the abolition of older, looser, more egalitarian forms of life — clan independence, traditional patronage networks, hereditary privilege. The gain was a single integrated military system whose loyalty pointed up to the khan and whose violence pointed out at neighbors. Once you have built that machine, you cannot stop using it without losing legitimacy. Chinggis had created an organization that depended on continual external conquest to satisfy its own internal logic. Everything that follows is a consequence of that.

## Concept 3 — The conquests: Jin, Khwarazmia, and the geography of revenge

The first targets were the obvious ones. The Tangut Xi Xia, who had refused to recognize Mongol overlordship, were brought into tributary status by 1210. The Kara-Khitan offered tribute preemptively. The Jin — the same Jurchen state that had crushed the Northern Song eighty years earlier — was the prize.

Chinggis's conquest of the Jin in the 1210s used a method that would become characteristic. He identified an internal grievance — in this case the resentment of the original Khitan population against their Jurchen rulers — and exploited it. Khitan defectors swelled his army. The Mongols laid siege to Zhongdu, the Jin capital, and starved it into surrender. The city was systematically looted, tens of thousands of inhabitants enslaved, and an unknown but very large number massacred. Chinggis took the spoils back to the steppe and built the first permanent structures Mongolia had ever known — warehouses to hold the captured goods.

He did not, at this point, set out to take the rest of the Islamic world. The expansion that did so was triggered, almost on principle, by an insult.

In 1218, Chinggis sent a trade caravan of several hundred Muslim merchants westward to the ruler of Khwarazmia — a Persianate empire that stretched from the Caspian through Persia into central Asia — proposing trade. We have his letter, preserved by later chroniclers: "I have no need to covet other dominions. We have an equal interest in fostering trade between our subjects." A Khwarazmian governor at the city of Otrar confiscated the goods and executed the merchants as spies. Chinggis sent emissaries to demand redress. The Khwarazmian ruler killed most of them and sent the rest back disfigured.

The Mongol response was the most thoroughly destructive campaign of the thirteenth century. Between 1219 and 1223, Chinggis took Otrar (the governor was reportedly executed by having molten silver poured into his eyes and ears), Bukhara, Samarkand, Merv, Nishapur, and Herat. Three of those — Merv, Nishapur, Herat — were among the great cities of the eastern Islamic world. Merv, in particular, had been a major center of Persian learning. Persian chroniclers, writing afterward, claimed casualty figures of over a million per city. The actual numbers are uncertain and probably lower, but the cities were depopulated, their irrigation systems destroyed, and many would not recover for centuries.

While the western campaign was underway, two of Chinggis's generals, Jebe and Subutai, took a reconnaissance force on a long sweep around the Caspian, through the Caucasus, into the lands of the Rus. At the Battle of the Kalka River in 1223, they shattered a combined Rus and Cuman army. Then they rode home.

Chinggis died in 1227, possibly from a fall from a horse during a campaign against the Xi Xia. The empire he left to his sons stretched from the Pacific to the Caspian. He had specified, in advance, that the next great khan would be his third son Ogedei. The succession held — for one generation.

**Worked example: the trade caravan.** The Khwarazmia campaign matters because it displays the empire's logic in pure form. Chinggis genuinely seems to have wanted trade. He also genuinely could not let an insult go unanswered, partly out of personal honor, partly because his army's loyalty depended on continued plunder. When the Khwarazmian ruler chose to murder the merchants, he gave Chinggis both a casus belli and a chance to feed his army. The empire was built such that diplomacy and atrocity ran on the same machinery. You either took the trade offer or you became the next campaign.

## Concept 4 — Pax Mongolica: what the empire built when it stopped fighting

Ogedei, who became khan in 1229, looked nothing like his father. He was a gregarious drunk who threw spectacular parties, depleted the treasury on coronation gifts, and made enemies by being too generous. He was also responsible for the institutional architecture that turned conquest into empire.

His chief administrator was a Khitan ex-mandarin named Yelu Chucai, who had been captured in the Jin campaign and brought into Chinggis's service. Yelu's most-quoted sentence — "An empire can be conquered from horseback, but it cannot be ruled from horseback" — captures his position. He talked Ogedei out of the proposal that northern China be turned entirely into pasturage and convinced him instead to keep the Chinese tax base intact, on the argument that systematic taxation would yield more revenue than periodic looting. He was right. Ogedei rebuilt the bureaucracy along Confucian lines, established a regular census, and put taxation on a recurring rather than ad hoc footing.

The infrastructure that resulted was the *yam* — a system of horse relay stations established by Chinggis for military communication and dramatically expanded by Ogedei. Stations were placed roughly a day's ride apart along the major routes. Each had food, fresh horses, and shelter. A courier carrying a *paiza* — a metal tablet authorizing use of the system — could ride from one end of the empire to the other without arranging supplies. Goods, diplomats, and merchants traveled the same roads. Marco Polo, who arrived in the 1270s, would estimate ten thousand stations on the network.

Ogedei built a capital, Karakorum, near the original Mongol heartland. He also restarted the conquests. In 1234 he finished off the rump Jin state. In 1235 he called a *kurultai* and decided to attack Europe and the Southern Song simultaneously. The European campaign, under Subutai and Chinggis's grandson Batu, devastated the lands of the Rus, took Kyiv in 1240, and pushed into Poland and Hungary in 1241. At Liegnitz and Mohi the Mongol cavalry annihilated combined Polish-German and Hungarian armies. They were entering Bohemia in early 1242 — Vienna was within reach — when word arrived that Ogedei had died the previous December. Mongol custom required all senior princes to return for the *kurultai* that would choose his successor. The army turned around. Western Europe, by historical accident, was spared.

It is worth dwelling on what was being built during this run. The *yassa* — the legal code — applied across the empire, in principle, regardless of religion or ethnicity. Religious leaders of every faith — Buddhist, Christian, Muslim, Daoist, Manichaean, Tengriist — were tax-exempt and protected. The result was that the long-distance trade routes were now operating under a single legal regime, with imperial protection, with a postal-relay infrastructure better than anything between Rome's *cursus publicus* and the nineteenth-century telegraph. The Silk Roads had existed for fourteen hundred years. The Pax Mongolica turned them on like a switch.

This is when Marco Polo's family rode east. When Ibn Battuta — a Moroccan jurist who would travel further than any pre-modern person we know of — passed through Yuan China in the 1340s, he traveled on Mongol roads. When black plague crossed the steppe in the 1330s and 1340s and reached Caffa on the Black Sea and from there Italy, it traveled on Mongol roads too. The same mechanism that connected Genoese merchants to Khitan craftsmen also connected the bacterium *Yersinia pestis* to a previously unexposed Eurasia. We will return to this in Chapter 20.

The trade-off named. The Pax Mongolica was a real economic achievement and a genuinely cosmopolitan one. It was also paid for by terror. The reason a courier could ride across Eurasia with a metal tablet and no escort was that everyone along the route knew exactly what would happen to the next city that interfered with him. The peace was kept by the visible memory of the cities that had not been spared. This is a model of order — security through unanswerable threat — that works as long as the threat remains credible. When the threat decays, the system goes with it.

## Mid-chapter checkpoint

You have, up to this point, three things.

First, an empire that began with a clanless boy who learned, the hard way, that loyalty had to be engineered rather than assumed. Second, a Song China whose technological advantage could not be cashed out into strategic advantage because the political class would not allow it, leaving the door open to whichever steppe federation could organize itself. Third, a postal-trade system stretching from Korea to Hungary, secured by terror and run by literate Khitan and Persian bureaucrats who had learned the hard way that running an empire required more skills than conquering it.

Hold those three. We will need all of them when we look at how the empire broke.

## Concept 5 — The four-khanate split: Hulagu, Kublai, and the limits of the world

When Ogedei died in 1241, the Mongol succession was already slipping. His widow Toregene served as regent. Their son Guyuk became khan in 1246 and died after two years on the throne, possibly poisoned. By 1251 the line had moved sideways. Mongke, a grandson of Chinggis through the youngest son Tolui, was elected great khan with backing from his brother Hulagu and his cousin Batu of the Golden Horde.

Mongke was the last great khan to rule a unified empire. He took a fresh census, restored discipline, and parceled out the next round of expansion among his brothers. Kublai got the campaign against the Southern Song. Hulagu got the Islamic west.

Hulagu took Persia, then the Assassins (the famous Nizari Ismaili sect, whose mountain fortress of Alamut had survived for 166 years), then Mesopotamia. In February 1258 Baghdad fell. The destruction was catastrophic. The caliph was killed. The five-hundred-year Abbasid line ended. Hulagu pressed west through Syria, took Aleppo and Damascus, and was poised to continue into Egypt when, in 1259, Mongke died. Hulagu, like every Mongol prince, had to return for the *kurultai*. He left a token force of about twenty thousand under his general Kitbuqa.

Egypt at this moment was ruled by the Mamluks — slave-soldiers of Turkic origin who had overthrown the Ayyubid sultans who had once owned them. Their leader Baybars was a former Kipchak Turk who had been sold into slavery as a boy by Mongols. In September 1260, at Ain Jalut in Galilee — the same site, by tradition, where David fought Goliath — the Mamluks met Kitbuqa's reduced force and annihilated it. Kitbuqa was killed. Hulagu, now back in Persia with his hands full of his own succession crisis, did not return.

Ain Jalut is one of the genuinely consequential battles of the medieval world. Not because the Mamluk force was overwhelming — it was not — but because it broke the spell. The Mongols, until that day, had been undefeated in pitched battle for fifty years. After Ain Jalut, they were defeatable. The Islamic Levant, the holy cities of Sunni Islam, and the southern Mediterranean were preserved from Mongol rule. The arc of the conquest had hit its western limit.

It also hit its eastern limit. Kublai's campaign against the Song lasted from 1265 to 1279. The Southern Song fought hard on rivers — the Mongols had to learn naval warfare from scratch, recruiting Chinese and Korean shipbuilders — and the last loyalist Song court eventually retreated to a fleet of a thousand ocean-going ships off the coast at Yamen. In 1279, after the supplies on board ran out, the Song chancellor jumped into the sea holding the eight-year-old emperor. Tens of thousands of officials and family members followed. China was reunited for the first time in three centuries. The new dynasty was the Yuan, and its emperor was a Mongol.

But during these same years, Mongke had died, and the great-khanate had dissolved. Kublai claimed it; his brother Ariq Boke also claimed it; civil war broke out. Kublai eventually won and ruled what is generally called the Great Khanate of Yuan China, but his authority over the western khanates was now nominal. Hulagu's heirs, the Il-Khans, ruled Persia and Mesopotamia independently. Batu's heirs, the Golden Horde, ruled the steppe north of the Caspian and the lands of the Rus. The Chagatai Khanate held Central Asia. From 1260 forward, there were four Mongol empires, not one.

What pulled them apart was partly succession politics — the Mongols never solved the problem of who replaced a khan, and a system that requires every prince to return for an election does not scale across continents — and partly an underlying cultural cleavage. Some Mongols wanted to keep living on the steppe. Others wanted cities. Kublai built Khanbaliq (modern Beijing) and adopted Chinese administrative practices. Ariq Boke embodied the old nomadic ways. After Kublai won, the cleavage did not heal; it just expressed itself as four different settlements with four different local cultures.

The Il-Khanate converted to Islam — formally under Ghazan in 1295, and the conversion was deep, with the *yassa* eventually replaced by sharia. The Golden Horde converted to Islam more gradually, with Uzbeg making it official in 1313. The Chagatai Khanate converted last, under Tarmashirin in the 1320s. The Yuan dynasty, surrounded by Buddhists and Daoists and Confucians, did not. By the end of the thirteenth century, three of the four Mongol successor states were Muslim — and three Mongol khans were the most powerful Muslim rulers in the world.

**The trade-off named.** The conquests had created a portfolio of territories so diverse — Buddhist Tibet, Confucian China, Muslim Persia, Christian Rus — that no single legal or religious framework could administer them all. The empire could only stay unified by remaining at war. Once Ain Jalut and Yamen marked the limits, the political system had no source of new wealth to redistribute, no enemy to focus its energies, and no ideological glue beyond the personal authority of a khan most subjects had never seen. It split because there was no longer anything holding it together except inertia and habit.

## Concept 6 — Yuan China and the failure of paper money

Kublai's Yuan dynasty (1271–1368) was the most thorough Mongol attempt at running a settled empire on its own terms. Kublai kept the Confucian civil service in skeletal form but suspended the examinations for over forty years. He installed a four-tier ethnic class system: Mongols at the top, then non-Chinese foreigners (Persians, Central Asians, Europeans), then northern Han Chinese (former Jin subjects), and at the bottom the southern Song Chinese, who made up over ninety percent of the population.

The system staffed itself with foreign administrators specifically because they had no local base. This is how Marco Polo ended up working for the Yuan — he and his uncles had come east to trade, and Kublai, recognizing that a Venetian merchant had no Chinese kinship network to mobilize, employed him as a regional administrator for nearly twenty years. Most of the foreign bureaucrats were Muslim merchants from western parts of the empire. The system was deliberately rotational and deliberately disconnected from the population it governed.

The most consequential Yuan policy decision concerned money. The Song had pioneered paper currency, backed by deposits of silver and silk and used alongside metallic coins. The Yuan made paper currency mandatory for transactions with the government. In principle each note was redeemable for silver. In practice the government printed many more notes than it had silver to back. Inflation followed. A decree could not stop it; the population still had to obtain paper to pay taxes, and the government's expenses kept rising — partly because Kublai's foreign adventures were extraordinarily expensive and almost uniformly disastrous. Two invasions of Japan, in 1274 and 1281, were destroyed by typhoons (the *kamikaze*, "divine wind"). Two invasions of Vietnam failed against guerrilla resistance. An invasion of Java in 1293 collapsed when Kublai's Javanese ally turned on him.

By the 1320s the Yuan were in serious difficulty. By the 1330s, when reports of a strange illness killed ninety percent of the population in parts of Hebei within three years, the dynasty was beginning to disintegrate. We will pick up that story in Chapter 20.

**Worked example: paper money and trust.** The Yuan inflation crisis is a clean case study in monetary economics, and worth running carefully. Paper money works when the issuer credibly promises to redeem it, or when the user community treats the paper as a unit of account regardless of redeemability — which requires the same trust by another route. The Yuan promised redeemability and broke the promise. Each broken promise reduced the value of the next promise. As the value of each note fell, the government had to issue more notes to meet its obligations, which fell faster, which required more notes. A government with a credible currency can run a deficit; a government with an incredible currency can run only the printing press, until the press runs out the trust. The Yuan ran out the trust by about 1340.

## Concept 7 — The other side of Eurasia: what the Mongols did not conquer

While the Mongols were redrawing Asia, Europe was, mostly, busy with itself. A few intersections matter.

In 1215, the same year Genghis was finishing the Jin campaign, English barons forced King John to accept Magna Carta — a document affirming, among other things, that "no free man shall be seized or imprisoned, or stripped of his rights or possessions, or outlawed or exiled... except by the lawful judgment of his equals." It is the ancestor of trial by jury. In 1265 Edward I called the first Parliament. In 1231, Frederick II of the Hohenstaufens promulgated the Constitutions of Melfi in southern Italy — the oldest written constitution that survives — replacing local vassals with salaried royal bureaucrats. In France, Philip IV moved the papacy to Avignon in 1305, where it stayed for seventy years.

Meanwhile the church, which had been the strongest unifying institution of Latin Christendom, hardened. In the 1230s Pope Gregory IX created the Office of Papal Inquisition. In 1252 Pope Innocent IV authorized the use of torture on suspected heretics. The administrative machinery of religious uniformity — what would become a centuries-long apparatus of surveillance and execution — dates from these decades.

The crusades continued and got worse. The Fourth Crusade (1202–1204) never reached the Holy Land. Diverted by Venetian creditors, it ended up sacking Constantinople — a Christian city — in April 1204, killing thousands, looting the Hagia Sophia, and installing a Latin emperor on the Byzantine throne. The Eastern Empire never fully recovered. The Fifth (1217–1221), Sixth (1228–1229), and Seventh (1248–1254) Crusades all failed. The Sixth, led by an excommunicated Frederick II, achieved a negotiated truce that gave Christians titular control of Jerusalem; it scandalized everyone and lasted ten years. The Seventh was led by King Louis IX of France, who was captured in the Nile Delta and ransomed for over six million ounces of silver — about $134 million in 2020 dollars.

In the Iberian Peninsula, the Almohad Caliphate collapsed after the Battle of Las Navas de Tolosa in 1212. By the 1260s, Muslim rule in Iberia was reduced to a strip around Granada that paid vassal tribute to Castile. The remaining Muslim population was permitted to stay and worship but was subject to discriminatory dress codes, separate jurisdiction under Islamic scholars, and prohibitions on intermarriage and proselytizing.

In the Levant, the Mamluks who had defeated Hulagu at Ain Jalut went on to mop up the surviving crusader states. Tripoli fell in 1289; Acre fell in 1291. The crusader presence in the Holy Land, which had begun in 1099, ended.

In northern India, the Delhi Sultanate — itself a Muslim state founded by Turkic mamluks in 1206 — beat back at least six Chagatai Mongol invasions between 1296 and 1306. The Sultanate has a striking parallel with Mamluk Egypt, including the brief reign of a remarkable woman: Sultana Raziya, daughter of Iltutmish, who ruled for four years from 1236, dressed in men's clothing, led armies, and was eventually deposed and killed by a coalition of nobles unwilling to be governed by a woman.

What unifies these disparate threads is the geography of the limit. The Mongols did not conquer Western Europe (turned around at Bohemia in 1242), Egypt (stopped at Ain Jalut in 1260), or northern India (stopped repeatedly by the Delhi Sultanate). The cultural and political forms that survived in these three zones — parliamentary England, the Mamluk sultanate, the Delhi Sultanate — became the sources of late medieval and early modern world history. The Mongol Empire shaped what came next as much by what it left alone as by what it conquered.

## Synthesis

Pull back. We started with a boy on the steppe in the 1180s and ended with a four-way division of Eurasia in the 1340s. What changed?

First, the steppe stopped being a problem to be managed and became, briefly, the political center of gravity for the Eurasian landmass. The pattern that had held since the Han dynasty — settled civilizations on the periphery, raiders periodically harassing them — was inverted. For a century, the periphery ran the center. The walls and tribute systems and managed alliances that had defined the Tang and the Song approach to the steppe became obsolete the day the steppe organized itself.

Second, a single legal and infrastructural framework briefly knit the entire continent. Genoese merchants in Tabriz; Persian astronomers in Beijing; Chinese gunpowder in the Mamluk arsenals at Ain Jalut; Mongol embassies to the pope; Marco Polo and Ibn Battuta walking the same roads. The world that emerged after the Mongol collapse — a world of European exploration, gunpowder warfare, and direct contact between previously distant civilizations — was incubated in the Pax Mongolica.

Third, the conquests broke the Abbasid caliphate, the Southern Song, and the Khwarazmian state in the same generation. The destruction of these institutions opened space that was filled by new regional powers: the Mamluks, the Yuan, the various successor states of Persia. Two centuries later three new empires — the Ottomans, the Safavids, the Mughals — would assemble themselves in the wreckage, and each would owe its institutional shape partly to Mongol antecedents.

Fourth, the conquests displaced people. Turkic peoples from Central Asia, pushed by Mongol expansion, ended up as mamluks in Egypt, as Seljuks in Anatolia, as the founders of the Delhi Sultanate, as the eventual Ottoman dynasty. The Turkic-speaking world we now know — from Istanbul to Xinjiang — was substantially shaped by Mongol pressure rather than directly by Mongol conquest.

What the Mongols did not do is also worth naming. They did not produce a major literary or philosophical tradition of their own. They did not introduce a new religion. They did not invent significant technologies. What they did was move other people's technologies, religions, and ideas across distances that had previously been impassable. They were a transmission system, sometimes a benevolent one, often a homicidal one, and the world they passed through was permanently altered by the passage.

Now come back to the boy with no clan. He had wanted, *The Secret History* says, to end the constant raiding that had destroyed his family and made his childhood unsurvivable. Within his own people, he succeeded. He banned the practices that had abandoned his mother, enslaved him as a child, and stolen his wife. The cost was that he had to point all that violence somewhere else. He pointed it outward, and a hundred million people died under the policies he created — perhaps more. The reform that was a gift to his own people was a catastrophe for everyone else. This is not a small moral fact, and it is not one that Mongol pride or Western horror has yet fully absorbed.

A last sentence, then, before the exercises. The Mongols were not exceptional in their violence; many empires have killed on this scale. They were exceptional in the speed of their conquest and the breadth of their reach. The combination produced something the world had not seen before and would not see again until steamships and machine guns: a single political system spanning continents, run by people who had chosen, deliberately, not to live in cities. They built nothing. They moved everything.

## Chapter summary

The Song dynasty (960–1279) presided over a near-industrial revolution in China — population doubling, iron production exceeding eighteenth-century Britain, gunpowder weaponized — but its Confucian preference for civilian over military rule left it strategically vulnerable. The Khitan Liao, Tangut Xi Xia, and Jurchen Jin successively dominated north China; the Jurchen drove the Song south of the Huai River in 1127.

Temujin (c. 1162–1227) unified the Mongolian steppe through a series of innovations — promotion by merit rather than kinship, the decimal military structure, the *yassa* legal code — that turned tribal raiding into integrated imperial conquest. He took the title Chinggis Khan at the *kurultai* of 1206. By his death in 1227, the empire stretched from Korea to the Caspian.

Ogedei (r. 1229–1241), advised by Yelu Chucai, built the bureaucratic architecture of empire — regular taxation, the *yam* relay system, the capital at Karakorum — and presided over the Pax Mongolica. The European campaign of 1241 reached Hungary and Bohemia before withdrawing on news of his death.

Mongke (r. 1251–1259) was the last unified great khan. His brother Hulagu sacked Baghdad in 1258, ending the Abbasid caliphate, but was halted by the Mamluks at Ain Jalut in 1260 — the first major Mongol defeat. His brother Kublai conquered the Southern Song by 1279, founding the Yuan dynasty. After Mongke's death, the empire split permanently into four khanates: Yuan China, the Il-Khanate of Persia, the Chagatai Khanate of Central Asia, and the Golden Horde of the western steppe.

The three western khanates converted to Islam over the following century. The Yuan dynasty, plagued by hyperinflation, succession crises, failed foreign expeditions, and eventually plague, collapsed in 1368. Meanwhile, in the lands the Mongols did not conquer — Latin Europe, Mamluk Egypt, the Delhi Sultanate — institutional developments were proceeding that would shape the world after the Pax Mongolica ended: Magna Carta (1215), the Inquisition (1230s), the Constitutions of Melfi (1231), the Avignon papacy (1305), the Mamluk sultanate (1250), the Delhi Sultanate (1206).

## Key terms

*Yassa* — Chinggis Khan's law code; abolished theft and Mongol enslavement, codified religious tolerance, regulated marriage and hunting.

*Kurultai* — Mongol assembly of senior princes and warriors, called to confirm a great khan or major decision.

*Yam* — horse-relay postal and supply station system; backbone of Pax Mongolica trade and communication.

*Paiza* — metal medallion authorizing use of the *yam* network; the ancestor of every diplomatic passport.

*Mandarin* — Confucian-trained civilian bureaucrat selected by examination; the dominant administrative class in pre-modern China.

*Mamluk* — Turkic or Caucasian slave-soldier; the institution that produced the Ayyubid armies, the Egyptian Mamluk dynasty (1250–1517), and the founding dynasty of the Delhi Sultanate.

*Pax Mongolica* — the ~1230s–1340s period of relative peace and integrated trade across Eurasia under Mongol political dominance.

*Khanate* — successor state of the unified Mongol Empire after the 1260 split: Yuan, Il-Khanate, Chagatai, Golden Horde.

*Sufism* — Islamic mystical tradition emphasizing meditation and asceticism; played a major role in the conversion of steppe Mongols to Islam.

*Ulama* — Islamic religious scholars; effectively administered much of the Il-Khanate as Mongol political authority weakened.

*Constitutions of Melfi* — 1231 legal code of the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies under Frederick II; oldest surviving written constitution.

*Magna Carta* — 1215 English document affirming baronial rights and the principle of trial by peers.

*Inquisition* — papal institution established in the 1230s to identify and punish heresy; authorized to use torture from 1252.

## Further reading

- *The Secret History of the Mongols*, trans. Igor de Rachewiltz (2004) — closest to a Mongol-perspective primary source; includes the Jamukha-Temujin friendship oath and the Baljuna Covenant.
- Jack Weatherford, *Genghis Khan and the Making of the Modern World* (2004) — accessible synthesis; argues a strong "Mongols built modernity" thesis you should test against the evidence.
- Marie Favereau, *The Horde: How the Mongols Changed the World* (2021) — focuses on the Golden Horde; revisionist on Mongol economic and administrative sophistication.
- Timothy May, *The Mongol Empire* (2018) — comprehensive scholarly survey.
- Marco Polo, *The Travels* (c. 1300) — primary source, Yuan China seen by a Venetian; read with skepticism, since the manuscript history is complicated and some scholars doubt Polo went as far east as he claimed.
- Ibn Battuta, *Rihla* (c. 1355) — primary source; covers Mongol India and Yuan China by a Moroccan jurist who walked roughly seventy-five thousand miles in thirty years.
- Morris Rossabi, *Khubilai Khan: His Life and Times* (1988) — definitive biography of the Yuan founder.

## What would change my mind

If new archaeological or genetic evidence demonstrated that the population collapses Persian chroniclers attributed to the Khwarazmia campaign were exaggerated by an order of magnitude — turning Mongol violence into something closer to ordinary medieval conquest — the moral arithmetic of this chapter would need to be substantially revised.

## Still puzzling

I do not fully understand why Chinggis's specific organizational innovations — the decimal military system, merit-based promotion, the redistribution of fallen soldiers' shares — were not copied successfully by his neighbors. The technical knowledge was not secret. The Khwarazmians, the Jin, the Rus, all had a chance to observe Mongol methods before being defeated by them. Why did no opponent reproduce the system? It may be that the institutions required more than imitation — they required the social ground that the *yassa* prepared. But I am not satisfied with that answer yet.
