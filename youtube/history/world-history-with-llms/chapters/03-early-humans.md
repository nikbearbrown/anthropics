# Chapter 3 — Early Humans
*Three Million Years, Two Migrations, and the Decision to Stop Moving.*

## Three suggested titles

- Early Humans: The Long Walk That Made Us
- From Lucy to Çatalhöyük: Three Million Years of Becoming Human
- The Paleolithic, the Neolithic, and the Bargain We Struck With Wheat

---

## Chapter overview

By the end of this chapter you will know how the species *Homo sapiens* came to exist, how it spread across the planet, and how it settled down. You will know who Lucy was and why an upright-walking creature 3.2 million years old changed how we read our own evolution. You will know the chain *Australopithecus → Homo habilis → Homo erectus → Homo sapiens* and the diversity of cousin species — Neanderthals, Denisovans, *Homo heidelbergensis* — that walked the same Earth as our direct ancestors. You will know how Paleolithic people lived for hundreds of thousands of years in small egalitarian bands and how those bands gradually accumulated the tools and language that let them spread across the planet. You will know the Neolithic Revolution as a *bargain* — agriculture provided more calories and larger populations at the cost of leisure, equality, and a more varied diet — and you will know why some peoples accepted the bargain and others did not. You will know the early settlements of Çatalhöyük, Jericho, Mehrgarh, and the Yangtze valley as concrete instances of the choice. The chapter spans roughly three million years. The mechanisms are concrete. Each transition has a cost.

## Opening case — Hadar, Ethiopia, 1974

On a hot November morning in 1974, an American paleoanthropologist named Donald Johanson and his graduate student Tom Gray were walking back to camp from a survey trip in the Afar region of northeastern Ethiopia. Johanson glanced at the eroding edge of a gully. He saw a piece of arm bone. Then a piece of skull. Then a piece of jaw. Within a few weeks, his team had recovered roughly forty percent of a small adult skeleton that had been buried for 3.2 million years.

That night the team played the Beatles' *Lucy in the Sky with Diamonds* in camp. The skeleton was named Lucy.

She was about three and a half feet tall. Her brain was about a third the size of a modern human's. She had powerful arms suited to climbing in trees and a hairy body more like a chimpanzee's than ours. Her hips and knees, however, were unmistakably built for walking upright on two legs. She had been doing it long before her species evolved a brain large enough to plan or to argue. She belonged to the species *Australopithecus afarensis* — named for the Afar region where Johanson found her — and she was the most complete early hominin skeleton ever recovered at the time.

Her significance was that she settled an old debate. Before Lucy, scientists had argued for decades about which evolved first in the line that became us — the upright walking, or the big brain. The answer turned out to be the walking. Lucy's brain was small. Her stride was almost modern. The brain came later, in stages, over more than two million years.

This chapter is a long walk, in two senses. It is the literal walk from Lucy to us — a journey of three million years, during which our ancestors descended from the trees, learned to make stone tools, controlled fire, developed language, painted bison on cave walls, and migrated to every habitable continent. It is also the conceptual walk from a small-brained, hairy ape to a creature that built circular stone enclosures at Göbekli Tepe in Turkey, thirty-foot mud-brick walls at Çatalhöyük, and a city of six thousand people surrounded by the world's first known monumental wall at Jericho. The walk has direction. It has stages. It has costs we tend to forget.

We pick it up with Lucy, look at the chain that produced us, and end at the bargain that produced civilization.

## Concept 1 — From Australopithecus to Homo sapiens

The genus *Homo* — the genus to which we belong — emerged from *Australopithecus* somewhere between two and three million years ago. The transition was not a single event. It was a long process during which several species coexisted and exchanged genes.

**Lucy's people** (*Australopithecus afarensis*, c. 4–2.5 million years ago) lived in eastern and southern Africa. They walked upright. They probably used simple tools — sticks, stones picked up unmodified — though stone tools they had *made* are rare. Their brains were about 400 cubic centimeters, roughly a third of ours.

**Homo habilis** ("handy man") appeared in the African archaeological record about two to three million years ago. Their brains were larger. Their faces were flatter, their teeth smaller. They are traditionally credited with the first deliberately knapped stone tools — the **Oldowan** tradition, named for the Olduvai Gorge in Tanzania where they were first identified by the Leakey family in the 1930s. Oldowan tools are crude by later standards: a fist-sized rock hit at an angle to produce a sharp flake. The flake butchered animals, scraped hides, cracked bones for marrow. The pre-flake "core" was kept as a chopper. The toolkit was simple. It was also revolutionary: the first systematic application of the principle that one rock can be used to shape another.

**Homo erectus** ("upright man") emerged in East Africa around two million years ago and survived for an astonishing 1.9 million years — perhaps until as recently as a hundred thousand years ago in some regions. They were nearly our height. They lived entirely on the ground. They made and used substantially better stone tools — the **Acheulean** tradition, named for Saint-Acheul in France, characterized by the symmetric teardrop hand-axe shaped by chipping flakes from both sides of a core. They used fire. They built shelters. They cared for their sick — bones of *Homo erectus* individuals show healed injuries that would have been fatal without the help of others. They probably had the anatomical equipment for some kind of vocal communication, though whether they had language in our sense is debated.

The most important thing *Homo erectus* did, for our story, is leave Africa. Sometime between 1.8 and 1.5 million years ago, populations of *Homo erectus* migrated north and east, reaching North Africa, the Near East, Europe, and East Asia. Their reasons probably involved climate change and the movement of prey animals. They followed rivers and lakes. The descendants of these migrant populations evolved, in different regions, into different species: *Homo heidelbergensis*, the Neanderthals (*Homo neanderthalensis*) in Europe and western Asia, and the Denisovans in central Asia. *Homo sapiens* emerged in Africa from another *erectus* descendant lineage roughly 200,000 years ago.

For most of our species' existence, we shared the planet with cousins. Neanderthals were not stupid brutes. They had brains slightly *larger* than ours. They made sophisticated tools — the **Mousterian** tradition, characterized by carefully prepared flake tools struck from carefully prepared cores. They buried their dead, sometimes with grave goods. They cared for the disabled. They survived ice-age Europe for several hundred thousand years before *Homo sapiens* arrived.

Then they disappeared. Around 40,000 years ago, the Neanderthal population collapsed. The cause is still debated. Modern humans may have outcompeted them for resources. There may have been violence. There was certainly some interbreeding — modern Europeans and Middle Eastern populations carry between one and four percent Neanderthal DNA. A bone fragment from the Denisova Cave in Siberia, dated to about 90,000 years ago, belonged to a thirteen-year-old girl whose mother was Neanderthal and whose father was Denisovan. The lineages were close enough to interbreed and produce children. Their descendants, in some sense, are still walking around. But the Neanderthals as a distinct species — bones, tools, distinct genetic lineage — did not survive past about 40,000 years ago, and by perhaps 15,000 years ago all the cousin species were gone.

We were left alone.

## Concept 2 — Out of Africa: the global migration

Modern humans emerged in Africa. Modern humans then left Africa. Both statements are widely accepted. The details are still being argued.

The dominant model is **Out of Africa**: *Homo sapiens* originated in Africa around 200,000 years ago, and groups of *sapiens* began migrating out around 100,000 years ago, eventually replacing the cousin species that *Homo erectus* descendants had become. An older alternative — the **multiregional model** — proposed that *Homo sapiens* evolved in several regions independently from local *erectus* populations. The genetic data favor Out of Africa. The story may be partly both: a single African origin followed by limited interbreeding with the cousin populations the migrants encountered.

The migration was not an exodus. It was a slow leak over tens of thousands of years.

By 70,000 years ago, *Homo sapiens* had reached India and southern China by following the southern coasts of Asia. By 45,000 years ago they had reached New Guinea and Australia, requiring boats or rafts to cross water gaps that even an ice-age sea level had not closed. By 40,000 years ago they had entered Europe, either via the Mediterranean coast or up the Danube valley. By 25,000 years ago they were in Siberia. Around 15,000 years ago — the date is contested and pushing earlier with new finds — humans crossed from Siberia into North America, probably across the Bering land bridge that the lowered sea levels of the last ice age had exposed. Within a few thousand years they had reached the southern tip of South America.

The motive force was probably climate combined with hunter-gatherer ecology. Hunter-gatherer groups need a large foraging range; when local resources thin, the group splits and one branch moves on. Over hundreds of generations, this slow drift reached every habitable continent.

The migrations had consequences for the wildlife. Australia before humans arrived was home to a number of "megafauna" — giant kangaroos, marsupial lions, enormous wombats, large lizards. Most of these went extinct shortly after humans arrived around 45,000 years ago. The Americas similarly lost mammoths, mastodons, giant ground sloths, native horses, and saber-toothed cats around the time human populations spread. Climate change was part of the story. Human predation was part of the story too. The question of how much each contributed is still being argued by archaeologists, paleontologists, and historical ecologists, and the proportions probably differ region by region.

Discovery occasionally revises the timeline. The 1996 discovery of the eight-thousand-year-old "Kennewick Man" remains in Washington State produced anatomical features more consistent with Southeast Asian populations than with the populations historically presumed to have peopled the Americas. The interpretation is debated; the underlying point is that the migration was almost certainly more complex than a single wave from Siberia. It was many waves, in many directions, over a long time.

## Concept 3 — Paleolithic technologies: stone, fire, language, art

The **Paleolithic Age** ("old stone age") runs from the earliest stone tools — perhaps 3.3 million years ago, perhaps as recent as 2.6 million depending on which finds you accept — to the beginning of the Neolithic Revolution about twelve thousand years ago. It is by far the longest period in human history, longer than every other period combined by orders of magnitude.

**Stone tools** are the period's signature artifact because stone preserves. Wood, bone, leather, plant fiber — anything biological — is mostly gone. Almost everything we know about the Paleolithic, we know through stone, occasionally through bone, and through the rare cave paintings preserved by accidental geology.

The toolkit progressed in three major stages.

The **Oldowan** tradition (c. 2.6 million to 1.7 million years ago, attributed to *Homo habilis* and early *Homo erectus*) used core-and-flake stone tools — a hammer-stone struck at an angle against a target stone to produce a sharp flake. The flake cut. The core became a chopper. Crude in shape, the Oldowan toolkit nonetheless solved the problem of cutting through hide and breaking bone for marrow.

The **Acheulean** tradition (c. 1.7 million to 250,000 years ago, attributed to *Homo erectus* and later *Homo heidelbergensis*) introduced the symmetric teardrop hand-axe — chipped on both sides to produce a long, thin, sharp implement. Acheulean hand-axes are remarkably consistent across hundreds of thousands of years and across continents from southern Africa to India. The level of motor coordination and forward planning required to make one is not trivial; some archaeologists argue the consistency reflects a kind of cultural transmission that approaches teaching.

The **Mousterian** tradition (c. 250,000 to 30,000 years ago, attributed to Neanderthals and early modern humans) shifted emphasis from the core to the flake. Tool-makers now carefully *prepared* a core specifically so that they could strike off flakes of pre-determined size and shape. The Mousterian craftsperson was working backward from a desired result through several mental steps. By 45,000 years ago, modern humans had elaborated the technique further into the Upper Paleolithic toolkits — long blades, scrapers, engraving tools, and bone implements specialized for distinct tasks.

**Fire** matters as much as stone. Whether *Homo erectus* could *make* fire or merely *use* and *carry* fire from natural sources is debated. By 125,000 years ago at the latest, modern humans were starting and controlling fires reliably. Cooked food is easier to chew, easier to digest, and absorbs more calories per ingested gram. The cooking of starchy tubers — possibly very early — may have been the calorie boost that allowed brain size to keep growing. Fire also extended the day, enabled colonization of cold environments, deterred predators, hardened the points of wooden spears, and gathered the social group around a single warm point at night.

**Language** is harder to date. Anatomical evidence — the position of the larynx, the hyoid bone — suggests that *Homo erectus* and Neanderthals had at least the physical equipment for some kind of vocal communication. Modern humans, by 100,000 years ago at the latest, were producing the full range of sounds modern languages use (about fifty distinct phonemes, combinable into tens of thousands of words). Language transformed cooperative activity. A hunting band coordinating without language is one thing; a band that can plan, debate strategy, transmit knowledge to children, name landmarks, and discuss what happened yesterday is a categorically different kind of group.

**Art** is the period's most arresting evidence. Cave paintings from Chauvet (France, ~36,000 years ago), Altamira (Spain, ~36,000 years ago), Lascaux (France, ~17,000 years ago), and many other European, Indonesian, and Australian sites depict bison, horses, deer, bulls, lions, and the occasional human figure. The artists used charcoal and ochre, sometimes mixed with binders. They incorporated the natural contours of the cave walls into the bodies of their subjects, producing a three-dimensional effect that still works on modern viewers. The paintings are usually deep inside the caves, in places no one would see them by accident, lit by torches and oil lamps. They were probably not decoration. They probably had ritual or shamanic significance whose specifics are lost.

Smaller portable art has also survived. Carved animal figures, bone flutes (the oldest are about 40,000 years old), and the so-called **Venus figurines** — small carved female figures, often with exaggerated breasts and hips, found at sites from Germany (Hohle Fels and Willendorf) to central Russia. The Venus of Hohle Fels, the oldest known, is about 40,000 years old; the Venus of Willendorf is about 30,000 years old. Their meaning is debated. Fertility figurines, self-portraits by women, ritual amulets, talismans for safe childbirth — the interpretations multiply because the makers left no notes.

What the art tells us, beyond any specific interpretation, is that Paleolithic humans had abstract symbolic thought. They could represent the world. They could carry meaningful objects long distances. They had something we recognize as religion, even if we cannot recover its content.

## Concept 4 — How they lived: small bands, egalitarian, gendered

Paleolithic hunter-gatherers lived in small groups. The typical band was perhaps fifteen to forty people, organized as several closely related families. The constraints were ecological: an environment can support a finite number of foragers working over a finite range. Larger groups would have outstripped the local resource base. Smaller groups would have lacked the labor needed for cooperative hunting and child-rearing.

The diet varied dramatically by environment. In lush tropical environments — much of pre-agricultural Africa, parts of Southeast Asia, the Amazon basin — gathered plants did most of the calorie work: nuts, fruits, tubers, honey, insects. In Arctic and sub-Arctic environments — the Eurasian and American steppes, the high Arctic — meat and fish dominated, supplemented by the few hardy plants the cold permitted. In between, the mix shifted. Small game (rabbits, birds, fish, shellfish) was probably more important than large game on most days. Big-game hunts — bison drives, mammoth kills — happened, but they were dangerous, intermittent, and required substantial cooperation.

The social organization is reconstructed from the bones, the artifacts, and from comparison with hunter-gatherer societies that survive into modern times. Three modern groups have been studied closely: the **San** of the Kalahari (southern Africa), the **Inuit** of the Arctic (Canada, Alaska, Greenland), and the **Awá** of the Brazilian Amazon. None of them is a Paleolithic relic — they are modern peoples with their own histories — but their social structures show the kinds of arrangements that small mobile foraging populations tend to produce.

What the comparisons suggest is **egalitarianism**. With no surplus to accumulate and no land to inherit, social hierarchy in hunter-gatherer bands is shallow. There is differentiation — a particularly skilled hunter, an especially wise elder, a respected shaman — but the differences do not produce permanent class structures. Decisions are usually made by discussion. Goods are usually shared. Authority is usually situational and revocable.

There is, however, a gender division of labor. Across the modern foraging societies that have been studied, men tend to do most of the long-distance hunting; women tend to do most of the gathering, though the proportions vary. The older textbook image of "man the hunter" was overstated — gathered plant foods provided more total calories in many environments — but the division was real. Inuit life, with its extreme dependence on hunted meat, places more of the productive labor on men. Awá life in the Amazon, where plant foods are abundant year-round, distributes more evenly. The constraints of childbirth and child-rearing — pregnancy, lactation, the care of small children incompatible with multi-day hunts — shaped what each gender could practically do.

The religious life is recoverable only in fragments. Modern hunter-gatherer groups generally practice some form of **animism** — the belief that spiritual force is distributed across living and natural things, including animals, plants, weather, and natural features like mountains and rivers. They generally have shamans — community members thought to have access to the spirit world through trance, ritual, and dream. They generally bury their dead, sometimes with goods that suggest a belief in some kind of persistence. The Paleolithic burials we have found — fewer in number, more carefully preserved — match this pattern. Some are extraordinary: the 30,000-year-old Sungir burial in Russia, where two children were buried with thousands of mammoth-ivory beads, ivory spears, and animal carvings. The labor invested in these burials was enormous. Whatever the makers believed, they believed it strongly.

## Concept 5 — The Neolithic Revolution: a bargain

About twelve thousand years ago, in several places at roughly the same time, people stopped following the food and started growing it.

The shift was not universal. It was not even particularly fast. In a few centers — the **Fertile Crescent** of the Near East (modern Iraq, Syria, Israel, Lebanon, Jordan, Turkey, Iran), the Yangtze and Yellow River valleys of China, the river valleys of Mesoamerica, and the Andean highlands of South America — populations began deliberately planting and tending wild grasses. In the Fertile Crescent the grasses were einkorn wheat and emmer wheat, joined later by barley, peas, lentils, olives, dates, and figs. In China the crops were millet (north) and rice (south). In Mesoamerica the central crop was maize, with squash and beans. In the Andes the central crop was potatoes. In sub-Saharan Africa, sorghum was domesticated independently, perhaps around six thousand years ago.

The mechanism — by which a wild grass becomes a domesticated crop — is artificial selection. Wild wheat has hard husks that protect the seed. Humans wanted the seed; they preferentially gathered, replanted, and consumed plants whose husks split more easily. Over generations, the gathered seeds were the seeds of plants with thinner husks. The domesticated wheat that resulted has husks so thin the seed cannot survive a winter on its own. Domesticated wheat depends on human planting. Humans depend, in turn, on the wheat. Each domesticated, in some sense, the other.

Why did the shift happen? The honest answer is that we do not know completely. The leading hypotheses:

**Climate change.** The end of the most recent ice age, roughly 12,000 years ago, brought warmer and drier conditions to the Near East. Wild grain became more abundant in some areas. Pressure on big game from hunting plus climate change reduced the alternatives. Settled life around naturally productive stands of wild grain became viable, and a small step from "we'll come back to this stand next year" to "we'll plant some over here, where we live now" closed the gap.

**Population pressure.** Hunter-gatherer populations grew slowly but steadily. Eventually, in productive regions, populations grew dense enough that some groups could no longer support themselves through pure foraging. They had to intensify, and intensification of plant collection led, by degrees, to cultivation.

**Religion and ritual.** This is the surprising one. The site of **Göbekli Tepe** in southeastern Turkey, excavated since the 1990s by the German archaeologist Klaus Schmidt and his successors, is a complex of stone-pillar enclosures dated to around 11,600 years ago — roughly a thousand years *before* clear agricultural cultivation in the same region. The pillars are massive (some weighing tens of tons), carved with reliefs of foxes, snakes, scorpions, and birds. They were built by people who were still hunter-gatherers in their food economy. Schmidt's interpretation: the religious site came first, and the agricultural settlement followed because the religious site required a settled population to build and maintain. More recent work by Lee Clare suggests that Göbekli Tepe may have been a *resistance* to the looming agricultural transition — a way of preserving a hunter-gatherer cultural identity even as the surrounding landscape was changing. The interpretations differ. The fact that they need to differ — that a clearly pre-agricultural ritual complex exists at all — challenges the older sequence in which agriculture came first and religion was a later elaboration. Religion may have been the cause, not the consequence.

**Co-domestication.** The journalist and naturalist Michael Pollan has argued, only half-playfully, that wheat domesticated humans as much as humans domesticated wheat. Humans cleared the fields, irrigated the plants, fought off insects, removed competitors. Wheat got planetary dominance. Humans got hard work and bad backs. The argument is provocative but has the merit of forcing the question: in a relationship between species, who is the agent and who is the instrument?

The probable answer is that all four mechanisms operated together. Climate created the opportunity. Population pressure created the necessity. Religious life created the settled communities that could practice cultivation. The relationship between humans and a few key plants and animals became a tighter mutual dependence that neither could leave.

### What the bargain cost

The traditional textbook framing celebrates the Neolithic Revolution as progress. Larger populations, food surpluses, civilization, writing, cities. All of that is true.

The other side of the ledger is harder to celebrate.

**Less leisure.** Hunter-gatherer foraging in a productive environment takes about twenty hours a week, by modern ethnographic measure. Early agriculture takes thirty or more, and is more physically demanding. The "more time for art and contemplation" that was supposed to follow the agricultural surplus did not, on average, follow.

**Worse diets.** Hunter-gatherers eat a wide variety of foods — hundreds of plant species across the year, multiple animal sources. Early farmers eat mostly one or two staple grains, with a small supplement. Skeletal evidence from the early Neolithic shows reduced height, more dental disease, more vitamin and mineral deficiencies, and shorter lifespans on average than the immediately preceding hunter-gatherer populations.

**More disease.** Settled populations living near domesticated animals and concentrated waste exposed themselves to a new spectrum of infectious diseases. Many of the diseases that became part of the human story — measles, smallpox, influenza, tuberculosis — appear to have crossed from domesticated animals to humans, then spread through the dense settled populations agriculture made possible.

**More inequality.** Land could be inherited. Surplus could be stored. Stored surplus could be controlled. With agriculture came private property; with private property came classes, hierarchies, and eventually the political institutions that would dominate the next ten thousand years. Hunter-gatherer egalitarianism became impossible at agricultural densities.

**More gender stratification.** Where hunter-gatherer gender division was reciprocal — both genders' work was visible, valuable, and difficult to do without — agricultural life increasingly confined women to the domestic sphere. Plowing required strength. Field labor was often gendered male. Domestic labor — milling grain on stone, weaving cloth, raising children, processing food — was often gendered female and largely invisible. The political and military life that grew up around agricultural settlements was, in most places, male.

The bargain was not obviously a good one. It was made anyway. Once made, it was extremely hard to reverse. Populations grew to densities that hunting and gathering could not support. The path back was closed.

Some peoples resisted. The Indigenous Australians, in environments that could have supported some agriculture, declined the bargain and continued a hunter-gatherer life until European contact. The San in southern Africa, in a less hospitable environment, continued largely as hunter-gatherers until very recently. The Awá in the Brazilian rainforest only abandoned settled life *recently* — in the nineteenth century CE — in flight from colonial pressure, choosing a hunter-gatherer existence as an active strategy. The bargain was not a one-way ratchet. But once a region committed to agriculture, the surrounding hunter-gatherer populations either were absorbed, were displaced, or pushed into more marginal environments.

## Concept 6 — The first settlements

The earliest agricultural societies built places that look, even now, recognizably like towns.

**Çatalhöyük** in southern Turkey was occupied roughly between 7200 and 6000 BCE — for about 1,200 years. At its peak, perhaps six thousand people lived there. The houses were built of mud brick. They shared walls. There were no streets between them: residents entered their homes through ladders down through the roofs. The closely packed plan provided protection against outsiders and internal cohesion against environmental risk. The interior walls of the houses were plastered and decorated. Wall paintings showed hunts, animal forms, and what may be representations of mythic scenes (a woman giving birth to a bull, for example). Bull horns protruded from furniture. The cattle imagery was constant; whatever the religion was, cattle were central to it.

The economy was a hybrid. The people of Çatalhöyük cultivated wheat, barley, and lentils. They raised domesticated cattle. But they also continued to hunt wild animals and to gather wild plants, especially nuts, fruits, and tubers from the surrounding hills. They were not yet pure agriculturalists. They were experimenting.

**Jericho** in the Jordan River valley, near modern Jerusalem, was older — possibly occupied as early as 8300 BCE. Its design was different. It was surrounded by a thick stone wall and a deep ditch — the world's earliest known monumental fortifications. Inside the wall stood a stone tower whose purpose remains unclear (defensive lookout? religious monument? grain silo?). The fortifications imply that there was something worth taking, and something worth defending. Settled communities near other settled communities had to manage the new problem of organized aggression that hunter-gatherer bands rarely faced.

**Mehrgarh** in modern Pakistan, north and west of the Indus River, was settled by 7000 BCE. Its people farmed barley, raised goats and sheep, and — over the next few thousand years — domesticated cotton, beginning the long history that would lead to the textile economy of the Indian subcontinent. The houses were rectangular mud-brick structures divided into multiple rooms. The artisans worked with sea shells, sandstone, and lapis lazuli that had been traded long distances. The presence of imported materials means that even the earliest agricultural settlements were embedded in regional trade networks.

**The Yangtze and Yellow River valleys** of China saw their first Neolithic settlements around 8000 BCE — Yellow River farmers cultivated millet, while Yangtze farmers cultivated rice. Sites like Pengtoushan and Bashidang in the Yangtze valley preserve some of the earliest cultivated rice anywhere. The houses were partially excavated into the ground (a design that holds heat in winter and stays cool in summer) or built on raised earth platforms. Bashidang was surrounded by a ditch — possibly for drainage, possibly defensive — that may be the prototype of the moat that would surround Chinese cities for the next four thousand years.

**The Americas** developed agriculture independently. By 6500 BCE, peppers and squash were being cultivated in northeastern Mexico. Maize cultivation began in central Mexico in the same broad period and gradually spread. The Andean highlands were domesticating potatoes by 3000 BCE. The Americas had no native species suitable for plow-pulling and no domesticated horses, so their agricultural systems took a different shape than the Eurasian ones — but they were, on their own terms, equally productive.

The settlements established the pattern. Mud-brick or stone houses, often clustered for defense. A surrounding wall or ditch. Cultivated fields outside. Domesticated animals. A central religious structure or symbolic focus. Crafts — pottery, weaving, stone-working — practiced by specialists. Trade with other settlements for materials the local environment lacked. Burial of the dead within or near the settlement, sometimes with grave goods.

These were the seeds. The next chapters of this textbook tell what grew from them.

You can trace the Out-of-Africa migration: from East Africa around 100,000 years ago, into Asia by 70,000, Australia by 45,000, Europe by 40,000, the Americas by 15,000 (and pushing earlier with new finds).

You can name the three major stone-tool traditions of the Paleolithic — Oldowan, Acheulean, Mousterian — and explain what each represents about the cognitive capacities of its makers.

You can describe the everyday life of Paleolithic hunter-gatherers — band size, gendered labor division, egalitarianism, animist religion, art, burial — drawing on both archaeological evidence and the modern San, Inuit, and Awá comparisons (with awareness of those comparisons' limits).

You can articulate the Neolithic Revolution as a *bargain*: agriculture provided more calories per acre, larger populations, and surplus, at the cost of leisure, dietary diversity, equality, and exposure to new diseases. You can defend a ranking of its causal factors: climate, population pressure, religious/ritual life, co-domestication.

You can describe the earliest agricultural settlements — Çatalhöyük, Jericho, Mehrgarh, the Yangtze valley sites — as concrete instances of the Neolithic transition, each with its own architecture, economy, and surrounding trade.

The thing to watch for, going forward, is the temptation to read the Neolithic Revolution as obvious progress. It was a structural choice with real costs. The human story of the next ten thousand years happens within the constraints that choice created.

## Key terms

- **Acheulean tools.** Stone tools dating from c. 1.7 million to 250,000 years ago, characterized by symmetric teardrop hand-axes shaped by chipping flakes from both sides of a core.
- **Animism.** The belief that spiritual force is distributed across living and natural things — animals, plants, weather, natural features — characteristic of many hunter-gatherer religions.
- **Australopithecus.** The genus to which Lucy and her kin belonged, c. 4–2.5 million years ago. Walked upright; small brains; transitional toward the genus *Homo*.
- **Çatalhöyük.** A Neolithic settlement in southern Turkey, occupied c. 7200–6000 BCE. Densely packed mud-brick houses with elaborate interior decoration.
- **Domestication.** The process by which humans, through selective harvesting and reproduction, alter the genetic makeup of plants or animals to favor characteristics useful to humans.
- **Fertile Crescent.** The arc-shaped region from southeastern Turkey through the Levant to Mesopotamia where the Neolithic Revolution first emerged in the Old World.
- **Göbekli Tepe.** A pre-agricultural ritual complex in southeastern Turkey (c. 11,600 years ago) that has reshaped the chronology of religion versus agriculture.
- **Homo erectus.** Member of the genus *Homo* that emerged in East Africa around 2 million years ago and spread across Eurasia. The first hominin known to have left Africa.
- **Homo habilis.** Possibly the earliest member of the genus *Homo*, c. 2–3 million years ago, traditionally credited with the first deliberate stone tools.
- **Homo sapiens.** Modern humans. Emerged in Africa about 200,000 years ago.
- **Hunter-gatherers.** Peoples who survive by hunting wild animals and gathering wild plants rather than by cultivating crops or raising livestock.
- **Lucy.** The 3.2-million-year-old skeleton of *Australopithecus afarensis* discovered by Donald Johanson at Hadar, Ethiopia, in 1974.
- **Mousterian tools.** Stone-tool tradition c. 250,000 to 30,000 years ago, characterized by carefully prepared cores and flake-based tools. Associated with Neanderthals and early modern humans.
- **Neanderthals (*Homo neanderthalensis*).** Cousin species that lived in Europe and western Asia from c. 200,000 to 30,000 years ago. Disappeared after the arrival of modern humans, with some interbreeding.
- **Neolithic Age.** The period beginning around 12,000 years ago when humans began cultivating crops and domesticating animals.
- **Neolithic Revolution.** The transition from hunting and gathering to settled agriculture.
- **Oldowan tools.** The earliest deliberately knapped stone tools, c. 2.6 million to 1.7 million years ago. Crude core-and-flake implements.
- **Out-of-Africa model.** The dominant explanation for the global distribution of *Homo sapiens*: a single African origin around 200,000 years ago, followed by migrations starting around 100,000 years ago.
- **Paleolithic Age.** The "Old Stone Age," from the earliest stone tools (perhaps 3.3 million years ago) to the beginning of the Neolithic (c. 12,000 years ago).
- **Venus figurines.** Small carved female figures from sites across Europe and central Asia, c. 40,000 to 25,000 years ago. Interpretation contested.

## Further reading

- **Johanson, Donald, and Maitland Edey.** *Lucy: The Beginnings of Humankind.* 1981. Johanson's account of the 1974 discovery and what it changed.
- **Tattersall, Ian.** *Masters of the Planet: The Search for Our Human Origins.* 2012. A clear modern synthesis of human-evolution research, including the most recent genetic evidence on Neanderthals and Denisovans.
- **Diamond, Jared.** *Guns, Germs, and Steel.* 1997. The most-cited single book on why agriculture emerged where and when it did, with his characteristic geographic-determinist argument that other readers continue to dispute.
- **Mithen, Steven.** *After the Ice: A Global Human History 20,000–5,000 BC.* 2003. A narrative history of the late Paleolithic and early Neolithic written as a globe-spanning travelogue.
- **Scott, James C.** *Against the Grain: A Deep History of the Earliest States.* 2017. A political scientist's argument that the Neolithic Revolution is best read as a story of state formation and coercion, not of progress.
- **Schmidt, Klaus.** *Göbekli Tepe: A Stone Age Sanctuary in South-Eastern Anatolia.* 2012 (English ed.). The principal excavator's account of the site that reshaped early Neolithic chronology.
- **Lee, Richard B.** *The !Kung San: Men, Women, and Work in a Foraging Society.* 1979. The classic ethnography of a hunter-gatherer society as observed in the twentieth century. Note carefully both what it reveals and what (per Exercise 3.6) it doesn't.

## LLM Exercise — Chapter 3: The Commodity Before Agriculture

**Project:** *A Commodity's Journey*.

**What you're building this chapter:** Section 2 — where your commodity sits in the Paleolithic and Neolithic worlds. For some commodities (salt, ochre, stone, fire) this is the first chapter that has substance to add. For others (gunpowder, paper, coffee) this is the chapter that asks *what came before*.

**Tool:** Claude Project (your *Commodity Biography* project).

**The prompt:**

```
Section 2 — The Commodity Before Agriculture.

Chapter 3 covered the Paleolithic and Neolithic eras — the long
hunter-gatherer period and the bargain of the Neolithic Revolution.
Key concepts: the chain Australopithecus → Homo habilis → Homo erectus
→ Homo sapiens; the Mousterian, Acheulean, and Oldowan tool
progressions; small egalitarian bands; the trade-offs of agriculture
(more calories, larger populations, but less leisure, more disease,
less varied diet); Çatalhöyük, Jericho, Mehrgarh, Yangtze valley.

Write Section 2 of the commodity biography (~600–1,000 words):

1. **Did my commodity exist in the Paleolithic?** Be honest. For
   some commodities (salt, fire, ochre, stone, bone, fur, hides,
   shells) the answer is yes — find the earliest documented
   archaeological evidence and describe it. For others (gunpowder,
   sugar, coffee, paper, gold-as-currency) the answer is no — name
   the *precursor* the Paleolithic had instead, and why.

3. **Did Neolithic life change what my commodity meant?** The
   transition from hunter-gatherer to farming changed everything
   about how humans related to materials. Salt became a
   preservation necessity rather than a casual licking-mineral.
   Stone became refined into specialized blades and grinding
   surfaces. Plants became seeds to save and trade. Find the
   Neolithic shift for my commodity.

4. **Identify a specific archaeological site.** Çatalhöyük, Jericho,
   Mehrgarh, the Yangtze valley, or another. Did my commodity show
   up there? In what form? What does its presence (or absence) tell
   us about that society?

5. **Confront the great-man problem.** Most of human history is
   pre-literate. We have no named individuals. The historiography
   schools that depend on named actors (great man, intellectual
   history) simply do not apply yet. What lens *does* work for
   pre-literate history of my commodity? Material culture
   analysis? Ecological reasoning? Comparative ethnography of
   recent hunter-gatherer societies?

6. **End with the threshold.** What changes about my commodity
   when humans start writing things down? When does that happen
   for my commodity specifically?

Save to running draft under "Before the Word: The Commodity in
Pre-History." Cite at least one named archaeological site with
date and a specific published archaeologist's interpretation.
Flag verification needs.
```

**What this produces:** A section that grounds your commodity (or its precursor) in concrete archaeological evidence — or honestly names the absence — and identifies the Neolithic threshold change.

**How to adapt this prompt:**

- *For your own project:* If your commodity is purely post-Neolithic (printing type, sugar refining, gunpowder), the section becomes about the *pre-conditions* — the tools, knowledge, and social structures the Neolithic established that later made the commodity possible.
- *For ChatGPT / Gemini:* Both work. Cross-check specific dates and site interpretations against current published archaeology — archaeological consensus shifts frequently.
- *For Claude Code:* Not the fit.
- *For a Claude Project:* Native fit. The pre-literate framing established here is the methodological constraint many later sections will reference.

**Connection to previous chapters:** Builds on Chapter 2's opening section by applying its interpretive framework to the pre-literate evidence base.

**Preview of next chapter:** Chapter 4 (Early Civilizations) is the urban-revolution chapter — Uruk, Mohenjo-Daro, the first cities, the first writing, the first kings. Section 3 will ask how your commodity fared in the first cities.
