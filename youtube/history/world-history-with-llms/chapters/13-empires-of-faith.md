# Chapter 13 — Empires of Faith
*Late Antiquity, the Religious Re-Sorting of the World, and the End of the Classical Age.*

## Three suggested titles

- Empires of Faith: Religion and Statecraft in Late Antiquity
- From Constantine to the Eve of Islam: 300 Years of Religious Empires
- The Religious Horseshoe: Christian Rome, Sasanian Persia, Aksum, Himyar

---

## Chapter overview

By the end of this chapter you will know how the Mediterranean and Near Eastern world transformed itself between roughly 250 CE and 650 CE — the period historians call **Late Antiquity**. You will know the **Crisis of the Third Century**, the reforms of **Diocletian** and **Constantine**, and the political-religious transformation that turned the Roman Empire into a Christian state. You will know the **fall of the Western Roman Empire** in 476 CE — what fell, what didn't, and how the **Germanic successor kingdoms** (Visigoths, Vandals, Ostrogoths, Franks) absorbed Roman institutions while transforming them. You will know the **Eastern Roman / Byzantine Empire** that survived the Western collapse and continued for another thousand years. You will know the **Sasanian Persian Empire** (224–651 CE), the principal eastern rival to Rome and Byzantium, and the Zoroastrian state religion that gave it its identity. You will know the **Kingdom of Aksum** in Ethiopia, the **Kingdom of Himyar** in Yemen, and the religious transformation of the Red Sea region into a Christian-Jewish-pagan corridor on the eve of Islam. You will know the **Kushan Empire** in northwest India, where Greek, Indian, and Iranian religious traditions met and produced the Buddhist art and theology that would shape the religion's spread to East Asia. By the end, you will see how the world that produced Islam in the seventh century was a world already organized around imperial-religious pairings — and you will be ready for the next chapter, which tells what happened when a new universalist religion arose into that world.

## Opening case — Milvian Bridge, October 28, 312 CE

On the afternoon of October 28, 312 CE, two Roman armies met at the Milvian Bridge over the Tiber River, just north of Rome. On one side stood the army of **Constantine**, the son of a tetrarch and the claimant to the western half of the Roman Empire. On the other side stood the army of **Maxentius**, the rival claimant who had held Rome for six years. The two armies were both Roman. Their soldiers spoke Latin. Their officers had probably trained together. The fight was a civil war within the multi-emperor system that Diocletian had set up a generation earlier and that had immediately collapsed into competition.

Constantine won. Maxentius drowned in the Tiber as his army retreated across a temporary pontoon bridge that collapsed under the weight of fleeing soldiers. Constantine entered Rome the next day as sole emperor of the West.

His subsequent biographer **Eusebius of Caesarea** would write, decades later, that on the night before the battle Constantine had seen a vision in the sky — a Christian symbol, the *chi-rho* monogram of Christ's name, with the words *In hoc signo vinces* — "in this sign you will conquer." Whether the vision was real, embellished, or invented for political effect is debated. What is not debated is what Constantine did next. He had his soldiers paint Christian symbols on their shields. He attributed the victory to the Christian God. The following spring, in 313 CE, he and his eastern colleague **Licinius** issued the **Edict of Milan**, ending Christian persecution and restoring confiscated church property. Across the next twenty-five years of his reign, Constantine progressively integrated Christianity into Roman state institutions. He built churches. He convened church councils. He made his sons Christian. He was baptized on his deathbed in 337 CE.

The Roman Empire had crucified Jesus three centuries earlier. By 380 CE, under the emperor **Theodosius I**, Christianity was the empire's official religion and other cults were suppressed. The transformation from persecuted minority to dominant faith took less than seventy years.

This chapter is about that transformation and its consequences — for the Roman world, for the post-Roman world, and for the other empires of Late Antiquity that watched the Roman shift and made their own religious-political arrangements in response. Persia's Sasanian dynasty bound itself to Zoroastrianism. Ethiopia's Kingdom of Aksum bound itself to Christianity. Yemen's Kingdom of Himyar bound itself, briefly, to Judaism. India's Kushan Empire produced a fusion Buddhism that carried the religion to China. By 600 CE, every major state from the Atlantic to the Indus had a state religion. Into that world, in the next century, came Islam.

We start with the Roman transformation.

## Concept 1 — Crisis and reform, c. 235–337 CE

The Roman Empire of Augustus and Trajan had been based on the assumption that the empire's expansion paid for the empire's costs. By the late second century CE, that assumption had failed: the empire had reached the limits of profitable expansion, and the army was now a permanent fiscal burden. The third century broke the system that had held the empire together.

The **Crisis of the Third Century** (235–284 CE) was the result. Within fifty years, perhaps fifty different men were declared emperor — most by their armies, most for short reigns, most ending in violence. The empire fragmented temporarily into three competing pieces (the Gallic Empire in the west, Palmyra in the east, and a rump central empire). A wave of plague (the **Plague of Cyprian**, beginning around 249 CE, possibly an early viral hemorrhagic fever) killed huge fractions of the urban population. Currency was repeatedly debased — the silver content of the *denarius* fell from about 50% to under 5% across the century — and runaway inflation followed. Cities contracted. Trade slowed. Provincial economies de-monetized.

Then came two reformers who saved the empire by remaking it.

**Diocletian** (reigned 284–305 CE) was an Illyrian soldier who came to power through the same kind of military coup that had produced his predecessors. Unlike them, he survived and instituted structural reforms that lasted. He divided the empire into a four-emperor **Tetrarchy** — two senior emperors (*Augusti*) and two junior emperors (*Caesars*) — to manage the empire's competing fronts simultaneously. He reorganized the provincial system, doubling the number of provinces while grouping them into larger **dioceses**. He instituted price controls (the **Edict on Maximum Prices** of 301 CE — economically counterproductive, but indicative of imperial concern with inflation). He stabilized the currency. He abdicated in 305 CE — the only Roman emperor before Constantine to do so voluntarily. His abdication produced another civil war.

The civil war's winner was **Constantine** (reigned 306–337 CE). Constantine kept Diocletian's administrative reforms and added his own. He moved the imperial capital from Rome to a new city, **Constantinople** (built on the site of the old Greek city of Byzantium on the Bosporus straits, dedicated 330 CE), at the strategic crossroads of trade and military communication between Europe and Asia. He reformed the military. He stabilized the gold currency (the *solidus*, which would remain the gold standard of the eastern Mediterranean for the next seven centuries). And he aligned the empire with Christianity.

## Concept 2 — Roman Christianity, 313–600 CE

Christianity in 313 CE had been growing for nearly three centuries but was still a minority religion — perhaps 5–10% of the empire's population, concentrated in eastern cities, particularly visible among urban poor, women, and slaves, but increasingly attracting elite converts. Constantine's patronage transformed the religion's social position rapidly.

### The institutional changes

The Edict of Milan (313 CE) ended persecution. Constantine returned confiscated church property. He exempted Christian clergy from taxation. He funded the construction of major churches — the original Basilica of St. Peter in Rome, the Hagia Sophia in Constantinople (the first version, replaced under Justinian), the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem. He gave bishops authority in secular legal disputes. He used church councils — bishops convened from across the empire to settle theological disputes — as instruments of imperial policy.

The most important council of the fourth century was the **Council of Nicaea** (325 CE), which Constantine personally convened to settle the **Arian controversy** — a dispute over whether Christ was fully co-eternal with God the Father (the position of bishop Athanasius of Alexandria) or a created being subordinate to God (the position of the priest Arius). Nicaea endorsed Athanasius's position. The **Nicene Creed** that emerged from the council remains the foundational statement of Trinitarian Christianity, recited in church liturgies across the world today.

Theological disputes did not stop with Nicaea. The fourth and fifth centuries produced a series of **Christological controversies** — disagreements about the relationship between Christ's divine and human natures. Subsequent councils — Constantinople (381), Ephesus (431), Chalcedon (451) — tried to settle them. Each council produced both winners and losers; the losers often broke off to form independent church traditions that have survived to today. The **Coptic** (Egyptian) and **Ethiopian** Orthodox churches reject the Council of Chalcedon's formulation. The **Assyrian Church of the East** rejected the Council of Ephesus's formulation. The Christian world was already, by 500 CE, a network of competing church traditions, most of which would survive the fall of the Western Empire and persist in some form into the modern era.

### The cultural transformation

By the late fourth century, the empire that had once worshipped Jupiter and persecuted Christians was actively suppressing the old religions. **Theodosius I** (reigned 379–395 CE) issued edicts closing pagan temples, ending the Olympic Games (last held in 393 CE), and removing the **Altar of Victory** — the symbolic centerpiece of pagan Roman state religion — from the Senate house in Rome. Old senatorial families converted, many reluctantly. The lights of public sacrifice that had marked Roman cities went out across the fourth century.

The cultural production of the Christianized empire was substantial. **Augustine of Hippo** (354–430 CE), the bishop of a small North African coastal city, produced *Confessions* (an autobiographical theological work that essentially invents the genre of spiritual autobiography in Western literature) and *The City of God* (a defense of Christianity written in the aftermath of Alaric's sack of Rome in 410 CE). His theology of original sin, divine grace, and the relationship between earthly and heavenly cities would shape Western Christianity for the next millennium. **Jerome** (c. 347–420 CE) translated the Bible from Greek and Hebrew into Latin, producing the **Vulgate** that would be the standard Western Bible until the Reformation. The desert fathers and mothers of Egypt and Syria — **Anthony the Great**, **Pachomius**, **Mary of Egypt** — invented Christian monasticism, retreating to the wilderness to seek God through asceticism. Their practices would shape Christian institutions across the next thousand years.

## Concept 3 — The Western Roman collapse, 376–476 CE

The political collapse of the Western Roman Empire across the fifth century CE has been studied for fifteen hundred years and remains contested. What happened was a long migration crisis from beyond the empire's frontiers. What it produced was the political end of the unified Western Empire by 476 CE.

### The migrations

In 376 CE, large groups of **Goths** appeared on the northern bank of the Danube, fleeing the **Huns** — a nomadic confederation from the Eurasian steppe whose westward expansion was pushing the Germanic peoples of central and eastern Europe in front of them. The eastern emperor Valens admitted the Goths into the empire. Mismanagement of their resettlement led to revolt. In 378 CE, Gothic forces destroyed a Roman army at **Adrianople** and killed the emperor — the worst Roman defeat in centuries.

Through the fifth century, Germanic peoples crossed the Roman frontiers in a series of waves: **Visigoths**, **Vandals**, **Suevi**, **Burgundians**, **Alans**, **Franks**, **Saxons**, **Lombards**. Some came as invaders. Many came as *foederati* (allied peoples settled within the empire in exchange for military service). They were not a tidal wave of barbarian destruction — most were small populations seeking land and security — but their numbers and the empire's weakening capacity to absorb them strained the system past its breaking point.

The Visigoth leader **Alaric** sacked Rome in **410 CE** — a symbolic shock that prompted Augustine's *City of God*. The Vandals crossed Spain into North Africa, took Carthage, built a kingdom, and sacked Rome themselves in **455 CE**. The Visigoths settled in Gaul, then Spain, founding the kingdom that would last until the Islamic conquest of 711 CE. The Franks expanded across northern Gaul under **Clovis** (reigned 481–511 CE), who converted to Catholic Christianity and founded the dynasty that would become Charlemagne's. The Ostrogoths, under **Theodoric the Great** (reigned 493–526 CE), built a substantial kingdom in Italy that maintained much of the Roman administrative system.

In **476 CE**, the Germanic general **Odoacer** deposed the last Western emperor, the boy-puppet **Romulus Augustulus**. Odoacer did not declare himself emperor. He sent the imperial regalia to Constantinople and ruled Italy as a king under nominal eastern allegiance. The conventional date of the "fall of the Western Roman Empire" is 476 CE, but no contemporary observer thought of it as a single dramatic event. The Western Empire had been hollowing out for a century. What happened in 476 was the formal acknowledgment that an emperor was no longer needed in the West.

### What fell, what didn't

The Roman political institution of the Western emperor fell. The **Roman provincial cities** mostly survived in reduced form. **Roman law** survived: the Germanic kingdoms that took over the Western provinces continued to use Roman legal forms for their Roman subjects, and various law codes (the **Code of Theodosius II**, 438 CE; the **Justinianic Code**, 529 CE) preserved Roman jurisprudence into the medieval period. **Latin** survived: it remained the language of the Western Catholic church for the next thousand years and gradually became the ancestor of French, Italian, Spanish, Portuguese, and Romanian. **Roman Christianity** survived: bishops in the Western provinces filled the political vacuum left by the disappearing Roman administration; the **Bishop of Rome** (the **Pope**) emerged as the most important religious authority in Western Christianity. **Roman roads, aqueducts, and architectural conventions** persisted.

What fell was the unified imperial state. What persisted was much of what that state had created.

## Concept 4 — The Eastern Empire and Justinian

The Eastern Roman Empire — what historians often call the **Byzantine Empire** — survived. Constantinople was a magnificent city, well-defended by sea walls and the famous **Theodosian Walls** (built in the early fifth century, never breached by force until 1453). The eastern provinces were wealthier, more urbanized, and more easily defended than the western ones. The eastern emperors continued to call themselves Roman emperors; they continued to speak Latin in their court (later Greek); they continued to administer their empire on Roman institutional lines.

The most consequential eastern emperor of Late Antiquity was **Justinian I** (reigned 527–565 CE). Born to a peasant family in the Balkans, Justinian rose through the imperial bureaucracy and married **Theodora**, a former actress whose intelligence and political instincts shaped his reign. (The ancient historian Procopius, who served at Justinian's court, wrote both an admiring official history and a viciously hostile *Secret History* in which Theodora is portrayed as the most morally compromised woman in the empire — a useful illustration of how source criticism works.)

Justinian's accomplishments were considerable. He commissioned the **Justinianic Code** — the most comprehensive codification of Roman law ever produced, compiled by the jurist **Tribonian** between 529 and 534 CE. The Code preserved Roman legal thinking and would become the foundation of the **civil law** tradition that still governs most of continental Europe and Latin America. He rebuilt the **Hagia Sophia** in Constantinople after fire destroyed the previous building during the **Nika revolt** of 532 CE; the new church, completed in 537 CE, was the largest church in the world for the next thousand years and remains one of the great architectural achievements of human history. He launched the *renovatio imperii* — the **reconquest** — sending his general **Belisarius** to retake the lost western provinces. Belisarius's armies recovered North Africa from the Vandals (533–534 CE) and Italy from the Ostrogoths (535–554 CE), and parts of southern Spain from the Visigoths.

The reconquest exhausted the empire. The wars of reconquest devastated Italy more than the original Germanic kingdoms had. The **Plague of Justinian** (541–549 CE, with periodic recurrences for two centuries) — the first historically attested outbreak of bubonic plague — killed perhaps a quarter of the eastern Mediterranean's population, wrecking imperial finances. The recovered western territories began slipping away within a generation: the Lombards invaded Italy in 568 CE and reduced Byzantine Italy to a few coastal strips. The empire that emerged from Justinian's reign was rich in legal-religious-architectural inheritance and overstretched in every other dimension.

The seventh century would test it.

## Concept 5 — Sasanian Persia

The Roman/Byzantine Empire's principal eastern rival across all of Late Antiquity was the **Sasanian Persian Empire** (224–651 CE). The Sasanians had replaced the Parthians (whom we encountered briefly in Chapter 5) when **Ardashir I** overthrew the last Parthian king around 224 CE and founded a centralized Iranian empire that would last more than four hundred years.

### Structure and religion

The Sasanian state was much more centralized than the Parthian one had been. The Sasanian king (the **Shahanshah**, "King of Kings") ruled through a hierarchical bureaucracy, with provincial governors reporting directly to the court at **Ctesiphon** in central Mesopotamia. The aristocracy was organized into ranked classes; the priesthood, the warriors, the bureaucracy, the agriculturalists. Roman observers (Ammianus Marcellinus, Procopius) described Sasanian government as more orderly and structurally similar to Rome's than they had expected.

The state religion was **Zoroastrianism**, the ancient Iranian religion of the prophet Zarathustra (Chapter 5). The Sasanians elevated Zoroastrianism into a more developed institutional church — a **clerical class** of *mobeds*, fire-temples (the **Atash Behrams** at the major sites), a codified scripture (the **Avesta**), and an inquisitional apparatus that suppressed religious dissent. Sasanian Persia was more religiously homogeneous than the Roman/Byzantine East. Christians, Jews, and Manichaeans existed in the empire but were treated as second-class subjects or, in periods of religious crisis, persecuted.

### Conflict with Rome

The Sasanians fought Rome (and later Byzantium) for four centuries. The wars produced famous victories on both sides. **Shapur I** captured the Roman emperor **Valerian** at the Battle of Edessa in 260 CE — an event commemorated in the great Sasanian rock relief at Naqsh-e Rostam, where Valerian is depicted kneeling before the mounted Shapur. Roman emperors invaded Mesopotamia repeatedly; **Trajan** had occupied it briefly in 115 CE, **Julian** died fighting the Sasanians in 363 CE.

The conflict reached its climax in the early seventh century CE. The Sasanian king **Khusrau II** (reigned 590–628 CE) invaded the Byzantine empire, captured Antioch, took Jerusalem in 614 CE (carrying off the True Cross as a trophy), occupied Egypt, and besieged Constantinople in 626 CE. The Byzantine emperor **Heraclius** (reigned 610–641 CE) responded with a counter-invasion that defeated Khusrau II at Nineveh in 627 CE and forced a peace treaty in 628 CE. Both empires emerged from the war exhausted, depopulated by plague, and with their treasuries empty. They were in this condition when the Arab armies of Muhammad's first successor swept out of the Arabian Peninsula in the 630s CE. The Sasanians fell to the Arabs in 651 CE; Byzantium lost Syria, Egypt, and North Africa within a generation. The Roman-Persian war of 602–628 CE is often called the war that no one won, because the real victor turned out to be Islam.

## Concept 6 — The Red Sea horseshoe: Aksum and Himyar

The Red Sea and southern Arabia became, in the fourth through sixth centuries CE, a religious-political contest zone connecting Africa, Arabia, and the eastern Mediterranean. Two kingdoms anchored the system.

### Aksum (c. 100–700 CE)

**Aksum** was an Ethiopian highland kingdom whose capital lay in modern Tigray, with access to the Red Sea through the port of **Adulis**. Aksum traded across the Red Sea and Indian Ocean — sending ivory, gold, ostrich feathers, and incense to the Roman world; receiving textiles, glass, wine, and metalwork. It controlled significant territory in modern Ethiopia, Eritrea, and (at times) southern Arabia. At its third-century peak it was, alongside Rome, Persia, and China, one of the four great trading powers identified by the Persian prophet Mani.

Aksum's most consequential ruler was **King Ezana** (reigned mid-fourth century CE), who converted to **Christianity** around 330 CE — a few decades after Constantine. The Ethiopian Orthodox Church traces its founding to Ezana and his contemporary, the Syrian Christian Frumentius who became the first bishop of Aksum. Ethiopian Christianity has survived continuously from the fourth century to today, making it one of the world's oldest continuous Christian traditions, with its own liturgical language (Ge'ez), its own scriptural canon (which includes texts not in the Western or Eastern Orthodox canons, including the books of Enoch and Jubilees), and its own monastic and architectural traditions.

The Aksumite kingdom built monumental stelae — granite obelisks up to a hundred feet tall, carved to resemble multistoried buildings — that mark the royal cemeteries at Aksum. Some still stand. Aksum minted gold coins (one of the few African states to do so before the modern period), inscribed with both Greek and Ge'ez. The kingdom briefly invaded and conquered Himyar in southern Arabia in the early sixth century CE, then withdrew. By the seventh century, Aksum was in decline; the Islamic expansion across the Red Sea cut its trade routes, and the kingdom retreated to the highland interior. Its successor states would persist as Christian kingdoms in the Ethiopian highlands through the medieval and modern periods.

### Himyar (c. 100 BCE–525 CE)

**Himyar** was a kingdom in southern Arabia, in modern Yemen. Like Aksum, it sat astride a major trading route — the southern entrance to the Red Sea, the route between the Indian Ocean and the Mediterranean. It controlled the Yemeni highlands and lowlands and, at its peak, much of Arabia.

Himyar's religious history is unusual. In the late fourth century CE, the Himyarite king **Abu Karib As'ad** converted to **Judaism** — possibly under the influence of Jewish merchant communities that had settled in Arabia after the Roman destruction of the Jewish temple in Jerusalem (70 CE) and the failed Jewish revolts of the second century. The conversion became royal policy: subsequent Himyarite kings continued the Jewish allegiance, built synagogues, and ruled a partially Judaized population.

Himyar's last Jewish king, **Yusuf As'ar Yathar** (reigned c. 522–525 CE), launched a persecution of the Christian population of his kingdom — the **Najran massacre** of 523 CE, in which several thousand Christians were killed at the city of Najran. The persecution drew an Aksumite invasion (the Aksumite king **Kaleb** invaded across the Red Sea in 525 CE), which conquered Himyar and ended the Jewish kingdom. Aksumite rule in Himyar lasted about half a century before a Sasanian-backed Arab dynasty took power. By the early seventh century, southern Arabia was fragmented and impoverished, with religious traditions including Judaism, Christianity, and various forms of polytheism contesting the cultural ground.

The collapse of the Red Sea trading system, the Sasanian-Byzantine wars, and the religious volatility of southern Arabia all contributed to the conditions in which Islam would emerge in the early seventh century. **Muhammad** (c. 570–632 CE), born in **Mecca** in the Hijaz region of western Arabia, lived in a world that knew Christianity (in Aksum, Ethiopia, Egypt, Syria), Judaism (in Himyar's recent past, in Medina's Jewish tribes), Zoroastrianism (across the eastern frontier), and various Arabian polytheisms. ## Concept 7 — The Kushan Empire and the Buddhist transmission

Far east of the Roman-Sasanian conflict zone, in what is now northern Pakistan, eastern Afghanistan, and northwestern India, another religious-imperial fusion was producing consequential cultural innovations.

The **Kushan Empire** (c. first century BCE–third century CE) was founded by the **Yuezhi** people, originally a steppe confederation that had been pushed south by the Xiongnu (Chapter 6) into Bactria in the second century BCE. Under their leader Kujula Kadphises and his successors, they expanded south across the Hindu Kush into northern India.

The Kushan capital region — **Gandhara**, around modern Peshawar and Taxila — sat at the meeting point of three civilizational traditions. **Greek** culture had been present since Alexander's conquest in the fourth century BCE and the subsequent Greco-Bactrian and Indo-Greek kingdoms (Chapter 8). **Iranian** culture was present through the Persian and post-Persian populations of the region. **Indian** culture was present through the Buddhist and Hindu traditions of the subcontinent.

The cultural synthesis produced by Gandhara was extraordinary. The most famous expression is **Greco-Buddhist art**: depictions of the Buddha that combined Greek sculptural conventions (idealized human bodies, naturalistic drapery, classical contrapposto poses) with Indian Buddhist iconography (mudras, *ushnishas*, bodhisattva attendants). The image of the Buddha as a serene, draped, idealized human figure — which became the canonical form of Buddhist religious art across all of East and Southeast Asia — was largely a Gandharan invention.

The Kushan emperor **Kanishka I** (reigned c. 127–150 CE) was a major patron of Buddhism. He convened the **Fourth Buddhist Council** in Kashmir, which compiled an authoritative Sanskrit version of Buddhist scriptures. He sponsored Buddhist missionary expeditions north along the Silk Roads, which carried the religion into the oases of the Tarim Basin and from there into China. Buddhism in China — which would become one of the dominant religious traditions of East Asia for the next two thousand years — entered through the routes the Kushan Empire opened.

The Kushan Empire fragmented in the third century CE under pressure from the rising Sasanians and from internal succession crises. Its successor states in northern India persisted into the fourth century, when the **Gupta Dynasty** (Chapter 6) would absorb much of the territory. The Kushan-period Buddhist transmission, however, had already done its decisive cultural work: the religion was now established in Central Asia and in China, and would not retreat from either region until much later.

You can describe the Crisis of the Third Century, the Diocletianic Tetrarchy, and Constantine's reforms — and explain how the third- and fourth-century reorganization of the Roman Empire saved it for two more centuries while changing it fundamentally.

You can trace the **Christianization of the Roman Empire** from Constantine's vision at the Milvian Bridge in 312 CE through Theodosius's establishment of Christianity as the official religion in the late fourth century — and identify the major theological controversies (Arianism, the Christological disputes) that produced the early ecumenical councils and the divisions of the early Christian world.

You can describe the **Western Roman collapse** as a long migration crisis culminating in the deposition of the last emperor in 476 CE — and explain what fell (the Western imperial state) and what survived (Roman law, Latin language, Christianity, urban institutions).

You can describe the **Eastern Roman / Byzantine Empire** under Justinian — the Justinianic Code, the rebuilt Hagia Sophia, the brief reconquest of the West, the Plague of Justinian — and place it as the surviving half of the Roman world.

You can describe the **Sasanian Persian Empire** as Rome/Byzantium's principal eastern rival, with its own state religion (Zoroastrianism), its own institutional church, and a four-century rivalry that exhausted both empires by the early seventh century.

You can describe the religious diversity of Late Antiquity beyond the two great empires: **Aksumite Christianity** in Ethiopia (continuous from the fourth century to today), **Himyarite Judaism** briefly in Yemen, **Kushan Buddhism** in northwest India and the Buddhist transmission to China.

The thing to watch for, going forward, is the temptation to read Late Antiquity as decline and fall. The classical Mediterranean unity did break down. What replaced it was a series of confessional states whose religious identities would shape the next millennium. The world that produced Islam in the seventh century was a world of empires of faith.

## Key terms

- **Aksum.** Ethiopian highland kingdom (c. 100–700 CE) that converted to Christianity under King Ezana.
- **Arian controversy.** The fourth-century theological dispute over Christ's relationship to God the Father, addressed at the Council of Nicaea (325 CE).
- **Augustine of Hippo.** North African bishop (354–430 CE) and author of *Confessions* and *The City of God*.
- **Byzantine Empire.** The Eastern Roman Empire that survived the Western collapse and continued from Constantinople until 1453.
- **Christological controversies.** Fourth- and fifth-century disputes over the relationship between Christ's divine and human natures.
- **Constantinople.** The new eastern capital founded by Constantine in 330 CE on the site of Byzantium.
- **Council of Nicaea.** The 325 CE church council that produced the Nicene Creed.
- **Diocletian.** Roman emperor (reigned 284–305 CE) who ended the Crisis of the Third Century with structural reforms including the Tetrarchy.
- **Edict of Milan.** The 313 CE proclamation by Constantine and Licinius that ended Christian persecution.
- **Foederati.** Germanic peoples settled within Roman territory under treaty obligations.
- **Hagia Sophia.** Justinian's church in Constantinople, completed 537 CE.
- **Himyar.** Kingdom in southern Arabia (c. 100 BCE–525 CE) that briefly adopted Judaism.
- **Justinian I.** Byzantine emperor (reigned 527–565 CE) who codified Roman law and rebuilt the Hagia Sophia.
- **Justinianic Code.** Justinian's compilation of Roman law (529–534 CE), the foundation of later civil law.
- **Khusrau II.** Sasanian king (reigned 590–628 CE) whose invasion of Byzantium was reversed by Heraclius.
- **Kushan Empire.** Northwest Indian state (c. first century BCE–third century CE) where Greco-Buddhist art emerged.
- **Late Antiquity.** The period roughly 200–650 CE, between classical antiquity and the medieval period.
- **Plague of Justinian.** The first historical bubonic plague pandemic, 541–549 CE with recurrences.
- **Sasanian Empire.** The Iranian empire (224–651 CE), Rome/Byzantium's principal eastern rival.
- **Tetrarchy.** Diocletian's four-emperor system for governing the Roman Empire.
- **Theodosius I.** Roman emperor (reigned 379–395 CE) who established Christianity as the official religion.
- **Vandals.** Germanic people who built a kingdom in North Africa from 429 CE; sacked Rome in 455 CE.
- **Visigoths.** Germanic people who sacked Rome under Alaric in 410 CE and founded a long-lasting kingdom in Iberia.
- **Zoroastrianism.** The ancient Iranian religion that became the state religion of Sasanian Persia.

## Further reading

- **Brown, Peter.** *The World of Late Antiquity, AD 150–750.* 1971. The classic short synthesis that essentially defined the field of Late Antiquity.
- **Brown, Peter.** *The Rise of Western Christendom: Triumph and Diversity, A.D. 200–1000.* 3rd ed. 2013. A magisterial follow-up volume on the Christian half of the period.
- **Heather, Peter.** *The Fall of the Roman Empire: A New History of Rome and the Barbarians.* 2006. A modern restatement of the classic "fall" narrative; argues for the Germanic invasions as the decisive cause.
- **Goldsworthy, Adrian.** *How Rome Fell: Death of a Superpower.* 2009. A military historian's account, emphasizing internal political failures.
- **Daryaee, Touraj.** *Sasanian Persia: The Rise and Fall of an Empire.* 2009. The standard one-volume history of Sasanian Persia in English.
- **Bowersock, G. W.** *The Throne of Adulis: Red Sea Wars on the Eve of Islam.* 2013. A close study of the Aksum-Himyar conflict and its religious geography.
- **Foltz, Richard.** *Religions of the Silk Road: Premodern Patterns of Globalization.* 2010. A useful synthesis of the religious traffic across the Kushan and post-Kushan zones.
