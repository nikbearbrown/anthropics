# Chapter 16 — The Post-Roman West and the Crusading Movement
*From Charlemagne's Coronation to the First Crusade.*

## Three suggested titles

- The Post-Roman West and the Crusading Movement: Latin Europe Looks Outward
- Charlemagne, the Schism, and Clermont: Medieval Europe to 1099
- How a Confederation of Knightly Monasticism Decided to Invade Jerusalem

## Chapter overview

By the end of this chapter you will know how the western half of the former Roman world reorganized itself into Latin Christian Europe between roughly 500 and 1100 CE. You will know the **Germanic successor kingdoms** that filled the vacuum left by the Western Empire's fall — Visigoths in Iberia, Franks in Gaul, Anglo-Saxons in Britain, Lombards in Italy. You will know the **Carolingian Empire** of **Charlemagne** (reigned 768–814 CE) and his successors, the closest Latin Europe would come to political unification before modern times. You will know the **feudal-manorial system** that organized economic and political life in the absence of a strong central state. You will know the **Catholic Church** as the institution that gave Latin Europe its cultural, legal, and intellectual coherence. You will know **Al-Andalus** (Islamic Iberia) — Cordoba's caliphate, the cultural meeting point between the Latin and Arabic worlds. You will know the **East-West Schism** of 1054 CE that split Latin (Catholic) Christianity from Greek (Eastern Orthodox) Christianity. You will know the **Seljuk Turks** who, by the late eleventh century, had taken over much of the Abbasid territory and had begun cutting off Christian pilgrim access to Jerusalem. And you will know the **First Crusade** (1095–1099 CE), launched by Pope Urban II at Clermont in 1095 CE — the moment Latin Europe began projecting military power outside its own borders, with consequences that would shape Mediterranean politics for the next four centuries.

## Opening case — Clermont, France, November 27, 1095 CE

In late November 1095 CE, a sixty-year-old French pope named **Urban II** stood on a wooden platform in a field outside the city of Clermont in central France and gave the most consequential sermon of the eleventh century. He was speaking to perhaps several hundred bishops and laypeople who had gathered for what had been billed as a routine church council. The actual sermon does not survive in its original form. Five chroniclers, all writing later, gave us their reconstructions, each different in detail, but agreeing on the central points.

Urban told the audience that the Christian East was suffering. The **Seljuk Turks**, a recently converted Muslim military aristocracy who had conquered most of the eastern Islamic world over the previous fifty years, had invaded Byzantine territory and were, in his telling, mistreating Christians, restricting pilgrim access to the holy sites in Jerusalem, and threatening Constantinople itself. The Byzantine emperor **Alexios I Komnenos** had asked Western Christians for military aid the previous year. Urban's response: an armed pilgrimage to recover Jerusalem from Muslim rule.

The terms he offered were striking. Anyone who took up the cross — *signum crucis* — would have all their sins forgiven (a **plenary indulgence**, the most generous spiritual reward the Church could offer). Anyone who died on the expedition would go directly to heaven. Anyone who took up the cross but failed to follow through would be excommunicated. Christian property would be protected by the Church while its owners were on campaign.

The response exceeded any reasonable expectation. By the spring of 1096, perhaps a hundred thousand Western European Christians — a mixture of mounted knights, foot soldiers, women, children, peasants, clerics, and the occasional opportunist — were on the road east, organized into several armies, with no real central command. The first wave (the so-called **People's Crusade**) reached Constantinople in mid-1096, was rushed across into Anatolia by the alarmed Byzantine emperor, and was almost immediately wiped out by Turkish forces. The second wave (the **Princes' Crusade**, the proper First Crusade in conventional terms) did better. Through 1097 and 1098 it captured the cities of **Nicaea** and **Antioch** in northern Syria. On July 15, 1099, after a five-week siege, it captured **Jerusalem**. The Crusaders massacred the Muslim and Jewish inhabitants of the city, founded a Christian "**Latin Kingdom of Jerusalem**" that would last (in declining form) until 1291 CE, and sent the news back to Europe that something extraordinary had happened: an army of Western Christians had walked two thousand miles, conquered the holy city, and held it.

How a fragmented post-Roman Latin Europe had arrived at the institutional capacity to project this kind of power is the chapter's subject. The Crusaders themselves would have said God had ordained it. The historian's question is what structures and what events had to fall into place for Urban's sermon to produce a hundred thousand mobilized Westerners.

We start with the post-Roman world.

## Concept 1 — Latin Europe after Rome

The Western Roman Empire's political collapse in the fifth century (Chapter 13) left Western Europe in the hands of the Germanic kingdoms that had taken over its provinces. By 600 CE, the political map looked like this:

**Frankish kingdom**: The Franks had crossed the Rhine into Gaul during the fifth century. Under **Clovis I** (reigned 481–511 CE), they unified, accepted Catholic Christianity (a politically consequential choice — the Franks chose Catholic over the Arian Christianity that most other Germanic kingdoms had adopted), and built a kingdom that would, with various reorganizations, become the dominant Latin Christian state of the early medieval period.

**Visigothic kingdom of Iberia**: The Visigoths had settled in Iberia in the late fifth and sixth centuries CE after their earlier presence in southern Gaul. They built a kingdom that controlled most of modern Spain and Portugal until the Islamic conquest of 711 CE.

**Lombard kingdom of Italy**: The Lombards entered Italy in 568 CE and built a kingdom that would last until Charlemagne's conquest in 774 CE.

**Anglo-Saxon kingdoms of Britain**: After the Roman withdrawal in the early fifth century, Germanic Anglo-Saxon peoples had migrated from northwestern Europe into Britain and established several small kingdoms (the **heptarchy** — Northumbria, Mercia, Wessex, East Anglia, Essex, Sussex, Kent) that competed for dominance through the early medieval period.

**Smaller kingdoms** in modern Scandinavia, the Low Countries, central Europe, and the Balkans operated outside the former Roman frontier.

These kingdoms varied in their adoption of Roman institutional inheritance. The Franks and Visigoths preserved much of Roman law (**Lex Salica**, the **Visigothic Code**); the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms developed customary law on Germanic foundations. All of them used the Catholic Church as a partial substitute for the disappeared Roman administrative state — bishops took on civic functions, monasteries became economic and educational institutions, the Latin language survived as the language of literate culture (and would survive throughout the medieval period as the language of Catholic liturgy, scholarship, and high culture).

### The Carolingian Empire

The most consequential post-Roman political project was the **Carolingian Empire**, named for **Charles Martel** ("Charles the Hammer") and his successors. Charles Martel, the Frankish ruler of his time, had defeated an Arab raiding force at the **Battle of Tours** (or Poitiers) in 732 CE — a battle that, in retrospective Latin Christian historiography, was credited with stopping the Islamic expansion into Western Europe. (The actual scale and significance of Tours is debated; the raid was probably a foraging expedition rather than a serious conquest attempt.)

Charles Martel's son **Pepin the Short** displaced the previous Frankish ruling dynasty (the Merovingians) and was anointed king in 751 CE with papal approval — a political-religious bargain that mattered for the next thousand years. Pepin's son was **Charlemagne** ("Charles the Great"), who reigned from 768 to 814 CE and built an empire that included most of modern France, Germany, the Low Countries, northern Italy, and parts of Spain.

On Christmas Day, 800 CE, in St. Peter's Basilica in Rome, Pope **Leo III** crowned Charlemagne **Emperor of the Romans**. The act was politically loaded. The Byzantine emperor in Constantinople still held the title "Emperor of the Romans"; the imperial title had not formally been used in the West since 476 CE. Leo and Charlemagne were creating something new: a Latin Christian empire whose emperor was crowned by the pope. The relationship between secular and religious authority in Latin Europe — the **two-swords doctrine**, the medieval debates over investiture, the long tension between popes and emperors — would shape the next millennium.

The Carolingian Empire also produced the **Carolingian Renaissance** — a substantial cultural revival sponsored by Charlemagne and his court scholar **Alcuin of York**. Manuscripts of classical Latin authors were copied; standards of Latin prose were revived; a uniform writing system (**Carolingian minuscule**) was developed and would become the basis of all subsequent European book hands and most modern Roman alphabets. Many of the Latin texts that survive from antiquity exist today only in copies made during the Carolingian Renaissance.

The empire fragmented after Charlemagne's death. His grandsons divided it three ways in 843 CE (the **Treaty of Verdun**), and the resulting kingdoms (West Francia, Middle Francia, East Francia) became the rough ancestors of France and Germany. The political unity Charlemagne had achieved did not return to Latin Europe for another thousand years.

## Concept 2 — Feudalism and the manorial system

Latin Europe across the eighth through twelfth centuries operated under what historians call the **feudal-manorial system** — a political and economic arrangement that emerged from the absence of a strong central state and the need to organize military defense and agricultural production at small scales.

The vocabulary is contested by modern medievalists, who note that "feudalism" was a sixteenth-century invention applied retrospectively to medieval institutions that were never as systematic as the term implies. The basic structures, however, were real.

### Feudal political organization

A **lord** held land from a higher lord (ultimately, in theory, from the king). The lord owed his higher lord military service (typically as a mounted knight, or providing knights), counsel, and certain financial obligations. The higher lord owed his vassal protection, justice, and the use of the land. The relationship was a personal contract sealed by a ceremony of **homage** in which the vassal placed his hands between his lord's and swore allegiance. The chain ran from king to high nobles to lesser nobles to **knights** at the bottom of the noble class.

The system was the response to a specific problem: how to maintain a class of mounted military specialists in an economy where central state finance had collapsed. The mounted knight required an enormous investment — armor, weapons, horses, training time. Supporting knights through state taxation, on the Roman or Tang Chinese model, was no longer possible in early medieval Latin Europe. Supporting them through grants of land — *fiefs* — was. A knight given a fief had the income of a peasant village to pay for his equipment and training; in exchange, he was available for military service when his lord called.

### The manorial system

The economic unit at the bottom of the system was the **manor** — typically a village and its surrounding agricultural land, owned by a lord and worked by peasants. The peasants were either **free** tenants, who paid rent to the lord and had legal independence, or **serfs**, who were legally bound to the land (could not leave without permission) and owed labor service on the lord's *demesne* (the land the lord kept for his own use). The arrangement varied widely across regions. The principal point is that the manor was a substantially self-sufficient unit — producing most of its own food, processing its own grain at the lord's mill, baking bread in the lord's oven, settling its own legal disputes through the lord's manor court.

Agricultural productivity in the early medieval period was low by modern standards but rose steadily over the period. The **three-field system** of crop rotation, the **heavy plow** (which could turn the wet, heavy soils of northern Europe), the **horse collar** (which let horses pull more efficiently than the older yoke designs), and the **horseshoe** all spread across northern Europe between the eighth and eleventh centuries CE. Yields rose. Population grew. By 1100 CE, Latin Europe had recovered from the post-Roman demographic decline; it would expand sharply in the next two centuries.

### The Catholic Church as the binding institution

The institution that gave Latin Europe coherence beyond the local manor was the **Catholic Church**. Bishops and abbots ran ecclesiastical territories that often included substantial land. Monasteries — particularly the **Benedictine** order founded by Benedict of Nursia in the early sixth century, and the reforming **Cluniac** order founded in 910 CE — became centers of agriculture, education, manuscript copying, and law. The papacy, weak in the early medieval centuries, grew substantially during the **Gregorian reform** of the eleventh century (under **Pope Gregory VII**, reigned 1073–1085 CE), which asserted papal authority over secular rulers and reorganized church governance.

The **investiture controversy** of the late eleventh and early twelfth centuries — the dispute between the papacy and the Holy Roman Empire over who had the right to appoint bishops — is one of the period's defining political conflicts. The Concordat of Worms in 1122 CE produced a compromise: spiritual investiture (granting the bishop's spiritual authority) belonged to the Church; secular investiture (granting the bishop's temporal lands and powers) could remain with the secular ruler. The conflict left both papacy and empire institutionally stronger but in permanent tension.

By 1100 CE, Latin Europe had a recognizable institutional shape. A patchwork of feudal kingdoms (France, the German-Italian Holy Roman Empire, England, Spain, the Italian city-states beginning to emerge) operated in tension with a strong, increasingly centralized Catholic Church under a reforming papacy. The legal and intellectual revival of the period was producing the first European universities (**Bologna** for law, c. 1088 CE; **Paris** for theology, organized in the twelfth century; **Oxford** soon after). Long-distance trade was reviving — Italian cities like **Venice**, **Genoa**, and **Pisa** were developing maritime trading empires; the **Hanseatic League** was forming in the Baltic. Latin Europe, fragmented though it remained, was institutionally coherent in ways it had not been a few centuries earlier. It was about to project that coherence outward.

## Concept 3 — Al-Andalus and the Iberian frontier

The Iberian Peninsula occupies an unusual place in this chapter because, alone among the post-Roman territories of Latin Europe, most of it was conquered by the Islamic caliphate in the early eighth century and would remain at least partially Islamic until 1492 CE.

In **711 CE**, an Arab-Berber army under **Tariq ibn Ziyad** crossed the Strait of Gibraltar (whose name comes from *Jabal Tariq*, "the mountain of Tariq") and, within seven years, conquered most of the Iberian Peninsula from the collapsing Visigothic kingdom. The Islamic state that resulted was first a province of the Umayyad caliphate, then — after the Abbasid revolution of 750 CE killed most of the Umayyad family — an independent emirate established by the Umayyad survivor **Abd al-Rahman I** at **Córdoba**. In 929 CE, **Abd al-Rahman III** declared an independent caliphate, rivaling the Abbasids in Baghdad and the Fatimids in Cairo.

**Caliphal Córdoba** in the tenth century was the largest city in Western Europe — perhaps half a million inhabitants, with a magnificent **Great Mosque** (begun in 785 CE, expanded over the next two centuries) whose forest of horseshoe arches still stands. Córdoba's library was reportedly four hundred thousand volumes — at a time when the largest Latin Christian library in Europe held perhaps a few hundred. The city's intellectual production combined Arabic, Hebrew, and Latin scholarship: Muslim scholars like **Ibn Rushd** (Averroes, 1126–1198) and **Ibn Hazm**, Jewish scholars like **Maimonides** (1138–1204), and Christian translators all worked in or near the Caliphate of Córdoba's intellectual networks. **Toledo** after its 1085 CE Christian reconquest became the most important center for the translation of Arabic-language scientific and philosophical works into Latin — the route through which much of the Greek classical inheritance, preserved in Arabic, would re-enter the Latin West.

The **Reconquista** — the gradual Christian reconquest of the Iberian Peninsula — proceeded across the eighth through fifteenth centuries. Small Christian kingdoms in the mountainous north (Asturias, León, Castile, Aragon, Navarre, Portugal) gradually expanded south, conquering Islamic territory. **Toledo** fell to Christians in 1085. **Córdoba** fell in 1236. **Seville** fell in 1248. The last Islamic kingdom of Iberia, **Granada**, would fall in 1492 — coincident with Columbus's first voyage.

The Iberian frontier was, throughout these centuries, a zone of continuous cultural exchange. Christian, Muslim, and Jewish populations lived in close contact, sometimes peacefully and sometimes not. Christian rulers employed Muslim and Jewish administrators; Muslim rulers employed Christians; Latin, Arabic, and Hebrew operated as parallel literate languages. The exchange would profoundly shape the Iberian peninsula's culture, language, and architecture, and through Iberia would shape Latin Europe more broadly.

## Concept 4 — The Eastern Mediterranean on the eve of the Crusades

To understand why the First Crusade happened when it happened, the chapter has to look at what was happening on the other end of the Mediterranean.

### The decline of the Abbasid caliphate

The Abbasid caliphate (Chapter 14) had been declining politically since the mid-ninth century. By the tenth century, the Abbasid caliph was a religious figurehead while real power was held by a series of dynasties — the **Tahirids**, **Saffarids**, and **Samanids** in Persia; the **Fatimids** in Egypt (who broke away in 909 CE and built a Shia caliphate that controlled North Africa, Egypt, and parts of Syria and Arabia); the **Buyids**, who effectively controlled Baghdad from 945 CE onward.

In the eleventh century, a new force entered the Islamic world: the **Seljuk Turks**. The Seljuks were a Turkic confederation from Central Asia who had recently converted to Sunni Islam. Under their leader **Tughrul Bey** (d. 1063), they entered Iran in the 1030s and conquered most of the Abbasid territory. In **1055 CE**, Tughrul entered Baghdad and was acknowledged as **sultan** (a new title meaning "secular ruler") under the figurehead Abbasid caliph. The Seljuk Sultanate over the next several decades became the dominant political power of the eastern Islamic world, controlling most of Iran, Iraq, Syria, and much of Anatolia.

### The Battle of Manzikert (1071 CE)

In **August 1071 CE**, the Seljuk sultan **Alp Arslan** met a Byzantine army under emperor **Romanos IV Diogenes** at **Manzikert** in eastern Anatolia. The battle was a Byzantine catastrophe. Romanos was captured. The Byzantine field army was destroyed. The empire's central Anatolian heartland — its principal source of soldiers, taxes, and food — lay open to Turkish migration and settlement.

Within a generation, most of Anatolia (the historical heartland of the Eastern Roman Empire and the recruiting ground for its army) had fallen under Turkish control. Christian populations were not displaced en masse, but they came under Muslim political authority. The Byzantine state was reduced to its core territories around Constantinople and northern Greece. Subsequent emperors fought back tactically but never recovered the pre-1071 position. Manzikert was the moment from which the Byzantine decline that would end at 1453 began.

### The East-West Schism of 1054 CE

The relationship between Latin (Catholic) and Greek (Orthodox) Christianity had been fraying for centuries — over theological disputes (the addition of *filioque* — "and from the Son" — to the Nicene Creed by the Latin church, which the Greek church rejected), over papal claims of universal authority over all Christianity (which the Greek church rejected), over liturgical practices (whether priests should be married, whether leavened or unleavened bread should be used in communion, etc.). In **1054 CE**, papal legates in Constantinople and the Patriarch of Constantinople mutually excommunicated each other in a tense dispute. The act, intended at the time as a contained dispute, became the formal start of what is now called the **East-West Schism** — the institutional separation of Latin Catholic Christianity from Greek Orthodox Christianity that has persisted for nearly a thousand years.

The schism mattered for the crusading movement. When Byzantine emperor Alexios I Komnenos asked Western help against the Seljuks in 1095, he was asking from a Christian tradition formally separated from the Latin one. Pope Urban II's response — sending Western armies to fight in the East — was partly a gesture toward reuniting the Christian world under Latin leadership. The Crusaders themselves often viewed the Greek Christians as schismatic, untrustworthy, and ultimately as deserving of conquest in their own right. The **Fourth Crusade** (1202–1204) would, scandalously, sack Constantinople rather than fighting Muslims at all.

## Concept 5 — The First Crusade

Pope Urban II's call at Clermont produced an extraordinary mass response. The chapter's opening case described what happened. We can now look at it more systematically.

### Who went and why

The Crusaders included perhaps four major contingents under different aristocratic leaders: the army of **Godfrey of Bouillon** and his brothers from Lorraine; the army of **Bohemond of Taranto** from southern Italy (a Norman of considerable military experience); the army of **Raymond of Toulouse** from southern France; the army of **Hugh of Vermandois** from northern France (with smaller contributions from England and elsewhere). Total Crusader forces probably numbered 50,000–70,000 at maximum — much smaller than the 100,000+ figures of contemporary chroniclers but still extraordinary for the period.

The motives for going were various and overlapped. **Religious** motives — the plenary indulgence; the prospect of pilgrimage to Jerusalem (a deeply prestigious religious goal); the chance to die fighting for the cross and go directly to heaven — were primary for many participants and at least real for nearly all. **Material** motives — the prospect of land in the East, the loot of conquest, the social advancement available to a successful military commander — operated alongside the religious. **Family-political** motives — the obligation to follow a lord, the social pressure of accompanying friends and kin — moved many. **Adventure** and **escape** moved others. The mix varied by individual.

The Crusaders themselves did not call themselves crusaders. The term *cruciatus* / "crusader" was coined later. They called themselves **pilgrims** (*peregrini*), and the expedition the *iter* ("the journey") or *peregrinatio* ("the pilgrimage"). This self-understanding mattered: many of them genuinely believed they were on an armed pilgrimage to recover Jerusalem rather than on a war of conquest. The fact that the result was war of conquest does not mean the participants saw it that way.

### The course of the Crusade

After the People's Crusade was destroyed in Anatolia in 1096, the main Crusader armies converged on Constantinople in late 1096 and early 1097. Emperor Alexios I, who had asked for some experienced mercenary auxiliaries and had received instead an army of seventy thousand Westerners camped outside his walls, negotiated oaths of allegiance from the Crusader princes (most agreed to return former Byzantine territories to Byzantine control once recaptured) and shipped them across the Bosporus into Anatolia.

The Crusaders captured **Nicaea** in May 1097 (turning it over to Alexios per the agreement). They then defeated a Seljuk army at **Dorylaeum** in July 1097. They marched south across Anatolia in punishing summer heat; the army reached **Antioch** in northern Syria in October 1097. The siege of Antioch lasted seven and a half months; the city fell in June 1098 — only for the Crusaders to be immediately besieged inside the city by a relieving Muslim army. They sortied and won an unlikely victory after the alleged discovery (during the siege) of the **Holy Lance** — the spear that supposedly pierced Christ on the cross — which became a rallying symbol for the army. From Antioch, the army marched south toward Jerusalem.

**Jerusalem** was held by an Egyptian Fatimid garrison that had taken the city from the Seljuks just a year earlier. The Crusader army arrived in June 1099, conducted a five-week siege, and took the city by direct assault on July 15, 1099. The massacre that followed has been documented by both Christian and Muslim sources: most of the city's Muslim and Jewish inhabitants were killed in the streets and in the al-Aqsa mosque (where many had taken refuge). The chronicler Raymond of Aguilers, a Christian eyewitness, wrote with apparent satisfaction that "in the Temple and porch of Solomon men rode in blood up to their knees and bridle reins."

The Crusaders established the **Latin Kingdom of Jerusalem** under Godfrey of Bouillon (who took the title "Defender of the Holy Sepulchre" rather than "King," an act of religious humility) and his successor Baldwin I (who took the king's title). Three additional Crusader states were carved out in the Levant: the **County of Edessa**, the **Principality of Antioch**, and the **County of Tripoli**. These four **Crusader states** would persist for nearly two centuries — Edessa to 1144, Jerusalem to 1187 (under the Muslim general **Saladin**), Antioch to 1268, Tripoli to 1289, and the last Crusader stronghold at **Acre** to 1291.

### Consequences

The First Crusade produced several lasting consequences:

**Latin presence in the Near East.** For nearly two hundred years, Latin Christian states held territory in the Eastern Mediterranean, with Latin populations, churches, governments, and economies. The institutions they developed — the **Knights Templar**, the **Knights Hospitaller**, the eventually established military-religious orders — would shape European institutional history for centuries.

**Long-term Latin-Eastern engagement.** The Crusades opened sustained diplomatic, commercial, and intellectual contact between Latin Europe and the Mediterranean Islamic world. Italian merchant cities (Venice, Genoa, Pisa) built their later commercial empires substantially on the trade with the Crusader states. Arabic-language scholarship continued to be translated into Latin in increasing volumes.

**Continuing Crusades.** The First Crusade was followed by a long series of similarly framed expeditions. The **Second Crusade** (1145–1149, in response to the Muslim recapture of Edessa) was a failure. The **Third Crusade** (1189–1192, in response to Saladin's recapture of Jerusalem) recovered the coastal cities but failed to retake Jerusalem itself. The **Fourth Crusade** (1202–1204) sacked Christian Constantinople. There were perhaps a dozen subsequent expeditions, with diminishing returns. The Crusader states in the Levant were finally extinguished in 1291.

**Religious-political consolidation in Europe.** The crusading movement legitimized organized military violence in religious framing. It produced the **Albigensian Crusade** (1209–1229) against Cathar heretics in southern France; the **Northern Crusades** against pagans in the eastern Baltic; the **Reconquista** in Iberia. Crusading became a paradigm of holy war that Latin Christianity would deploy against heretics and pagans for centuries — and that would shape, indirectly, the language of European wars of religion in the Reformation period.

**Damaged interfaith relations.** The Crusades and the violence accompanying them — including substantial pogroms against Jewish communities in Europe during the buildup to the First Crusade and subsequent expeditions — produced lasting damage to relations among the three Mediterranean monotheisms. The image of the Crusader has been a contested historical symbol ever since, with very different reception in Western, Eastern Christian, Jewish, and Muslim historiographical traditions.

Latin Europe in 1100 CE was producing the universities, the Gothic cathedrals, the troubadour poetry, the scholastic philosophy, the reformed papacy, the rising Italian city-states, the parliaments and *cortes* — every one of which mattered as much as the crusading movement for the long-term shape of European civilization. ## Key terms

- **Al-Andalus.** The Islamic part of the Iberian Peninsula, c. 711–1492 CE.
- **Battle of Manzikert.** The 1071 CE Seljuk victory over Byzantium that opened Anatolia to Turkish settlement.
- **Battle of Tours.** The 732 CE Frankish victory over an Arab raiding force, traditionally credited with stopping Islamic expansion into Western Europe.
- **Carolingian Empire.** The Frankish empire built by Charlemagne and his predecessors, peaked early ninth century.
- **Charlemagne.** Frankish king and emperor (reigned 768–814 CE), crowned Emperor of the Romans by Pope Leo III in 800 CE.
- **Cluniac reform.** Tenth- and eleventh-century reform of monasticism centered at the Abbey of Cluny.
- **Crusader states.** The four Latin Christian states established in the Levant after the First Crusade: Jerusalem, Antioch, Tripoli, Edessa.
- **East-West Schism.** The 1054 CE formal separation of Latin Catholic from Greek Orthodox Christianity.
- **Feudalism.** The political-social system of personal vassalage between lord and retainer that organized medieval Latin Europe.
- **First Crusade.** The 1095–1099 CE expedition that captured Jerusalem.
- **Gregorian reform.** Eleventh-century reform of the papacy under Gregory VII and his successors.
- **Holy Land.** Christian European term for Palestine and the surrounding regions.
- **Indulgence.** Remission of the temporal punishment for sin granted by the Church; the **plenary indulgence** offered for the First Crusade was unusually generous.
- **Investiture controversy.** The eleventh- and early-twelfth-century dispute between papacy and empire over the appointment of bishops.
- **Manorial system.** The economic organization of the medieval village under a lord's authority.
- **Pope Urban II.** The pope (reigned 1088–1099 CE) who called the First Crusade at Clermont.
- **Reconquista.** The gradual Christian reconquest of the Iberian Peninsula from Islamic rule, c. 711–1492 CE.
- **Seljuk Turks.** Turkic dynasty (peak 1037–1194 CE) that took over much of the Abbasid territory.
- **Treaty of Verdun.** The 843 CE division of the Carolingian Empire among Charlemagne's grandsons.
