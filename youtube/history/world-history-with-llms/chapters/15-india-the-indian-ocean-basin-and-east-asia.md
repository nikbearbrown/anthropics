## Chapter overview

By the end of this chapter you will know how the eastern half of Eurasia and the Indian Ocean rim functioned as a connected world between roughly 500 and 1200 CE. You will know post-Gupta India — a network of regional kingdoms (the **Cholas**, the **Pala**, the **Chalukya**) without a unified imperial center but with thriving trade, religion, and culture. You will know **Sui** (581–618 CE) and **Tang** China (618–907 CE), the dynasty that built the first great unified Chinese empire after centuries of disunity, and **Tang Chang'an** as the largest and most cosmopolitan city in the world at its height. You will know the **Silk Roads** as a multi-century overland trading system carrying silk, paper, spices, and ideas across Eurasia — and the **Sogdians** of Central Asia who actually ran much of it. You will know the **Indian Ocean trade** as the maritime equivalent — connecting East Africa, Arabia, India, Southeast Asia, and China — and the **Swahili coast** that grew up at its African end. You will know how Buddhism spread from India to China, Korea, and Japan, how Islam spread across the Indian Ocean, and how Hinduism and Buddhism shaped early Southeast Asian states (Funan, Srivijaya, Khmer). You will know early **Korea** (the **Three Kingdoms** and **Unified Silla**) and early **Japan** (the **Yamato**, **Asuka**, **Nara**, and **Heian** periods). By the end, you will see Eurasia not as separated civilizations but as a single connected system, with Tang China and the Abbasid caliphate at the two ends and a continuous fabric of trade, religion, and migration running between them.

## Opening case — Chang'an, c. 750 CE

In the middle of the eighth century CE, the city of **Chang'an** — the capital of Tang China, in the Wei River valley near modern Xi'an — held perhaps a million inhabitants. It was the largest city in the world. Its plan was geometric: a rectangle nearly six miles by five miles, divided into 108 walled wards along a north-south grid, with the imperial palace at the north end and the great markets at the south. The streets were wide enough — up to 500 feet for the central avenue — to accommodate the imperial processions that moved between the palace and the Buddhist temples.

The population was extraordinarily diverse. Within the city walls lived Persian merchants from the Sasanian diaspora, Sogdian caravan masters from Central Asia, Korean and Japanese students at the imperial academy, Buddhist monks from India and Tibet, Christian missionaries of the Assyrian Church of the East, Jewish traders, Muslim Arab and Persian merchants who had arrived along the Silk Road, Vietnamese and Champa visitors from the south. Their mosques, churches, monasteries, and temples occupied identifiable quarters of the city. The cosmopolitanism reached the imperial court itself — the **An Lushan**, whose 755 CE rebellion would shake the dynasty to its foundations, was an Iranian-Sogdian general in the Tang military service. Court fashion adopted Central Asian styles. Tang women rode horses, played polo, wrote poetry. Foreign religions enjoyed imperial patronage until the religious persecutions of the ninth century.

This chapter is about the world Chang'an sat at the eastern end of. From China the Silk Roads ran west — through the oases of the Tarim Basin, into Sogdiana around modern Samarkand, across Central Asia, to the great commercial cities of the Islamic world. From China the Indian Ocean ran south and west — Tang ships sailed to Southeast Asia, Indian merchants came to Tang ports, Persian and Arab dhows carried goods between China, India, the Red Sea, and the East African coast. From China the routes ran east — to Korea, where the Tang model of government and Buddhism would shape the unified Silla state, and to Japan, where the Asuka and Nara periods wholesale imported Tang court conventions, Chinese script, and continental Buddhism.

Chang'an in 750 CE is the easternmost end of a single Eurasian civilizational system. Baghdad, at almost the same moment, sat at the western end of the same system — the second-largest city in the world after Chang'an, the cosmopolitan capital of the Abbasid caliphate, with its own population of Persians, Turks, Greeks, Indians, Africans, and Arabs. Between them ran a continuous fabric of trade, scholarship, religious transmission, and diplomatic correspondence. This is the eastern Eurasia of Chapter 14's later pages and the world we now examine in detail.

## Concept 1 — Post-Gupta India

The Gupta Dynasty (Chapter 6) had unified northern India from c. 320 to c. 550 CE in what later Indian historiography calls the Classical Age. The Gupta collapse under Hunnic invasions in the late fifth and sixth centuries did not produce another unified Indian empire for almost a thousand years. The early medieval Indian subcontinent was a patchwork of regional kingdoms — vigorous, prosperous, and diverse, but never under a single ruler.

### The major regional dynasties

Across northern, central, and southern India between roughly 600 and 1200 CE:

**The Pala dynasty** (c. 750–1170 CE) ruled Bihar and Bengal in the eastern Gangetic plain. The Palas were Buddhist patrons; they sponsored the great Buddhist universities of **Nalanda** and **Vikramshila**, which drew students from across South and East Asia. Pala-period India was the source of much of the Mahayana and Tantric Buddhism that spread to Tibet, Burma, and eventually East Asia.

**The Chola dynasty** (c. 850–1279 CE) ruled the Tamil south. The Cholas were the most successful South Indian dynasty, with naval power that projected into Sri Lanka, the Maldives, and at one famous moment (under **Rajendra I**, in the 1020s) into Southeast Asia, where Chola fleets attacked the Srivijaya kingdom in modern Indonesia. The Cholas built the great Hindu temple complexes at Thanjavur (the **Brihadeshvara Temple**, completed 1010 CE) and patronized the South Indian bronze sculpture traditions that produced some of the most refined religious art in world history.

**The Chalukya** dynasties (Western Chalukyas in the Deccan, Eastern Chalukyas in Andhra) ruled large stretches of central India through much of this period.

**The Pratihara** kingdom dominated north-central India for centuries before fragmenting under pressure from the **Ghaznavid** raids that began the Islamic incursions into the subcontinent.

**The Rashtrakuta** dynasty produced the famous rock-cut Hindu temple complex at **Ellora**, including the monolithic Kailasanatha Temple carved out of a single mountainside in the eighth century CE.

The political fragmentation did not impede economic and cultural development. Indian craft production — textiles (cotton, silk), metallurgy, spices, sugar, gemstones — fed both internal markets and the great Indian Ocean trade network. Indian mathematics and astronomy were transmitted west into the Abbasid caliphate (Chapter 14: the decimal place-value system, the concept of zero, trigonometry) and east into China.

### Religion

Indian religious life in this period was dominated by the consolidation of **classical Hinduism** out of the older Vedic-Brahmanic tradition. The major Hindu deities (**Vishnu**, **Shiva**, **Devi** in her many forms) acquired the iconographic and theological identities they hold today. The **Puranas** (long mythological-philosophical Sanskrit texts) were composed and circulated. Temple architecture flourished. Hinduism became the religion of the majority of the South Asian population.

**Buddhism** in India entered a long decline across this period. The Gupta and post-Gupta era saw Buddhist patronage primarily in eastern India under the Palas and in some Buddhist enclaves elsewhere. Outside these regions, Buddhism was gradually absorbed back into the broader Hindu fold — in some traditional Buddhist arguments, the historical Buddha became one of the *avatars* of Vishnu in syncretic Hindu-Buddhist regions. By the time of the Islamic conquests of the eleventh and twelfth centuries, Buddhism in most of India was already weakened; the Islamic destruction of Nalanda and the other major Buddhist universities (Bakhtiyar Khilji's sack of Nalanda, c. 1200 CE) was the final blow rather than the original cause of Buddhism's decline in its homeland. Indian Buddhism survived in the Himalayan regions (Tibet, Bhutan, Ladakh) and in the religion's Southeast and East Asian diaspora.

**Islam** entered India through three routes: maritime trade with Arab and Persian merchants on the western coast (where small Muslim communities have existed since the seventh century CE); a brief Arab military expedition into Sind in the early eighth century; and, more consequentially, the Ghaznavid and Ghorid raids and conquests beginning in the late tenth and twelfth centuries CE. The major Islamic political establishment in India would come with the **Delhi Sultanate** (founded 1206 CE), which we cover in Chapter 17.

## Concept 2 — Sui and Tang China

After the fall of the Han dynasty in 220 CE, China entered four centuries of disunity — the **Three Kingdoms** period, the **Jin dynasty**, the **Northern and Southern Dynasties** period — during which the country was divided between competing regional states, often with the north controlled by non-Chinese (mostly steppe-origin) dynasties and the south controlled by Han Chinese ones. Reunification finally came under the **Sui dynasty** (581–618 CE).

### The Sui dynasty (581–618 CE)

The Sui was short but consequential. Its founder **Yang Jian** (Emperor Wen, reigned 581–604 CE) had served as a high official under the previous northern dynasty and seized the throne in 581 CE. By 589 CE, Sui armies had reconquered the south and reunified the country for the first time since 220 CE. The Sui rebuilt central institutions: a unified law code (the Sui legal codes that would become the basis of the **Tang Code**), a centralized bureaucracy, the **Equal Field System** for distributing agricultural land to peasants, and a military reorganization. It commissioned the **Grand Canal** — a 1,500-mile waterway connecting the Yangtze rice basin to the Yellow River bureaucratic center — that would shape Chinese political-economic geography for the next thirteen hundred years.

The Sui also overextended itself. Emperor **Yang** (reigned 604–618 CE), Wen's son, launched massive military expeditions against Goguryeo in Korea (three failed campaigns between 612 and 614 CE) and undertook expensive building projects. The combined cost broke the dynasty's finances. Rebellions began in the late 610s. In 618 CE, Emperor Yang was assassinated; the dynasty collapsed. A general named **Li Yuan** seized the capital and founded the **Tang dynasty**.

### The Tang dynasty (618–907 CE)

The **Tang** ruled China for nearly three hundred years and is often considered the height of pre-modern Chinese civilization. Its capital at **Chang'an** was the largest city in the world. Its territorial extent — across most of modern China, into the Tarim Basin in Central Asia, into northern Vietnam and parts of Korea — was greater than any previous Chinese dynasty's. Its cultural production — the poetry of **Li Bai** (701–762 CE) and **Du Fu** (712–770 CE), the painting of Wang Wei, the fiction-prose of the *chuanqi* tradition — set the standards by which subsequent Chinese literary culture would judge itself. The Tang is the dynasty in which Chinese civilization arrived at most of the institutional features it would carry through the next millennium.

**Civil service examinations.** The Tang formalized a system in which officials of the imperial bureaucracy were selected by competitive examination. Candidates studied the Confucian classics and were tested on their ability to compose essays, write poetry in classical forms, and analyze policy questions. The system was not yet fully meritocratic — aristocratic background still mattered, and many officials still entered through inheritance or recommendation rather than examination — but the principle that officials should be selected by demonstrated learning was now institutionalized. Subsequent dynasties (Song, Ming, Qing) would expand the examinations into the dominant route to political office. The Chinese examination system was the world's first large-scale meritocratic civil service and would be a model for later European civil service reforms.

**Empress Wu Zetian** (reigned 690–705 CE as the only female emperor in Chinese history; she had earlier been empress consort and empress dowager) reorganized the bureaucracy, expanded the examination system, and — controversially — formally declared a new dynasty, the Zhou, during her own reign. After her death the Tang was restored. Wu's reign was a dramatic case of how the imperial system could produce female political authority despite its formal patriarchal structure; her historical reputation has been contested for thirteen centuries.

**Religion.** The Tang court was famously religiously tolerant in its first century and a half. Buddhism flourished — Buddhist temples and monasteries spread across the empire; Tang princes and princesses became monks and nuns; the great Buddhist pilgrim **Xuanzang** (602–664 CE) traveled to India and back, returning with hundreds of Buddhist texts that were translated into Chinese under imperial sponsorship. **Daoism** received substantial state patronage; the imperial family claimed descent from **Laozi**. **Christianity** of the Assyrian Church (the so-called "Nestorian" tradition) had a presence in Chang'an attested by the famous **Nestorian Stele** of 781 CE. **Islam** arrived through both Silk Road and maritime routes; the **Huaisheng Mosque** in Guangzhou is traditionally said to date from this period.

The religious tolerance ended in the mid-ninth century. The **Huichang persecution** of 845 CE, ordered by Emperor Wuzong, dissolved 4,600 Buddhist monasteries, returned 260,000 monks and nuns to lay life, and confiscated monastic land. The persecution was part economic (the great monasteries had become major landholders and tax-exempt economic units) and part Confucian-Daoist reaction against foreign religion. Buddhism in China survived the persecution but never returned to its earlier institutional dominance.

**The An Lushan rebellion** (755–763 CE) was the dynasty's central trauma. An Lushan, a half-Sogdian half-Turkic general, commanded the largest concentrated military force in the empire (in the northeast frontier region near modern Beijing). In 755 he rebelled. The rebellion captured Chang'an, the imperial court fled, and the dynasty barely survived eight years of warfare. The recovery cost the Tang state much of its central authority over the provinces; military governors in many regions became effectively autonomous. The dynasty limped along for another century and a half, finally collapsing under regional warlordism in 907 CE. The post-Tang **Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms** period (907–960 CE) would last just over half a century before the **Song dynasty** (960–1279 CE) reunified China.

## Concept 3 — The Silk Roads and the Sogdians

The **Silk Roads** were not a single road. They were a continuously evolving network of overland trade routes that connected China to Central Asia, the Iranian plateau, the Mediterranean, and beyond. The "Silk Road" name was coined by the German geographer Ferdinand von Richthofen in the nineteenth century; the people who actually used the routes called them by various local names or simply "the road."

The routes had been operating since the second century BCE (when the Han Chinese ambassador Zhang Qian's missions to Central Asia opened them) and would continue, with rises and falls, until the maritime alternatives of the early modern period made them less important. Their golden age was the period from roughly 600 to 1200 CE — the period this chapter covers — when Tang China and the Abbasid caliphate at the two ends produced the demand and stability that kept the trade flowing.

The goods moving along the routes:

- **Eastward**: glass, gold, silver, gemstones, horses (especially the prized "heavenly horses" of Ferghana), furs, woolen textiles, luxury goods from the Mediterranean, and ideas (Buddhism most importantly, but also Zoroastrianism, Manichaeism, Christianity, Islam, and various scholarly traditions).
- **Westward**: silk (for centuries the Chinese state monopoly that gave the route its modern name), porcelain, paper (the technology of papermaking traveled west from China and reached the Islamic world by 750 CE; the Battle of Talas in 751 CE allegedly produced Chinese papermaker prisoners who introduced the craft to Samarkand), spices (transshipped from the Indian Ocean), tea, and ideas.

### The Sogdians

Most of the actual long-distance carrying on the Silk Road through this period was done by the **Sogdians** — an Iranian-language-speaking people from the region around modern Samarkand and Bukhara in Uzbekistan and Tajikistan. The Sogdians had built a network of trading colonies along the Silk Road from China to the Black Sea. They were multilingual (Sogdian, Chinese, Persian, and various local languages), diplomatically skilled, and religiously tolerant in practice (Sogdian individuals followed Zoroastrianism, Buddhism, Manichaeism, and Christianity at various times and places). Sogdian merchants are visible in the Tang archaeological record — paintings of Sogdian-faced traders, Sogdian-language documents, even Sogdian-named officials in the Tang government like An Lushan himself.

Sogdiana as a culturally and politically distinct region was destroyed by the Arab-Islamic conquest in the early eighth century CE. The Sogdian language lost ground to Persian and Arabic over the next several centuries. But Sogdian descendants merged into the populations of Central Asia and the Islamic world, and the trading traditions they had built continued under new auspices.

The Silk Road also carried disease. The **Black Death** of the fourteenth century (Chapter 20) probably moved from East Asia to Europe along the Silk Road. The recurrences of bubonic plague and other diseases across Eurasia in this period are partially products of the connectivity that the routes produced.

## Concept 4 — The Indian Ocean trade

The **Indian Ocean** was, for most of premodern history, the world's most active maritime trading region. It connected East Africa, the Arabian Peninsula, Persia, India, Southeast Asia, and southern China through monsoon-driven shipping routes that allowed reliable seasonal travel.

**Monsoon winds**: the winds blow from southwest to northeast in summer (roughly April–September) and reverse to northeast-to-southwest in winter (October–March). The pattern allowed sailors to plan two-season round trips: a ship leaving Arabia or East Africa in the spring monsoon would reach India by midsummer, conduct trade, and return on the winter monsoon. The same pattern operated farther east between India and Southeast Asia / China. Monsoon sailing was reliable, predictable, and inexpensive compared to overland routes; an Indian Ocean ship could carry far more cargo than an equivalently provisioned camel caravan.

The trade had been operating since at least the first millennium BCE; it dramatically expanded from about 600 CE onward. Major ports included **Aden** at the mouth of the Red Sea, **Hormuz** at the entrance to the Persian Gulf, **Cambay** in Gujarat (western India), **Calicut** on the Malabar coast (southwestern India), **Quanzhou** on the south Chinese coast, **Palembang** in Sumatra (central Southeast Asia), **Kilwa** on the East African coast.

### Religion and trade

Religious traditions spread along these maritime routes, often with the merchants who carried the cargo. **Buddhism** had reached Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia by the early centuries CE; the great Buddhist monument at **Borobudur** in central Java (built c. 800 CE by the Shailendra dynasty) and the Hindu temple complex at **Angkor Wat** (built early twelfth century CE by the Khmer empire's Suryavarman II) testify to the deep integration of Indian religion into Southeast Asian states. **Islam** spread along the Indian Ocean from the eighth century onward; Arab and Persian Muslim merchants established communities in the major ports of India, Southeast Asia, and East Africa, and over centuries these communities Islamized substantial fractions of the local population. **Hinduism** spread with Indian traders into Southeast Asia, though it was eventually largely replaced by Buddhism (in mainland Southeast Asia) and Islam (in the maritime regions, except for Bali, where Hinduism survived).

### Southeast Asian states

The major Indian Ocean Southeast Asian states of this period:

- **Funan** (c. first century CE–550 CE), in the lower Mekong delta of modern Cambodia and Vietnam — the earliest large Southeast Asian state, oriented to Indian cultural traditions.
- **Srivijaya** (c. 650–1377 CE), based at Palembang in Sumatra — a maritime trading empire that controlled the Strait of Malacca, the choke point between the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea. Srivijaya was Buddhist and Sanskrit-using and projected influence across maritime Southeast Asia.
- **The Khmer empire** (802–1431 CE), based at **Angkor** in modern Cambodia — a Hindu and later Buddhist state whose capital city housed perhaps a million people in the early thirteenth century, with the temple complexes of Angkor Wat and Angkor Thom as its monumental signature.
- **Champa** (c. second century–1832 CE), along what is now central Vietnam — a Hindu Cham-language state in long contact with both India and China.

### The Swahili coast

On the East African coast, between roughly 800 and 1500 CE, a string of trading city-states emerged: **Mogadishu**, **Lamu**, **Mombasa**, **Zanzibar**, **Kilwa**, **Sofala**. The local populations were Bantu speakers (Chapter 11); they combined with Arab and Persian merchant communities to produce a distinctive Swahili culture, with **Swahili** language (a Bantu language with extensive Arabic loanwords) emerging as the lingua franca of the coast and becoming an Islamic culture by the eleventh century or so.

The Swahili cities exported gold, ivory, mangrove timber, and slaves to the Indian Ocean trade. They imported textiles, glass, ceramics, and porcelain (Chinese porcelain has been found in archaeological excavations at every major Swahili site). At their height, the Swahili cities were participants in a global economy: a thirteenth-century coin from Kilwa might carry an Arabic inscription, contain silver from Persia, and circulate through markets that also dealt in Chinese ceramics and Indian textiles. The wealthiest of the Swahili cities, **Kilwa** at its fourteenth-century peak, hosted a substantial mosque whose ruins still stand and is described in Ibn Battuta's travel narrative (1331 CE) as one of the most beautiful cities of the world.

## Concept 5 — Korea and Japan

China's eastern neighbors built civilizations partly modeled on Chinese institutions, partly on local foundations.

### Early Korea

Korea's **Three Kingdoms** period (Goguryeo in the north, Baekje in the southwest, Silla in the southeast) lasted from roughly the first century BCE to 668 CE. Buddhism arrived from China in the fourth century CE and was adopted by all three kingdoms. Chinese script, Confucian institutions, and Tang governance models were imported selectively. In **668 CE**, **Silla**, allied with Tang China, defeated the other two kingdoms and unified the peninsula under what historians call **Unified Silla** (668–935 CE). The Tang relationship turned hostile after unification — Silla expelled Tang garrisons and asserted Korean independence — but Korean institutions remained heavily influenced by the Chinese model.

Unified Silla's capital at **Gyeongju** was a planned city on Tang lines. The state sponsored Buddhism extensively; the bronze **Buddha at Seokguram Grotto** and the wooden **Pulguksa Temple**, both completed in the 770s CE, are masterpieces of East Asian Buddhist art. Silla scholars studied at Tang Chang'an. Silla aristocratic women sometimes traveled to the Tang court. The cultural traffic was substantial in both directions.

The successor state, **Goryeo** (918–1392 CE), gave Korea its English name. Goryeo continued the Buddhist-Confucian institutional pattern. Its most distinctive achievement was the production of the **Tripitaka Koreana** — a complete Korean-Chinese Buddhist canon carved on more than 81,000 wooden blocks (the surviving second version, completed in 1251 CE) that remains the most authoritative version of the Buddhist canon in classical Chinese. The Goryeo-period printing press used metal movable type as early as the early thirteenth century — over two centuries before Gutenberg.

### Early Japan

Japan's documented history begins with the consolidation of the **Yamato** clan in the Nara basin in the third and fourth centuries CE (Chapter 6). Buddhism arrived from Korea in the sixth century CE and became official state religion under **Prince Shotoku** (c. 574–622 CE), who modeled Japan's emerging state on the Chinese imperial model. The **Asuka period** (538–710 CE) saw the importation of Chinese script, Buddhism, and the basic forms of Tang court ritual.

The **Nara period** (710–794 CE) was a more thoroughgoing Tang transplant. The capital city of **Nara** was built on the Chang'an grid plan. The court adopted Chinese-style legal codes (the **Taiho Code** of 701 CE). Buddhist monasticism became a major institution, with the **Todaiji Temple** at Nara housing the **Great Buddha** statue (completed 752 CE), one of the largest bronze statues ever cast.

The **Heian period** (794–1185 CE) followed the move of the capital to Heian-kyo (modern Kyoto). Heian Japan saw a partial turn away from direct Chinese imitation toward the development of distinctively Japanese cultural forms. The court adopted phonetic scripts (*hiragana* and *katakana*) derived from Chinese characters but adapted to Japanese phonology. Court women wrote some of the world's first novels — most famously **Murasaki Shikibu's** **Tale of Genji** (early eleventh century), which is often called the world's first novel and remains one of the masterpieces of world literature. **Sei Shonagon's Pillow Book** (also early eleventh century) is the brilliant prose-essay companion to Genji. Heian poetry, painting, calligraphy, and ritual elaborated a court aesthetic of great refinement.

By the late twelfth century, the Heian court's centralized authority had eroded under the rise of provincial military aristocrats — the *bushi* or **samurai** class. In 1185 CE the **Genpei War** ended with the establishment of the first **shogunate** (a military government) at Kamakura under **Minamoto no Yoritomo**. The Japanese state from this point onward would have a dual structure — the imperial court at Kyoto retained ceremonial authority while real political power lay with a series of military dictators. The shogunate system would last (in various forms) until the Meiji Restoration of 1868.

You can describe post-Gupta India as a politically fragmented but economically and culturally vigorous region of regional kingdoms — Cholas in the south, Palas in the east, Chalukyas and others in the center — and identify the specific cultural products (Hindu temple architecture, classical Hinduism, Mahayana and Tantric Buddhism, Indian mathematical tradition) it produced.

You can describe the Indian Ocean trade as the maritime equivalent of the Silk Roads — connecting East Africa, Arabia, India, Southeast Asia, and China — and identify monsoon-driven sailing, the major port cities, and the Swahili coast as a substantial African participant in the system.

You can trace the spread of major religions — Buddhism, Hinduism, Islam — along these trade routes and identify the major polities (Srivijaya, Khmer, Pala) whose religious orientation reflected the routes' connections.

You can describe early Korea (Three Kingdoms, Unified Silla, Goryeo) and early Japan (Yamato, Asuka, Nara, Heian) as civilizations developing in the long shadow of Tang China while preserving distinctive local institutions and cultural forms.

The thing to watch for, going forward, is the temptation to read this period of Eurasian connectedness as merely a prelude to the better-known later periods (the Mongol world, European maritime expansion). The connectedness is its own story, with its own logics. The next chapter follows the Latin Christian European world's own development during the same period.

## Key terms

- **Abbasid caliphate.** The Islamic dynasty (750–1258 CE) that ruled from Baghdad; covered in Chapter 14.
- **Angkor.** The capital of the Khmer empire, site of Angkor Wat.
- **Borobudur.** The great ninth-century Mahayana Buddhist monument in central Java.
- **Chang'an.** The Tang capital, now Xi'an, the world's largest city in the eighth century CE.
- **Chola dynasty.** South Indian Tamil dynasty (c. 850–1279 CE) with substantial maritime power.
- **Goryeo.** The Korean dynasty (918–1392 CE) that gave Korea its English name; produced the Tripitaka Koreana and metal movable type.
- **Grand Canal.** The Sui-built waterway connecting the Yangtze and Yellow rivers.
- **Heian period.** Japanese era (794–1185 CE) of the imperial court at Heian-kyo (Kyoto), notable for the rise of distinctive Japanese cultural forms.
- **Indian Ocean trade.** The monsoon-driven maritime trading network connecting East Africa, Arabia, India, Southeast Asia, and China.
- **Khmer empire.** Cambodian state (802–1431 CE) that built Angkor.
- **Murasaki Shikibu.** Heian-period author of *The Tale of Genji*.
- **Nara period.** Japanese era (710–794 CE) of explicit Tang Chinese institutional importation.
- **Pala dynasty.** Eastern Indian Buddhist dynasty (c. 750–1170 CE), patron of Nalanda.
- **Silk Roads.** The overland trade routes connecting China to the Mediterranean.
- **Sogdians.** Iranian-language-speaking people of Central Asia who dominated long-distance Silk Road trade.
- **Srivijaya.** Sumatra-based Buddhist maritime empire (c. 650–1377 CE) that controlled the Strait of Malacca.
- **Sui dynasty.** Chinese dynasty (581–618 CE) that reunified China after centuries of disunity.
- **Swahili coast.** The East African coast of trading city-states from Mogadishu to Sofala.
- **Tale of Genji.** Early-eleventh-century Japanese novel by Murasaki Shikibu.
- **Tang dynasty.** Chinese dynasty (618–907 CE), often considered the height of pre-modern Chinese civilization.
- **Tripitaka Koreana.** The complete Korean-Chinese Buddhist canon carved on wooden blocks during the Goryeo period.
- **Unified Silla.** The Korean state (668–935 CE) that unified the peninsula.
- **Wu Zetian.** The only female emperor in Chinese history (reigned 690–705 CE).
- **Xuanzang.** Tang-dynasty Buddhist pilgrim (602–664 CE) who traveled to India and brought back hundreds of texts.
- **Yamato.** The early Japanese clan and state from which the imperial line derives.
