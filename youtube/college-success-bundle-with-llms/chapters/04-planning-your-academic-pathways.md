# Chapter 4 — Planning Your Academic Pathways
*How to stop drifting and start building a degree that fits.*

It is a Thursday in early February. A student in a red puffer jacket is standing at the registrar's counter, holding a printed transcript. Thirty-eight credits. Two semesters. The registrar slides a form across — *Change of Major* — and looks up.

*What are you switching from and to?*

*From economics. To… I'm not sure yet. That's actually what I came in to ask.*

Thirty-eight credits is not a small number. Some of those credits were economics requirements that won't transfer cleanly to whatever comes next. Some are general-education credits that count toward anything. Some are electives, fine in any direction. The student doesn't know which is which. They will spend the next ninety minutes on the second floor learning that distinction from an academic advisor named Kim, with a curriculum map on the wall and a degree audit on the monitor.

This chapter is about having that conversation earlier. Not in month fourteen, at the registrar's counter, already past the point where the question is cheap. In week three. Before the drift has taken you somewhere you didn't choose.

---

## Values Before Plans

The move that comes before any planning is a question most students never think to ask: *what do you actually value?*

Not what you are good at. Not what your parents think you should do. Not what the job market is paying for this year. What you value — the criteria your brain applies, mostly without your noticing, when it decides whether a thing is worth your time.

Here is why this matters. Goals are destinations; values are the criteria that make a destination feel worth arriving at. A SMART goal that is specific, measurable, attainable, relevant, and time-bound — but not connected to anything you actually care about — runs out of fuel by the third week of the semester. It looks rigorous on paper. It is sustained by nothing. Conversely, a fuzzy intention connected to a clear value can hold across years, because the criterion is always running, quietly eliminating options that don't fit and pulling toward the ones that do.

The order is: values first, goals second, plans third. Reverse the order and you build a plan that gets executed and possibly completed and, on graduation day, leaves you looking around a room of people you don't recognize, doing work you don't care about, wondering what happened. The plan worked. You just weren't the person who needed it.

Consider Mateo, sitting between two uncles at a June picnic. Uncle Nico says: figure out which jobs will be hiring in four years at the salary you want, then pick the major that gets you there. The other uncle says: pick what interests you — if you love what you do, you'll never work a day in your life. Mateo nods at both of them and realizes they might both be right. He wants something interesting. He also wants to be employed. The advice doesn't tell him what to do. It tells him he has at least two values in tension. That is already more information than most incoming students have.

There is a straightforward exercise for surfacing what you value. Take a list of words — *achievement, adventure, balance, belonging, community, contribution, creativity, curiosity, fairness, family, freedom, growth, honesty, independence, justice, security, service, stability* — and any others you want to add. Pick the five that produce the greatest sense of *this is worth my time*. Rank them. Sit with the ranking for a day. Pay attention to which small decisions in your week — how you spent Tuesday evening, why you liked or hated a particular class — align with the top three and which contradict them. The contradictions are information about the gap between your stated values and your operating ones.

Drew Carter, a sociology student, arrived at college undecided and changed his major three or four times. Each change was a recalibration against his values: *what does this major produce that I actually care about?* He weighed the trade-offs each time. He landed on sociology because it offered what he had identified as his core values — social awareness, understanding how systems shape lives. The path was not a straight line. The values were the compass.

Two warnings about this exercise. First, values don't have to be impressive. *I want financial security for my family* is a genuine value. So is *I want to work in my hometown*. The job of a value is to be loud enough to guide decisions, not to be heroic. Second, values are more stable than mood but not permanent. A first job you hate will teach you a value you didn't know you had. A class you love will surface another. The list you make today is a starting reading, not a contract.

## The Architecture of a Degree

Spread on an advisor's desk: a curriculum map. Color-coded. General education in pale blue. Major requirements in green. Electives in white. Each box is a course. Lines between boxes labeled *prereq*. The whole layout fills the page.

A student seeing this for the first time sees a wall of boxes. After ten minutes with the advisor, the boxes resolve into a structure. The structure is the architecture of a degree — and you cannot plan within it until you can see it.

Callout labels point to: "~60 credits, any major counts these," "~30 credits, sequence matters," "free choice — use deliberately," "optional, list on transcript." Student should see the degree not as a list of courses but as a structure with different kinds of slots that have different planning implications.] -->

A bachelor's degree runs about 120 credits, roughly 40 courses across four years of full-time study. Those 120 credits divide into four kinds.

**General education.** About half the degree, sometimes more. Set as requirements by the state or the institution. Composition, lab science, mathematics, social science, humanities, cultural studies. The institution's claim that a college graduate should be literate in certain domains regardless of major. Think of gen-ed as the base of the building — it holds up whatever you build on top, and most of it is poured whether you like it or not in your first two years.

**Major courses.** About a quarter of a bachelor's degree — roughly ten to twelve courses. These are the courses in your declared field, usually taken in sequence with prerequisites. You are typically required to declare a major by the time you have completed thirty credits, which is roughly the end of your first year. The major is not the whole building; it is the apartment you are responsible for furnishing.

**Electives.** Free-choice courses. The amount of room varies: some majors are credit-heavy and leave little slack; others leave substantial room. Electives serve three purposes. Exploration — try something you don't know yet if you'll love. Specialization — go deeper in an adjacent area. Or filler — you need credits to hit 120 and you pick what fits the schedule. Used well, electives can fund a minor. Used badly, they dissolve into whatever was convenient.

**Minor.** Optional. About five to eight courses in a secondary field, listed on your transcript as a structured collection of electives. A minor is useful when it complements the major — a business major with a foreign-language minor, a biology major with a statistics minor — or when it protects against narrowness in an otherwise technical degree. A minor is not magic. If you declare one, declare it for a reason you can articulate.

The bachelor's is not the only shape. An **associate's degree** runs sixty credits, two years, and comes in two flavors: applied (designed to enter a technical field directly) and transfer-focused (designed to complete the first two years of a bachelor's at a four-year institution). The flavor matters — an applied associate's may not transfer cleanly. A **master's degree** requires a bachelor's for entry and runs thirty to sixty credits, one to two years of full-time study. No general education, no electives — all major. A **doctorate** requires an additional ninety to 120 credits beyond the bachelor's, typically four to seven years, and is organized around producing original research. **Professional degrees** — MD, JD, PharmD, EdD — are doctorate-level credentials designed for practice rather than research, each with its own admission test, its own prerequisite coursework, and its own application timeline that ideally begins during the bachelor's.

Three things follow from understanding this architecture.

The first is that early courses are mostly insurance. Most first-year courses are general-education requirements that count toward any major. This is a feature, not a bug. It gives you twelve to fifteen credits of exploration before you have to commit. The system breaks when a student avoids choosing all the way through year two. It does not break when the choice is held open through year one.

The second is that major-specific sequences have hidden lock-in. Some majors — engineering, nursing, music performance, education leading to teacher licensure — require course sequences that begin in the first or second semester. A student who waits until year three to declare engineering will likely lose at least a year, because the prerequisite sequence hasn't been started. If you are seriously considering a sequenced major, start the sequence in year one even before you formally declare. The worst case is that the courses count as electives.

The third is that pre-professional tracks are cumulative. Pre-med, pre-law, pre-pharmacy are not majors. They are sequences of recommended courses and activities that run alongside whatever major you choose. A pre-med biology student takes the biology major plus the pre-med chemistry sequence. A pre-med English major takes English plus the same sciences. Either works. What doesn't work is beginning the pre-med sequence in year three. The courses won't fit, and the application timeline won't close.

Several majors also carry requirements beyond coursework. Nursing has clinicals — direct patient care in supervised settings, scheduling-intensive, often impossible to combine with outside work. Education has student teaching — a full semester in a real classroom, always in fall or spring, displacing most other commitments. Social work has practicums. Some majors require service learning tied to course goals. These cannot be added at the end. A student who decides in year three to add a teaching credential to an English degree may be looking at three additional semesters. The same student who declares that goal in year one routes all four years accordingly and finishes on the same timeline.

The architecture knowledge accelerates something important: the recognition of errors. The cost of a course-choice mistake scales with how late it is discovered. A student in week six of their first semester who realizes they don't want to be an economics major loses almost nothing — most of what they have taken is gen-ed. The same realization in week six of their fifth semester is six courses of economics that may not transfer to the new major. Earlier recognition, cheaper correction. Architectural awareness is what makes early recognition possible.

## Resources, Mentors, and the Inevitability of Change

Anton chose information technology because he was excited about computers and networking. Halfway through his first semester, he is failing an introductory IT course. He doesn't know what to do, and he doesn't know whom to ask. His high-school guidance counselor knew his name; she walked him through applications and celebrated his acceptance. She is not at State University.

He goes to the campus fitness center to clear his head. He runs into Noura from the same class. *I just came from my advisor*, she says.

Anton learns three things in the next ten minutes that he didn't know that morning. Advising is free. It's walk-in. He has an advisor assigned to him by name. He goes the next morning. Within thirty minutes, that person has helped him plan how to approach his instructor about the failing grade, located the tutoring lab, and described where senior students hang out so Anton can find out whether the major is right for him. He leaves still considering a change of major, but with a clear next step rather than a vague despair.

The resources Anton found exist on every campus. Most first-year students don't use them.

**Curriculum maps.** Single-page diagrams of the courses and sequences required for a specific degree. Available on the college website, in the academic catalog, in the advisor's office. Most students never read theirs cover to cover. Reading it carefully — once, in your first semester, with a pen — is one of the highest-leverage twenty minutes you can spend in college.

**Academic advisors.** People paid to help you navigate the institution. They know the requirements, the policies, the technology, the patterns of which decisions cause problems and which don't. The most expensive mistake a first-year student makes around advising is not visiting until something has gone wrong. Advising is most valuable when used preventively, in week three of every semester, not reactively in week eleven. Going twice a semester for the first two years builds the relationship; by year three, your advisor is recommending you for opportunities you wouldn't have heard about otherwise.

**Degree audit tools.** Online systems that compare your completed courses against your degree requirements and tell you what's left. Usually clunky. Usually accurate. Twenty minutes inside a degree audit in week three tells you what to register for next semester and what to ask your advisor about. Students who never open the tool plan from rumor and end up retaking courses.

Beyond the institutional resources, there is a category the literature calls the *support call list* — people in your life or on campus who play different roles in your planning. The categories are worth naming.

Someone who knows your interests. A parent, an old teacher, a close friend. Knows what you love and what you hate sometimes before you do. Useful for value clarification — you bring an unstated preference; they name it for you.

Someone who knows the institution. Your academic advisor, your professor at office hours, the registrar. Knows the policies and procedures. Useful for plan construction.

Someone who knows the field you're heading into. A working professional, a senior in your major, a faculty member whose research connects to what you want to do. Useful for the texture of the work — what a day in that career actually looks like, what the seasonal cycle is, what the trade-offs feel like from inside.

Someone who is a cheerleader. A best friend, a parent, a partner. Not informed about your specific plan; deeply invested in you. Useful for the days the plan is hard.

A mentor — someone who has done it. Someone further along in the field you want to enter, who can guide you in ways the other categories can't. Mentors are different from advisors: an advisor is paid by the institution; a mentor chooses you because they want to. Many career services and alumni offices facilitate mentor matches.

Building this network is slow and pays off years later. One of these people, on a hard day in year three, says something you needed to hear and didn't know was findable.

Now: plans change. The mature response to change is not to abandon the plan. It is to revise the plan while protecting the values.

Elena was pursuing a nursing degree when her grandmother — the person who inspired the path — fell ill and needed regular care. Elena stopped out. Her advisor helped her map an alternative: take prerequisite courses part-time at a community college near home, transfer them back when she returns. The values that drove the nursing path — care for family, honoring her grandmother's example — are not violated by stopping out. They are honored by it. The plan changes. The values do not.

Ray's parents wanted him to study business. He took the introductory business course and did fine. He took early Western civilization as a gen-ed and loved it. At the end of semester one, he's unsure. His advisor structures a second semester where the courses count toward either possible major — keeping options open while Ray gathers more information. The advisor's move is to lower the cost of the choice by refusing to commit prematurely.

The pattern in both stories is the same. When plans face pressure — external in Elena's case, internal in Ray's — the right move is neither to grit through with the original plan nor to give up on the goals. It is to revise the plan in light of what the values actually require now that more is known.

## The Three Things Together

Values, architecture, resources. They are not three versions of the same advice. They operate in different registers.

*Values* are the criteria. They precede plans. Without them, the plan you build will be optimized for someone else's goals. With them, every choice in the next four years has a reference point.

*Architecture* is the structure of the degree. Without architectural awareness, course choices are made through rumor, and students mistake electives for major requirements and gen-ed for waste. With it, every course in the schedule is recognized for what it does and what it does not commit you to.

*Resources* are the people and tools that close the gap. Your advisor knows the architecture; your advisor doesn't yet know your values. You bring the values; they bring the architecture. The conversation between the two produces a plan that fits.

Take Mateo again. In month one, he runs the values exercise. His top five: contribution, family, security, growth, fairness. He notices that contribution and fairness keep appearing together when he imagines kinds of work he would actually want. An introductory public-health course and a literature course on twentieth-century social movements both light him up against those values. Different fields; same criteria at work.

In week six, he reads his curriculum map with a pen. His current schedule is almost entirely gen-ed. He has committed to almost nothing. He uses week seven to attend a public-health major information session and week nine to talk with a sociology professor. He learns that the public-health major has a prerequisite sequence starting in semester two. He registers for the introductory public-health course as insurance — if he commits, he is on track; if he doesn't, it double-counts as gen-ed social science.

In week three of semester one, he goes to academic advising. By week eleven, the advisor mentions a career-services panel of alumni working in public-health policy. Mateo attends. He meets someone five years out who works on health equity at the state level. They exchange emails. He has a mentor in a field he didn't know existed when he sat between his uncles in June.

By the end of semester two, Mateo declares a public-health major with a sociology minor. Both uncles, at the next picnic, agree he picked something interesting and employable. Privately, Mateo knows that what worked was not balancing his uncles' advice. It was that he stopped letting them define the question and started running the values exercise on himself.

The student-success literature has converged on a finding that runs through all of this: students who succeed aren't the ones who arrive with the perfect plan. They are the ones who treat the plan as a continuously revised document and invest in the resources that let them revise it cheaply. The institution provides the architecture. The student provides the values. The advisors and mentors and tools are the bridge between the two. What looks like academic success from the outside is, on closer inspection, the disciplined practice of running this loop — values surfaced, architecture studied, resources used — across four years.

The registrar's counter on a February Thursday is not a failure. It is a plan meeting reality, which is what plans do. The question is only whether you meet it in month fourteen, having spent thirty-eight credits on a path you didn't choose, or in week three, when the correction costs almost nothing.

## LLM Exercises

**Exercise 4A — Values clarification via AI.**
Open a conversation with an AI assistant. Tell it you want to clarify your top five values and how they relate to your academic and career direction. Give it your initial list and let it ask follow-up questions. After the conversation, write a paragraph: where the AI's probing shifted your ranking, where it missed something, and what your actual top five are in your own words — with one sentence per value explaining what choosing that value *rules out*, not just what it rules in.

**Exercise 4B — Architecture audit with AI.**
Describe your degree program to an AI assistant — your major, the number of credits required, what you've completed so far, and any special requirements like clinicals or student teaching. Ask it to help you identify which of your completed courses fall in which architectural slot (gen-ed, major, elective), which prerequisite sequences are most time-sensitive, and what would happen to your timeline if you changed your major today. Then take the AI's output to one real advising appointment and ask your advisor where the AI was right, where it was wrong, and what it missed.

**Exercise 4C — Support call list via AI.**
Describe your current support network to an AI assistant using the five-category framework from the chapter: interest-knower, institution-knower, field-knower, cheerleader, mentor. For each category, say who currently fills it (or that the slot is empty). Ask the AI to help you identify which empty slots are most urgent and what a concrete first step looks like for filling each one. Then evaluate: which suggestions are realistic for your campus and situation, and which require the AI to know things about your life that it doesn't?
