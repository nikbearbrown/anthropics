# Chapter 7 — Visual Hierarchy and the Painter's Algorithm

## Opening: The change that should have taken thirty seconds

A science-communication figure — a diagram of how a vaccine trains the immune system, generated by Claude Code from a clean brief — is open in Illustrator. The task is trivial: the annotation pointing at the "memory B-cell" step is the wrong color, a default gray that should be the editorial red. Change one fill. Thirty seconds.

You open the Layers panel to find the annotation. It is not there. Or rather, it is everywhere and nowhere: the panel shows a stack of groups named `<Group>`, `<Group>`, `<Group>`, nested seven deep, and the annotation text is somewhere inside the fourth one — no, the fifth — buried among the arrow paths and the cell shapes and a clipping mask. You expand a group; it contains two more groups and a `<Path>`. You expand the next; three glyph outlines that used to be a word. There is no group called `annotations`. There is no group called anything.

So you start clicking directly on the canvas. You click the annotation text. Illustrator selects a clip group. You click again to enter it. You select an arrow that happens to share the group. You finally isolate the text, change the fill, and discover that the change moved the text *behind* the cell it was labeling — because the thing you thought was "the annotation layer" was actually painted *before* the cell, and the cell is now on top of it.

Forty minutes in, you understand the trap, and it is not incompetence — yours or the generator's. The file is organized by **the order it paints in.** And the order it paints in is, for the purpose of editing, organized by *nothing at all.* This chapter is about why those are two different organizations, why they conflict, and how to satisfy both at once. It is the hardest structural idea in the book, and the only way through is to understand both halves. [High]

## The Painter's Algorithm: how SVG decides what is on top

SVG has no z-index in the way CSS does. It has something older and simpler, borrowed from how a painter works: **later elements paint over earlier ones.** The renderer walks the document tree from top to bottom and lays down each element in turn, like a painter who paints the sky first, then the mountains over the sky, then the tree over the mountains. Whatever is painted last is on top. This is the **Painter's Algorithm**, and in SVG it is *the entire rule* for stacking. [High]

The consequence is strict and unforgiving: **document order is paint order is visual depth.** If you want the cell shape behind the annotation, the cell's markup must come *before* the annotation's markup in the file. Move the annotation earlier in the document and it slides behind the cell — which is exactly what happened in the opening, when editing reordered something and the label vanished under the shape it was labeling.

So the *rendering* requirement dictates a particular sequence: background first, then plot field, then data marks, then reference lines, then annotations, then the headline — back to front, far to near. A correct figure *must* be authored in roughly that order, because the order is the depth. [High]

## Editorial grouping: how a designer needs the file organized

Now stand at the Illustrator end. The designer's question is never "what painted first?" It is "where is *the annotation layer*?" "Where is *the median series*?" "Where is *the axis*?" The designer needs the file organized by **what things are** — by editorial meaning — so that "select all the annotations and recolor them" is one click on one named layer.

That is a *semantic* organization: a layer called `annotations` holding every annotation, a layer called `data` holding the series, a layer called `axes` holding the scaffolding. It groups by *kind*, by editorial role, by the unit a human wants to manipulate as a unit. [High]

Here is the collision, stated plainly: **the rendering order is back-to-front by depth; the editorial order is by-kind by meaning; and these are not the same grouping.** Annotations might need to paint at several different depths (some behind a callout box, some in front of the data), yet the designer wants them all in one editable layer. The data series paints in the middle of the stack, but it is the conceptual center the designer reaches for first. A file optimized purely for paint order is editorially mute — the opening's seven anonymous groups. A file optimized purely for editorial meaning, naively built, paints in the wrong order and the labels disappear behind the shapes. You cannot satisfy one organization by ignoring the other. This is the structural conflict, and it is *real*, not a matter of tidiness. [High]

## Why the conflict is invisible until Illustrator

The cruelty of this is that the browser never tells you. As Chapter 4 established, the browser is satisfied by correct rendering, and correct rendering only needs correct *paint order* — which the generator gets right, because it authored back-to-front by construction. The figure looks perfect. The conflict is dormant. It activates only at the editorial boundary, when a human opens the file to *change* something and discovers that the structure encodes depth but not meaning. AI-generated SVG is especially prone to this: it is technically valid and editorially hostile, correct for the first reader and useless to the third. [Medium]

## The resolution: a two-axis architecture

Before the architecture, one more idea earns its place, because it is what *visual hierarchy* means at the level the reader experiences. Paint order is not only an editing concern — it is also how the figure tells the eye what to read first. The headline paints last and sits on top, dominant; the data sits in the middle, central; the gridlines paint early and recede into the background. The Painter's Algorithm, read this way, is the mechanism of *visual hierarchy itself*: depth in the document tree becomes prominence on the page. A figure read in the right order — headline, then primary series, then the supporting reference line, then the source — is a figure whose paint order was authored to match its editorial priority. So the conflict is sharper than "naming versus stacking." It is that the *same* sequence has to do two jobs: signal prominence to the reader and resist editorial reorganization by the designer. The resolution must honor both. [High]

The way out is to stop treating "paint order" and "editorial meaning" as one organization and build them as two axes of the *same* structure. The Brutalist convention is a **multi-layer architecture**: a small number of top-level layers ordered by visual depth (satisfying the Painter's Algorithm *and* the reader's hierarchy), each containing **semantic sub-groups** named by editorial kind (satisfying the designer). You get depth from the *outer* order and meaning from the *inner* names.

![Paint-order depth stack on the left, editorial by-kind layers on the right, a red collision marker between them, and a resolution panel making depth the outer axis and meaning the inner axis](images/07-visual-hierarchy-and-the-painters-algorithm-fig-01.png)

*Figure 7.1 — Paint order organizes by depth and editorial grouping organizes by meaning; the collision (red) where a naively grouped label slides behind its mark is resolved by making depth the outer axis and meaning the inner axis of one tree.*

```svg
<svg viewBox="0 0 800 500">
  <defs> <marker id="arrow" .../> </defs>

  <g id="layer-background" data-name="Background">
    <rect width="800" height="500" fill="#FFFFFF"/>
    <rect x="64" y="48" width="..." height="..." fill="#F5F5F5"/>
  </g>

  <g id="layer-axes" data-name="Axes">
    <g id="axis-x" data-name="X Axis"> ... </g>
    <g id="axis-y" data-name="Y Axis"> ... </g>
  </g>

  <g id="layer-data" data-name="Data">
    <g id="series-median" data-name="Median series"> ... </g>
    <g id="series-mean"   data-name="Mean series">   ... </g>
  </g>

  <g id="layer-reference" data-name="Reference lines">
    <line ... stroke-dasharray="5 4"/>
  </g>

  <g id="layer-annotations" data-name="Annotations">
    <g id="callout-threshold" data-name="Threshold callout"> ... </g>
  </g>

  <g id="layer-titles" data-name="Titles">
    <text ...>Median vs. mean household income</text>
  </g>
</svg>
```

Read the structure twice. **Top to bottom, the outer layers are paint order**: background paints first and sits at the back; titles paint last and sit on top. The Painter's Algorithm is satisfied by the *sequence* of the layers. **Inside each layer, the sub-groups are editorial kinds**: `series-median`, `axis-x`, `callout-threshold`. The designer who wants "all annotations" clicks `layer-annotations` and gets them all, as a unit, because they were grouped by meaning even though they live at the depth the renderer requires. The two organizations are reconciled by making depth the *outer* axis and meaning the *inner* axis of one tree. [High]

This is the single design decision that turns a forty-minute editing nightmare into a thirty-second fill change — and no code generator will make it for you unless you specify it, because the generator optimizes for the rendering reader and the architecture serves the editorial reader.

## The naming details that break it

Three concrete failures sit between a correct architecture on paper and a correct file in Illustrator. Each is a place the design intelligence has to anticipate the tool.

**The dual-attribute strategy.** Illustrator maps an SVG `id` to a layer name — but it *escapes* characters illegal in its internal naming. An `id="2008-spike"` can re-emerge in the Layers panel as `_x32_008-spike`, because an XML name token may not begin with a digit, so Illustrator hex-escapes the leading `2` to `_x32_`. The same fate awaits spaces and other illegal characters. The convention defends against this two ways: author IDs that are *already legal* (start with a letter, hyphens not spaces, no leading digits — `spike-2008`, not `2008-spike`), and carry a parallel `data-name` attribute holding the human-readable label, which passes through untouched and gives the designer a clean name to read. The `id` is for the machine; the `data-name` is for the person. [Medium — verify the exact escaping pattern against the current Illustrator version]

**Clipping masks reorder editing, not just rendering.** A `clipPath` (Chapter 4) does more than lock geometry — when released in Illustrator, the clip group can collapse, scattering its contents and disturbing the careful layer order. Release masks deliberately, in the Verify phase, and re-confirm the layer order afterward.

**The one-way ticket.** Once the file is open in Illustrator, masks released, names confirmed, and the `.ai` saved, do not regenerate the SVG over it. Re-import compounds nesting — Illustrator wraps the already-wrapped tree, deepening the hierarchy each round-trip until the architecture you built is buried under generator scaffolding. Generation is upstream; editing is downstream; the trip runs one way. [Medium]

## Worked example: from anonymous pile to editable architecture

The science-communication figure from the opening, tracked across the boundary in the five-column form the research specifies.

| Design intent | Generated structure | Verification finding | Correction | Remaining risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Recolor all annotations in one move | Annotations scattered across paint-order groups; no `annotations` layer | "Select all annotations" is impossible | Author `layer-annotations` holding all annotation sub-groups | None if generated under the multi-layer spec |
| Label sits in front of the cell it points to | Annotation authored before the cell in document order | Editing reorders it; label slides behind the shape | Place `layer-annotations` after `layer-data` in document order | Manual reordering errors during editing |
| Layers panel reads to a stranger | Groups named `<Group>` ×7 | Panel encodes paint order, not meaning | `id` + `data-name` on every layer and sub-group | — |
| Callout for the 2008 step | `id="2008-callout"` | Panel shows `_x32_008-callout` | Author `id="callout-2008"`; add `data-name="2008 callout"` | Other illegal chars still escape; audit IDs |
| Clipped diagram stays movable | Diagram wrapped in `clipPath` | Releasing mask scrambles layer order | Release in Verify phase; re-confirm outer order | Easy to forget; checklist-enforced |

The lesson, in one sentence: **paint order and editorial meaning are two organizations of the same file, and production-ready SVG encodes both — depth as the outer axis, meaning as the inner.** [High]

The limit, in one sentence: the architecture is a human decision because *which* groupings are editorially meaningful — that "the median series" is a unit worth isolating, that "all annotations" is a thing someone will want to recolor at once — depends on what the figure is arguing, and that is not recoverable from the paint order the generator produced.

## AI Wayback Machine — Ivan Sutherland

> **Why the inventor of interactive graphics belongs here.** Ivan Sutherland's *Sketchpad* (1963) was the first system in which a drawing on a screen was not a picture but a *structured object* — lines and constraints the machine understood as parts, which a human could grab, move, and reorganize. Sutherland drew the line, decades early, between a *rendered image* and an *editable structure* — the same line this chapter walks. He also worked in the lineage that gave computer graphics the depth-ordering problem the Painter's Algorithm answers: deciding what is in front of what is one of the founding questions of the field he helped start.
>
> The connection to AI-assisted infographic production is direct. Sutherland's insight was that the value of a graphic on a computer lies in its *structure* — that an image you can only look at is worth less than one you can take apart and rebuild. An AI-generated SVG that renders but cannot be edited has regressed to the pre-Sketchpad world: a picture pretending to be an object. The multi-layer architecture is how you keep the file on Sutherland's side of the line.
>
> Try this prompt with an AI assistant: *"Explain how Ivan Sutherland's Sketchpad — treating a drawing as an editable structure rather than a rendered image — helps an engineer see why SVG paint order and editorial grouping must both be designed."* The model will likely reconstruct the structure-versus-image distinction; the judgment about *which* structure encodes *your* editorial intent is, again, the part it cannot supply. (Wayback target: Wikipedia "Ivan Sutherland"; the system itself is at "Sketchpad.")

## Bridge

Hierarchy organizes the structure — paint order and editorial grouping reconciled into one tree. But organization is not yet communication. The reader still has to *see* the right thing first, distinguish the primary series from the reference line, read the headline as a headline. That work is carried by color and type: not as decoration, but as the channels that signal what matters most. Chapter 8 takes the seven tokens and the three-font stack from Chapter 5 and deploys them as a semantic system — and confronts the accessibility checks, the opacity-compounding traps, and the text-export bugs that surface only when the file crosses into production.
