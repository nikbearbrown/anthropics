# Chapter 6 — Marks, Channels, and the Grammar of Infographics

## Opening: Six pictures, one truth, six different readings

The instructor puts up two variables and asks the room to read them. The variables are simple: the share of electricity generated by six energy sources in a single country, last year. Six categories, six numbers that sum to one hundred percent. Nothing exotic.

Then the same two variables appear six ways. A pie. A donut. A stacked bar split into six bands. A treemap of six nested rectangles. A bubble chart with six circles sized by share. And a plain horizontal bar chart, six bars, longest on top.

The instructor asks one question of each: *which source is third-largest, and by how much does it beat the fourth?* The room answers fast and confidently from the bar chart, and almost everyone is right. The room answers slowly from the pie and splits — was that wedge bigger than the one next to it? The bubble chart produces near-random guesses; people systematically underestimate the gap because they are comparing *areas*, and the eye is bad at areas. Same data. Same truth. Radically different reading accuracy. [High]

Here is the failure the room just lived through, and it is the failure this chapter exists to prevent: **the designer started from the form** — "I'll make a pie, pies are for parts of a whole" — instead of starting from the *relationship in the data and the task the reader has to perform.* The pie is not wrong because pies are bad. It is wrong because the reader's task was *ranking and comparison*, and a pie encodes part-to-whole on a channel — angle and area — that the human visual system reads poorly. The bar chart wins because it puts the same numbers on the channel the eye reads best: position along a common scale. [High]

The form-first habit is the single most common defect in AI-generated figures, because a code generator, asked for "a chart," will produce *a* chart — a plausible one — without ever asking what relationship the data expresses or what the reader needs to do with it. Supplying that question is the design intelligence layer. This chapter is its grammar.

## Marks and channels: Bertin's vocabulary

The foundational move belongs to Jacques Bertin, whose *Semiology of Graphics* (1967 French original; English translation by William J. Berg of the revised second French edition, University of Wisconsin Press, 1983) gave visualization its first systematic grammar. [High] Bertin's insight was to separate two things that intuition fuses.

A **mark** is the thing on the page: a point, a line, an area. Bars, dots, lines, nodes, icons, regions — these are marks. They are the nouns.

A **channel** (Bertin called these the *retinal variables*) is the visual property you vary to encode data *into* a mark: its position, its length, its size/area, its hue, its lightness (luminance), its orientation, its shape, its texture. These are the adjectives. You take a mark and you modulate one of its channels in proportion to a value.

The same data can ride very different channels. Encode energy share as the *length* of a bar and you have used position-on-a-common-scale, the best channel there is. Encode the same share as the *area* of a circle and you have used size, a much weaker one. The mark changed from rectangle to circle, but the decisive change was the channel — and the channel is what governs how accurately a human can decode the value. [High]

This is why "pick a chart type" is the wrong first question. The chart type is a *bundle* of mark-and-channel choices that someone made for you. The grammar of graphics — formalized by Leland Wilkinson in *The Grammar of Graphics* (1st ed. Springer 1999; 2nd ed. 2005) and operationalized in tools like ggplot2 and Vega — makes the bundle explicit: a graphic is marks plus channel encodings plus scales plus a coordinate system, composed deliberately rather than chosen off a menu. [High] You are not picking a pie; you are deciding to encode a part-to-whole relationship on the angle channel in polar coordinates, which, once stated plainly, you can evaluate against the alternatives.

The practical reason this matters for AI-assisted production is that the grammar *is decomposable into a specification*. A chart type is a black box you either accept or reject whole; a grammar is a set of independent decisions — mark, channel, scale, coordinate system — each of which you can state, defend, and hand to a code generator as a discrete instruction. When you decompose "pie" into "angle channel, area mark, polar coordinates, part-to-whole relationship," you can see, decision by decision, *where* the encoding goes wrong, and you can change one element without discarding the rest. The grammar turns an opaque preference into an auditable chain of choices — which is exactly the form the CAJAL specification (Chapter 10) and the DESIGN.md constitution require. A chart-type request is unspecifiable; a grammar sentence is a prompt. That decomposability is what lets the human keep authority over an encoding while the machine does the drawing.

## Some channels are more accurate than others — and we have measured it

The claim that bar beats pie is not aesthetic. It is the central, falsifiable result of visualization science, and it comes from a specific experiment.

In 1984, William Cleveland and Robert McGill published *Graphical Perception: Theory, Experimentation, and Application to the Development of Graphical Methods* in the *Journal of the American Statistical Association*. They ran controlled experiments in which people estimated the ratio between two encoded values, and they measured the error. From the results they ranked the channels — what later authors call the **elementary perceptual tasks** — from most to least accurate for reading *quantitative* values: [High]

1. **Position along a common scale** (most accurate) — e.g., bars on a shared baseline, dots on one axis.
2. **Position along non-aligned scales** — e.g., small multiples with separate baselines.
3. **Length** — e.g., stacked-bar segments not sharing a baseline.
4. **Angle / slope** — e.g., pie wedges, line gradients.
5. **Area** — e.g., bubble size, treemap rectangles.
6. **Volume / curvature** — e.g., 3-D charts.
7. **Color hue / saturation** (least accurate for quantity) — e.g., a heat map read as numbers.

![Ranked ladder of seven visual channels from position at the top to color hue at the bottom, with the position rung in red](images/06-marks-channels-and-the-grammar-of-infographics-fig-01.png)

*Figure 6.1 — Cleveland and McGill's perceptual channels, ranked by decoding accuracy; position on a common scale (red) sits at the top, hue at the bottom.*

Read the ladder twice, because two lessons live in it. First: **for quantitative comparison, prefer the top of the ladder.** Position dominates; area and hue are for when the task is *not* precise comparison. Second — and this is the rule the Brutalist color system already encoded in Chapter 5 — **hue is at the bottom for *quantity*, which is exactly why the system rations it to one accent and forbids it from carrying graded values.** Color is excellent for *categorical* distinction (this group versus that group) and poor for *magnitude* (how much more). The token rules were Cleveland and McGill's ladder, applied. [High]

Jock Mackinlay's *Automating the Design of Graphical Presentations of Relational Information* (1986, *ACM Transactions on Graphics*) took the next step: he built the ladder into an algorithm that matched data *types* and *tasks* to encodings, and showed that the matching could be partly automated. [High] That matters for this book's thesis in a precise way. Mackinlay proved that a *candidate* encoding can be generated mechanically — which is exactly what an AI code generator does well. What his system, and every system since, still cannot do reliably is select *relationship-first* against a specific communicative purpose and audience. The machine proposes; the human disposes. The gap between "a defensible candidate" and "the right choice for this reader" is the gap this chapter teaches you to close.

## Relationship-first: the FT Visual Vocabulary move

How do you start from the relationship instead of the form? The Financial Times' *Visual Vocabulary* gives the most usable practitioner taxonomy: it organizes chart families not by appearance but by the *relationship in the data* you are trying to show. [Medium — practitioner guide, not a research paper; cite as such] Name the relationship first, and the channel choice narrows itself:

- **Ranking** — order matters most → position on a common scale (bars, ordered dots).
- **Magnitude / comparison** — size-of versus size-of → length, position.
- **Change over time** — trend, trajectory → line (position over a time axis).
- **Part-to-whole** — components of a total → stacked bar or, sparingly, a single pie; but if the reader must *compare* parts, fall back to bars.
- **Distribution** — spread, shape, outliers → histogram, box plot, dot strip.
- **Correlation** — two variables move together → scatter (position on two axes).
- **Deviation** — above/below a reference → diverging bars from a baseline.
- **Flow** — movement between states → Sankey, network.
- **Spatial** — geography matters → choropleth, cartogram.

The discipline is one sentence: **say the relationship out loud before you name a chart.** "The reader needs to *rank* six energy sources" forces position-on-a-common-scale and rules out the bubble chart before it is ever drawn. This is the relationship-first rule the FT taxonomy encodes, and it is the antidote to the form-first failure the opening demonstrated.

## Infographics versus data visualization: a distinction with consequences

The two words are used interchangeably in practice, and that conflation is a design hazard worth naming. The distinction this book draws is by *purpose*, not by appearance: [Medium — the distinction is the book's framing, widely held but not a single-source empirical claim]

A **data visualization** is *analytical*. Its job is to let a reader extract values and relationships accurately — to *read off* the data. Optimize for perceptual accuracy: top-of-ladder channels, minimal furniture, the data unobscured.

An **infographic** is *editorial*. Its job is to make an argument or explain a process to a specific audience — to *carry a claim*. It still must be perceptually honest, but it adds annotation, sequencing, decomposition, and emphasis to guide the reader to a point. An infographic is a data visualization with an argument wrapped around it, and the wrapping is itself a set of design decisions.

The consequence is operational. Classify your concept first. If the task is "let the analyst find the outlier," you are building a data visualization and the channel ladder is nearly the whole story. If the task is "convince a policy audience that renewables crossed a threshold," you are building an infographic and the annotation, the single red accent on the crossing point, and the exclusion of everything irrelevant matter as much as the channel. The companion volume, *Brutalist D3 × Claude*, drills the 61 chart types for the analytical case; this book teaches the editorial design layer that wraps them. Keep the boundary clean.

## Worked example: the energy mix, six ways, corrected

Hold the data constant — six energy sources, one year, shares summing to 100% — and isolate the single decision. The research calls for side-by-side panels with the relationship held fixed; here is that comparison as a decision table.

| Reader's task | Relationship | Weak version (form-first) | Channel it uses | Corrected version (relationship-first) | Channel it uses |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rank the six sources | Ranking | Pie / donut | Angle + area | Ordered horizontal bars, longest on top | Position on common scale |
| Compare 3rd vs. 4th precisely | Magnitude | Bubble chart | Area | Bars with direct value labels | Position + length |
| Show one source crossed a threshold | Deviation / change | Rainbow stacked bar | Hue (×6) | Single line over time, red accent at the crossing, rest in ink/gray | Position + one categorical hue |
| Show the total splits into parts | Part-to-whole | 3-D exploded pie | Volume + angle | Single stacked bar, parts labeled directly | Length, with labels |

Three things to read out of the table. First, every corrected version climbs the Cleveland–McGill ladder toward position. Second, the corrected versions obey the Brutalist two-color ceiling — one red accent, the rest neutral — because the relationship, once named, almost never needs six hues; the rainbow stacked bar was using color to *substitute* for a clear channel choice, which is the tell. Third, the form-first column is exactly what a code generator returns when handed "make a chart of the energy mix" with no relationship specified.

The lesson, in one sentence: **the chart type is an output of the relationship-and-task decision, never an input to it.** [High]

The limit, in one sentence: the ladder tells you that position beats area for ranking, but it cannot tell you that *ranking* is the reader's task rather than *part-to-whole* — that classification depends on what you are arguing and to whom, and it is yours to make.

## AI Wayback Machine — Jacques Bertin

> **Why the cartographer who built the grammar.** Jacques Bertin, working at the French national mapping institute and later the École des Hautes Études, did something no one had done systematically: he treated graphics as a *language* with a grammar, separating the marks from the visual variables that encode data into them. Before Bertin, "make a good chart" was craft lore; after him, it was a system you could teach, test, and — crucially for this book — specify to a machine.
>
> The connection to AI-assisted production is exact and a little uncanny. Bertin's separation of mark from channel is precisely what lets you write a *specification* a code generator can execute: "encode share on the length channel of a bar mark" is a Bertin sentence, and it is also a prompt. The generator can render the sentence. It cannot decide that *length* — and not area, and not hue — was the right variable for this reader's task. Bertin gave us the grammar; the choice of which sentence to say remains human.
>
> Try this prompt with an AI assistant: *"Explain how Jacques Bertin's separation of marks from visual variables (channels) lets an engineer write an encoding specification that an AI can execute but cannot author."* Compare the answer to Cleveland and McGill's accuracy ladder — the model will likely reproduce both, and the place it goes quiet is the relationship-first task classification, which is yours. (Wayback target: Wikipedia "Jacques Bertin.")

## Bridge

Marks and channels govern the *individual* encoding — how one variable becomes one visible property. But an infographic is many encodings at once: a headline, a primary series, a reference line, three annotations, a source. The question of how those elements are *organized* — which sits in front, which recedes, what the eye sees first — is visual hierarchy. And here the book reaches its hardest structural moment: the order in which SVG *paints* its elements and the order in which a designer needs them *organized for editing* are not the same order. They are, in fact, in direct conflict. Chapter 7 makes that conflict experiential — and then resolves it.
