# Chapter 34 — Development and Inheritance
*One cell, 37 trillion destinations, and the shuffled instructions that made it possible.*

A single cell becomes a person in nine months. That sentence is so familiar it has lost its strangeness. Let me try to give it back.

The cell that becomes you starts with one nucleus containing 46 chromosomes — half from your mother, half from your father — in a volume about 120 micrometers across. Nine months later, that one cell has become roughly 37 trillion cells, organized into 78 organs, wired by a nervous system processing tens of billions of signals per second, and housed in a skeleton of 206 bones. This happened not by addition — by simply making more cells — but by differentiation: cells becoming different from each other, becoming skin or neuron or cardiac muscle, through a process so precisely choreographed that a deviation in week 4 can cause an absence of a heart chamber, while the same deviation in week 20 causes nothing at all.

This chapter is the story of how that choreography works, and why the instructions that run it are inherited the way they are.

---

## Fertilization: The Collision That Starts Everything

Of the hundreds of millions of sperm ejaculated, only a few thousand reach the fallopian tube. The vagina's acid environment kills most of the rest. The cervical mucus filters more. The uterine immune cells — stationed to protect against bacterial invasion — destroy more still. What reaches the egg is a remnant, and even those survivors must undergo capacitation: a four-to-six-hour chemical editing of their surface membrane, during which the cholesterol that stabilizes the membrane is stripped away. A sperm cell that has not been capacitated cannot complete the acrosome reaction. An uncapacitated sperm at the zona pellucida is useless. The body requires that a sperm earn its way into the female reproductive tract before it is allowed to compete.

The egg waits in the fallopian tube for about 24 hours after ovulation, surrounded by two concentric structures: an outer corona of follicle cells and an inner glycoprotein shell called the zona pellucida. No single sperm can penetrate the zona alone. The mechanism is collective: when sperm reach the zona, their acrosomes — enzyme-packed caps on the sperm head — rupture and release digestive enzymes. Hundreds of sperm contribute enzymes simultaneously, tunneling through the zona. Eventually, one sperm — the beneficiary of the hundreds of others' sacrifice — reaches the egg's plasma membrane and fuses with it. The sperm nucleus enters.

What happens next is one of the fastest and most decisive events in all of biology: the cortical reaction. A wave of calcium floods the egg cytoplasm within seconds. Vesicles just under the egg's surface rupture, releasing enzymes that harden the zona pellucida and destroy any remaining sperm-binding receptors on the membrane. The zona, which was permeable to sperm, becomes impermeable in less than a minute. Sperm still attached to the outside fall away. The egg can now accept no additional nuclear material. The reason is simple and brutal: if two sperm nuclei were to enter, the resulting cell would have 69 chromosomes instead of 46, and the developmental program it tries to execute would fail immediately.

The egg nucleus, which has been suspended in meiosis II since before ovulation, now completes that division, ejecting a second polar body. The female and male pronuclei swell, drift toward each other, and merge. You now exist as a diploid cell — 46 chromosomes, half from each parent. Within 12 hours, that cell divides.

## Eight Days of Becoming

The zygote divides, and its daughters divide, but at first they do not grow. The total mass stays roughly constant: the original volume of the egg distributed among increasingly many, increasingly small cells. By day three, the cluster is a morula — about 16 cells, still free-floating, roughly the size of a dust particle, living on secretions from the uterine lining. By day five, it is a blastocyst: 100 or so cells divided into two populations. The outer shell — the trophoblast — will become the placenta and fetal membranes. The inner cell mass, a clump of about 30 cells pressed against one side of the hollow sphere, will become the embryo itself.

At the end of the first week, the blastocyst presses against the uterine wall and adheres. The trophoblast cells release enzymes that digest the endometrial tissue, and the blastocyst burrows in. Roughly half to three-quarters of blastocysts fail at this step — the endometrium is not receptive, or the blastocyst itself has an undetectable defect, or the timing is wrong. When implantation succeeds, the trophoblast fuses into a syncytium — a single continuous mass of cytoplasm containing many nuclei — that continues digesting maternal tissue and anchoring the embryo. This syncytiotrophoblast begins secreting human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG). hCG travels to the corpus luteum — the transient endocrine structure left behind when the follicle ruptured at ovulation — and tells it: keep making progesterone. Without progesterone, the uterus would shed its lining. With it, the lining is maintained. A pregnancy test detects the hCG itself, which is present in urine at high enough concentrations by about day 12.

By day 14, implantation is complete. The embryo is 1.5 millimeters long. It is a flat disc of cells.

## Gastrulation and the Three Layers That Build Everything

Around week 3, something happens that determines what every cell in your body will become. A groove appears along the back surface of the embryonic disc — the primitive streak. Cells that were sitting on the disc's surface begin migrating toward this groove, sliding down through it, and then spreading laterally and downward underneath. The result is three cell layers stacked like a sandwich: ectoderm on top, endoderm on the bottom, mesoderm in the middle.

The division of fate is almost complete at this moment. Ectoderm cells will become your entire nervous system, your skin, your eyes, your ears, the lens of your eye, the enamel of your teeth. Mesoderm cells will become your muscles, your skeleton, your heart, your kidneys, your blood vessels, your connective tissue. Endoderm cells will become the lining of your gut, your liver, your pancreas, your lungs, your bladder. Every cell in every tissue in your body descends from one of these three layers.

The transformation is driven not by external force but by cell signaling — molecules secreted by one cell population that change the behavior of neighboring cells. The notochord, a rod-shaped structure forming along the embryo's midline, secretes sonic hedgehog protein, which in a gradient tells the cells above it what to become. Wnt proteins, fibroblast growth factors, and dozens of other signaling molecules establish gradients that cells read and respond to by activating different sets of genes. Each cell is essentially asking: what is my chemical environment, and what should I do given that environment? The answer changes depending on where the cell sits relative to the sources of these signals.

Neurulation follows gastrulation almost immediately. The ectoderm above the notochord thickens into a neural plate. The edges of this plate fold upward, forming a neural groove. The folds meet at the midline and fuse, sealing the groove into a hollow neural tube — the precursor to your entire central nervous system: spinal cord, brainstem, cerebellum, and cerebral cortex. The tube closes from the middle outward, reaching the head end by about day 25 and the tail end by day 28. If the tube fails to close — because of a folate deficiency, or a genetic defect, or both — the result is a neural tube defect. Spina bifida occurs when the posterior end fails to close, leaving spinal cord tissue exposed. Anencephaly occurs when the anterior end fails, and the brain does not form above the brainstem. The developing nervous system requires folate to synthesize the DNA needed for rapid cell division. A deficiency of 400 micrograms per day during weeks 3 to 6 of pregnancy substantially increases the risk. Because this window precedes most confirmed pregnancies, folate supplementation is recommended for anyone who might become pregnant — not just those already pregnant.

By week 4, the embryo has a heartbeat. A tube of cardiac cells has begun contracting before any circulatory system is complete. By week 5, limb buds have appeared — stubby projections from the body wall that will become arms and legs. By week 6, the hands have finger rays, which become distinct fingers only through apoptosis: programmed cell death kills the tissue between them, and the spaces between fingers are what remains when the cells that were there are gone. By week 7, the face has formed — nostrils, ear structures, fused eyelids. By week 8, the embryo is recognizably human. It is 3 centimeters long and weighs 8 grams. Every major organ system exists in some form.

## Nine Months of Fetal Development: What Happens After the Blueprint Is Drawn

After week 8, the entity is called a fetus rather than an embryo. The change is not merely semantic: organogenesis — the laying down of the basic architectural plan — is largely complete. What follows is growth, maturation, and refinement.

Weeks 9 through 12: bone marrow begins making red blood cells, taking over from the liver. The kidneys become functional and begin producing urine, which enters the amniotic fluid. The fetus begins practicing swallowing movements using that same fluid.

Weeks 13 through 20: sensory organs mature. Eyes move from the sides of the head toward the front. Ears migrate upward. By week 16 or 17, the pregnant person may feel the fetus moving — quickening — first as a flutter, then as unmistakable kicks. The skin is covered in lanugo, fine silky fetal hair, and vernix caseosa, a waxy coating that protects the skin from amniotic fluid.

Weeks 21 through 30: the lungs begin producing surfactant. This is one of the most critical developmental milestones because survival outside the womb depends on it. Surfactant is a mixture of phospholipids that lines the alveoli and reduces surface tension, preventing them from collapsing during expiration. Without surfactant, every breath requires reinflating collapsed alveoli from scratch — work so enormous that a premature infant cannot sustain it. Myelination of nerve fibers begins, coating axons in the lipid sheath that allows rapid signal conduction.

Weeks 31 through 40: fat accumulates under the skin. The fetus gains roughly 200 grams per week in the final trimester. Lanugo is shed. By week 40, the fetus weighs 2.5 to 4 kilograms. It is prepared to breathe, to regulate its own temperature, to feed, and to begin integrating sensory experience.

The fetal circulation is an engineering solution to a problem: the fetus cannot breathe, so the lungs are useless as an oxygenation organ, yet a cardiovascular system must function. Oxygenated blood arrives from the placenta via the umbilical vein. Much of it bypasses the liver through the ductus venosus — a shunt that allows blood to flow directly to the inferior vena cava. This blood reaches the right atrium, where most of it passes through the foramen ovale — a flap-like hole in the wall between the right and left atria — directly into the left atrium, then to the left ventricle, then to the aorta and the brain. Blood that does go to the right ventricle would normally flow to the lungs through the pulmonary trunk, but another shunt, the ductus arteriosus, connects the pulmonary trunk to the aorta, diverting that blood away from the lungs and into the systemic circulation.

The result: oxygenated blood reaches the brain and heart before it can be diluted with poorly oxygenated blood from the non-functional lungs. The system is optimized for a fetus with a placenta, not a fetus with lungs.

The first breath changes everything. When the infant inhales for the first time, the lungs inflate, pulmonary blood vessels dilate, and vascular resistance in the pulmonary circuit drops dramatically. Blood that was flowing through the foramen ovale — driven by higher pressure in the right atrium — now finds lower resistance through the lungs. Pressure in the left atrium rises. The pressure difference reverses, pressing the foramen ovale's flap against its rim and sealing it. The rising oxygen concentration in the blood causes the smooth muscle of the ductus arteriosus to contract, closing the shunt within hours. Over months, scar tissue closes both former shunts permanently. The rewiring happens automatically, triggered by the single event of inflation of the lungs with air. One breath, and the fetal circulation ceases to exist.

## Inheritance: Why You Are Not Quite Either Parent

The fertilized egg contains 46 chromosomes arranged in 23 pairs. For each pair, one chromosome came from your mother and one from your father. At each gene locus, you therefore have two alleles — two versions of each gene.

When both alleles are identical, you are homozygous at that locus. When they differ, you are heterozygous. In heterozygotes, the relationship between the two alleles determines what you look like — your phenotype. If one allele produces a functional protein even in the presence of the other, it is dominant. The other is recessive. A recessive allele only produces its phenotypic effect when both alleles at that locus are recessive — when there is no dominant allele present to produce the functional protein.

Gregor Mendel, working with pea plants in the 1860s, established the mathematical framework for understanding this. He crossed tall plants with dwarf plants. All offspring were tall — the tall allele dominated. He crossed those offspring with each other. Three-quarters of the next generation were tall, one-quarter were dwarf. The dwarf trait had not been lost; it had been hidden, carried silently in the heterozygous plants of the first generation and expressed when two recessive alleles finally came together in the second.

The mathematics is straightforward. If both parents are heterozygous carriers (call the dominant allele A and the recessive allele a), each parent produces two types of gametes: A and a. The possible combinations in offspring are AA, Aa, Aa, and aa — in proportions 1:2:1. AA and both Aa genotypes produce the dominant phenotype. Only aa produces the recessive phenotype. Three-quarters dominant, one-quarter recessive — Mendel's ratio.

But this ratio appears reliably only across large numbers. In a family with four children, the most likely outcome is three dominant and one recessive — but families with four recessives or four dominants are not rare, because each conception is independent. The dice have no memory. A couple who are both carriers of cystic fibrosis and have already had three unaffected children face exactly 25 percent probability with their fourth child. The prior outcomes provide no immunity.

Sex introduces another layer. Males have one X chromosome and one Y chromosome; females have two X chromosomes. Genes on the X chromosome that have no counterpart on the Y are called X-linked. A recessive allele on the X chromosome expresses in males because there is no second X to carry a dominant counterpart. In females, a recessive X-linked allele is masked if the other X carries the dominant version — she is a carrier. This is why color blindness, hemophilia, and Duchenne muscular dystrophy occur far more frequently in males: one copy of the recessive allele is sufficient for expression. A carrier female has a 50 percent chance of passing the allele to each son, and a 50 percent chance that each son will be affected.

Not all inheritance is simple dominance and recessiveness. In codominance, both alleles contribute to the phenotype simultaneously. ABO blood type is the canonical example: the IA and IB alleles both produce surface proteins on red blood cells, and a person with one of each (genotype IAIB) expresses both proteins, resulting in blood type AB. The i allele, which produces no surface protein, is recessive to both. A person with one i allele and one IA or IB allele expresses type A or B. A person with two i alleles has type O.

Most traits that vary continuously — height, skin color, intelligence, blood pressure — are polygenic: they are influenced by many gene loci simultaneously. Add in environmental effects — nutrition, sun exposure, education, stress — and the relationship between genotype and phenotype becomes a probability distribution, not a one-to-one correspondence. A person with genes for tall stature and chronic malnutrition grows short. A person with genes for average intelligence and exceptional education may perform at an exceptional level. Genes are instructions, not outcomes. They constrain possibilities but do not dictate them.

The evolutionary logic of sexual reproduction, which shuffles these alleles each generation, is this: it maintains diversity. In an environment that stays constant, diversity is expensive — most variants are worse than the optimum. But environments change, and infections evolve. A population that is genetically diverse is a population that is unlikely to be entirely wiped out by a novel pathogen, because some individuals will, by chance, have alleles that confer partial resistance. The cost of sex — its enormous inefficiency compared to asexual reproduction — is paid for by this insurance against catastrophe.

## The Permanent Instructions, Shuffled Each Generation

You began as a collision between one cell carrying half of your mother's genetic instructions and one cell carrying half of your father's. The collision fused them into a blueprint, and that blueprint spent nine months instructing the assembly of an organism. Every tissue was built from three ancestral cell populations. Every organ was orchestrated by cascades of chemical signals. Every nerve fiber was sheathed in myelin. Every alveolus was coated in surfactant. One breath rewired the cardiovascular system.

The instructions that ran all of this were inherited — half at random from one parent, half at random from the other. The randomness is the point. You are a new combination of old alleles, expressing some of them, carrying others silently. You will pass half of yours to your children, and which half is random again. The diversity produced this way is what made your ancestors able to survive ice ages, migrations, famine, and epidemic disease.

You are the temporary product of permanent instructions, shuffled anew each generation. The instructions will outlast you. What you do with the organism they built is the part they cannot control.

## Exercises

**Warm-up**

1. The cortical reaction occurs within seconds of sperm-egg fusion. Explain the mechanism — what triggers it, what it does to the zona pellucida, and what would happen if it were delayed by 60 seconds.

2. The neural tube closes from the middle outward, reaching the anterior (head) end by day 25 and the posterior (tail) end by day 28. A folate deficiency in week 5 affects the posterior closure more than the anterior closure. Explain why — and predict whether the resulting defect would be spina bifida or anencephaly.

3. In Mendel's pea experiments, crossing two tall heterozygous plants (Aa × Aa) produced a 3:1 ratio of tall to dwarf offspring across hundreds of plants, but a family with four children from two carrier parents might have zero dwarf children. Explain why, and explain why this does not contradict Mendel's law.

**Application**

4. A pregnant person drinks alcohol at week 6 of pregnancy. The embryo at this stage is undergoing which developmental process — gastrulation, neurulation, or organogenesis? Which organ systems are at highest risk from alcohol exposure at this specific stage?

5. The foramen ovale closes in response to the first breath. Trace the causal chain from lung inflation to foramen ovale closure, naming each intermediate step and explaining the pressure changes involved.

6. Two parents are both carriers for cystic fibrosis (genotype Cf/cf where cf is the recessive disease allele). They already have two unaffected children. What is the probability that their third child will have cystic fibrosis? What is the probability that their third child will be a carrier? Show your work using a Punnett square.

**Synthesis**

7. A woman who is a carrier for hemophilia A (X-linked recessive) has children with an unaffected man. Predict the probability that each of the following offspring will have hemophilia: (a) a son, (b) a daughter. Now predict the probability that each will be a carrier. Explain why the son has no probability of being an unaffected carrier.

8. Identical twins have the same genotype. One twin develops type 2 diabetes at age 45; the other does not. Using what you know about polygenic inheritance and the relationship between genotype and phenotype, explain how this is possible — and what the existence of such discordance tells us about the relative roles of genes and environment in type 2 diabetes.

9. A newborn is found to have a persistent ductus arteriosus — the shunt between the pulmonary trunk and the aorta failed to close after birth. Predict the consequences for: (a) blood oxygenation, (b) pulmonary blood flow, and (c) the long-term structural consequences for the heart and lungs if the defect is not corrected. Explain the mechanism at each step.

**Challenge**

10. Sickle cell trait (one normal allele, one sickle allele — genotype HbA/HbS) confers partial resistance to malaria caused by *Plasmodium falciparum*. The frequency of the HbS allele is much higher in populations from malaria-endemic regions of sub-Saharan Africa than in populations from non-endemic regions. Using Hardy-Weinberg logic and what you know about heterozygote advantage, construct an argument for why natural selection would maintain a deleterious allele (HbS causes severe disease in homozygotes) at high frequency in a malaria-endemic population — and predict what would happen to HbS frequency over many generations if malaria were eradicated from that region.
