# Chapter 19 — The Neurological Exam: Reading Damage by Its Function
*A cotton swab and a reflex hammer know more than they look like they do.*

---

A man arrives at the emergency department unable to move his left arm. An hour ago he felt pins and needles down his left side, then weakness spreading into his leg. The physicians do not yet know where the stroke has occurred. The brain contains perhaps eighty-six billion neurons distributed across structures that would take an atlas to name.

But in five minutes, a neurologist runs him through a series of tasks. Raise your eyebrows. Stick out your tongue. Feel this? What about this? Follow my finger. Squeeze my hands.

By the time those five minutes are over, the clinical team has a map. The lesion is not in the motor cortex. Not in the brainstem. It is in the right internal capsule — a white matter bundle carrying commands from the cortex down toward the spinal cord. Imaging will confirm this. But the imaging is not what pointed them there.

A cotton swab and a reflex hammer did.

This is the thing I want you to understand before we go any further: the neurological exam is not a collection of quirky clinical tests. It is a structured interrogation of the nervous system using behavior as the output. Every test asks one question: *if this part of the nervous system worked, could you do this?* The nervous system cannot lie in the way a patient sometimes can. If the reflex is absent, the arc is broken. If the Babinski sign is extensor, the corticospinal tract is damaged. The body is carrying the evidence, whether the patient knows it or not.

---

## Five Questions, Five Regions

The exam is divided into five sections, and the division is anatomical. Each section probes a different level of the nervous system and reveals what that level controls.

The **mental status exam** tests the highest functions: memory, language, orientation, judgment. You ask the patient their name, the date, who is the president. You ask them to remember three words and recall them five minutes later. You ask them to generate as many words beginning with a given letter as they can in sixty seconds. When these fail, something is wrong in the cerebral cortex — the outer layer of the brain where conscious experience lives. The cortex is organized anatomically: language in the left hemisphere in most people, spatial reasoning distributed across both, memory consolidation requiring structures in the temporal lobe. Mental status deficits tell you not just that the cortex is damaged, but often where in the cortex.

The **cranial nerve exam** tests the twelve nerves that serve the head and neck and the brainstem structures they connect to. Smell. Vision. Eye movement. Facial sensation and chewing. Facial expression and taste. Hearing and balance. Swallowing and the organs of the chest and belly. Neck muscles. The tongue. The exam is a tour through the brainstem — midbrain at the top, pons in the middle, medulla at the bottom. When the patient cannot feel the right side of their face, the trigeminal nerve is implicated. When both eyes won't move together, something has gone wrong in the oculomotor complex or the white matter tract that coordinates it.

The **sensory exam** tests the pathways of the spinal cord that carry information from the body to the brain. Light touch. Pain. Vibration. Proprioception — the sense of where your limbs are in space. These different sensory qualities travel in different pathways through the cord. Fine touch and vibration travel in the dorsal columns, ascending on the same side the stimulus entered before crossing in the medulla. Pain and temperature cross almost immediately upon entry into the spinal cord, traveling upward on the opposite side. This segregation means sensory deficits can tell you not just where the lesion is, but how the cord has been divided.

The **motor exam** tests the corticospinal tract — the pathway from the motor cortex down through the brainstem and spinal cord to the muscles. You watch for muscle tone. You test strength against resistance. You look for asymmetry: a patient whose left arm drifts downward when both arms are held out straight is showing you a lesion on the right side of the brain. The motor commands cross in the medulla, so right-brain damage causes left-body weakness.

The **coordination exam** tests the cerebellum, which compares motor commands with sensory feedback to keep movement smooth and accurate. The patient touches their finger to their nose, then to the examiner's finger, back and forth, with eyes open and then closed. The patient walks heel-to-toe along a line. The cerebellum does not initiate movement — the motor cortex does that. The cerebellum corrects movement in real time, like a feedback controller that is always comparing intended trajectory to actual trajectory and adjusting. When it fails, movement becomes clumsy. The arm overshoots. The gait staggers. Not because the muscle is weak, but because the error-correction system is offline.

Together these five sections create a map. They do not always point to one location cleanly — a patient who cannot remember three words might also fail to follow commands because language itself is damaged, and both symptoms live in the cortex. But each section illuminates a different level of the nervous system, and the pattern of intact versus impaired function is the signature of a lesion.

## The Brainstem as a Tour Map

The twelve cranial nerves emerge from the brainstem at specific levels. This makes cranial nerve deficits extraordinarily useful for localization, because the brainstem is small — perhaps the width of your thumb — and its structures are densely packed together. Damage that disrupts one cranial nerve almost always disrupts its neighbors.

Take the hypoglossal nerve, the twelfth cranial nerve, which controls the tongue. The test is simple: stick out your tongue. In a healthy patient, it goes straight. In a patient with a lesion on one side of the medulla, the tongue deviates toward the side of damage.

Why toward the damaged side? Here is the mechanism, and it is worth slowing down to understand it. The corticospinal tract — the major motor pathway — has already crossed in the medulla before it reaches the hypoglossal nucleus. So damage to the right side of the brain affects the left side of the body. But the hypoglossal nerve controls the ipsilateral tongue muscles. A right-sided medullary lesion damages the right hypoglossal nucleus directly, weakening the right-side tongue muscles, so the tongue points right — toward the damage — when you push it out.

This seems counterintuitive when you first encounter it. The motor weakness from a cortical stroke goes to the opposite side. But the cranial nerve nuclei in the medulla are on the same side as the muscles they control. When the lesion is in the cortex, the weakness is contralateral. When the lesion is in the medullary nucleus itself, the weakness is ipsilateral. The tongue deviation in a stroke patient thus tells you something specific: is the damage in the cortex or in the medulla?

Now notice what else lives near the hypoglossal nucleus in the medulla: the vagal nuclei that regulate heart rate and swallowing, the respiratory nuclei, the cardiovascular control centers. A lesion large enough to damage the hypoglossal nucleus likely damages these neighbors too. The tongue becomes a window onto the whole region. Testing one nerve tells you about the neighborhood.

Eye movements give you a tour through a different region. Three nerves — the oculomotor (III), trochlear (IV), and abducens (VI) — control the six muscles that move each eye. These nerves must be coordinated so that both eyes move together. The abducens nucleus is in the pons; the oculomotor nucleus is in the midbrain. A white matter bundle called the medial longitudinal fasciculus, running up through the brainstem, connects these nuclei and synchronizes their activity. When you track a moving object, both eyes move smoothly in the same direction because the MLF is keeping the nuclei coordinated.

When the MLF is damaged — which happens characteristically in multiple sclerosis, where demyelinated plaques occur in the brainstem — one eye fails to move inward while the other moves outward during horizontal gaze. This is called internuclear ophthalmoplegia, and it is almost pathognomonic for MS when it appears in a young patient. The symptom is seemingly trivial: one eye doesn't track quite right. But it is evidence of a specific lesion in a specific white matter tract in the brainstem. The exam found it with a moving finger.

The gag reflex is a tour through the medulla in functional form. The examiner touches the back of the throat. Sensory information travels along the glossopharyngeal nerve (IX) to the nucleus in the medulla. The motor response — contraction of the pharyngeal muscles — travels back out along the vagus nerve (X). These two nuclei are anatomical neighbors. A stroke affecting one commonly affects the other. The absence of a gag reflex is evidence of medullary damage. Its presence confirms the arc is intact.

This is the principle of the cranial nerve exam. Each nerve emerges from a specific location. Its location predicts its neighbors. Testing the nerve tests the region, and the region contains more than just that nerve's nucleus. The brainstem is dense enough that almost every clinically significant cranial nerve sign implicates a neighborhood rather than a single structure.

## Upper Motor Neuron vs. Lower Motor Neuron: The Reflex as Evidence

Motor control has two stages. The first is the upper motor neuron — a cell in the motor cortex whose axon descends through the brainstem and spinal cord as the corticospinal tract. The second is the lower motor neuron — a cell in the ventral horn of the spinal cord whose axon extends out to skeletal muscle. Most upper motor neuron axons cross in the pyramids of the medulla.

Damage at different levels produces different signatures, and those signatures are the key to localization.

A patient with an upper motor neuron lesion — a stroke in the motor cortex, a tumor pressing on the pyramids, a lesion in the spinal cord above the lower motor neuron — loses voluntary movement. But the resting muscle tone is preserved. In fact, it is increased. The muscles are tight, contracted, spastic. The deep tendon reflexes — the knee-jerk, the ankle-jerk — are exaggerated. Tap the patellar tendon and the quadriceps fires vigorously. Stretch a muscle passively and the patient cannot relax it — the muscle resists, then suddenly releases like the closing of a pocket knife. This clasp-knife response is almost diagnostic.

A patient with a lower motor neuron lesion — damage to the nerve root, peripheral nerve, or the motor neuron itself — has the opposite picture. The muscle is paralyzed or severely weakened. But the tone is gone. The muscles are flaccid, floppy, and atrophied. The deep tendon reflexes are absent or severely diminished.

To understand why, you need to understand what the upper motor neuron is actually doing. It is not the only signal the muscle receives. The lower motor neuron maintains a background level of activity even without cortical commands — it receives input from the spinal cord's own circuits and from the stretch reflex. The upper motor neuron modulates this activity, in part by inhibiting reflex responses that would otherwise be exaggerated. When the upper motor neuron is cut, this inhibition is lost. The lower motor neuron is still alive and reflexively active. The reflex arc — sensory input from the muscle to the spinal cord, direct synapse onto the motor neuron, motor output back to the muscle — is intact. With the inhibitory descending command gone, the reflexes run unchecked. The muscle becomes hyperreflexive and spastic.

When the lower motor neuron is cut, the reflex arc itself is broken. The neuron that would respond to the stretch stimulus is dead. There is nothing to fire, nothing to contract, nothing to maintain tone. The muscle wastes.

The Babinski sign reveals this distinction beautifully. Brush the outer sole of the foot from heel to toe. In a healthy adult, the toes curl downward — plantar flexion. In an infant, or in an adult with upper motor neuron damage, the big toe extends upward and the other toes fan out — the extensor response. This reversal happens because the corticospinal tract, as it matures during the first two years of life, establishes descending inhibition of the extensor reflex. When that descending control is lost — through damage anywhere along the corticospinal tract — the primitive reflex re-emerges. The foot reverts to its infantile response.

A third inset showing the corticospinal tract descending from cortex through medullary pyramids to the spinal cord, with a marker indicating "UMN lesion anywhere along this path produces positive Babinski."] -->

This is the power of the reflex in clinical localization. A positive Babinski sign in an adult is not just a reflex abnormality. It is evidence that the corticospinal tract is damaged somewhere between the motor cortex and the level of the spinal cord that serves the foot. Combined with the other motor signs, it answers the question: is the damage above or below the point where the corticospinal tract synapses on the lower motor neuron?

Consider now the patient from the emergency department: weakness in the right arm and leg, extensor Babinski on the right, normal mental status, intact cranial nerves except the tongue deviates to the right. The weakness is on the right, so the lesion must be on the left — the corticospinal tract has crossed. But where? Mental status is intact, so the cortex is not broadly damaged. Cranial nerves are mostly intact. But the tongue deviates to the right, toward the patient's right. This means the right hypoglossal nucleus is damaged — a structure in the medulla. Simultaneously, the corticospinal tract was damaged at that same level, before it had fully crossed, affecting the ipsilateral descending tract. The lesion is in the right medulla.

In five minutes, without imaging, the exam has narrowed the lesion to one side of one level of the brainstem. The MRI will confirm it. But the exam told the story first.

## The Sensory Exam and the Architecture of the Cord

The sensory exam has a feature that makes it especially powerful for spinal cord localization: different sensory modalities travel in different parts of the cord.

Fine touch and vibration enter the dorsal horn and immediately ascend in the dorsal columns on the same side. They do not cross until they reach the medulla. Pain and temperature cross in the cord almost immediately after entry, ascending in the spinothalamic tract on the opposite side.

<!-- → [DIAGRAM: Spinal cord cross-section showing the two major ascending sensory pathways — dorsal columns (ipsilateral ascent, crosses in medulla) carrying fine touch and proprioception, and spinothalamic tract (crosses immediately in cord, contralateral ascent) carrying pain and temperature. A Brown-Séquard scenario shown: right-side cord hemisection marked, with arrows showing which modalities are lost on which side of the body below the lesion. Caption: "The anatomy of the cord predicts the exam findings before the exam is performed."] -->

This means that a lesion cutting one half of the spinal cord — the classic Brown-Séquard syndrome, usually from penetrating trauma — produces a distinctive pattern: on the side of the lesion, the patient loses fine touch and proprioception (dorsal column on that side is gone), but retains pain and temperature (those signals crossed to the other side and are ascending safely). On the opposite side, the patient loses pain and temperature below the level of the lesion (the spinothalamic tract from those segments is gone), but retains fine touch (ascending on the intact ipsilateral dorsal column).

No imaging needed to predict this pattern. The anatomy of the cord predicts it. The exam confirms it. When you find that a patient has lost pain sensation on the left below the waist but retained it on the right, while losing proprioception on the right below the waist but retaining it on the left, you know the right half of the cord is damaged. The level of the lesion is the dermatomal level where the deficits begin.

Dermatomes are the clinical geography of the cord's sensory organization. Each spinal nerve supplies sensation to a specific band of skin. T4 supplies the chest at the nipple level. L1 supplies the groin. S1 supplies the heel. A band of reduced sensation corresponds to a compressed or damaged nerve root. A level below which all sensation is reduced or absent corresponds to a cord lesion. The examiner works systematically down the body, asking the patient at each level: can you feel this? Equal on both sides? Is this sharp or dull?

The answers draw a map. The map has an address. The address tells you where to look.

## The Exam as an Instrument

The neurological exam is structured the way it is because the nervous system is structured the way it is. The brain contains many functions — cognition, sensation, movement, balance, memory — but these are not distributed randomly. They are localized. A lesion in one region produces a specific pattern of loss. The exam is designed to elicit that pattern quickly and convert it into an anatomical address.

In the emergency department, this address is urgent. A stroke patient has hours — sometimes two or three — during which thrombolytic therapy might restore blood flow and save tissue. The exam does not diagnose the stroke. Imaging does that. But the exam tells the team where to look and how serious the damage might be. It is the first step in triage.

In the classroom, the exam serves a different purpose. It makes the nervous system visible. The cranial nerves are not just twelve items to memorize — they are a functional tour through the brainstem, and working through the exam means working through the brainstem's geography. The sensory exam is not just a test of touch and pain — it is evidence of the segregation of function within the spinal cord, a division that the Lectures on Physics style of thinking would call elegant: the same input (peripheral tissue), two different pathways, two different crossing levels, producing two different deficits when damaged. The reflex exam is not just a measure of reflexive response — it is evidence of a two-stage motor system with inhibitory top-down modulation, evidence that disappears in predictable ways when the system breaks.

You cannot fully understand the exam without understanding the anatomy. You cannot fully understand the anatomy without working through the exam. This is not circular. It is the nature of a system where structure and function are inseparable: the function is how you read the structure, and the structure is how you explain the function. The exam is the instrument that makes this reading possible.

## LLM Exercises

The following exercises are designed to be worked through with a language model. In each case, do not just ask for the answer — ask the model to explain its reasoning, then probe that reasoning.

**Exercise 1 — Localizing a stroke from its deficits.** Describe this patient to a language model: "A 58-year-old presents with sudden right-sided weakness in the arm and leg, an extensor Babinski sign on the right, and loss of sensation to pain and temperature on the left side of the body below the neck. Mental status and cranial nerves are intact." Ask the model to localize the lesion using the anatomy of the corticospinal tract and spinothalamic tract. Then push: why is the weakness on the right but the sensory loss on the left? At what level must the lesion be, and why is that level consistent with the absence of cranial nerve deficits?

**Exercise 2 — Upper vs. lower motor neuron.** Ask a language model to compare the presentation of a patient with an upper motor neuron lesion to one with a lower motor neuron lesion — specifically: muscle tone, strength, deep tendon reflexes, and presence or absence of the Babinski sign. Then ask: what is the underlying mechanism that explains why upper motor neuron lesions produce hyperreflexia while lower motor neuron lesions produce areflexia? Why does the Babinski sign emerge in adults with upper motor neuron damage but disappears as normal infants mature?

**Exercise 3 — The tongue as a localizing sign.** A patient has left-sided weakness in the arm and leg (upper motor neuron pattern) and a tongue that deviates to the right on protrusion. Ask a language model to explain why the tongue deviation is ipsilateral to the brainstem lesion while the limb weakness is contralateral. Draw a diagram of the relevant anatomy if helpful, then ask the model to predict what other deficits might be present given the location of the lesion in the medulla.

**Exercise 4 — Brown-Séquard reasoning.** Ask a language model to explain Brown-Séquard syndrome — the pattern of deficits that results from damage to one half of the spinal cord. Then give it a specific scenario: "A patient with a knife wound to the right side of the neck at C5 has lost proprioception and vibration sense in the right arm and leg, but retains pain sensation on the right. On the left side, they have lost pain and temperature below the neck but retain proprioception." Ask the model to explain why these deficits are on opposite sides of the body, tracing each modality's pathway through the cord and the level at which it crosses.

**Exercise 5 — Designing a five-minute exam.** Ask a language model to design a rapid five-minute neurological exam for an emergency department patient suspected of having a stroke. For each test in the sequence, ask the model to explain: what nervous system structure is being tested, what a positive finding would indicate, and what level of the neuraxis the deficit would implicate. Then ask: what is the minimum set of tests that would reliably distinguish a cortical stroke from a brainstem stroke from a spinal cord lesion?
